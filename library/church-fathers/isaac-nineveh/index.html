St Isaac of Nineveh, Isaac the Syrian

TimeSeventh century. Born perhaps about 613 (a modern estimate). Bishop about 676 to 680. Died about 700, very old
WhereBorn in Beth Qatraye, on the Persian Gulf (today Qatar and the coast around it). Bishop of Nineveh (near today's Mosul, Iraq). Hermit and monk in Beth Huzaye (today Khuzestan, south west Iran), near the monastery of Rabban Shabur
What he wasMonk, hermit, for a few months bishop, spiritual teacher
LanguageSyriac
Known asIsaac the Syrian (so called in the Greek and Slavonic Churches; in Russian, Isaak Sirin); Mar Isaac
HonouredA saint "by all traditions", in the words of Pope Francis. Inscribed in the Roman Martyrology of the Catholic Church, announced by Pope Francis on 9 November 2024. Byzantine Churches keep his day on 28 January, with St Ephrem. A father of the Church of the East, whose East Syriac tradition the Chaldean and Syro-Malabar Churches share. Not a Doctor of the Church
His honour On 9 November 2024, receiving Mar Awa III, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, Pope Francis announced that "the great Isaac of Nineveh, one of the most venerated Fathers of the Syro-Oriental tradition", would be added to the Roman Martyrology. The Pope said that Isaac is acknowledged "as a teacher and a saint by all traditions". The Dicastery for Promoting Christian Unity, in a note published by the Holy See Press Office, explained that this shows that holiness reaches beyond the divisions between Churches, and recalled the Second Vatican Council (Unitatis Redintegratio 4). Isaac belonged to the Church of the East, which was not in communion with Rome. The Pope had received the consent of Mar Awa III and of the Chaldean Patriarch. We have not seen the new entry in a printed Martyrology, so we do not give a Catholic feast day here. This page uses the words of the East Syriac tradition, as the Syro-Malabar Church does, where it speaks of that liturgy.
His life

What we know, and from where. Our main source is a short notice in the Book of Chastity by Ishoʿdnah of Basra, a writer of the Church of the East in the ninth century. A later West Syriac account adds more stories, but scholars treat it with caution. So the life is short, and some of it is uncertain.

From the Gulf. Isaac was born in Beth Qatraye, the region of eastern Arabia on the Persian Gulf around today's Qatar. The Vatican's note of 2024 says simply that he was born in present-day Qatar. One modern estimate puts his birth about 613. The Church of the East had many Christians there, with bishops and monasteries, and the region produced several Syriac writers. Isaac became a monk. He studied the Scriptures deeply and the writings of the monastic Fathers in Syriac.

Bishop of Nineveh. The Catholicos Giwargis (George) I, who led the Church of the East from 661 to 680, ordained Isaac bishop of Nineveh, the ancient city across the Tigris from today's Mosul. This was perhaps about 676, when Giwargis held a synod in Beth Qatraye. Isaac was bishop for only a short time; the old notice says five months. Then he resigned. The notice says only that the reason is one that God knows. Later stories guess at it, but we do not know.

Hermit. He withdrew to the mountain of Matut in Beth Huzaye, in today's south west Iran, to live in solitude among hermits. Later he lived in the monastery of Rabban Shabur, near the city of Shushtar. There he wrote the discourses that made him famous. He read so much, the sources say, that in old age he became almost blind. He died very old, about the end of the seventh century, and was buried at Rabban Shabur.

The world he lived in

A Church from the Euphrates to China. Isaac lived in the Church of the East, the Church of the Persian Empire. In his century its monks and missionaries reached Central Asia and China, and it cared for the Christians of St Thomas in India, who received their bishops from it for many centuries. Its language of worship and learning was Syriac.

A new empire. In Isaac's youth the Arab armies brought the Persian Empire to an end (the 630s and 640s). The lands of the Church of the East came under the rule of the caliphs. Christians remained numerous, with their bishops, schools and monasteries, but now under Muslim rulers.

A golden age of monks. The seventh and eighth centuries were a great time for East Syriac monastic writers. Isaac read the earlier masters, especially John the Solitary (Yohannan Ihidaya) and Evagrius, whose works were in Syriac. Other spiritual writers, such as John of Dalyatha and Joseph the Seer, came soon after him.

The Church of the East and the councils. His Church had not received the Council of Ephesus (431). The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910 called him a "Nestorian bishop", as was usual then. But his writings are about prayer, humility and the love of God, and they speak to all Christians. Since the Common Christological Declaration of 1994, signed by Pope St John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV, the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East confess together that Christ is true God and true man, one Person, and say that the divisions of the past "were due in large part to misunderstandings".

What he taught
  • God's love is beyond measure. At the heart of his teaching is the immensity of God's love, and our answer of wonder and humility, as Sebastian Brock sums it up. Everything God does, even his correction, comes from love.
  • A merciful heart for every creature. Asked what a merciful heart is, he answers: "The burning of the heart unto the whole creation, man, fowls and beasts, demons and whatever exists", so that the eyes shed tears for compassion, and such a person prays even for enemies of the truth and for those who do harm (Part I, Wensinck's translation, treatise 74, p. 341).
  • Repentance, purity, perfection. In the same place he gives the whole spiritual course in three steps: repentance, which is to turn from sins and grieve for them; purity, which is "a heart full of mercy unto the whole created nature"; and perfection, which is deep humility.
  • The Kingdom is within. "Be on peaceful terms with thy soul; then heaven and earth will be on peaceful terms with thee." "The ladder unto the Kingdom is hidden within thee." (Part I, Wensinck's translation, p. 8.)
  • Know your sins, and know his mercy. Pope Francis quoted Isaac in his Chrism Mass homily of 28 March 2024: "The one who forgets the greatness of his sins forgets the greatness of God's mercy in his regard." Pope Francis also quoted his teaching that those who acknowledge their sins and grieve for them are greater than those who raise the dead by their prayers.
  • Believe in the Resurrection. "The greatest sin is not to believe in the power of the Resurrection" (Ascetical Homilies I, 5). Pope Francis quoted this at Easter 2023, and Pope Leo XIV quoted it to the Eastern Churches on 14 May 2025.
  • Stillness and prayer. He loves silence and solitude, the quiet in which the heart can hear God. Prayer begins with words, but it can grow into a wonder beyond words. True prayer needs humility and tears of repentance.
  • Hell and the scourge of love. He writes that those in hell are tormented by the scourge of love: the grief of having sinned against love is sharper than any other pain (Part I, Wensinck's translation, p. 136). God's love does not stop; but those who refuse it suffer from it.
  • Humility above all. "Humility is the garment of divinity", he writes, for the Word made man put it on and spoke with us in it; whoever puts it on takes his likeness. "Who lacks humility, lacks perfection" (Part I, Wensinck's translation).

A hope for all, and the Church's teaching

In the Second Part of his writings, found only in 1983, Isaac goes further. He speaks of a hope that God's love will in the end overcome all evil, so that the suffering of Gehenna will not be without end. "It is not the way of the compassionate Maker to create rational beings" in order to deliver them to unending affliction, he writes (Part II, chapter 39, translated by Sebastian Brock). Scholars still debate how to read these chapters. Some see in them a full teaching that all will be saved; others see a prayerful hope and a meditation on God's mercy.

Here the Catholic Church does not follow him. She teaches that hell is real and eternal, the state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God, chosen by refusing love to the end (CCC 1033 to 1035). At the same time she prays that all may be saved, because God "desires everyone to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4; CCC 1037, 1821). Isaac's words can be read with profit as a cry of mercy and a call to pray for all, while the reader holds to the Church's teaching.

How he taught

Short chapters for monks. He wrote discourses and "chapters", short sections, many of them answers to questions from monks. Some are a few lines, some are long letters. They were meant to be read slowly, in the cell, with prayer.

From experience. He writes as one who has prayed for many years. He often says that only one who has tasted these things can understand them. His tone is warm and personal.

Sayings that stay. He has a gift for short, strong sentences that a reader can carry all day: on peace with oneself, on the ladder within, on the merciful heart.

Scripture and the Fathers. He quotes the Bible constantly and draws on the earlier monastic masters, especially Evagrius and John the Solitary, read in Syriac.

Centuries. In the Second Part, chapter 3 holds four "Centuries", collections of a hundred short sayings each, on spiritual knowledge, modelled on Evagrius.

His works

Abdishoʿ bar Brikha, a writer of the fourteenth century, says Isaac wrote seven volumes. What is known today is arranged in "Parts".

First Part

82 chapters, the best known. Much of it was put into Greek at the monastery of Mar Saba near Jerusalem in the late eighth or early ninth century. From Greek it passed into Slavonic, Russian (in the Philokalia), Latin and other languages. Arabic versions also exist

Second Part

41 chapters, kept complete in one early manuscript. Sebastian Brock found it in the Bodleian Library, Oxford, in April 1983. Chapter 3 contains the four "Centuries" on spiritual knowledge

Third Part

17 chapters, known from a manuscript in Tehran copied about 1900. Two chapters repeat the First Part and one the Second

Fifth Part, and works not his

Some fragments are said to come from a Fifth Part; scholars do not agree. The Book of Grace, once given to him, is probably by Shemʿon d-Taybutheh. The Greek collection also added three works of John of Dalyatha and a shortened letter of Philoxenus under Isaac's name

Editions and translations. Paul Bedjan published the Syriac of the First Part (1909). A. J. Wensinck translated it into English as Mystic Treatises by Isaac of Nineveh (Amsterdam, 1923). The Holy Transfiguration Monastery translated the Greek version as The Ascetical Homilies of Saint Isaac the Syrian. Sebastian Brock edited and translated most of the Second Part. Sabino Chialà edited the Third Part, and Mary Hansbury translated it into English (2016).

A short taste

"And what is a merciful heart? ... The burning of the heart unto the whole creation, man, fowls and beasts, demons and whatever exists." Part I, translated by A. J. Wensinck (1923), p. 341.

"Be on peaceful terms with thy soul; then heaven and earth will be on peaceful terms with thee." Part I, translated by Wensinck, p. 8.

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Pope Francis quoted Isaac several times. At Easter 2023, in his Urbi et Orbi message, he recalled Isaac's word that "the greatest sin is not to believe in the power of the resurrection". In his General Audience of 3 January 2024, in the series on vices and virtues, he said: "Isaac of Nineveh said that, in the Church, those who know their sins and mourn them" are "greater than those who raise a dead man". At the Chrism Mass of 28 March 2024, preaching to priests on compunction, he quoted Isaac twice. On 9 November 2024 he announced Isaac's inscription in the Roman Martyrology, praying "through the intercession of St. Isaac of Nineveh and the Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of Christ our God and Savior" for the Christians of the Middle East.

Pope Leo XIV quoted Isaac's word on the Resurrection to the Eastern Churches at their Jubilee, on 14 May 2025. On 27 October 2025, receiving Mar Awa III, he asked for the prayers of the saints, "especially Saint Isaac of Nineveh, whose name was added to the Roman Martyrology last year".

Benedict XVI did not give him a catechesis, as far as we have found.

Other Churches. The Byzantine Churches have honoured him as a saint for more than a thousand years and keep his day on 28 January. The Russian tradition loves him through the Philokalia. In the Church of the East he is Mar Isaac. The Vatican note of 2024 says that his writings were translated into many languages and that he is venerated across the traditions.

For the Syro-Malabar Church. Isaac belongs to the East Syriac tradition from which the Syro-Malabar liturgy comes. We have not found his name in the Syro-Malabar calendars we checked (2024-25 and 2025-26); one Friday of Denha remembers "the Syriac Fathers" together.

Why he matters today

Mercy that reaches everything. In an age of cruelty, and of harm done to creation, his merciful heart that weeps even for the reptiles teaches a love as wide as God's.

A guide to prayer. For anyone who wants to pray in silence, Isaac is a patient teacher. Priests, religious and lay people still read him.

Repentance without despair. He keeps together a deep sense of sin and a deeper trust in God's mercy, as Pope Francis stressed.

A saint who unites. Honoured by the Church of the East, the Orthodox and the Catholic Church, Isaac is a sign that holiness is a bridge. For Christians in Kerala, of many Churches, he is a common treasure.

A voice from the Gulf. Many Kerala Christians live and work in the Gulf countries today. Isaac was born there, and he shows that the Church in Arabia has very old roots.

Sit with this

  1. For whom, or for what part of creation, does my heart need to "burn" with mercy?
  2. Can I find ten minutes of silence today to climb the ladder that is within?
  3. Do I remember my sins in a way that leads me to God's mercy, and not to despair?
