St Isidore of Seville

TimeBorn about 560; died 4 April 636
WhereHis family came from Cartagena (south-east Spain) and settled at Seville (southern Spain), where he lived and worked; councils at Seville and Toledo (central Spain)
What he wasArchbishop of Seville for some thirty-six years, teacher, encyclopaedist, historian, legislator of councils, writer on Scripture, liturgy and monastic life
LanguageLatin
Known asIsidore of Seville (Latin Isidorus Hispalensis); often called "the last of the Latin Fathers"
HonouredSaint. Doctor of the Church, declared by Pope Innocent XIII in 1722. Latin Church: 4 April (optional memorial). Venerated also in the Mozarabic (Hispanic) rite of Toledo. Basilica of San Isidoro, León (Spain), keeps his relics.
His life

A displaced family. Isidore was born about 560. His father was Severianus. The Catholic Encyclopedia gives his mother's name as Theodora; the New Catholic Encyclopedia notes that it may have been Turtur. The family were Hispano-Romans, Catholics of the old Roman population, from Cartagena on the south-east coast. That province was torn by war between the Byzantine Empire and the Visigoths. Around the time of his birth the family moved to Seville, either fleeing the fighting or forced to move by the Goths. Historians cannot say whether Isidore was born before or after the move. Benedict XVI says that in his boyhood he "was obliged to experience the bitterness of exile".

Four children, four saints. Isidore was the youngest of four children. All of them are honoured as saints. His eldest brother, St Leander, became Archbishop of Seville. Another brother, St Fulgentius, became Bishop of Écija (ancient Astigi). His sister, St Florentina, was a nun and the head of a community of women. Isidore later dedicated a book to her. The old story that she ruled forty convents and a thousand nuns is now judged unlikely by historians.

Raised by his brother. Their father seems to have died early, and Leander, much older, brought up the boy. Benedict XVI describes Leander as "exacting, studious and austere". He made the home a place of monastic discipline and serious study. The house had a large library of pagan and Christian books. Isidore was drawn to both, and his brother taught him to study "with discretion and discernment", in the Pope's words. He was taught at the school attached to the cathedral at Seville, where Leander and other learned men taught the seven liberal arts. Older writers said he mastered Latin, Greek and Hebrew. Modern scholars think his Latin was excellent, but that he knew only some Greek and a little Hebrew, and that he read the Greek Fathers mostly in Latin translation.

A kingdom turns Catholic. The Visigoths who ruled Spain were Arian Christians. They held that the Son of God was not fully God. Most of their Hispano-Roman subjects were Catholic. In about 579 Hermenegild, son of the Arian King Leovigild, became a Catholic under Leander's influence and later rebelled against his father. He was defeated and put to death in 585. He is honoured as a martyr. Leander was sent into exile for a time. In Constantinople Leander became a close friend of the future Pope St Gregory the Great, who dedicated his great Moralia on Job to him. In 587 Leovigild's other son, King Reccared, became a Catholic. At the Third Council of Toledo in 589, led by Leander, the Visigothic people formally gave up Arianism. Isidore was then a young man. Benedict XVI speaks of "the rapture of contributing to the formation of a people that was at last rediscovering its unity".

Was he a monk? Historians do not know whether Isidore ever lived in a monastery. He loved and protected monks. He wrote a rule for them, and as bishop he defended monasteries against interference. At a council in 619 he pronounced anathema on any cleric who would harm a monastery.

Archbishop of Seville, about 600. Leander died about 600; the reference works give 599, 600 or 601. Isidore succeeded him as Archbishop of Seville and held the see for more than thirty years. Benedict XVI speaks of an inner struggle: he wanted solitude and the quiet reading of God's word, yet charity bound him to his people. In his Sentences he wrote that the man of the Church must be "crucified to the world", and yet must "devote himself humbly to government, even if he does not wish to" (Sentences 3, 33, 1).

Counsellor to kings. He was close to several Visigothic kings: Gundemar, at whose court in Toledo he was present in 610; Sisebut (612 to 621), a learned king for whom he wrote On the Nature of Things; and later Suinthila and Sisenand. The New Catholic Encyclopedia says that his counsel was "not always" followed.

Forced baptism. King Sisebut ordered the Jews of his kingdom to accept baptism or leave. Isidore did not approve. In his History of the Goths he wrote that the king had zeal, but not according to knowledge, since he compelled by force those whom he should have invited by reason of faith (in substance; compare Romans 10:2). The New Catholic Encyclopedia says that Isidore "opposed the increasingly harsh anti-Jewish legislation" of the kingdom and that his counsel seems to have held it back for a time. Yet the councils of his age, including the one he led in 633, still passed harsh measures. We say this plainly. The Church today teaches that no one may be forced to believe (CCC 160, Second Vatican Council, Dignitatis Humanae 10) and rejects every form of contempt for the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4).

The council of 619. In November 619 he presided over the Second Council of Seville, with bishops of the southern province. A Syrian bishop named Gregory was teaching that Christ had only one nature. Isidore answered him from Scripture and the Fathers. According to the acts of the council, the bishop was persuaded and accepted the Catholic faith. Isidore's statement on the two natures of Christ so impressed the bishops that it was placed among the council's decrees. In our own time the Popes and several Oriental Orthodox Churches have confessed together, in common declarations, that Christ is perfect God and perfect man.

The great council of 633. On 5 December 633 the bishops of the whole kingdom met at Toledo, the Fourth Council of Toledo. Isidore, now old, presided, and most of its decrees are judged to be his work. It called for one form of prayer and psalmody throughout Spain and Gaul. It ordered schools for clergy in cathedral cities, on the model of Seville. It set rules for the life of clerics, the election of bishops and the duties of kings. In its fifty-seventh canon it forbade forcing anyone to the faith, though it kept bound to the Church those who had already been baptised under Sisebut. Canon 75 set out the duty of the king to rule justly and the duty of subjects to keep their oath of loyalty.

The scholar at work. Through all these years he wrote. His friend St Braulio, Bishop of Saragossa, begged him for years to finish the Etymologies. The two exchanged letters about it. When Isidore died, it was still not fully finished, and Braulio divided it into twenty books. Braulio wrote that God had raised Isidore up "to restore the monuments of the ancients, so that we would not fall completely into barbarism" (as quoted in the reference works).

His death, 636. A cleric of Seville named Redemptus, who was present, left an account of his last days. As Butler retells it, when he knew his end was near, Isidore had himself carried to the church of St Vincent. There, before the people, he received ashes and sackcloth, asked pardon for his sins and begged the prayers of all. He forgave his debtors and gave what remained of his goods to the poor. He was carried home and died four days later, on 4 April 636. He was buried at Seville, it is said with Leander and Florentina.

Praise and relics. In 653 the Eighth Council of Toledo called him "the extraordinary doctor, the latest ornament of the Catholic Church, the most learned man of the latter ages, always to be named with reverence" (as translated in the Catholic Encyclopedia). After the Muslim conquest of 711 his tomb at Seville remained a place of prayer for the Christians living under Muslim rule, the Mozarabs. In the eleventh century King Ferdinand I of León obtained his remains from the ruler of Seville and had them carried north to León, where the Basilica of San Isidoro was built around them. The usual date given is 1063. Some relics are also venerated elsewhere in Spain.

The world he lived in

Isidore lived between two worlds. The Western Roman Empire had fallen more than eighty years before his birth. Spain was ruled by the Visigoths, a Germanic people who had settled there in the fifth century. Along the south-east coast the Byzantine Empire still held a strip of land, including Cartagena, until about 625.

The kingdom had two peoples, two laws and, until 589, two churches. The Goths were a ruling military class and Arian. The Hispano-Romans were the majority, Catholic, and the keepers of Latin learning. The conversion of 589 began to make them one people. Isidore worked for that unity all his life, through councils, schools, one liturgy and one law.

Learning was in danger. Books were rare and costly. Few people could read. The old Roman schools had gone. What survived of ancient knowledge was scattered in handbooks and commentaries. Isidore set himself to gather it before it was lost.

The kings of Toledo saw themselves as protectors of the Church, much as the Roman emperors had done, and the bishops often met in council at the king's call. This brought blessings, such as the end of Arianism, and also dangers, since royal policy entered the councils. The treatment of the Jews is the sharpest example. The kingdom had also to face the Byzantine presence, Basque and Frankish wars, and plots for the throne.

In the East, St Maximus the Confessor was his younger contemporary. Within a lifetime of his death, Muslim armies would cross into Spain (711) and the Visigothic kingdom would end. His books went north with refugees and spread over Europe.

What he taught
  • Christ, true God and true man. Against the Syrian bishop who taught one nature in Christ, Isidore set out the faith of Chalcedon: one Person in two natures, divine and human. The council of 619 adopted his statement. His Differences, book 2, also explains the Trinity and the two natures of Christ.
  • Prayer and reading. "When we pray, we speak with God; when we read, God speaks with us" (Sentences 3, 8, 1 to 2, in substance from the Latin). He adds that prayer purifies us and reading instructs us, and that both are good if both can be done.
  • Action and contemplation. Those who seek rest in contemplation must first train "in the stadium of active life", and only a pure heart sees God (Differences 2, 34, 133). Yet he counsels a middle way. Christ worked signs by day in the towns and prayed by night on the mountain. "Just as we must love God in contemplation, so we must love our neighbour with action" (Differences 2, 34, 135, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • The duty of those who lead. The bishop and the ruler must serve. He gave the old saying its Christian sense: a king is called rex from recte agere, acting rightly; if he acts rightly he keeps the name of king, and if not he loses it (Etymologies 9, 3, in substance). Kings will give account to God for how they ruled (Sentences 3).
  • All are equal before God. In his Rule for Monks he admits men of every rank, slaves too, because God made no difference between the soul of a slave and the soul of any other person, and in the monastery all are equal in the sight of God (Rule, in substance, as summarised by the Catholic Encyclopedia).
  • Penance and hope. In the Synonyms a man in despair over his sins argues with Reason. Reason leads him to confession, tears and hope of pardon. No sin is greater than God's mercy.
  • The liturgy explained. His On the Offices of the Church, book 1, explains the Mass, the hours of prayer, feasts and fasts, and gives one of the earliest descriptions of the old Spanish liturgy. It speaks of the Eucharist as the Body and Blood of Christ, offered and received. Book 2 explains the orders of clergy and the states of life.
  • Scripture as the heart of learning. He wrote introductions to every book of the Bible, a book on its numbers, and a book of allegories on about 250 persons of the Old and New Testaments. All other learning, for him, serves the reading of Scripture.
  • Knowledge is a gift to be kept and shared. Benedict XVI notes his "nagging worry not to overlook anything" that human experience had produced, pagan, Jewish or Christian. He wanted nothing good to be lost.
  • Grace and predestination. In the Sentences he speaks of a "twofold predestination", of the elect to rest and of the reprobate to death. The Church teaches that God predestines no one to evil or to hell (Council of Orange, 529; CCC 1037). Read charitably, Isidore means that God foresees and judges justly; the phrase itself is best not used without that care.
How he taught

He collected. Benedict XVI says that rather than the gift of synthesis Isidore had the gift of collatio, of gathering. He quotes one hundred and fifty-four authors in the Etymologies, Christian and pagan, some read whole and many through handbooks. He often joins excerpts with only a few words of his own.

He explained through words. His method was to seek the origin of a word, believing that the root of a name opens its meaning. Many of his etymologies are fanciful by modern standards. Yet the method made the book easy to remember and to use.

He wrote short and clear. His Latin is simple and plain, close to the speech of his time. The Catholic Encyclopedia notes about 1,640 Spanish words in his writings. He arranged matter by topic in short chapters, so that teachers could find what they needed.

He taught through law and liturgy. Many of his teachings live in the canons of councils, in a rule for monks, and in the order of worship. He built schools and asked other bishops to do the same.

Honest limits. Benedict XVI notes that his learning "was not always ordered as might have been desired" and that he did not always filter what he gathered "in the purifying waters of the Christian faith as he would have wished". Yet, the Pope adds, his intentions were always in tune with the Catholic faith "which he staunchly upheld".

His works

Braulio drew up a list of Isidore's books soon after his death. Most survive. The dates are approximate; the New Catholic Encyclopedia places his writing between 598 and 633.

Encyclopaedia and words

Etymologies (Etymologiae or Origines), twenty books, left unfinished and divided by Braulio. Grammar, rhetoric and logic; mathematics, music and astronomy; medicine; law and time; the Bible, its books and the Church's offices; God, angels and saints; the Church and the sects, Jewish and pagan religion; languages and peoples; words; the human person; animals; the world; geography; buildings; stones and metals; farming; war and games; ships, houses and clothing; food and tools. More than a thousand medieval manuscripts survive.
Differences (Differentiae), two books: on the difference between words of like meaning, and on theological and spiritual matters.
Synonyms (Synonyma, also called Lamentations), a dialogue between Man and Reason on sin and pardon.

Nature

On the Nature of Things (De natura rerum), for King Sisebut, about 613: days, months, seasons, sky, stars, winds, seas.
On the Order of Creatures (De ordine creaturarum). Disputed; many modern scholars think it is the work of an Irish writer of the seventh century.

History

Chronicle (Chronica maiora), a history of the world from Creation, first issued about 615 and revised about 626.
History of the Kings of the Goths, Vandals and Sueves, in two forms, the longer of 624 with the Praise of Spain.
On Famous Men (De viris illustribus), lives of Christian writers, continuing St Jerome and Gennadius.

Scripture

Introductions to the Books of the Old and New Testaments (Prooemia).
On the Birth and Death of the Fathers (De ortu et obitu patrum), on persons of the Bible. Accepted, though questioned in the past.
Allegories of Holy Scripture, on about 250 persons.
Book of Numbers (Liber numerorum), on the meaning of numbers in Scripture.
Questions on the Old Testament (Mysticorum expositiones sacramentorum), Genesis to Maccabees, drawn from earlier Fathers.
Questions on the Old and New Testaments. Doubtful.

Doctrine and life

Sentences (Sententiae, also called On the Highest Good), three books: God and creation; virtues and vices; the Church, rulers and judgement. Drawn largely from St Augustine and St Gregory the Great. It is thought to have influenced Peter Lombard's later Sentences.
On the Catholic Faith against the Jews (De fide catholica contra Iudaeos), two books, written for his sister Florentina. It sets out the prophecies of Christ. Its polemical tone belongs to its age; see the note under "His life".
On Heresies. Attributed to him; authorship discussed.

Church, liturgy, monks

On the Offices of the Church (De ecclesiasticis officiis), two books, written for his brother Fulgentius.
Rule for Monks (Regula monachorum), milder than other rules of the time.
Canons of the Second Council of Seville (619) and the Fourth Council of Toledo (633), largely his.
The Hispana, the great Spanish collection of canons of councils, thought to have been begun under his direction.
Prayers and other texts of the old Spanish liturgy are traditionally linked with him, but which texts are his cannot be shown.

Letters and verses

A small number of letters, including those with Braulio; some are doubtful.
Verses said to have been written for the cupboards of his library, naming the authors kept there.

A short taste

"Just as we must love God in contemplation, so we must love our neighbour with action." (Differences 2, 34, 135, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 18 June 2008)

"When we pray, we speak with God; when we read, God speaks with us." (Sentences 3, 8, in substance from the Latin)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The councils of Toledo. In 653 the Eighth Council of Toledo honoured him as "the glory of the Catholic Church", in Benedict XVI's rendering; the Fifteenth Council, in 688, repeated the praise.

Recognition as a saint and Doctor. He was honoured as a saint in Spain from early times. The reference works give the late sixteenth century for his formal recognition at Rome (1589 or 1598). Pope Innocent XIII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1722.

Benedict XVI spoke of him in his General Audience of 18 June 2008. He recalled that Isidore is "considered to be the last of the Christian Fathers of antiquity", praised his care to lose nothing of human learning, and held up his teaching on action and contemplation as "the lesson that the great Bishop of Seville has bequeathed to us".

Dante placed him in the heaven of the Sun, among the great teachers, beside the Venerable Bede (Paradiso 10).

The Hispanic rite. The old Spanish liturgy, called Visigothic or Mozarabic, still celebrated in Toledo, owes much to the work of Isidore and Leander and the councils they led.

A modern title. Because the Etymologies gathered all knowledge in one ordered work, some people today call him the patron of the internet. This is a popular custom only; no Pope has declared it.

Why he matters today

He lived when a world was ending and nobody knew what would come. He did not panic. He gathered, ordered and handed on. Teachers in later centuries learned from his books when other books had been lost.

He shows how a Christian can love learning without fear. Pagan poets, Roman law and Greek medicine all had a place in his library, and all of it was meant to serve the reading of God's word.

He shows the balance that Benedict XVI drew out: prayer and work, reading and service, God loved in contemplation and the neighbour loved in action. Few of us can live only one of these.

He also shows that a saint can stand against the policy of his king. He said that faith must be offered and not forced. And his death, asking pardon before his people, reminds every leader that he, too, is a sinner in need of mercy.

Sit with this

  1. When did I last let God speak to me through a slow reading of Scripture, and not only speak to him?
  2. Is my life tilted too far toward work, or too far toward withdrawal? What would Isidore's "middle way" ask of me this week?
  3. What good thing have I received that I should keep and hand on before it is lost?
