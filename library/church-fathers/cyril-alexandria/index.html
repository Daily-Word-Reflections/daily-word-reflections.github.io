St Cyril of Alexandria

TimeBorn between 370 and 380; bishop from 412; died 27 June 444
WhereAlexandria (Egypt); journeys to Constantinople (today Istanbul, Turkey) in 403 and to Ephesus (near Selçuk, Turkey) in 431
What he wasPatriarch of Alexandria, theologian, interpreter of Scripture, defender of the title "Mother of God"
LanguageGreek
Known as"Seal of the Fathers"; "guardian of exactitude"; in the Coptic tradition "the pillar of faith"; in Syriac usage Mar Kurillos
HonouredSaint in East and West. Doctor of the Church, proclaimed by Pope Leo XIII in 1882. Latin Church: 27 June. Byzantine tradition: 9 June, and 18 January together with St Athanasius. Coptic Church: 3 Abib (Epip). The Oriental Orthodox Churches, among them the Churches of the West Syriac tradition, regard him as their great teacher. The Assyrian Church of the East, which did not receive the Council of Ephesus, does not keep his memory; the reasons are explained below.
His life

Nephew of a powerful bishop. We know almost nothing of Cyril's early years. Benedict XVI says he was probably born in Alexandria between 370 and 380. His uncle Theophilus had been bishop there since 385 and ruled, in the Pope's words, "with a prestigious and iron hand". Cyril was brought into Church life while still young and received a good education in letters and in theology. The Catholic Encyclopedia notes that he may for a time have lived as a monk, if he is the Cyril to whom St Isidore of Pelusium wrote.

The Synod of the Oak, 403. As a young cleric he went with his uncle to Constantinople. There, at the so-called Synod of the Oak, Theophilus and his allies deposed the bishop of the capital, St John Chrysostom. (Benedict XVI places Cyril's journey and the synod in 403; some older works say 402.) This was a sad beginning, and it left a long shadow. Rome took the side of John, and the Church of Alexandria was out of full communion with Rome and Constantinople for years.

Bishop, 412. Theophilus died on 15 October 412. After a riot between his supporters and those of a rival candidate, Timothy, Cyril was made bishop three days later. He would govern the Church of Alexandria for thirty-two years. The bishop of Alexandria was then the most important bishop in the East after Rome. He oversaw the Churches of all Egypt and Libya, the great monasteries of the desert, and a city of trade and learning.

Hard early years. Cyril was young and fiery, and the city was known for its riots. The church historian Socrates, writing a few years later, says that one of his first acts was to close the churches of the Novatians, a rigorist group. Tension grew between Cyril and the imperial prefect Orestes, who resented the bishop's power. Violence broke out between Christians and the large Jewish community of the city, and blood was shed. In about 414 Cyril led a crowd that drove the Jews out of Alexandria, where they had lived since the city was founded. This was a grave injustice to a whole people. The Church today rejects every such act and every hostility toward the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4). Soon after, about five hundred monks came down from Nitria to support the bishop, and one of them, Ammonius, struck Orestes with a stone. The prefect had him tortured to death. Cyril at first honoured him as a martyr, but, Socrates adds, he later let the matter fall into silence, since sober Christians knew Ammonius had died for violence and not for the faith.

The death of Hypatia, 415. In March 415 a mob of Christians, led by a church reader named Peter, seized the philosopher Hypatia, a much respected teacher of mathematics and Platonist philosophy and a friend of Orestes. They killed her in a church and burned her body. Socrates, the main early witness, says that rumours had spread that she was keeping the prefect and the bishop apart, and that the deed brought great disgrace on Cyril and on the whole Church of Alexandria. Socrates does not say that Cyril ordered it. A pagan writer of the next century, Damascius, does accuse him, but he wrote long afterwards and was hostile to Christians. Historians still debate how far Cyril bears blame for the climate in which it happened. What is beyond debate is that the murder was a terrible crime, contrary to the Gospel.

Healing old wounds. In 417 or 418, Benedict XVI notes, Cyril restored communion with Constantinople, which had been broken since 406 because of the deposition of Chrysostom. Under the urging of Isidore of Pelusium, his spiritual guide, he in time placed John Chrysostom's name in the diptychs, the lists of those remembered at the altar. Through these years he wrote much on Scripture and against the Arians.

Nestorius, 428. In 428 a monk from Antioch, Nestorius, was made bishop of Constantinople. He was a stern preacher. He objected to calling Mary Theotokos, "God-bearer" or "Mother of God", a title long dear to the faithful. He preferred Christotokos, "Mother of Christ". Benedict XVI explains that behind this lay a way of thinking that, to protect the full humanity of Christ, ended by dividing Christ in two, so that one could no longer truly say that God was born of Mary.

The letters, 429 to 430. Cyril answered almost at once. His Easter letter of 429 and a letter to the monks of Egypt defended the title Mother of God. Then he wrote to Nestorius himself. His Second Letter to Nestorius, of February 430, sets out his faith with great clarity, and later councils would approve it. Both men wrote to Pope Celestine I. Cyril, following "ancient custom", put the matter before the Pope. A Roman synod in August 430 condemned Nestorius' teaching and gave him ten days to recant, and Celestine entrusted Cyril with carrying out the sentence. Cyril then held a synod in Alexandria and sent a Third Letter, with twelve propositions, called the Twelve Chapters or anathemas, for Nestorius to accept. They were sharp in tone, and some of their wording troubled the bishops of Antioch.

The Council of Ephesus, 431. The Emperor Theodosius II called a general council to meet at Ephesus at Pentecost 431. Cyril came with about fifty Egyptian bishops. John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops were delayed. On 22 June, though sixty-eight bishops asked for a delay, Cyril opened the council without them. Nestorius refused to appear. The council approved Cyril's Second Letter as agreeing with the Creed of Nicaea, and deposed Nestorius. The people of Ephesus, the city linked in tradition with Mary's later years, rejoiced, and Benedict XVI calls the council "the first great triumph of devotion to Mary". When the legates of Pope Celestine arrived on 10 July they confirmed the sentence. But John of Antioch, arriving a few days after 22 June, held a rival meeting with his bishops and declared Cyril and the local bishop Memnon deposed. The Emperor for a time had all three men, Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon, held in custody. In the end Cyril was allowed to return home in the autumn, and Nestorius retired to his monastery near Antioch and was later exiled to Egypt. The Catholic Encyclopedia, which honours Cyril, still calls the hasty opening of the council "an initial error, which had disastrous results".

Peace with Antioch, 433. After the victory came the harder work. Through the patient efforts of envoys and of Pope Sixtus III, Cyril and John of Antioch made peace in 433. John accepted a statement of faith drawn up by the Antiochenes, the Formula of Reunion, which confessed one Lord Jesus Christ, born of the Father before all ages as God and born of the Virgin Mary as man, and called Mary Mother of God. Cyril received it with joy in his letter beginning "Let the heavens rejoice". Benedict XVI praises this: on the one hand, "the clarity of the doctrine of faith", and on the other, "the intense search for unity and reconciliation". Some of Cyril's own friends thought he had given away too much. He had not; he had recognised the same faith in other words.

Last years and death. In his last years he defended and explained his teaching in many letters and treatises, and he answered the old attack of the Emperor Julian on Christianity. He died on 27 June 444, after nearly thirty-two years as bishop.

The world he lived in

Alexandria was the second city of the Roman East, a great port on the Mediterranean with a famous library, schools of philosophy and medicine, and people of many faiths: Christians, Jews and followers of the old gods. Its crowds were known for riots, and the bishop, the prefect and the city's factions often clashed.

The Church of Alexandria was proud of its history. Tradition traced it to St Mark. It had given the Church St Athanasius, the great defender of the Council of Nicaea. Its theologians loved to stress the unity of Christ: the eternal Word himself became flesh. The school of Antioch, in Syria, stressed the full and true humanity of Christ, with a human mind and will. Both were defending something true. When either side spoke carelessly, it could sound to the other like heresy.

There was also rivalry between sees. Constantinople, the new capital, was rising in rank, and Alexandria resented it. Twice before Cyril's time, bishops of Alexandria had worked against bishops of Constantinople. Cyril's quarrel with Nestorius was not only about rank, but rank and politics made it harder.

The Emperor in Constantinople called councils and kept order, and the court was full of factions. Bishops sent gifts to win friends at court, which was the custom of the time. Cyril did so too, and his enemies made much of it.

Ephesus, Nestorius and the Church of the East today

This chapter of Cyril's life matters especially to Christians in India, because the Church of the East, whose Syriac tradition is the mother of the liturgy of the Syro-Malabar Church, never received the condemnation of Nestorius at Ephesus. It honours Mar Nestorius and Mar Theodore of Mopsuestia among its teachers, and its anaphoras include ones that bear their names. The Churches of the West Syriac tradition, including the Syro-Malankara, Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite Syrian Churches, honour Cyril as a great Father.

For centuries these Churches viewed each other with suspicion. Today the Catholic Church speaks with great care. On 11 November 1994 Pope St John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, signed a Common Christological Declaration. Together they confess that Christ is "true God and true man", and that his divinity and humanity "are united in one person, without confusion or change, without division or separation". The Assyrian Church prays to the Virgin Mary as "the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour". The Catholic tradition calls her "the Mother of God" and also "the Mother of Christ". Both Churches "recognize the legitimacy and rightness of these expressions of the same faith". The declaration adds that the divisions of the past "were due in large part to misunderstandings".

This does not unsay Ephesus. The Catholic Church still holds with the Council and with Cyril that Mary is truly Mother of God, because the one she bore is the eternal Son (CCC 466, 495). What has changed is that two Churches have found, by patient dialogue, that they confess the same Christ. In the same spirit, Popes have signed common declarations with the Oriental Orthodox Churches, who honour Cyril as their teacher and did not receive the Council of Chalcedon: Pope St Paul VI with Pope Shenouda III of the Coptic Church in 1973, and Pope St John Paul II with Patriarch Ignatius Zakka I of the Syriac Orthodox Church in 1984.

What he taught
  • One Christ. This is the heart of all he wrote. The eternal Word did not come to live in a man, as in a prophet; he himself became man. "Only one is the Son, only one the Lord Jesus Christ, both before the Incarnation and after the Incarnation" (First Letter to Succensus, quoted by Benedict XVI). Mary's Son is not one person and the Word another. The Catechism says that "St. Cyril of Alexandria and the third ecumenical council, at Ephesus in 431, confessed 'that the Word, uniting to himself in his person the flesh animated by a rational soul, became man'" (CCC 466).
  • Two natures, truly united. Cyril did not deny Christ's humanity. In his Second Letter to Nestorius he wrote that the natures are different but united "in one true unity", and from both comes one Christ and Son, "not because, due to their unity, the difference in their natures has been eliminated" (quoted by Benedict XVI). The Catholic Encyclopedia sums him up: he always held "that the one Christ has two perfect and distinct natures".
  • Mother of God. If the one born of Mary is God the Son, then Mary is truly Theotokos. This title honours Christ first: it says who her Son is. The Council of Ephesus taught that "Mary truly became the Mother of God by the human conception of the Son of God in her womb" (CCC 466). The title is not a claim that Mary is the source of the divine nature; it says that the Word took flesh from her (CCC 495).
  • A formula that needed care. Cyril sometimes used the phrase "one nature of God the Word made flesh". He took it from a writing he believed was by St Athanasius, though it came from another author. He meant only that Christ is one. Later, some used it to deny Christ's full humanity, and the Council of Chalcedon in 451 spoke more exactly of one person in two natures. The Church reads Cyril in the light of Chalcedon, as Chalcedon itself did, for it received his letters.
  • The life-giving flesh in the Eucharist. Because the flesh of Christ is the flesh of the Word, it gives life. Cyril taught this especially in his commentary on John. In his commentary on Luke, on the words "This is my body", he says, as quoted in the Catechism: "Do not doubt whether this is true, but rather receive the words of the Savior in faith, for since he is the truth, he cannot lie" (CCC 1381).
  • One in the Spirit. In his commentary on John he writes that all who have received the one Spirit "are in a sense blended together with one another and with God" (CCC 738).
  • Tradition. Benedict XVI stresses that Cyril constantly referred to earlier Fathers, above all Athanasius, to show that his teaching was not new. That is why he was called the "seal of the Fathers". He also saw the faith of ordinary believers as a sign of sound teaching: pastors must explain the faith to the people "in the most irreproachable way" (Second Letter to Nestorius, quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • The Trinity. Against the Arians he defended the full divinity of the Son and of the Holy Spirit in his Thesaurus and his Dialogues on the Trinity.
  • Christ in all the Scriptures. His long works on the Law of Moses read the Old Testament as a preparation for Christ and for worship "in spirit and in truth".
How he taught

By the Bible first. Most of his writings are commentaries on Scripture. Benedict XVI lists commentaries on the whole Pentateuch, Isaiah, the Psalms and the Gospels of John and Luke. He read the Old Testament in the Alexandrian manner, seeking the spiritual sense that points to Christ, but he also explained the plain meaning carefully.

By letters. Much of his teaching on Christ comes in letters to bishops, monks, the Emperor and the princesses at court. Every year he also sent an Easter letter to the Churches of Egypt, announcing the date of Easter and teaching on a point of faith or life.

By precise argument. He weighed words carefully: which ones divide Christ, which ones confuse the natures. Hence his old title "guardian of exactitude".

By strong action. He was a man of great energy and courage, and also of a hard temper. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that he listened with humility to the stern advice of Isidore of Pelusium, and that his natural vehemence was "repressed and schooled". His impatience at Ephesus made peace harder. Yet in 433 he showed he could also make peace.

His works

Old Testament

On Adoration and Worship in Spirit and in Truth, seventeen books on the Law of Moses as a figure of Christ.
Glaphyra ("elegant comments"), on chosen passages of the Pentateuch.
Commentary on Isaiah, five books.
Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets. Complete.
Other works on the Old Testament, including the Psalms, survive only in fragments.

New Testament

Commentary on John, twelve books; books 7 and 8 survive only in fragments.
Commentary on Luke, a series of homilies, preserved mainly in a Syriac translation.
Commentaries on Matthew and on some letters of Paul survive only in fragments.

On the Trinity

Thesaurus on the Holy and Consubstantial Trinity, against the Arians.
Dialogues on the Trinity, seven dialogues.

On Christ (the Nestorian controversy)

Letters to Nestorius, the Second (430) and Third, with the Twelve Chapters.
Against Nestorius, five books (430).
On the True Faith, addresses to the Emperor Theodosius II and to the princesses (430).
Explanation of the Twelve Chapters (431), and two defences of them, against the bishops of the East and against Theodoret.
Scholia on the Incarnation.
That Christ Is One, a late dialogue.
Against Those Who Will Not Confess the Holy Virgin to Be Mother of God, a short treatise.

Defence of the faith

Against Julian, a reply to the Emperor Julian's Against the Galileans, probably written in his last years. Ten books survive, of perhaps twenty, with fragments of others.

Letters and sermons

Festal (Easter) Letters, a yearly series; twenty-nine survive.
Letters, a large collection, including the Letter to John of Antioch of 433 ("Let the heavens rejoice") and the Letters to Succensus. Some letters in the collection were written to him by others.
Homilies, a small number, including sermons preached at Ephesus in 431. The famous Marian homily from Ephesus (Homily 4) is attributed to him, though some scholars question parts of it.
A few other works once listed under his name are doubtful or spurious.

A short taste

"God is eternal, he is born of a woman, and he stays with us every day." (Benedict XVI, summing up Cyril's faith, General Audience, 3 October 2007)

"We believe that the very One who was born before the ages was also born according to the flesh and of a woman." (First Letter to Succensus, as quoted by Benedict XVI)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The Councils. The Council of Ephesus in 431 approved his Second Letter to Nestorius. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 received it again, and later councils appealed to his letters as tests of sound faith.

Pope Leo XIII proclaimed him a Doctor of the Church in 1882, together with St Cyril of Jerusalem. Benedict XVI sees in this the love of that Pope for the Eastern Churches, showing that "both the Eastern and Western traditions express the doctrine of Christ's one Church".

Benedict XVI gave a General Audience on him on 3 October 2007. He calls him "an unflagging, staunch witness of Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word of God". He describes his faults and the turbulent events honestly, and he praises both his clarity and his search for reconciliation.

The Catechism names him in its teaching on Christ (CCC 466) and quotes him on the Holy Spirit (CCC 738) and the Eucharist (CCC 1381).

The liturgy. His memorial is kept on 27 June in the Latin Church. The Byzantine Churches honour him on 9 June and with St Athanasius on 18 January. The Coptic Church keeps his departure on 3 Abib. The Churches of the West Syriac tradition name him among the great teachers of the faith.

Honest judgment. The Church does not make every act of a saint a model. Catholic writers have long said plainly that Cyril was at times harsh and hasty, and his actions in his first years and at Ephesus caused harm. He is a saint because of his faith, his teaching and his life as a whole, which ended in reconciliation, not because he never sinned.

Why he matters today

Every time we say "Holy Mary, Mother of God", we use the words he fought for. They guard the truth that the child in the manger is God himself, and that God has entered our history to stay with us.

He reminds us that faith in Christ is not vague. The words we use about Jesus matter, because they tell us whom we meet in the Gospel and in the Eucharist.

His story is also a warning and a hope. Truth defended with anger and haste can wound the Church for centuries. Truth received in other words, as Cyril did in 433 and as the Churches did again in 1994, can heal what was broken. Catholics of the East Syriac tradition and of the West Syriac tradition in Kerala can both read him today as part of a shared story, and pray together for the full unity that has not yet come.

Sit with this

  1. When I say "Mother of God", do I think of who her Son is?
  2. Where do I defend something true with anger, so that people hear the anger and not the truth?
  3. Is there someone with whom I need to find, as Cyril did with John of Antioch, the same faith in other words?
