St Gregory Nazianzen, the Theologian

TimeBorn about 330 (some sources say 325 or 329), died about 390 (some say 389)
WhereArianzus and Nazianzus in Cappadocia (today near Aksaray, Turkey); studies at Caesarea in Cappadocia (today Kayseri, Turkey), Caesarea in Palestine (Israel), Alexandria (Egypt) and Athens (Greece); Seleucia in Isauria (today Silifke, Turkey); Constantinople (today Istanbul, Turkey)
What he wasPriest, bishop, for a short time Bishop of Constantinople, president of the Council of 381, theologian, orator, poet, lover of solitude
LanguageGreek
Known asGregory of Nazianzus; "the Theologian" in the Eastern Churches; in Syriac usage Mar Gregorios
HonouredSaint. Doctor of the Church, one of the four great Eastern Doctors recognized by St Pius V in 1568. Latin Church: 2 January, with St Basil (before 1969 it was 9 May). Byzantine tradition: 25 January, and 30 January with St Basil and St John Chrysostom as the Three Holy Hierarchs. Syro-Malankara calendar: 2 January, Mar Baselios and Mar Gregorios.
His life

A home given to God. Gregory was born about 330 at Arianzus, the family's country estate near the small town of Nazianzus in the south-west of Cappadocia. His father, also called Gregory, had belonged to a sect called the Hypsistarians, who worshipped a "Most High" God while keeping some Jewish and pagan customs. He came to the Christian faith through his wife, Nonna, and later became Bishop of Nazianzus, serving for about forty-five years. Benedict XVI notes that Nonna consecrated her son to God at his birth. There were three children: Gorgonia, Gregory and Caesarius. Gorgonia and Caesarius died before him, and he preached at the funeral of each. The Church honours Nonna, Gorgonia and Caesarius as saints too.

A long education. He studied first at Caesarea in Cappadocia, where he met Basil, then at Caesarea in Palestine, where he learned rhetoric, then at Alexandria. On the sea voyage to Athens a storm nearly sank the ship. Gregory was not yet baptized, and he was terrified that he might die without the sacrament. He prayed and promised his life to God. At Athens he again met Basil, and the two became inseparable. Among their fellow students was the young prince Julian, who would later become emperor and try to restore paganism. Gregory spent about ten years at Athens and became a master of Greek rhetoric. He left about 356, when he was about thirty, and went home by way of Constantinople.

Baptism and the pull of solitude. Back at Nazianzus he was baptized. Benedict XVI says that from this time he felt a strong attraction to the monastic life, to solitude and to prayerful study. For a time he joined Basil in Pontus. According to tradition, the two friends gathered the Philocalia, a selection from the works of Origen, and Gregory helped Basil with his first rules for monks. Gregory describes the life he loved as a way to "converse with oneself and with God" (Oration 2, 7, as quoted by Benedict XVI).

Made a priest against his will, about 361. His father needed help. The old bishop had signed a doubtful creed, and Gregory helped bring him and the monks of Nazianzus back to unity in the faith of Nicaea. Then, probably at Christmas 361, his father ordained him a priest, in the custom of the time, with the people pressing. Gregory felt forced. He fled to Basil in Pontus. A few weeks later he came back, and at Easter he preached his first sermon. He then wrote a long defence of his flight, known as Oration 2. It is a treatise on the priesthood: on how holy a priest must be, and how hard it is to guide souls. The Catechism quotes it (CCC 1589).

Sasima, about 371 or 372. When Basil became Bishop of Caesarea, the Emperor Valens divided Cappadocia, and Basil's authority was threatened. To hold the ground, Basil made Gregory bishop of Sasima, a small post station on the main road. Benedict XVI puts this in 371; other historians say 372. Gregory never took possession of the see. He felt that his friend had used him, and the hurt never fully healed. He stayed at Nazianzus and helped his father as his assistant bishop.

Losses and a retreat, 374 to 379. His father died early in 374, and his mother soon after. Gregory preached the funeral oration for his father (Oration 18). He gave his inheritance to the poor and kept only a small piece of land at Arianzus. He looked after the diocese for a while but refused to become its bishop. About 375 he withdrew to a monastery at Seleucia in Isauria, near the shrine of St Thecla, and stayed about three or four years. There he heard of Basil's death in 379. He was ill and could not travel. Later he would give the great oration in Basil's honour (Oration 43).

Constantinople, 379. In 379, with the Arian Emperor Valens dead, the small community of Christians in the capital who held the faith of Nicaea asked Gregory to come. The great churches were in Arian hands. Gregory began to preach in a private house, which was turned into a chapel and called the Anastasia, which means "Resurrection", because the faith rose again there. In 380 he preached his five Theological Orations there (Orations 27 to 31). People gave him the name "the Theologian" because of them. He suffered for it. During the Easter baptisms a mob attacked the chapel. St Jerome, then a young man, came to hear him and later called Gregory his teacher.

The intruder. While Gregory was ill, a man named Maximus, a former Cynic philosopher whom he had trusted, was secretly consecrated Bishop of Constantinople by some bishops from Egypt. The people rose against it, Maximus fled, and the Emperor Theodosius refused to accept him. Gregory was badly shaken.

Bishop of the capital, 380. The Emperor Theodosius, a Catholic, entered Constantinople in November 380. He removed the Arian bishop, Demophilus, and led Gregory into the cathedral himself. Gregory says the sky was dark that morning and the sun broke through as they entered.

The Council of 381. In May 381 Theodosius called about 150 bishops to Constantinople. This council is counted today as the Second Ecumenical Council. It completed the creed we now call the Nicene Creed, confessing the Holy Spirit as "the Lord, the giver of life". The council confirmed Gregory as Bishop of Constantinople. St Meletius of Antioch presided at first and died during the council, and Gregory took his place as president. But there were quarrels. Gregory wanted peace with the West over the see of Antioch and lost. Then bishops arriving from Egypt and Macedonia objected that his move from Sasima to Constantinople broke a church rule. Gregory chose to step aside rather than divide the Church. "We have divided Christ, we who so loved God and Christ!" he had once lamented (Oration 6, 3, as quoted by Benedict XVI). He gave a farewell sermon in the crowded cathedral (Oration 42), asking his people to guard the faith they had been given. Nectarius was chosen in his place.

Nazianzus again. He went home. The church of Nazianzus was troubled by followers of Apollinaris, who said that Christ had no human mind. Gregory wrote two famous letters to the priest Cledonius against this error (Letters 101 and 102). Toward the end of 383 his cousin Eulalius became bishop of Nazianzus, and Gregory was at last set at liberty.

Last years. He retired to Arianzus, his birthplace. He prayed, studied, received visitors, kept a strict ascetic life, and wrote most of his poems, including a long poem about his own life. He died there about 390. His body was later taken to Constantinople, and afterwards to Rome. In 1580 Pope Gregory XIII placed his remains under the altar of the new Gregorian Chapel in St Peter's Basilica. On 27 November 2004 St John Paul II gave a large part of these relics, with those of St John Chrysostom, to the Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I as a sign of reconciliation.

The world he lived in

Gregory's life spanned the last great storm of the Arian crisis. The Council of Nicaea in 325 had confessed that the Son is of one being with the Father. For fifty years after it, emperors kept changing sides, and in the East most bishops followed creeds that avoided the word of Nicaea. Under Valens, who ruled the East from 364 to 378, the Arian party was in power. Some went further and denied that the Holy Spirit is God. They were called Pneumatomachians, "fighters against the Spirit", or Macedonians.

In 361 to 363 the Emperor Julian, Gregory's old fellow student, turned away from Christ and tried to bring back the old gods. He forbade Christians to teach the classics. Gregory answered him with two sharp speeches (Orations 4 and 5). For Gregory, Greek learning belonged to Christians too.

In 378 Valens died in battle against the Goths at Adrianople. The new emperor, Theodosius, was a Nicene Christian. In February 380 he decreed that all his peoples should hold the faith of the Bishop of Rome and of Alexandria. This opened the way for the Council of 381, at which Gregory presided for a time.

Cappadocia, in the centre of what is now Turkey, was a high, dry land of estates and small towns. Gregory, Basil and Basil's brother Gregory of Nyssa came from it. Together they are known as the Cappadocian Fathers. Gregory was the poet and orator of the three.

What he taught
  • One God in three Persons. In the Theological Orations Gregory defends the faith of Nicaea: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, equal and distinct. Benedict XVI quotes his line from an evening hymn: the Trinity is "a triple light gathered into one splendour" (Carmina 2, 1, 32). The Catechism hands on his summary of the Trinity for those preparing for Baptism, which begins, "Above all guard for me this great deposit of faith" (Oration 40, 41, in CCC 256).
  • The Holy Spirit is God. Where others spoke with caution, Gregory spoke plainly: the Spirit is God, of one being with the Father (Oration 31). He explains why the Church came to see this slowly. The Old Testament proclaimed the Father clearly and the Son more dimly. The New Testament revealed the Son and gave a glimpse of the Spirit. Now the Spirit dwells among us and shows himself more clearly (Oration 31, 26, in CCC 684). He argues: if the Spirit is not to be worshipped, how can he make me divine in Baptism? (Oration 31, 28, in CCC 2670).
  • Christ is fully God and fully man. Against Apollinaris, who said Christ had no human mind, Gregory taught that Christ took the whole of our nature, body, soul and mind. His reason became a rule for later councils: "that which He has not assumed He has not healed" (Letter 101). Since Christ became like us, we can become like him (Oration 1, 5, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Mary, Mother of God. Gregory insists that Mary is truly the Mother of God, Theotokos: "If anyone does not believe that Holy Mary is the Mother of God, he is severed from the Godhead" (Letter 101). He also says she was purified beforehand for her mission (Oration 38, 13). Benedict XVI sees in this a distant foreshadowing of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.
  • Baptism, the great gift. His sermons for Epiphany (Orations 39 and 40) are a treasury of teaching on Baptism. He calls it "God's most beautiful and magnificent gift" and lists its names: gift, grace, anointing, enlightenment, garment of immortality, bath of rebirth, seal (Oration 40, 3 to 4, in CCC 1216). "Let us be buried with Christ by Baptism to rise with him" (Oration 40, 9, in CCC 537).
  • Theology comes from prayer. Benedict XVI stresses that his theology sprang from a life of prayer and holiness. For Gregory, the one who speaks of God must first be purified and live close to God. Not everyone may speak of God at any time, he says in substance, but only those who have been tested and cleansed (Oration 27, 3). "We must remember God more often than we draw breath" (Oration 27, 4, in CCC 2697).
  • The priest must first be holy. In his defence of his flight he writes, in the Catechism's words, "We must begin by purifying ourselves before purifying others; we must be instructed to be able to instruct, become light to illuminate" (Oration 2, 71, in CCC 1589).
  • Love the poor. His sermon On Love for the Poor (Oration 14) speaks for the sick and the lepers. All are one in Christ, rich and poor, slave and master, sick and healthy. "Make yourself God for the unfortunate, imitating God's mercy" (Oration 14, 26, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Prayer is a meeting of two desires. God thirsts that we may thirst for him (Oration 40, 27, in substance, as summarised by Benedict XVI). Through prayer the soul is purified and learns to see everything in the light of Christ.
  • Peace in the Church. His sermons on peace (Orations 6, 22 and 23) and his own resignation in 381 show a man who would rather give up his see than see the Church torn apart.
How he taught

The orator. Gregory was the finest Christian orator of his age. His sermons are carefully built, with rhythm, balance and striking images. He used the art he learned in Athens for the Gospel. Benedict XVI praises the five Theological Orations for their sound doctrine, clear reasoning and beauty. Byzantine schools later studied his sermons as models of style, and scholars say that after the Bible they were among the most copied texts of the Byzantine world.

The poet. He wrote poems in classical Greek metres, nearly 18,000 lines in all, according to Benedict XVI. Some teach doctrine in verse. Some are prayers. Some, like the long poem On His Own Life, tell his story with great honesty, his hurts and his weaknesses included. He wanted Christians to have beautiful poetry of their own.

The letter-writer. He collected his own letters for his grand-nephew Nicobulus, and in one of them he sets out the rules of good letter-writing: be brief, be clear, be graceful (Letter 51). His letters are short, warm and often witty.

Feast-day preaching. Many of his best sermons were given on great feasts: Christmas, Epiphany, Easter and Pentecost. Lines from them passed into the prayer of the Byzantine Churches. The Christmas canon still sung in those Churches opens with words from his Christmas sermon.

Honesty about himself. He speaks openly about his fears, his love of quiet and his wounded feelings. Benedict XVI notes that he often turned to Christ when he felt weary and unstable. His readers meet a real man who struggled, and who prayed.

His works

Orations

45 orations have come down under his name; one (Oration 35) is generally judged not to be his. They include:
The five Theological Orations (27 to 31), 380, on God and the Trinity.
On his flight (2), about 362, on the priesthood.
Against Julian (4 and 5), after 363.
Funeral orations for Caesarius (7), Gorgonia (8), his father (18) and Basil (43).
On Love for the Poor (14).
On peace (6, 22, 23).
In praise of St Athanasius (21), St Cyprian (24) and the Maccabees (15).
Feast sermons: Christmas (38), the Holy Lights (39), Baptism (40), Pentecost (41), Easter (1 and 45).
Farewell sermon at Constantinople (42), 381.

Letters

About 245 letters, depending on how they are counted; a few are doubtful.
Letters to Cledonius (101 and 102) and to Nectarius (202) against Apollinaris. They are among the most important texts on the person of Christ before the Council of Chalcedon.
Letter on style to Nicobulus (51).
Many letters to Basil, Gregory of Nyssa and friends.

Poems

Poems of nearly 18,000 lines, of several kinds:
Dogmatic poems on God, creation, Christ and the Scriptures.
Moral poems on virtue, virginity and the vanity of life.
Poems about himself, chief among them On His Own Life, nearly 2,000 lines, written after 381.
Epitaphs and epigrams, including twelve for Basil.
Some poems are lost; ancient writers knew of more.

Disputed or shared

Christ Suffering (Christus Patiens), a drama on the Passion that uses lines from Euripides. Once given to Gregory, today it is generally thought to be a later work.
His will (Testament), in which he leaves his goods to the poor of Nazianzus. It is generally accepted.
Philocalia of Origen, a selection made, according to tradition, with St Basil.

A short taste

"Christ is born, glorify Him. Christ from heaven, go out to meet Him." (Oration 38, 1, tr. C. G. Browne and J. E. Swallow)

"For that which He has not assumed He has not healed." (Letter 101, same translation)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave two General Audiences on him, on 8 and 22 August 2007. He presents him as a man of culture who put his gifts at the service of the Gospel, a gentle soul who sought peace in a divided Church, and a theologian whose thinking grew out of prayer. He draws out Gregory's teaching on the Trinity, on the full humanity of Christ, on Mary as Mother of God, on charity to the poor and on prayer. He sums up Gregory's teaching in this way: the soul's task is to find the true light and the true nobility of life in meeting God.

St Pius V counted him among the four great Doctors of the East in 1568, with St Athanasius, St Basil and St John Chrysostom.

Gregory XIII placed his relics in the Gregorian Chapel of St Peter's Basilica in 1580.

St John Paul II, on 27 November 2004, handed over a part of his relics, with those of St John Chrysostom, to Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I. In his letter for the occasion he called the two saints Doctors of the Church and holy Patriarchs of Constantinople, and he hoped the gesture would help the Churches toward unity.

The Catechism quotes him more than once: on the Trinity (CCC 256, 684), on Baptism (CCC 537, 1216), on the Holy Spirit and prayer (CCC 2565, 2670, 2697) and on the priesthood (CCC 1589).

The Eastern Churches give the title "the Theologian" to only a very few: St John the Evangelist, St Gregory and, later, St Symeon the New Theologian. On 30 January the Byzantine Churches keep the feast of the Three Holy Hierarchs, Basil, Gregory and John Chrysostom, as the great teachers of the Church.

Why he matters today

He shows that thinking about God is an act of love. He knew the best learning of his day and used it, but he never forgot that theology is born on one's knees. His warning still stands: we may talk about God only as far as we are willing to be purified by him.

He shows that a sensitive, wounded person can still serve God greatly. He was shy, he loved quiet, he was hurt by his best friend and pushed out of his see. Yet he spoke the faith clearly when it was needed, and his words shaped the Creed that Christians still pray.

He shows that a pastor may step aside for the sake of peace. When the council quarrelled over him, he chose to go rather than tear the Church apart.

And he shows that beauty serves the truth. Poetry, music and careful speech are not luxuries. They can carry the faith into hearts.

Sit with this

  1. Do I remember God "more often than I draw breath", or only now and then?
  2. Is there a wound from a friend that I still carry? What would it take to give it to Christ?
  3. When there is a quarrel, am I willing to step aside for the sake of peace?
