St Jerome

TimeBorn about 347 (older works say 340 to 342; estimates range wider); died at Bethlehem on 30 September 419 or 420
WhereBorn at Stridon, on the border of Dalmatia and Pannonia (its exact place is unknown; today somewhere in Slovenia, Croatia or Bosnia); studied in Rome (Italy); lived at Trier (Germany) and Aquileia (Italy); hermit near Chalcis, south of Aleppo (Syria); priest at Antioch (today Antakya, Turkey); in Constantinople (Istanbul); secretary in Rome; monk at Bethlehem (Palestine) for his last thirty-four years
What he wasPriest, monk, scholar of Scripture, translator, letter writer, spiritual guide
LanguageLatin; Greek and Hebrew learned with great effort, and some Aramaic
Known asTranslator of the Vulgate; "the greatest doctor in explaining Sacred Scripture" (older Roman Missal, prayer of his feast, in substance)
HonouredSaint. One of the four great Doctors of the Latin Church, declared by Pope Boniface VIII in 1298. Latin Church: 30 September (memorial). Byzantine tradition: 15 June, as "Blessed Jerome". The Syro-Malabar lectionary has proper readings for St Jerome on 30 September. Patron of translators, librarians and students of Scripture.
His life

Stridon. Jerome, in Latin Eusebius Hieronymus, was born about 347 into a Christian family of some means at Stridon, a small town on the border between Dalmatia and Pannonia. The town was destroyed by the Goths in his lifetime, and no one now knows exactly where it stood. He had a younger brother, Paulinian, who later followed him to Bethlehem, and a sister.

Rome and baptism. His parents sent him to Rome to study. He learned grammar under the famous teacher Donatus and studied rhetoric. He loved the Latin classics, Cicero and Virgil above all, and began to collect a library. As a young man he was drawn to the pleasures of the city, which he later regretted (Letter 22, 7). On Sundays he and his friends visited the tombs of the martyrs in the catacombs. He was baptized in Rome about 366.

Trier and Aquileia. He then went to Trier, an imperial city on the Moselle, where he first came to know the monastic life and copied books for himself. About 370 he joined a circle of fervent friends at Aquileia, at the head of the Adriatic, around Bishop Valerian. Among them was Rufinus, later his friend and then his opponent. Jerome called the group almost "a choir of the blessed" (Chronicle, year 374). The group broke up, for reasons that are not clear.

The dream, Antioch. About 373 or 374 he travelled east and stayed at Antioch. Two of his companions died, and he fell gravely ill. During that illness, in Lent, he dreamed that he stood before the judgment seat of God. Asked who he was, he said, "A Christian." The Judge replied that he lied: he was a Ciceronian, not a Christian. He was beaten, and promised never again to read worldly books (Letter 22, 30, in substance). In later years he did read the classics again, and quoted them. But from then on Scripture came first.

The desert of Chalcis. From about 375 to 377 he lived as a hermit in the desert of Chalcis, south of Aleppo. He fasted, prayed and fought hard temptations, as he told later with great frankness (Letter 22, 7). To tame his mind, he began to learn Hebrew from a Jewish convert, a labour he describes as bitter, with a fruit that was sweet (Letter 125, 12). He copied books and wrote his first letters. The monks around him were divided over the disputes of Antioch, and they pressed him to take sides. He wrote to Pope Damasus for guidance: "I am joined in communion with your Blessedness, that is, with the chair of Peter. On that rock I know the Church is built" (Letter 15, 2, in substance). He left the desert in about 377.

Priest. At Antioch, Bishop Paulinus ordained him a priest, on the condition that he would remain a monk and not be bound to serve a parish. A later tradition says that out of humility he seldom, if ever, celebrated the Eucharist in public.

Constantinople. About 379 to 381 he was in Constantinople. He heard St Gregory Nazianzen, whom he called his teacher, and met St Gregory of Nyssa. He translated into Latin homilies of Origen on the prophets and the Chronicle of Eusebius, which he continued to the year 378.

Rome with Damasus, 382 to 385. In 382 he went to Rome for a council with Bishop Paulinus and St Epiphanius. Pope Damasus kept him as secretary and adviser. The Pope asked him to revise the old Latin Gospels, which existed in many differing copies, against the Greek. He finished the Gospels in 383 or 384 and also revised the Psalter. In Rome a group of noble Christian women chose him as their teacher in Scripture: Marcella, Paula and her daughters Blesilla and Eustochium, Asella, Lea and others. They studied Hebrew and Greek with him. To Eustochium he wrote his long letter on virginity (Letter 22). He also wrote Against Helvidius, defending the lasting virginity of Mary.

Leaving Rome. Damasus died on 11 December 384. Jerome's sharp tongue had made enemies among the clergy, and there was gossip about his friendship with Paula. When young Blesilla died after severe fasting, people blamed him. He left Rome in August 385, bitter. Paula and Eustochium soon followed. Together they visited the holy places of Palestine and the monks of Egypt, and heard Didymus the Blind at Alexandria.

Bethlehem, 386. In the summer of 386 they settled at Bethlehem. Paula paid for a monastery for men, which Jerome led, a monastery for women in three groups, which she led, and a hostel for pilgrims, built, Jerome wrote, "remembering Mary and Joseph who had found no room" (Letter 108, 14). He lived in a cell close to the cave of the Nativity. He taught the local boys Latin and the classics, guided the monks, received pilgrims, and above all studied and wrote.

The great translation. From about 390 to 405 he translated the Old Testament from the Hebrew. It was huge work. He compared the Hebrew with the Greek versions in Origen's Hexapla, consulted Jewish teachers, and dictated to secretaries, often by night, with weak eyes. Some readers were alarmed, because people were used to the old Latin taken from the Greek Septuagint. St Augustine worried that a new version would divide the Churches of East and West. At Oea in Africa, a congregation nearly rioted when they heard in Jonah 4:6 that the prophet sat under an "ivy" and not the familiar "gourd".

Controversies. His life at Bethlehem was full of debates. Against Jovinian (393) he defended celibacy and fasting, and used words about marriage that even his friends found too harsh; he had to explain himself. From 393 the quarrel over the teachings of Origen broke out. Jerome, who had greatly admired Origen, turned against his errors. His old friend Rufinus, now at Jerusalem, defended Origen, and the two exchanged bitter writings until about 402. St Augustine begged them to stop. Against Vigilantius (406) he defended the honour paid to the martyrs and their relics. In 415 he wrote Dialogue against the Pelagians.

Jerome and Augustine. The two great Latin Doctors never met. Their letters, from 394 to about 419, began with misunderstandings: a letter of Augustine questioning Jerome's reading of Galatians 2 went astray and reached Rome before it reached Bethlehem. Jerome was hurt. Over the years they came to real respect, and in the Pelagian crisis they stood together.

The fall of Rome, 410. When the Goths sacked Rome in 410, refugees reached Bethlehem. Jerome wrote that the brightest light of the world had gone out and that in one city the whole world had perished (Commentary on Ezekiel, preface to book 1, in substance). Marcella died from the shock and the beatings she suffered.

Last years. Paula had died in January 404, and Jerome wrote her epitaph in Letter 108. In 416 a band, said to be stirred up by supporters of Pelagius, attacked and burned the monasteries at Bethlehem. Eustochium died about 419. Jerome, old, nearly blind and weak, kept working on his commentary on Jeremiah, which he never finished. He died on 30 September 419 or 420 and was buried near the cave of the Nativity. According to a later tradition, his remains were taken to Rome; Benedict XV wrote in 1920 that they lie in the Basilica of St Mary Major, beside the relic of the Lord's crib.

The world he lived in

Jerome's life spans the last strong years of the Roman Empire and the beginning of its fall in the West. He was a boy when Constantine's sons ruled, a monk when Theodosius made Nicene faith the faith of the Empire, and an old man when Rome was sacked.

It was a world on the move. Christians from Rome and Gaul went east to see the holy places and the desert monks. Noble families gave their fortunes to build monasteries and hostels. Jerome himself lived in great cities of West and East, in the desert, and at Bethlehem.

Latin Christians read the Bible in many old Latin versions, made from the Greek by different hands. Jerome said that there were almost as many versions as copies. Few Latin Christians knew Greek, and almost none knew Hebrew. This is the gap that he filled.

It was also the age of the great disputes: about Origen's ideas, about celibacy and marriage, about grace and human choice with Pelagius. Jerome took part in nearly all of them, always on the side of the Church's faith, if not always with gentleness.

What he taught
  • "Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ." His most famous line (Commentary on Isaiah, prologue), quoted by the Second Vatican Council (Dei Verbum 25) and the Catechism (CCC 133).
  • Scripture is God speaking. "If you pray, you speak with the Spouse; if you read, it is he who speaks to you" (Letter 22, 25, as quoted by Benedict XVI). The Bible is the means "by which God speaks every day to the faithful" (Letter 133, 13).
  • Go back to the source. Where Latin copies disagree, turn to the Greek of the New Testament and the Hebrew of the Old: "we shall find in the streams all that flows from the source" (Letter 106, 2, in substance). In Scripture "the order of the words is also a mystery" (Letter 57, 5, as quoted by Benedict XVI), and so a translator must be very careful.
  • Read with the Church. "Remain firmly attached to the traditional doctrine that you have been taught" (Letter 52, 7, as quoted by Benedict XVI). Scripture is read rightly in communion with the faith of the Church and the See of Peter (Letters 15 and 16).
  • The help of the Spirit. "In the interpretation of Sacred Scripture we always need the help of the Holy Spirit" (Commentary on Micah 1, 1, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Mary ever virgin. Against Helvidius he showed from Scripture that Mary remained a virgin, and that the "brothers" of the Lord were close relatives, a usage common in the Bible (see CCC 500). He also praised Joseph as virgin.
  • Christ in the poor. "What is the use of decorating the walls with precious stones if Christ dies of hunger in the person of the poor?" (Letter 58, 7, as quoted by Benedict XVI). Christians should "clothe Christ in the poor" (Letter 130, 14).
  • The life of the monk. Prayer, reading, work with the hands and fasting with prudence; obedience above all. Idleness is the enemy (Letter 125, 11).
  • Bringing up children. In his letters on the education of girls (Letters 107 and 128) he urges parents to teach by example, to make learning joyful, and to give daughters a full education, human, literary and religious, as Benedict XVI noted.
  • Preachers must live what they say. "May your actions never be unworthy of your words" (Letter 52, 7, to the priest Nepotian, in substance).
How he taught

He worked from the original. Jerome was the first great Christian scholar of the West to study Hebrew deeply. He visited places in the Holy Land to understand the geography of the Bible, and he asked Jewish scholars about words and customs.

He translated for the reader. In a famous letter on how to translate (Letter 57) he says that a good translator renders sense for sense, not word for word, except in Scripture, where he must keep very close to the text because even the word order holds a mystery.

He gathered opinions. In his commentaries he often set out the views of earlier writers, Jewish and Christian, Origen among them, and left the wise reader to judge "like an expert moneychanger" (Against Rufinus 1, 16, as quoted by Benedict XVI). This helped preserve much older learning, though it also caused him trouble when he reported views without judging them.

He wrote letters as works of art. About a hundred and twenty of his letters survive, with others written to him. Many were meant to be read by many. They are among the finest Latin prose of the age, lively, sharp and very personal.

He could be harsh. Benedict XVI speaks of his "difficult, hot-tempered character". He could mock his opponents and wound his friends. He knew this and wept over his faults.

He prayed as he studied. For Jerome, study of Scripture was a form of prayer and a remedy for the passions: "Love the knowledge of Scripture, and you will not love the vices of the flesh" (Letter 125, 11, in substance).

His works

The Bible in Latin

The four Gospels, revised from the Greek in Rome, 383 to 384.
The Psalter, revised more than once: a first revision in Rome (whether this is the "Roman Psalter" is debated); the "Gallican Psalter" from the Greek of Origen's Hexapla, about 386 to 390, which entered the Vulgate; and a Psalter from the Hebrew, about 392.
The Old Testament from the Hebrew, about 390 to 405: the whole Hebrew canon. Tobit and Judith quickly from Aramaic, and the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel.
He left Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and Maccabees in the old Latin. The rest of the New Testament in the Vulgate was revised, most scholars now think, by others.
Together, with later hands, this became the Vulgate.
Prefaces to many books, which explain his method.

Commentaries

On all sixteen prophets: the twelve minor prophets, Isaiah (18 books), Ezekiel (14), Daniel, and Jeremiah, unfinished at his death.
On Ecclesiastes.
On Matthew (398).
On Galatians, Ephesians, Titus and Philemon (386 to 388).
Hebrew Questions on Genesis, notes on the Hebrew text.
Book of Hebrew Names and Book of Places, the second a Latin version of Eusebius' list of biblical places with additions.
Brief notes on the Psalms (Commentarioli).

Homilies

About a hundred homilies to his monks at Bethlehem, on the Psalms, on Mark and on feasts, preserved as hearers' notes and recovered by Dom Germain Morin from the late nineteenth century.

Against errors

Dialogue against the Luciferians (about 382).
Against Helvidius, on the lasting virginity of Mary (383).
Against Jovinian, two books (393).
Against John of Jerusalem (about 397), unfinished.
Apology against Rufinus, three books (401 to 402).
Against Vigilantius (406).
Dialogue against the Pelagians, three books (415).

History and lives

Chronicle: Eusebius' world chronicle in Latin, continued from 325 to 378.
On Illustrious Men (392 to 393): short lives of 135 Christian writers, ending with himself.
Life of Paul the First Hermit (about 376); Life of Malchus; Life of Hilarion (about 391). The Life of Paul is the least reliable as history.

Letters and translations

Letters: about 120 by him, with others to him, about 150 in modern editions. Famous ones: 22 (to Eustochium), 52 (to Nepotian), 53 (to Paulinus), 57 (on translating), 107 (on a girl's education), 108 (on Paula).
Translations from Greek: homilies of Origen on the prophets, Luke and the Song of Songs (the Greek of some is lost); Didymus, On the Holy Spirit; the Rule of St Pachomius, from a Greek version.
Lost: his literal version of Origen's On First Principles, only fragments survive; most of his revision of the Old Testament from the Greek, apart from Psalms and Job.

A short taste

"Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ." (Commentary on Isaiah, prologue, as quoted in CCC 133)

"Seek to learn on earth those truths which will remain ever valid in Heaven." (Letter 53, 10, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 7 November 2007)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave two General Audiences on him, on 7 and 14 November 2007. He called him "a Church Father who centred his life on the Bible", who translated it, commented on it and "strove to live it". He drew out Jerome's love of Scripture, his reading in communion with the Church, his care for the poor, his teaching on education and his respect for the full education of women.

Benedict XV wrote the encyclical Spiritus Paraclitus on 15 September 1920, as the fifteenth centenary of his death drew near. He calls him the Church's "Greatest Doctor", "divinely given her for the understanding of the Bible".

Pope Francis wrote the Apostolic Letter Scripturae Sacrae Affectus on 30 September 2020, for the sixteenth centenary, on Jerome's love for Scripture.

The Council of Trent in 1546 declared the Vulgate "authentic" for public use in the Latin Church, meaning sound in faith and morals, not that other versions are wrong. After the Second Vatican Council a revision, the Nova Vulgata, was promulgated by St John Paul II in 1979, and remains the official Latin text.

The Second Vatican Council quotes his famous line in Dei Verbum 25, and the Catechism takes it up in CCC 133.

Where he was less sound. The Church honours him as a Doctor for Scripture, not for every opinion. Catholic scholars have noted that he valued the Septuagint too little once he discovered the Hebrew, that some of his words on marriage in Against Jovinian were excessive, and that on grace some of his phrasing is less exact than Augustine's. He also had doubts about the deuterocanonical books, though he used and translated some of them; the Church later settled their place in the canon.

Why he matters today

He reminds every Christian that the Bible must be read, and read often. "May your hands never set the Holy Book down" (Letter 52, 7, as quoted by Benedict XVI).

Every translator of the Bible, into Malayalam or any language, follows the road he opened: back to the Hebrew and Greek, with care, with the Church, and for ordinary readers.

He shows that a saint can have a hard temper and still become holy by fighting it. His faults were real, and he did penance for them.

He shows the worth of friendship in faith between men and women, as with Paula, Eustochium and Marcella, who studied, prayed and worked with him as partners.

And he links the Word with the Child of Bethlehem. He spent thirty-four years beside the cave where the Word was made flesh, putting the written Word into the language of his people.
