St Leo the Great

TimeBorn about 400 (the year is not known); Pope from 29 September 440; died 10 November 461
WhereTuscany (Italy), according to the Liber Pontificalis; Rome; a mission to Gaul (today France) in 440; met Attila near Mantua (northern Italy) in 452
What he wasDeacon of Rome, then Bishop of Rome and Pope; preacher, teacher of the faith about Christ, peacemaker
LanguageLatin
Known asLeo the Great (Leo Magnus); Leo I
HonouredSaint. Doctor of the Church, proclaimed by Pope Benedict XIV in 1754. Latin Church: 10 November (before 1969 it was 11 April). Byzantine tradition: 18 February. Syro-Malabar calendar: 10 November, among the feasts of the universal Church. His relics rest under the altar of St Leo in St Peter's Basilica.
His life

Beginnings. We know little of Leo's early years. The Liber Pontificalis, the old book of the Popes, says that he was a native of Tuscany and that his father was called Quintianus. The year of his birth is unknown. Most writers place it about 400.

Deacon of Rome. By about 430 he was a deacon of the Church of Rome and already respected far beyond the city. At his suggestion John Cassian, a monk in Gaul, wrote a work On the Incarnation of the Lord, against Nestorius, about 430 or 431, and dedicated it to him. About the same time St Cyril of Alexandria wrote to Rome on a dispute with the bishop of Jerusalem; from a later letter of Leo it is not clear whether Cyril wrote to Leo or to Pope Celestine.

The mission to Gaul, 440. In 440 the court of the Western Emperor Valentinian III, where his mother the Empress Galla Placidia still had great influence, sent Leo to Gaul to reconcile two quarrelling officials, the general Aetius and the magistrate Albinus. While he was there Pope Sixtus III died, on 19 August 440. The Roman Church chose Leo as his successor in his absence. He came home and was consecrated on 29 September 440. His pontificate lasted more than twenty-one years. Benedict XVI calls it "undoubtedly one of the most important in the Church's history".

Guarding the faith in the West. Leo at once took up the care of all the Churches. He learned that in Aquileia former followers of Pelagius were being received back without renouncing their error, and he asked for a synod there to make them profess the true faith. Many Manichaeans, a sect with a dark view of the body and of matter, had fled from Africa to Rome. In 443 Leo, with senators and clergy, held an inquiry into their secret community. Many were converted and reconciled. Others were banished by the civil power under imperial law. In Spain, Bishop Turibius of Astorga wrote to him about the Priscillianists, and Leo answered with a long letter in 447 (Letter 15). The Catechism notes that in this letter Leo already "confessed dogmatically" that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son (CCC 247).

Order in the Church. He wrote to the bishops of Sicily, who had begun to baptise on the Epiphany, asking them to keep the Roman custom of Easter and Pentecost (Letter 16). He gave detailed instructions to the bishops of Africa under Vandal rule (Letter 12) and to Bishop Rusticus of Narbonne (Letter 167). In 444 and 445 he clashed with St Hilary of Arles, who had deposed a bishop and acted beyond his rights. Leo restored the bishop and limited Hilary's authority, and an edict of Valentinian III of 8 July 445 supported him. The two later made peace, and after Hilary's death in 449 Leo spoke of him as "of blessed memory". In the East he confirmed the bishop of Thessalonica as his vicar for Illyricum, an office set up by earlier Popes, and he rebuked him sharply when he acted harshly.

The Tome to Flavian, 449. In Constantinople a respected old monk, Eutyches, taught that after the Incarnation Christ had only one nature, so that his humanity was absorbed into his divinity. His bishop, Flavian, condemned him in 448. Eutyches appealed to Rome. After studying the case, Leo sent Flavian a long doctrinal letter, dated 13 June 449, known as the Tome (Letter 28). It sets out in clear Latin the faith in one Christ, true God and true man.

The "Robber Synod", 449. That August a council met at Ephesus under Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria. Leo's letter was not read, his legates were pushed aside, Eutyches was restored, and Flavian was deposed and so badly treated that he died soon after. Leo refused to accept it, and in a letter to the Empress Pulcheria he called it not a council but a latrocinium, a "robbery". He wrote again and again to the Emperor Theodosius II, to Pulcheria, and to the Western court, asking for a true council.

The Council of Chalcedon, 451. After the death of Theodosius, the new Emperor Marcian and Pulcheria called a council at Chalcedon, across the water from Constantinople, in October 451. Benedict XVI notes that about 350 bishops took part, most from the East. When Leo's Tome was read, the proceedings record the bishops' cry: "Peter has spoken through the mouth of Leo". The council confessed that "one and the same Christ, Lord, and only-begotten Son, is to be acknowledged in two natures without confusion, change, division or separation" (CCC 467). Leo confirmed its teaching on the faith. He refused, however, the council's canon 28, which raised the see of Constantinople above the older sees of Alexandria and Antioch, because it went against the order set at Nicaea. In 453 he sent a circular letter confirming the definition of faith (Letter 114).

Attila, 452. In 452 Attila, king of the Huns, invaded northern Italy and destroyed Aquileia and other cities. At the Emperor's wish, Leo went north with the consul Avienus and the prefect Trigetius and met Attila by the river Mincio near Mantua. Attila turned back and left Italy. Benedict XVI says Leo "dissuaded him from continuing the war of invasion" and so saved the rest of the peninsula. A later legend, painted by Raphael, says that St Peter and St Paul appeared in the sky with swords above the Pope. Historians also point to hunger and disease in Attila's army. What is certain is that Leo went, unarmed, and that the Huns withdrew.

The Vandals, 455. In 455 the Vandals under Genseric reached Rome. Leo went out with his clergy to meet him. He could not stop the sack, which lasted two weeks, but, as Benedict XVI says, he "prevented Rome from being burned" and the basilicas of St Peter, St Paul and St John, where many had taken refuge, were spared. The Catholic Encyclopedia adds that he obtained a promise that the people's lives would be spared.

Pastor of Rome. Through all this he cared for his city. He preached on the great feasts and on the seasonal fast days. He helped refugees and the poor in a time of famine and war. He restored the roof of St Paul's Outside the Walls after it was struck by lightning, and repaired St Peter's. A Roman lady, Demetria, built a basilica of St Stephen on the Latin Way (Via Latina) in his time, as the Liber Pontificalis records.

Last years and death. In the East the conflict over Chalcedon continued, and Leo kept writing to emperors and bishops. His last letters, in 460, welcomed a new bishop of Alexandria in communion with Rome. He died on 10 November 461 and was buried in the porch of St Peter's. In 688 Pope Sergius I moved his remains into the basilica. Today they rest beneath the altar of St Leo, under Alessandro Algardi's great marble relief of the meeting with Attila.

The world he lived in

The Western Roman Empire was falling apart. Visigoths, Vandals, Huns and others moved across its lands. The Vandals had taken Africa, with Carthage, by 439. The emperors in the West were often young or weak, and real power lay with generals. Rome had been sacked by the Visigoths in 410, within living memory. Fifteen years after Leo's death the last Western emperor would be deposed.

In this collapse the Bishop of Rome became a figure people trusted. Benedict XVI notes that the times forced him "to play an important role in civil and political events", as would happen even more under Gregory the Great.

In the East the Empire was strong, and its capital, Constantinople, was rising in rank. There the great question was how to speak of Christ. After Ephesus (431) had confessed that Christ is one, some went too far and said that his humanity was swallowed up. Chalcedon (451) answered: one person, two natures. Benedict XVI calls Chalcedon "the sure goal of the Christology of the three previous Ecumenical Councils".

Rome itself was becoming a Christian city. The old pagan festivals still drew crowds, and some Christians bowed to the rising sun on the steps of St Peter's. Leo preached against such habits, and he taught his people that the Christian feasts were their true calendar.

What he taught
  • One Christ, true God and true man. This is the heart of the Tome. The Son of God "assumed" our nature without losing his own. "For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh" (Letter 28, 4, tr. Feltoe). The same Christ hungers and feeds the five thousand, weeps for Lazarus and raises him. The Roman liturgy sings what Leo preached: "What he was, he remained and what he was not, he assumed" (CCC 469).
  • Christ's Mysteries are present today. Benedict XVI stresses that for Leo the liturgy "is not the memory of past events, but the actualization of invisible realities". Easter is to be celebrated "not so much as something of the past as rather an event of the present" (cf. Sermon 64). In his sermon on the Ascension he says that "what was visible in our Savior has passed over into his mysteries", that is, into the sacraments (CCC 1115).
  • Christian dignity. "Christian, recognize your dignity", he says on Christmas Day; you share in God's own nature, so do not return to your former base condition by sinning (CCC 1691). The baptised are a royal and priestly people: "The sign of the cross makes kings of all those reborn in Christ and the anointing of the Holy Spirit consecrates them as priests" (Sermon 4, quoted in CCC 786).
  • Greater gifts than were lost. Why did God allow the first sin? "Christ's inexpressible grace gave us blessings better than those the demon's envy had taken away" (Sermon 73, quoted in CCC 412).
  • Peter in his successor. Leo saw himself as the heir of St Peter. "To one Apostle alone was entrusted what was communicated to all the Apostles" (Sermon 83, 2, quoted by Benedict XVI). The Church is built on the faith Peter confessed (cf. CCC 424). He used this office to serve the unity of the whole Church, East and West.
  • The Magi and the nations. In his Epiphany sermons the coming of the Magi is the beginning of the calling of all peoples to Christ (cf. CCC 528).
  • The Transfiguration. The glory shown on the mountain strengthens the disciples for the cross and shows what the whole Body of Christ will share (cf. Sermon 51, CCC 568).
  • The Holy Spirit. In his letter to Turibius he taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son (CCC 247).
  • Fasting with mercy. Fasting is joined to almsgiving and prayer. On the Ember Days, the fasts at the turn of each season, he urged the Romans to give to the poor what fasting saved. Benedict XVI notes that he linked "the practice of fasting with charity and almsgiving".
How he taught

By preaching to his people. Benedict XVI notes that he was the first Pope whose sermons to the people have come down to us. They are short, carefully built and easy to remember, written in a "splendid and clear Latin". He preached on the feasts of the year, from Christmas to Pentecost, and on the fasts.

By letters. His letters went to emperors, empresses and bishops in every province. They show a mind that is firm, exact and calm. He wrote with authority as Peter's successor, but he also explained and persuaded.

By choosing words carefully. The Tome sets the two sides of the mystery in balanced pairs: he was born and he is eternal; he was laid in a manger and the angels sang. This style made deep teaching easy to hear and repeat.

By acting in person. When his city was in danger he went out himself, to Attila in 452 and to Genseric in 455. His teaching on peace was not only in words.

By the means of his age. Like many bishops of his time, he called on the civil power against groups he judged harmful, such as the Manichaeans. The Second Vatican Council has since taught that no one may be forced to act against conscience in religious matters (Dignitatis Humanae 2). We can honour Leo's zeal for the truth while seeing that the Church now defends it differently.

His works

Sermons

Ninety-six sermons accepted as genuine (Benedict XVI says "almost 100"). They include five on the anniversaries of his consecration; ten on Christmas; eight on the Epiphany; many on Lent and the Ember fasts; nineteen on the Passion; sermons on Easter, the Ascension and Pentecost; on St Peter and St Paul, St Lawrence and the Maccabees; and on the collections for the poor.

Letters

About 143 letters from Leo survive, with about thirty written to him (Benedict XVI speaks of about 150 in all). Among them: Letter 28, the Tome to Flavian (449); Letter 15 to Turibius (447); Letter 16 to the bishops of Sicily on Baptism; Letters 104 to 106 on canon 28 of Chalcedon (452); Letter 114, confirming Chalcedon (453); Letter 165, a second doctrinal letter to the Emperor Leo I (458); Letter 167 to Rusticus of Narbonne.

Liturgy

The "Leonine Sacramentary" (today often called the Verona Sacramentary) is a collection of Roman Mass prayers made after his time, in the sixth century. Some of its prayers may go back to Leo or his circle, but which ones is debated.

Not his

Some works once printed under his name, such as the treatise On the Calling of All Nations, are now usually given to other writers, often to his secretary St Prosper of Aquitaine.

A short taste

"Our Saviour, dearly-beloved, was born today: let us be glad." (Sermon 21, 1, on the Nativity, tr. C. L. Feltoe)

"Christian, acknowledge your dignity." (Sermon 21, 3, tr. C. L. Feltoe; the Catechism renders it "Christian, recognize your dignity", CCC 1691)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The Council of Chalcedon received his Tome as the expression of the Catholic faith, with the cry "Peter has spoken through the mouth of Leo".

Benedict XIV proclaimed him a Doctor of the Church in 1754.

Benedict XVI gave a General Audience on him on 5 March 2008. He calls him "one of the greatest Pontiffs to have honoured the Roman See". He recalls the meeting with Attila as "an emblematic sign of the Pontiff's action for peace", and praises his courage before Genseric. He ends: "in faith we learn charity", and asks us "to believe in Christ, true God and true Man, and to implement this faith every day in action for peace and love of neighbour".

The Catechism quotes him often: on original sin (CCC 412), Peter's faith (CCC 424), the Incarnation (CCC 469), the Epiphany (CCC 528), the Transfiguration (CCC 568), the royal priesthood (CCC 786), the sacraments (CCC 1115), Christian dignity (CCC 1691), and the procession of the Holy Spirit (CCC 247).

The liturgy. His Christmas sermon "Christian, recognize your dignity" is read in the Office of Readings of the Roman Liturgy of the Hours on Christmas Day, and other sermons of his are read through the year. His feast is kept on 10 November in the Latin Church and on 18 February in the Byzantine Churches.

With the Churches that did not receive Chalcedon. The Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian and Malankara Orthodox Churches did not receive the Council of Chalcedon, and for centuries Leo's name was a point of division. In recent decades common declarations of the Popes with the heads of several of these Churches have confessed together that Christ is perfect God and perfect man, without mingling or separation, and have recognised that the old divisions came in large part from differences of words and history. Leo's concern for one faith in one Christ is now served by this dialogue.

Why he matters today

He answered the question every generation asks: who is Jesus? Not a man in whom God lived, not God dressed up as a man, but one Lord, wholly God and wholly human. When we pray before the crib or the cross, we pray with his faith.

He shows that the Church's feasts are not anniversaries only. Christmas, Easter and Pentecost make present what they celebrate, and so every Christian can say "today".

He shows that a pastor serves his people in body as well as soul. In a time of war, refugees and hunger, he preached, fed the poor, rebuilt churches and went out to face the invaders.

He shows what the ministry of Peter is for: to keep the Churches in one faith and one love. His firmness at Chalcedon and his care for the East are part of the long story that today seeks the full unity of all Christians.

Sit with this

  1. "Christian, recognize your dignity." What would change if I believed this about myself, and about the person I find hardest?
  2. Which feast of Christ can I live this year as "today", not only as a memory?
  3. Where am I asked, like Leo, to go out unarmed to meet a danger for the sake of others?
