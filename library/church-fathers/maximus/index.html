St Maximus the Confessor

TimeBorn about 580; died 13 August 662, aged about 82
WhereConstantinople (today Istanbul, Turkey), or Palestine by another account; monasteries at Chrysopolis (today Üsküdar, Istanbul) and Cyzicus (near today's Erdek, Turkey); North Africa around Carthage (today Tunisia); Rome (Italy); exile in Thrace (today European Turkey) and Lazica (today western Georgia), where he died
What he wasImperial official, then monk and abbot; theologian, spiritual teacher and defender of the faith. He was never a bishop and probably never a priest.
LanguageGreek
Known asMaximus the Confessor, because he confessed the faith under torture without being put to death; in Greek, Maximos Homologetes; "the theologian of the two wills"
HonouredSaint, venerated from soon after his death. Not declared a Doctor of the Church; the title is given formally and to few, and this is no judgment against him. Roman Martyrology: 13 August. Byzantine tradition: 21 January, and 13 August for the translation of his relics. He does not appear in the 2025 to 2026 Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara calendars that were checked.
His life

Two accounts of his youth. We have two very different stories of his early years. A Greek Life, written long after his death, says he was born about 580 in Constantinople, the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire, to a noble family, and was well educated. A Syriac Life, written by an opponent, says he was born in a village in Palestine, of humble and irregular origins, and was raised in a monastery there. Historians weigh these accounts differently; neither is fully trusted. Benedict XVI, in his catechesis, follows the Palestinian account: born in the land of the Lord, he entered monastic life as a boy and studied the Scriptures with the help of Origen's works. The Catholic Encyclopedia and the New Catholic Encyclopedia follow the Greek Life.

At the imperial court. According to the Greek Life, the young Maximus entered the civil service and became first secretary to the Emperor Heraclius, who reigned from 610 to 641 and valued him highly. Whether or not this is exact, his writings show a man who knew the court, its officials and its ways, and who wrote to governors and generals as one who had moved among them.

Monk at Chrysopolis. In 613 or 614, by the usual dating, he left the court to become a monk at a monastery in Chrysopolis, across the Bosporus from Constantinople. It is often identified as the monastery of Philippicus. He became its abbot, or at least a leading monk. There he met a young disciple, Anastasius, who would stay with him to the end.

Flight, 626. About 624 or 625 he moved to the monastery of St George at Cyzicus, on the southern shore of the Sea of Marmara. In 626 the Persians and the Avars advanced on Constantinople, and the monks scattered. Maximus fled. The sources speak of time on Crete, and perhaps on Cyprus, before he reached North Africa about 628 to 630.

Africa and Sophronius. In Africa he lived among Eastern monks near Carthage, in a community linked with the name of Eukratas. There he came under the guidance of Sophronius, a Palestinian monk whom Maximus called his master, father and teacher. Sophronius later became Patriarch of Jerusalem in 634 and was the first to raise an alarm against the new teaching on Christ that would shape the rest of Maximus's life. In these African years Maximus wrote most of his great spiritual and theological works: the Chapters on Love, the Questions to Thalassius, the Ambigua and the Mystagogy.

The quarrel begins. The Empire was wounded. Persia had taken Syria, Palestine and Egypt, and soon Arab armies would take them again. Many Christians in those lands did not accept the Council of Chalcedon (451), which had taught that Christ is one Person in two natures. To bring them back, Patriarch Sergius of Constantinople, with the Emperor's support, proposed a compromise: Christ has two natures but only one "activity", and then, only one will. In 638 Heraclius made this law in a statement called the Ecthesis. At first Maximus wrote cautiously, asking that the phrase "one activity" be withdrawn or explained. Over the following years he came to see that the question touched the heart of the faith. If Christ has no human will, he is not truly man, and what he did not take up he did not save.

The debate with Pyrrhus, 645. Pyrrhus, who had succeeded Sergius as Patriarch of Constantinople, lost his see in 641 and came to Africa. In July 645, at Carthage, before the governor Gregory and the bishops, Maximus and Pyrrhus held a public debate. The record survives as the Disputation with Pyrrhus. Pyrrhus was persuaded. He went to Rome, condemned his earlier teaching, and was received by Pope Theodore. Benedict XVI recalls his words, as reported: "I ask forgiveness for myself and for those who have preceded me". Pyrrhus later went back on this, but the debate showed the clarity of Maximus's thought. Maximus too went to Rome, probably with Pyrrhus or soon after.

The Lateran Council, 649. In 648 the Emperor Constans II issued the Typos, a decree that simply forbade anyone to speak of one will or two. Maximus would not be silent where the faith was at stake. In October 649 Pope St Martin I held a council at the Lateran in Rome with more than a hundred bishops, most from Italy, with Maximus and other Eastern monks present. It taught that Christ has two natural wills and two natural activities, divine and human, and condemned both the Ecthesis and the Typos. Many scholars think Maximus helped to prepare its texts.

The Pope taken, 653. The Emperor took this as rebellion. In June 653 Pope Martin was seized in Rome and taken to Constantinople. He was condemned, ill-treated and sent into exile at Cherson in the Crimea, where he died on 16 September 655. He is honoured as a martyr. Maximus and his disciple Anastasius were also arrested, in Rome according to the Greek Life, and brought to Constantinople. The exact date of their arrest is debated.

First trial, 655. The record of the first trial survives. Maximus was charged with political crimes: that he had betrayed Egypt, Alexandria and Africa to the Arabs, and had supported a rebel governor. He denied all of it. The real issue was the Typos. Asked to enter communion with the Church of Constantinople as it then stood, he refused. He was sent into exile at Bizya in Thrace (today Vize, in European Turkey); Anastasius was sent to Perberis.

Bizya, 656. In August 656 Bishop Theodosius of Caesarea in Bithynia came to Bizya with officials to persuade him. The record of their talk survives as the Dispute at Bizya. By the end, Theodosius seemed to agree with him, and promised to work for peace. Nothing came of it. Maximus was moved from place to place, stripped of his belongings, and taken at last to Perberis. In 658 envoys of the Patriarch came again, it is reported, with offers and threats.

The confession, 662. In 662 Maximus and the two Anastasii, his disciple and the papal envoy Anastasius the Apocrisiarius, were brought back to Constantinople and tried again. They were condemned. Their sentence was cruel. Maximus was beaten, his tongue was cut out and his right hand was cut off, so that he could neither speak nor write against the decree. He was paraded through the city and sent far away to Lazica, at the eastern end of the Black Sea, today in western Georgia. Benedict XVI calls this land Colchis. Weak and old, he did not live long. The disciple Anastasius died on the road in July. Maximus died on 13 August 662 at the fortress of Schemaris, at about eighty-two. A later account speaks of lights seen at his tomb; the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that this account is not contemporary.

Vindicated, 680 to 681. Eighteen years after his death, the Third Council of Constantinople, the sixth of the ecumenical councils, met under the Emperor Constantine IV, with the legates of Pope St Agatho. It taught that Christ has two wills and two natural operations, divine and human, not opposed to one another but working together, so that the Word made flesh willed humanly, in obedience to his Father, all that he had decided divinely with the Father and the Holy Spirit for our salvation (CCC 475). It was what Maximus had taught, and what he had suffered for.

The world he lived in

An empire under pressure. Maximus was born under the Emperor Maurice, who was murdered in 602. Then came war with Persia, which lasted a quarter of a century. Persia took Antioch, Damascus, Jerusalem and Egypt. In 614 the relic of the True Cross was carried off from Jerusalem. In 626 Persians and Avars stood at the walls of Constantinople. Heraclius won the war and brought back the Cross in 630. Within a few years, the armies of the new Arab state took Syria, Jerusalem in 637 or 638, and Egypt. North Africa itself would follow later in the century.

A Church divided over Christ. Since the Council of Chalcedon in 451, many Christians in Egypt and Syria, following St Cyril of Alexandria's phrase "one incarnate nature of God the Word", confessed Christ truly God and truly man but would not say "two natures", while the Church of the Empire held to two natures in one Person. In our own time the Catholic Church and the Oriental Orthodox Churches have declared together that they share the same faith in Christ, though they express it in different words. Emperors longed for unity in the face of their enemies. The proposals of "one activity" (monoenergism) and then "one will" (monothelitism) were meant as bridges. Maximus saw that they bought unity at the price of truth about Christ.

Rome and the East. In this crisis Rome held firm, and Maximus looked to Rome. He wrote, in substance, that since the Word came down to us in the Incarnation, all the Churches everywhere have held the great Church at Rome to be their basis and foundation, for the gates of hell have never prevailed against her; the Catechism cites him on this (CCC 834). Popes and monks stood together, and both paid for it: Pope Martin died in exile; Maximus died mutilated.

Monks. This was also an age of great monastic life in the East. Monks were scholars, spiritual guides, and often the voice of the faith when bishops kept quiet. Maximus, a simple monk without high office, carried the cause of orthodoxy on his own shoulders for years.

An honest note on Pope Honorius. In the 630s Pope Honorius I had written in favour of "one will" in Christ, meaning, it seems, that Christ had no sinful will divided against God. Maximus defended Honorius in that sense. The Third Council of Constantinople later condemned Honorius for helping the error, and Pope Leo II confirmed the condemnation. Catholic teaching sees here a Pope's failure of vigilance in a private letter, not a definition of faith.

What he taught
  • Christ has a true human will. This is the teaching for which he is called Confessor. Christ is one Person, the eternal Son, with two complete natures, divine and human. Each nature has its own will and its own activity. As Benedict XVI puts it, "It cannot be said that Christ has a single will!" A man without a will "is not a real man but an amputated man". He taught this in the Disputation with Pyrrhus, in many short treatises (the Opuscula theologica et polemica) and in his letters.
  • Gethsemane. In the garden Jesus prayed, "yet not what I want but what you want" (Matthew 26:39). Maximus saw here the human will of Christ, which naturally shrinks from death, willingly saying yes to the will of the Father. Benedict XVI draws out his thought: Adam thought that saying no to God was the height of freedom, but "The height of freedom is the "yes", in conformity with God's will." Man does not lose himself by uniting his will to God's; he finds himself.
  • Natural will and hesitating will. Maximus distinguished the natural will, which every human being has as part of being human, from the "gnomic" will, the wavering will of choice that weighs up good and evil because it does not see clearly. Christ has a perfect natural human will, but not our hesitating, divided will. His human will was never against the Father (Disputation with Pyrrhus; Opuscula).
  • Man is made to share God's life. God became man, Maximus says in substance, so that man might become god by grace. This sharing in the divine life, called deification (theosis), is the goal of all creation. It does not destroy our nature but heals and fulfils it (Ambigua; Questions to Thalassius 22, 60). The Catechism uses his words for the first sin: man wanted to "be like God", but "without God, before God, and not in accordance with God" (CCC 398, citing the Ambigua).
  • The words in creation. All things were made through the Word (John 1:3). Maximus teaches that each creature has its own logos, an inner "word" or reason, in the eternal Word of God. To see the world rightly is to read these words and be led to the Word (Ambigua 7).
  • Man the bridge of creation. Created in God's image, the human being stands where the world divides: between spirit and matter, heaven and earth. Our calling is to bring the world together and offer it to God. Adam failed; Christ did it in himself (Ambigua 41). Hans Urs von Balthasar called this vision the "cosmic liturgy", and Benedict XVI adopts the phrase: "We must live united to God in order to be united to ourselves and to the cosmos".
  • The liturgy as a picture of everything. In the Mystagogy he explains the church building and the Divine Liturgy. The nave and the sanctuary are like the visible and invisible worlds, like body and soul. The entrance, the readings, the kiss of peace and the Creed all lead to union with God. Benedict XVI singles this out as a work of special importance.
  • Love above all. His Four Hundred Chapters on Love teach that love is the goal of every practice. In substance: love is a good disposition of the soul by which a person prefers nothing to the knowledge of God (Chapters on Love 1, 1). He warns against the passions that turn us in on ourselves, and asks that we love even those who wrong us.
  • The Lord's Prayer. In his Commentary on the Our Father he finds in the prayer the whole mystery of salvation: the Father's name, the Kingdom, the gift of grace, forgiveness and the defeat of evil.
  • Christ's human knowledge. The Catechism cites him when it teaches that the human nature of God's Son, not by itself but by its union with the Word, knew and showed in itself all that pertains to God (CCC 473, citing Questions and Doubts 66).
  • Rome. He looked to the Church of Rome as the sure guide in faith and held that its communion was decisive (CCC 834, citing the Opuscula).

Some modern writers have read a hope of universal salvation into a few of his passages. Most scholars think he did not teach it as doctrine. The Church has not condemned anything in his teaching.

How he taught

He answered questions. Many of his works began as answers to friends: a monk, a bishop, an official. The Questions to Thalassius answer sixty-five hard questions on Scripture. The Ambigua explain difficult passages in St Gregory Nazianzen and in the writings that go under the name of Dionysius the Areopagite.

He wrote short chapters. In the Chapters on Love and the Chapters on Theology, he wrote in "centuries", sets of a hundred short sayings, each to be read slowly and pondered. This was a monastic form, made for prayer, not a quick reading.

He read Scripture deeply. He looked beneath the letter for the spiritual sense, as Origen had done, but always within the Church's faith and the teaching of the councils.

He was exact. In debate he was careful with every word. He asked what each term meant, and would not accept a phrase that could hide an error. Benedict XVI notes that his thought was never only theory: it was about the meaning of life and the salvation of the world.

He gathered the Fathers. He drew on St Gregory Nazianzen, St Gregory of Nyssa, Dionysius, Evagrius and others, and gave them a new unity around Christ. The New Catholic Encyclopedia quotes a scholar who called him perhaps the last independent theologian of the Byzantine Church.

He suffered for it. His last teaching was his silence. Without tongue or right hand, he taught by staying faithful.

His works

About ninety works survive, many short. Dates are scholars' estimates.

Spiritual

Four Hundred Chapters on Love (Capita de caritate), probably 620s. Complete.
Two Hundred Chapters on Theology and the Incarnation (Capita theologica et oeconomica). Complete.
The Ascetic Life (Liber asceticus), a dialogue between an elder and a young monk. Complete.
Commentary on the Our Father. Complete.
Further sets of chapters (Capita XV, Diversa capita); only some are his.

Scripture and the Fathers

Questions to Thalassius (Quaestiones ad Thalassium), 65 answers, about 630 to 633.
Questions and Doubts (Quaestiones et dubia).
Questions to Theopemptus.
Commentary on Psalm 59.
Ambigua to John, on difficult passages in St Gregory Nazianzen, and Ambigua to Thomas, on Gregory and Dionysius, about 630s. Put into Latin by John Scotus Eriugena in the 9th century.

Liturgy

Mystagogy (Mystagogia), on the church building and the Divine Liturgy. Complete.
Hymns.
Computus, on the calculation of dates, especially Easter.

On Christ and the two wills

Disputation with Pyrrhus, 645.
Theological and polemical treatises (Opuscula theologica et polemica), about twenty-seven short works, from about 626 to the 640s, including the Tome to Marinus.
Testimonies and syllogisms.

Letters

About forty-five letters in the main collection, and others, to bishops, monks, officials and friends. Some are full treatises. Some letters survive only in Latin, kept by Anastasius the Librarian.

Records of his trials

Record of the Trial (Relatio motionis), 655, and Dispute at Bizya, 656, written by his disciples. With letters of the two Anastasii, they are our best sources for his last years.

Disputed

Scholia on Dionysius: many are by John of Scythopolis, not Maximus.
Life of the Virgin, surviving in a Georgian version: the manuscripts name Maximus as author; some scholars accept this, many doubt it, and the question is still open.
The texts of the Lateran Council of 649: he may have helped to draft them.

A short taste

"Without God, before God, and not in accordance with God." Maximus on what Adam wanted, as quoted in the Catechism (CCC 398, citing the Ambigua).

In substance: love is a good state of the soul in which a person prefers nothing that exists to the knowledge of God (Chapters on Love 1, 1).

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave a General Audience on him on 25 June 2008. He tells of his life and his suffering, and explains that the Church calls him Confessor because he bore witness, even in suffering, to Christ fully God and fully man. He presents Gethsemane as the heart of his teaching. He ends by asking what Maximus says to us: that Christ is the one reference point by which all other values are judged. "Jesus Christ is the reference point that gives light to all other values." Without Christ, the Pope warns, "Dialogue that no longer knows what to discuss becomes empty chatter."

The Third Council of Constantinople (680 to 681) taught the faith he died for. The Catechism gives its teaching on Christ's two wills (CCC 475).

The Catechism cites him three times: on the first sin (CCC 398), on Christ's human knowledge (CCC 473), and on the Church of Rome (CCC 834).

The Roman Martyrology commemorates him on 13 August.

In the East he is among the most loved of the Fathers. The Philokalia, the great collection of spiritual writings used by Eastern monks, gives him a large place. In the West, John Scotus Eriugena put the Ambigua into Latin, and in the 20th century Hans Urs von Balthasar brought him to wide attention with his book Cosmic Liturgy.

Why he matters today

Freedom. We tend to think that we are most ourselves when we do what we want. Maximus says we are most ourselves when our will is one with God's, as in Gethsemane. That is not slavery; it is the healing of the will.

Christ fully human. Every time we pray to Jesus in our struggles, we pray to one who had a human will like ours, who felt fear before death and still said yes. Maximus fought so that we could say this.

Courage of conscience. He was one old monk against an emperor, a patriarch and almost every bishop of the East. He was not proud or harsh; he simply would not say what was not true. He shows that the truth about Christ is worth a whole life.

The world as a gift. His vision of the "cosmic liturgy" gives Christians a reason to care for creation: every creature carries a word from God, and we are meant to offer the world back to him.

East and West. He was a Greek who looked to Rome, a monk honoured equally in Catholic and Orthodox Churches. He is a sign of the unity the Church still seeks.

Sit with this

  1. Where in my life am I saying "my will" to God, and where am I learning to say "yours"?
  2. Do I believe that Jesus truly understands my fears, because he had a human will and a human heart?
  3. What truth would I keep to even if no one around me agreed?
