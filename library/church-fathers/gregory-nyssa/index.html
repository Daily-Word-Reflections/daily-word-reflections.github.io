St Gregory of Nyssa

TimeBorn about 335, died after 394, perhaps about 395
WhereProbably born at Caesarea in Cappadocia (today Kayseri, Turkey); family estate at Annesi in Pontus (northern Turkey); bishop of Nyssa, a small town of Cappadocia (thought to be near Harmandalı, Aksaray province, Turkey); journeys to Antioch (today Antakya, Turkey), Arabia and Jerusalem; councils at Constantinople (today Istanbul)
What he wasTeacher of rhetoric, then bishop, theologian, writer on the spiritual life, preacher
LanguageGreek
Known asGregory of Nyssa, Gregory Nyssen; one of the three Cappadocian Fathers; often called the father of Christian mysticism; "Father of Fathers" in the tradition of the Second Council of Nicaea
HonouredSaint. Byzantine tradition: 10 January. Roman Martyrology: 10 January (older editions had 9 March). He is not in the General Roman Calendar. He has not been declared a Doctor of the Church, a title the Popes have given to relatively few saints; this takes nothing away from his holiness or from the respect the Church has for his teaching.
His life

The younger brother. Gregory was born about 335, probably at Caesarea in Cappadocia, into the remarkable family of St Basil the Great. His father, Basil the Elder, was a teacher of rhetoric. His mother, Emmelia, was the daughter of a martyr. His grandmother, St Macrina the Elder, had handed on the faith she learned from the disciples of St Gregory the Wonderworker. There were ten children. His eldest sister, St Macrina, and his brothers Basil and Peter, later Bishop of Sebaste, are all honoured as saints. Gregory was one of the younger ones.

Taught by his brother. Gregory did not go to Athens like Basil. He was educated mostly at home, and his older brother Basil guided much of his learning. Gregory later called him "father and teacher" (Letter 13, 4, as quoted by Benedict XVI). Benedict XVI says that Basil and their sister Macrina guided his Christian education with special care, and that in his studies he especially valued philosophy and rhetoric.

A teacher of rhetoric, and married. As a young man he served as a reader in the Church. Then he turned to a career as a teacher of rhetoric. His family was disappointed, and St Gregory Nazianzen wrote to him gently but firmly, asking why he preferred to be called a rhetorician rather than a Christian. Benedict XVI notes that he was married. A letter of condolence from Gregory Nazianzen speaks of a woman named Theosebia, apparently his wife, who died later. In his early book On Virginity, written about 371, Gregory praises consecrated virginity and says with some sadness that he himself had not chosen it.

Toward the ascetic life. Like his brother and sister, he gave himself more and more to prayer and a disciplined life. Some historians think he spent time in Basil's monastery in Pontus; others say the sources do not prove it.

Bishop of Nyssa, about 371 or 372. Basil, now metropolitan of Caesarea, was struggling to hold his province against the Arian Emperor Valens. He needed reliable bishops. He made Gregory bishop of the small town of Nyssa. Gregory was reluctant. Basil sometimes complained that his brother was too simple for church politics. Benedict XVI calls him, nonetheless, a zealous pastor.

Exile, 376 to 378. His enemies accused him before the governor of wasting church money and of an irregular election. Soldiers came to arrest him; he escaped. In 376 a synod of bishops loyal to the Arian party met at Nyssa and deposed him. For two years he wandered from place to place. When Valens died in 378, the new emperor allowed exiled bishops to return. Gregory came home to Nyssa, and the people welcomed him with joy; he describes the scene in one of his letters (Letter 6).

Two deaths, 379. His brother Basil died in 379, probably on 1 January. Some months later Gregory went to visit Macrina at the family estate at Annesi in Pontus. He found her dying. They talked for a long time about death and the resurrection. He later wrote down that conversation as the dialogue On the Soul and the Resurrection, with Macrina as the teacher. He also wrote her life, The Life of Macrina, in which he records her last prayer. Benedict XVI holds up this prayer as an example of how a Christian may face death in peace.

Basil's heir. From now on Gregory carried forward Basil's work. In 379 he took part in a council at Antioch. Later, probably around the time of the Council of 381, he was sent to restore order in the Church of Arabia and visited Jerusalem; he was troubled by what he saw there, and wrote that holiness does not depend on travelling to holy places (Letter 2).

The Council of 381. At the Council of Constantinople in 381, which confessed the divinity of the Holy Spirit, Gregory played a leading part. Benedict XVI says so plainly. He preached the funeral oration for St Meletius of Antioch, who died during the council. On 30 July 381 an edict of the Emperor Theodosius named him among the bishops in the East whose communion was the test of the Catholic faith.

Trusted by the court. The Emperor gave him official missions. In 385 or 386 he preached at Constantinople the funerals of the little princess Pulcheria and of the Empress Flacilla. He probably attended a synod there in 383, and Benedict XVI notes that he took part in another in 394. After that he disappears from history. He probably died soon after, about 395. Nyssa was too small for fame, but his writings made the name of the town known across the world.

The world he lived in

Gregory lived in the generation after the Council of Nicaea (325). Nicaea had confessed that the Son is of one being with the Father. But under the Emperor Valens, who ruled the East from 364 to 378, the opposite party held power. A brilliant and radical Arian, Eunomius, taught that the Son was simply unlike the Father, and that the human mind could fully grasp God's essence in a single word, "unbegotten". Others, called Macedonians (after Bishop Macedonius, not the land of Macedonia) or Pneumatomachians, "fighters against the Spirit", denied that the Holy Spirit is God. Apollinaris of Laodicea, a friend of Nicaea, went wrong in another way: he taught that in Christ the divine Word took the place of a human mind.

Gregory answered all three. When Basil died in 379, the younger brother found himself one of the chief defenders of the faith of Nicaea in the East.

It was also the age of the first great monastic movement. Gregory's own sister Macrina had turned the family estate into a community of women, where former servants and noblewomen lived as sisters. Gregory saw there a picture of the Kingdom: people of every rank living as equals before God.

Greek philosophy was the language of educated people. Gregory knew Plato and the later Platonists well. He used their words and ideas, but he bent them to serve the Bible: a God who creates out of love, a world that is good, a body that will rise.

What he taught
  • One God, three Persons. Against Eunomius he defended the full divinity of the Son. In On Not Three Gods, written to Ablabius, he explains why Christians who confess Father, Son and Holy Spirit do not worship three gods: every work of God begins from the Father, passes through the Son and is completed in the Holy Spirit, one single action of the one God. His book On the Holy Spirit against the Macedonians defends the divinity of the Spirit. The Catechism quotes him on the Spirit as the anointing of Christ, coming to all who approach the Son in faith (CCC 690).
  • God is beyond our grasp. Eunomius thought the mind could hold God in a definition. Gregory answered that God is infinite. We know him by his works and by his love, but we never comprehend him. This is not a reason to despair. It is the reason the journey to God never ends.
  • Never-ending growth. This is Gregory's great insight. In the Life of Moses he reads Moses' climb up Mount Sinai into the cloud as the soul's ascent to God. The closer the soul comes, the more it desires. The Catechism quotes two of his lines: "Christian perfection has but one limit, that of having none" (Life of Moses, in CCC 2028), and "He who climbs never stops going from beginning to beginning, through beginnings that have no end" (Homilies on the Song of Songs 8, in CCC 2015). Benedict XVI explains: the soul is always on the way, always opening to greater good.
  • The human person, a royal image. God made man to rule creation as a king, in his image. In On the Making of Man Gregory says that God, "the best artist", shaped our nature for royalty (De hominis opificio 4, as quoted by Benedict XVI). Sin darkened the image. Purification lets it shine again: then "the divine beauty will shine in you" (On the Beatitudes 6, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Christ the physician. Why did God become man? Gregory answers in his Great Catechism: "Sick, our nature demanded to be healed; fallen, to be raised up; dead, to rise again" (Catechetical Oration 15, in CCC 457). He also wrote against Apollinaris that Christ had a full human soul and mind.
  • Death and resurrection. In death Christ's soul and body were separated, but the divine Person stayed united to both. So the Resurrection is the reuniting of the two (Catechetical Oration 16, in CCC 625; On the Resurrection of Christ 1, in CCC 650).
  • The Eucharist. In the Great Catechism (chapter 37) he teaches, in substance, that the bread and wine are changed by the word of blessing into the Body and Blood of the Word made flesh, so that by eating it our mortal bodies receive the seed of immortality.
  • The Beatitudes. His eight homilies on the Beatitudes are a school of the Christian life. "The goal of a virtuous life is to become like God" (On the Beatitudes 1, in CCC 1803). On poverty of spirit as God's own humility (CCC 2546). On seeing God: "Whoever sees God has obtained all the goods of which he can conceive" (On the Beatitudes 6, in CCC 2548). Benedict XVI adds his line: it is not a matter of knowing something about God, but of "having God within".
  • Prayer and the Our Father. His five homilies on the Lord's Prayer teach that we must contemplate the beauty of the Father without ceasing and adorn our own souls to match it (On the Lord's Prayer, in CCC 2784). Prayer, he says, is "a support and protection of charity, a brake on anger" (On the Lord's Prayer 1, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Christ in the poor. In his sermons On Love for the Poor he says that the poor "represent the Person of the Saviour" (as quoted by Benedict XVI). Fasting is worthless if we are unjust to others. All come from the one Father, so all are brothers and sisters, and wealth must be shared.
  • Against owning human beings. In his fourth homily on Ecclesiastes, on the verse "I bought male and female slaves" (Ecclesiastes 2:7), he asks, in substance, how anyone can put a price on the image of God, and he reproaches the owner for treating as property a human being whom God made master of the earth. Scholars debate how far he meant to call for an end to slavery in society, but few ancient writers spoke so strongly.
  • Mary. In his Christmas sermon he reads Mary's question, "How can this be, since I am a virgin?" (Luke 1:34), as the words of one who had given her virginity to God, and he praises her as virgin and mother. His Life of Gregory the Wonderworker tells how the Mother of God and St John appeared to the young Gregory and taught him the faith; this is often cited as the earliest written account of an appearance of Our Lady.
How he taught

The philosopher among the Fathers. Gregory was the deepest thinker of the three Cappadocians. He used philosophy boldly, as a tool for faith. He asked hard questions: What is the soul? How can the dead rise? How can God be one and three? Benedict XVI notes that he always sought, in his study, the light that shows what truly matters.

Scripture read in layers. He admired Origen and often read the Bible on several levels. The story is true, and it also carries a spiritual meaning about the soul's journey to God. His homilies on the Song of Songs read the bride's search for the bridegroom as the soul's search for Christ.

The dialogue. In On the Soul and the Resurrection he sits at the bedside of his dying sister and lets her answer his doubts. It is one of the first Christian dialogues modelled on Plato's, and the teacher in it is a woman.

A handbook for catechists. His Great Catechism was written for those who teach the faith. It explains God, creation, the Incarnation, Baptism and the Eucharist step by step, and Benedict XVI notes its value as a pattern for teaching.

Preaching for every occasion. He preached at funerals, on feasts, on martyrs, on the poor, and against those who put off Baptism. He spoke warmly and used images from ordinary life: painters, mirrors, ladders, climbing.

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave two General Audiences on him, on 29 August and 5 September 2007. He presents him as a man of deep reflection who sought the true light, a defender of the faith of Nicaea, a teacher of the spiritual life and a pastor who loved the poor. He sums up his teaching in this way: the full flowering of the human person is holiness, and the soul's goal is the contemplation of God. He stresses that for Gregory likeness to God is first of all God's gift, and that we become, with God's grace, "the painter of our own life".

The Catechism quotes him often: on the Incarnation (CCC 457), on Christ's death and Resurrection (CCC 625, 650), on the Holy Spirit (CCC 690), on virtue and perfection (CCC 1803, 2015, 2028), on the Beatitudes (CCC 2546, 2548) and on the Lord's Prayer (CCC 2784).

The Second Council of Nicaea (787) honoured him, according to the tradition of its acts, as "Father of Fathers".

Where he went beyond the Church's teaching. In a few places Gregory seems to hope that in the end all evil will be destroyed and every creature brought back to God, perhaps even the devil (for example Great Catechism 26 and On the Soul and the Resurrection). This idea is called the apokatastasis, the "restoration of all". Scholars disagree about what exactly he meant. The Church teaches clearly that hell exists and that its punishment is eternal (CCC 1033 to 1035), and in 543 a synod at Constantinople condemned the teaching that the punishment of the wicked and of demons will come to an end. Gregory himself was never condemned. He wrote before these questions were settled, he wanted with all his heart that God's goodness should win, and the Church honours him as a saint and a faithful teacher of the Trinity.

Why he matters today

He speaks to anyone who thinks they have reached the end of their spiritual life. For Gregory, there is no end. Each step toward God opens a wider view, and desire grows instead of being satisfied. That is good news for those who feel stuck: the road is always open.

He also speaks to those who feel that faith and thinking are enemies. Gregory asked the hardest questions and did not fear them. He shows that the mind, humbled and purified, is a path to God.

His respect for the human person, made in God's image and called to royal dignity, still challenges a world that buys and sells people, and his words on the poor are as sharp as ever.

And his love for his sister reminds us that great theologians often learn from those close to them. Gregory openly admits that Macrina was his teacher.
