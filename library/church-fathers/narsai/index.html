Mar Narsai, Harp of the Holy Spirit

TimeBorn about 399 (a modern estimate). Died about 500, in his nineties or later
WhereBorn at 'Ain Dulba in the district of Ma'alta, in the Persian Empire (today the Duhok region of Iraq). Taught at Edessa (today Şanlıurfa, Turkey) and at Nisibis (today Nusaybin, Turkey), where he died
What he wasMonk, teacher (Rabban, Malpan) and poet. Head of the School of Edessa, then founder and head of the School of Nisibis
LanguageSyriac
Known as"The Harp of the Holy Spirit". Also Narses
HonouredCalled Mar and honoured as a great teacher in the East Syriac Churches: the Assyrian Church of the East, and in communion with Rome the Chaldean and Syro-Malabar Churches. The Church of the East remembered him with the "Syrian Doctors". He is not in the Roman Martyrology as far as we have found, and he is not a Doctor of the Church
His honour, honestly Narsai is "Mar Narsai" in the East Syriac tradition: a holy teacher whose homilies were read in its schools and whose words echo in its prayer. The Church of the East commemorated a group called the "Syrian Doctors" together, among them Ephrem and Narsai (Fiey, Saints syriaques, no. 131). The Syro-Malabar calendar keeps a Friday in the season of Denha for "the Syriac Fathers" of the Church, without naming each one. We have found no fixed feast day for him in a Catholic calendar, and no papal text that names him. In the first centuries there was no canonisation process; saints and teachers were honoured by the worship of their Church. Narsai belonged to the Church of the East, which did not accept the Council of Ephesus (431). With respect, this page explains that question below, with the Common Christological Declaration of 1994. Where it speaks of his liturgy, this page uses the words of the East Syriac tradition, as the Syro-Malabar Church does: the Holy Qurbana.
His life

Childhood. Narsai was born, perhaps about 399, at 'Ain Dulba in the district of Ma'alta, in northern Mesopotamia. This was Persian territory, in the hills of today's Duhok region in Iraq. He was orphaned young. An uncle who was abbot of the monastery of Kfar Mari, west of the Tigris, near Beth Zabdai, took him in and raised him. So he grew up among monks, with the Psalms and the Scriptures.

Student and teacher at Edessa. He went west, across the frontier, to the School of Edessa in the Roman Empire, the great centre of Syriac learning. He studied there for about ten years. About 437 he returned to teach, and in time he became its head, its Rabban. His time as head probably began not before about 450 and lasted some twenty years. At Edessa he was formed by the biblical commentaries and the theology of Theodore of Mopsuestia (died 428), which had been put into Syriac and were taught in the School. Narsai revered him all his life.

Opposition and departure. Edessa was divided. Many Christians in the Roman East held to the teaching of St Cyril of Alexandria and opposed the followers of Theodore. Narsai's own opponents gave him a cruel nickname, "the Leper". His friends called him "the Harp of the Holy Spirit". After a conflict with Bishop Cyrus of Edessa (471 to 498), Narsai had to leave the city, perhaps in 471. The exact year is uncertain; scholars place it somewhere between 457 and 489.

Nisibis. He crossed back into Persia, to Nisibis, a frontier city that had passed to Persia in 363. Its bishop, Barsauma, welcomed him and persuaded him to open a school there. Narsai and Barsauma later fell out for a time; the sources blame Barsauma's wife, Mamai, and Narsai was away from Nisibis for a while. He came back and led the school until his death. In 489 the Emperor Zeno closed the School of Edessa, and many of its teachers and students came to Nisibis. The School of Nisibis became the great school of the Church of the East, with a rule of life for its students. Its Statutes, dated 496, bear Narsai's name, which shows he was still alive and active, by then in his nineties.

Death. He died about 500, or early in the sixth century, at Nisibis, very old. He was buried in Nisibis, according to tradition in a church that later bore his name. Among his pupils was Joseph Huzaya, a famous teacher of Syriac grammar.

His legacy. Later writers of the Church of the East, such as the Patriarch Timothy I, Ishoʿdad of Merv and Emmanuel bar Shahhare, read and used his homilies. The School of Nisibis that he shaped trained bishops, teachers and missionaries for the Church of the East, the Church whose missions reached Central Asia and China, and from which the St Thomas Christians of India received their bishops for many centuries.

The world he lived in

Between two empires. Narsai's life crossed the border between the Roman and the Persian empires more than once. Syriac was spoken on both sides. In Rome's provinces Christianity had become the faith of the emperors. In Persia it was a minority under Zoroastrian kings, and it had known hard persecutions in the fourth century. In 410 the Church in Persia organised itself in synod under its own Catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon.

The great quarrel about Christ. In Narsai's youth the Council of Ephesus (431) condemned Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, and confessed Mary as Theotokos, Mother of God. The Council of Chalcedon (451) then taught that Christ is one Person in two natures. Christians in the Persian Church, formed by Theodore of Mopsuestia, insisted strongly on the two natures, fully divine and fully human, kept without confusion. In Edessa, the followers of Cyril insisted on the oneness of Christ. Each side feared that the other was losing something precious. The words and their meanings were hard to agree on, in two languages, Greek and Syriac.

Schools as the heart of a Church. For Syriac Christians a school was a community of prayer and study, close to a monastery. Students lived under a rule, learned to read and chant the Scriptures, and studied the commentaries of the Fathers. Narsai's verse homilies were written to be recited there and in the churches.

His contemporary in the West. Mar Jacob of Serugh, the great West Syriac poet, also studied at Edessa. The two were the greatest verse preachers of their time, on different sides of the same quarrel. Scholars today count them as equals.

What he taught
  • God's plan is a school for humanity. In his homilies the history of salvation is a long education. God teaches the human race step by step, through creation, the Law and the prophets, until the coming of Christ, the centre of it all. Lucas Van Rompay sums up his view in this way in the Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage.
  • Christ, true God and true man. In the Incarnation, Narsai teaches, the divine and the human natures are each kept whole and real. He wishes to guard both the true divinity of Christ and his true humanity, his human soul, his growth, his suffering and death. He taught this in his homilies on the feasts of Christ: the Nativity, the Epiphany, the Passion, the Resurrection and the Ascension.
  • Creation and the human person. He preached a series of homilies on the creation (edited by Philippe Gignoux). In them, as in the school of Theodore, the human being, made in God's image, stands at the centre of the visible and invisible creation, which God made good.
  • Baptism is a new birth. In his homily on Baptism he says that Christ, "as in a furnace", recast our image in Baptism, and made us "spiritual gold" in place of our clay (Homily 22, translated by Connolly). The font is like a mother's womb; the newly baptised comes up "as a babe" and is embraced by all (Homily 21).
  • The Holy Qurbana and the Holy Spirit. A homily on the Mysteries, long counted his (Homily 17), describes the Holy Qurbana in detail. The priest calls the Holy Spirit to come down upon the bread and wine, and the Spirit makes them the Body and Blood of "King Messiah" (Connolly's words). "It is not the priest's virtue that celebrates the adorable Mysteries", it says, but the Holy Spirit. As the Body and Blood are given, the Church cries out, "Lo, the Medicine of life!" Some scholars doubt that this homily is by Narsai; a later tradition gives it to ʿAbdishoʿ of Elam. Either way it is a precious early witness to the East Syriac Qurbana.
  • Church and priesthood. One homily is on the Church and on the priesthood (Homily 32). The priest serves by the gift of the Spirit, as a minister of the Mysteries that heal and forgive.
  • The Old Testament prepares for Christ. Homilies on Old Testament figures such as Joseph, and on many biblical stories, read them as steps in God's teaching, pointing to Christ. Some of the Joseph homilies are only doubtfully his.
  • A life of study and prayer. His school rule and his homilies show a teacher for whom Scripture, worship and an upright life go together. The student learns in order to pray and to serve the Church.

His teachers, and the question of Ephesus, with respect

Narsai wrote a homily in honour of Diodore of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia and Nestorius, whom the Church of the East honours as its "Greek Doctors". Here the Catholic Church, with the Council of Ephesus (431), does not follow him. She rejected the teaching condemned there, and she confesses that Mary is truly Theotokos, the Mother of God, because the one she bore is the eternal Son of God made man (CCC 466, 495). The Second Council of Constantinople (553) also condemned the writings of Theodore. The Church of the East did not receive these councils.

Yet the Catholic Church today sees the past with new eyes. On 11 November 1994 Pope St John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, signed a Common Christological Declaration. It confesses together that Christ is true God and true man, one Person, and it says that the divisions of the past "were due in large part to misunderstandings". The Assyrian Church calls Mary "the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour", and the Catholic Church calls her "the Mother of God" and also "the Mother of Christ". The declaration says: "We both recognize the legitimacy and rightness of these expressions of the same faith." Pope Francis said in 2024 that it ended fifteen hundred years of controversy about Ephesus. So Narsai can be read as a great witness of a sister tradition to the faith in Christ, true God and true man, while the Catholic reader keeps the Church's teaching on the one Person of Christ and on Mary, Mother of God.

How he taught

The verse homily. Narsai's chief form is the memra, a homily in verse, recited before the people. Most are in lines of twelve syllables, some in lines of seven, in couplets. They are long and carefully built, like sung lectures.

Explaining the Bible plainly. He follows the school of Antioch and Theodore. He looks first at the plain, historical sense of the text and the story God was writing through real events. Then he shows how those events lead to Christ.

Drama and dialogue. He brings the scene before the eyes. In the homily on the Mysteries the listener stands in the church and sees the priest lift his hands, the deacons move, the Spirit descend. In the homilies on Baptism the listener is at the font.

Images. He loves vivid pictures: the furnace that recasts the image, the potter's clay turned to gold, the painter who paints on water, the womb of the font. These images helped simple people remember deep teaching.

Teacher of a school. Above all he was a teacher. His homilies were meant to form students who would become priests, teachers and missionaries. His school statutes show a man who cared about discipline, study and charity in common life.

His works

Verse homilies (memre)

Later writers say he wrote 360 homilies in twelve volumes, one for every day of the year. About eighty survive (some count 81). Among them: on the creation; on the feasts of the Lord (Nativity, Epiphany, Passion, Resurrection, Ascension); on Old Testament figures and stories; on the Mysteries (17, authorship doubted); on Baptism (22); on the Mysteries of the Church and on Baptism (21); on the Church and the priesthood (32); in honour of Diodore, Theodore and Nestorius; on the sanctification of the Church (33)

Other works

About ten dialogue songs (sugyotho) carry his name, but most are probably not his. Four homilies on Joseph are attributed to him, without proof. His prose works are lost. The Statutes of the School of Nisibis (496) bear his name

Editions and translations. Alphonse Mingana published 47 homilies in Syriac at Mosul in 1905, with the opening lines of 34 more. R. H. Connolly translated four liturgical homilies into English in 1909 (The Liturgical Homilies of Narsai, Cambridge). Philippe Gignoux edited the homilies on creation (1968). Frederick McLeod edited the five homilies on the feasts of the Lord (1979). A full English translation has begun: Narsai: The Homilies (from 2024). A photographic edition of a manuscript with 72 homilies appeared in 1970.

A short taste

"As in a furnace He re-cast our image in Baptism; and instead of our clay He has made us spiritual gold." Homily 22, On Baptism, translated by R. H. Connolly (1909).

"It is not the priest's virtue that celebrates the adorable Mysteries; but the Holy Spirit celebrates by His brooding." Homily 17, An Exposition of the Mysteries, translated by Connolly (1909); its authorship is doubted.

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The Popes. We have not found a papal catechesis or document that names Narsai. Benedict XVI's catecheses on the Fathers included the Syriac writers Aphrahat and Ephrem, but not Narsai.

The East Syriac Churches. For the Assyrian Church of the East, and for the Chaldean and Syro-Malabar Churches that share its tradition in communion with Rome, Narsai is one of the great teachers. His name is often given to schools and churches, such as the Mar Narsai Church in Duhok in the picture above, and the St Narsai Assyrian Christian College in Sydney.

The Syro-Malabar calendar. In the season of Denha the Syro-Malabar Church remembers on its Fridays the great witnesses of Christ: John the Baptist, Peter and Paul, the Evangelists, Stephen, and "the Greek and the Syriac Fathers of the Church". The 2025 to 2026 calendar gives readings for the Syriac Fathers. Narsai is among the Syriac Fathers in the tradition of the Church of the East.

The road to unity. The 1994 Common Christological Declaration, the meeting of Pope Francis with Mar Awa III in November 2024, and Pope Leo XIV's meeting with him in October 2025 show the Catholic Church's love for the Church of the East, Narsai's Church. A joint commission continues its dialogue, now on the constitution of the Church.

Why he matters today

Roots of our Qurbana. For Syro-Malabar Catholics, Narsai's homilies open a window onto the faith and worship of the Church from which our own liturgy grew. When we hear the priest call the Holy Spirit, we stand where his listeners stood.

Baptism as treasure. His images of the furnace and the gold help us see what we received at the font, and what we can still become.

Faith that studies. He founded a school. He reminds us that the Church needs teachers, and that study of the Scriptures is a form of prayer.

Truth and charity together. His life shows the pain of division among Christians. The Declaration of 1994 shows that patient listening can heal what centuries of suspicion wounded.
