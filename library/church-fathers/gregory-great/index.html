St Gregory the Great

TimeBorn about 540; Pope from 3 September 590; died 12 March 604
WhereRome (Italy); six years at Constantinople (today Istanbul, Turkey), about 579 to 585 or 586
What he wasPrefect of the city, monk and abbot, deacon and papal envoy, then Bishop of Rome and Pope; preacher, teacher of pastors, sender of missionaries
LanguageLatin
Known asGregory the Great (Gregorius Magnus); Gregory I; "servant of the servants of God"; "God's consul" (on his tomb); in the Byzantine tradition Gregory the Dialogist (Dialogos)
HonouredSaint, by popular acclaim from his death. One of the four great Doctors of the Latin Church, with St Ambrose, St Augustine and St Jerome, honoured together by Pope Boniface VIII in 1298. Latin Church: 3 September, the day of his consecration (before 1969 it was 12 March). Byzantine tradition: 12 March. Syro-Malabar calendar: 3 September, among the feasts of the universal Church.
His life

A Roman family of saints. Gregory was born in Rome about 540 into a rich and noble family. It was probably of the gens Anicia, and Benedict XVI notes that it gave the Church two Popes: Felix III, Gregory's great-great-grandfather, and Agapetus. His mother, Silvia, is venerated as a saint, as are two of his father's sisters, Tarsilla and Aemiliana, who lived as consecrated virgins in their own home. Benedict XVI counts his father, Gordian, among the saints too. The family house stood on the Caelian Hill, on the street called the Clivus Scauri.

A childhood of war. In his early years Rome was taken and retaken in the long war between the Goths and the armies of the Emperor Justinian. The Catholic Encyclopedia thinks these disasters left a "tinge of sadness" on his writings. Gregory of Tours says he was second to none in Rome in grammar, rhetoric and logic. He probably studied law too.

Prefect of Rome, 572 or 573. He entered public service like his father, and by 572 or 573 he was prefect of the city, its highest civil office. Benedict XVI notes that this taught him order, discipline and how to solve every kind of practical problem.

Monk, about 574. But the world did not satisfy him. About 574 he left public life, gave his estates in Sicily to found six monasteries, and turned his family home into the monastery of St Andrew. There he lived as a monk. He later remembered these years as the happiest of his life. His fasting then was so severe that it harmed his health for good.

Deacon and envoy, 579 to about 586. In 578 or 579 Pope Pelagius II made him one of the seven deacons of Rome and sent him to Constantinople as his apocrisiarius, permanent envoy to the imperial court. Benedict XVI compares the office to that of an Apostolic Nuncio. He was to ask the Emperor for help against the Lombards, who were pressing on Rome. Some monks from St Andrew's went with him, and he kept up the monastic life with them. There he met St Leander of Seville, at whose request he began his great commentary on Job. He also argued with the Patriarch Eutychius, who held that risen bodies would be impalpable, "more light than air". Gregory defended the real, touchable body of the risen Christ, and the Emperor took his side. He seems never to have learned Greek well. He also learned that Rome could no longer expect much help from Constantinople.

Abbot and secretary. Recalled to Rome in 585 or 586, he became abbot of St Andrew's and the Pope's close helper and secretary. He explained the Scriptures to his monks. To these years belongs, according to an English tradition told by St Bede, the famous meeting with fair-haired young men from Britain in the Roman Forum. When told they were Angles, he is said to have answered that they looked like angels. He set out himself to preach to them, but the people of Rome made the Pope call him back.

Floods, plague and election, 589 to 590. In 589 the Tiber flooded, destroying houses and the Church's grain stores. Plague followed. In February 590 Pope Pelagius II died of it. The clergy, the senate and the people chose Gregory at once. He tried to refuse and wrote to the Emperor Maurice asking him not to confirm the choice, but the letter was held back. While waiting, Gregory led the city in a great sevenfold procession of prayer, from the seven regions of Rome to the basilica of St Mary Major. A later legend says that St Michael was then seen on the tomb of Hadrian, sheathing his sword, and so the building is called Castel Sant'Angelo. The Emperor's confirmation came after six months. Gregory was consecrated in St Peter's on 3 September 590.

A sick man who never rested. As Pope he was often ill with stomach pain and fever, and later with gout. Benedict XVI notes that his voice was so weak that a deacon often had to read his homilies for him. Yet his biographer says he "never rested". He sent away the lay courtiers of the Lateran palace and lived with clerics and monks in a simple common life.

Bread for Rome. The Church owned large estates, chiefly in Sicily. Gregory managed them with great care, down to the weight of a measure of grain, and insisted that tenants be treated justly and cheated tenants repaid. From these revenues he fed the poor and the refugees who crowded into Rome, ransomed captives taken by the Lombards and helped poor priests, monks and nuns. Benedict XVI says the people quickly called him consul Dei, "God's consul".

Peace with the Lombards. When the Lombards threatened Rome in 592 and 593, and the imperial governor at Ravenna did nothing, Gregory acted himself. He appointed a commander for Naples and made a peace of his own. He saw the Lombards not as barbarians to be wiped out but as a people to be won for Christ. Benedict XVI notes that his talks with King Agilulf led to a truce of about three years (598 to 601) and a more lasting armistice in 603. In this he was helped by his friendship with the Catholic Queen Theodelinda, to whom he wrote warm letters and sent relics and gifts for her church at Monza.

The mission to England, 596 to 601. In 596 he sent Augustine, prior of St Andrew's, with about forty monks to the Anglo-Saxons. They landed in Kent in 597, and King Ethelbert was soon baptised. In 601 Gregory sent more helpers and the pallium to Augustine, now archbishop. In a letter preserved by Bede he advised them not to destroy the temples of the people but only the idols, and to turn the buildings into churches, so that the people might come more readily to the places they knew. Benedict XVI recalls that Gregory saw the conversion of the Angles as a sign of the Kingdom growing.

The universal Church. He wrote constantly to bishops in Italy, Gaul, Spain, Africa and the East, defending their rights and correcting abuses. When the Patriarch of Constantinople, John the Faster, took the title "Ecumenical Patriarch" at a synod in 588, Gregory protested. Benedict XVI explains that this was not to deny the patriarch's lawful authority but out of concern for the unity of the Church and for humility, and that Gregory himself chose the title "servant of the servants of God".

Last years and death. His last years were full of pain. He wrote that his only comfort was the hope of death. He died on 12 March 604 and was buried the same day in the porch of St Peter's. His epitaph, recorded by Bede, calls him "God's consul". He was honoured as a saint at once. Since 1606 his relics have rested in a chapel of St Peter's Basilica.

The world he lived in

The old Roman world in the West was gone. Rome had shrunk from the vast capital of its greatness to a much smaller, war-worn city. The Emperor lived far away in Constantinople and ruled Italy through a governor at Ravenna. In 568 the Lombards, a Germanic people, many of them following the teaching of Arius, had invaded Italy and taken much of it.

Floods, famine and plague came again and again. Many people, Gregory among them, thought that the end of the world was near. He often reminds his hearers to be ready. The Church, following Christ's own words, teaches that no one knows the day or the hour (Matthew 24:36). Gregory's urgency is a call to watchfulness and good works, not a date.

Beyond Italy the map of Europe was being redrawn. The Visigoths in Spain, led by King Reccared and guided by St Leander of Seville, had become Catholic in 589; Leander's younger brother, St Isidore, would later carry on his work. The Franks were Catholic, but their Church needed reform. Britain had been largely lost to the faith with the coming of the Anglo-Saxons. Gregory looked at all these peoples with a pastor's eyes.

Monastic life was spreading in the West. St Benedict had died only a few decades before Gregory became a monk, and Gregory would become his first biographer.

What he taught
  • Scripture grows with the reader. Scripture is food for life, not only for study. The Catechism takes up his saying that the Scriptures "grow with the one who reads them" (Homilies on Ezekiel 1, 7, 8; CCC 94). Benedict XVI notes his teaching that humility of mind is "the primary rule" for anyone who seeks to understand the things of God in the Bible, and that understanding God's Word means nothing "if it does not lead to action".
  • The care of souls is the art of arts. A pastor must first be a physician of souls, live what he preaches, and speak to each person according to his need: the poor and the rich, the joyful and the sad, the timid and the bold (Pastoral Rule, part 3). "The government of souls is the art of arts" (Pastoral Rule 1, 1, tr. J. Barmby).
  • Justice to the poor. "When we attend to the needs of those in want, we give them what is theirs, not ours. More than performing works of mercy, we are paying a debt of justice" (Pastoral Rule 3, 21, quoted in CCC 2446).
  • Holiness is possible in hard times. In the Dialogues he answers a friend who thought there were no more saints. Benedict XVI sums up: "holiness is always possible, even in difficult times".
  • The capital sins. He gave the West its classic list of the chief sins, with pride as their root. The Catechism says the capital sins were distinguished "following St. John Cassian and St. Gregory the Great" (CCC 1866), and quotes him on envy: "From envy are born hatred, detraction, calumny, joy caused by the misfortune of a neighbor, and displeasure caused by his prosperity" (Moralia 31, 45; CCC 2539).
  • The purifying fire. Some lesser faults can be forgiven after death. "As for certain lesser faults, we must believe that, before the Final Judgment, there is a purifying fire" (Dialogues 4, 39, quoted in CCC 1031). He also tells of the good done by offering the Mass for the dead, the source of the custom of thirty Masses called "Gregorian Masses".
  • Christ and his Church are one. "Our redeemer has shown himself to be one person with the holy Church whom he has taken to himself" (Moralia, preface; CCC 795).
  • Action and contemplation. Benedict XVI notes that in the Moralia Gregory always seeks to join "word and action, thought and deed, prayer and dedication to the duties of one's state". He himself longed for the quiet of the monastery but gave himself to the service of all.
  • The four Councils. "I confess that I receive and revere, as the four books of the Gospel so also the four Councils", that is, Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus and Chalcedon (Register of Letters 1, 25, tr. J. Barmby).
  • Humility in authority. He held firmly that the Apostolic See had a primacy over all the Churches, and he exercised it. But he wanted it lived as service. "Servant of the servants of God" was, Benedict XVI says, "a true manifestation of his way of living and acting".
How he taught

By simple preaching. He preached to the people in the churches where the Pope celebrated the "stations" of the Roman year. His homilies are plain explanations of the Gospel of the day, with stories from everyday life. They paved the way for the popular preachers of the Middle Ages.

By three senses of Scripture. Following the Fathers, he read the Bible in its literal, allegorical and moral senses, giving most weight to the moral: how shall we live? Benedict XVI notes that his Moralia became "a kind of summa of Christian morality" for the Middle Ages.

By letters. About 850 letters survive in his Register. They deal with everything from doctrine to the price of grain and show a mind both holy and practical.

By stories of saints. In the Dialogues he gathered stories of holy men and women of Italy, many of them recent. Book 2 is the only ancient life of St Benedict.

By the example of a monk-pope. He lived in community, wore simple clothes and kept the poor at his table. His teaching on pastors was first a mirror for himself.

Gentleness and force. His usual method with those outside the Church was persuasion. In a time when forced conversion was sometimes attempted, he forbade the forced baptism of Jews and insisted on their rights under the law. In a letter of 598 he set the rule later known from its first words, Sicut Iudaeis: Jews must not be harmed in the rights the law grants them. Yet in the manner of his age he also called on the civil power against heresy and paganism. The Church today teaches that no one may be forced to believe (Dignitatis Humanae 2).

His works

Scripture

Moralia in Job (Morals on Job), thirty-five books, begun at Constantinople and finished in Rome, about 595.
Homilies on Ezekiel, twenty-two homilies in two books, preached in 593 during the Lombard siege.
Forty Homilies on the Gospels, preached from Advent 590 to 593.
Exposition of the Song of Songs, on the first verses, from notes of his teaching; now widely accepted as his.
A long Commentary on First Kings under his name is disputed; many scholars now give it to a medieval writer.

Pastoral

Pastoral Rule (Regula Pastoralis), four parts, written at the start of his pontificate, 590 or 591. It was soon translated into Greek and, under King Alfred, into Old English.

Lives of saints

Dialogues, four books, about 593 to 594. Book 2 is the life of St Benedict; book 4 treats of the soul after death. A few modern scholars have doubted that the work is his, but most hold it genuine.

Letters

The Register, about 850 letters in fourteen books, from 590 to 604. They include the letters to St Augustine of Canterbury and Mellitus, to Queen Theodelinda, to St Leander, to the Emperors Maurice and Phocas, and to the Patriarchs of the East.

Liturgy and chant

He made some changes in the Roman Mass: he placed the Our Father after the Eucharistic Prayer, before the breaking of the bread, and added words to the Roman Canon asking for peace in our days. The Gregorian Sacramentary grew from Roman books of his time but was much revised later. Gregorian chant bears his name from a later tradition; how much he himself did for it is debated. In the Byzantine tradition the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, used on weekdays of Lent, is attributed to him.

A short taste

"The government of souls is the art of arts." (Pastoral Rule 1, 1, tr. J. Barmby)

"The preacher must dip his pen into the blood of his heart; then he can also reach the ear of his neighbour." (Homilies on Ezekiel, as given by Benedict XVI)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave two General Audiences on him, on 28 May and 4 June 2008. He calls him "one of the greatest Fathers in the history of the Church". He praises his care for the poor, his peacemaking with the Lombards, and the mission to England. He says that "during a desperate period of havoc, he was able to create peace and give hope". He ends with his humility: "Precisely because he was this, he is great and also shows us the measure of true greatness".

Boniface VIII in 1298 honoured him with Ambrose, Augustine and Jerome as the great Doctors of the Latin Church.

The Catechism quotes him on Scripture (CCC 94), on Christ and the Church (CCC 795), on purgatory (CCC 1031), on the capital sins (CCC 1866), on justice to the poor (CCC 2446) and on envy (CCC 2539). It also cites him on Christ's human knowledge (CCC 473).

The liturgy. His feast is kept on 3 September in the Latin Church and on 12 March in the Byzantine Churches, which call him the Dialogist after his Dialogues. England honours him as its apostle. The custom of Gregorian Masses, thirty Masses offered on thirty days for one who has died, comes from a story in his Dialogues.

A reading not followed today. In a homily on the Gospels (Homily 33) Gregory said that the sinful woman of Luke 7, Mary of Bethany and Mary Magdalene were one person. This reading shaped Western art and preaching for centuries. The Eastern Churches did not follow it, and the Roman calendar now honours Mary Magdalene, apostle of the Resurrection, on 22 July as a feast (since 2016), and Mary of Bethany with Martha and Lazarus on 29 July (since 2021).

Honest notes. Historians debate his letters to the Emperor Phocas, who seized the throne in 602 by murdering the Emperor Maurice and his sons. Gregory welcomed the new ruler warmly, perhaps hoping for relief for Italy. It is not his finest hour, and Catholic writers have long admitted it.

Why he matters today

He shows what a pastor is. He did not seek office, he dreaded it, and when it came he gave himself to it entirely, sick and tired as he was. His Pastoral Rule still speaks to priests, bishops, teachers, parents and anyone who must guide others.

He joined prayer and practical work. The same man wrote on the soul's longing for God and on how much grain a ship should carry to Rome. Holiness, he shows, is not an escape from responsibility.

He looked at newcomers and enemies as people to be won, not crushed. The Lombards and the Angles became Christian peoples because a Pope saw in them future brothers.

And he gave the Church a title for every one of its servants: "servant of the servants of God". It is still a measure of true greatness.
