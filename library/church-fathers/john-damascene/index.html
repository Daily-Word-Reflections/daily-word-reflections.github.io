St John of Damascus

TimeBorn about 675 (estimates run from about 650 to 676); died, by tradition, on 4 December 749, certainly before 754
WhereDamascus (Syria), then the monastery of Mar Saba in the Judean desert, near Jerusalem and Bethlehem (today in the West Bank, Palestine)
What he wasOfficial in the financial service of Damascus, then monk and priest; theologian, preacher, poet and hymn writer, defender of holy images
LanguageGreek; he also knew Arabic, the language of his city's rulers
Known asJohn Damascene; in Arabic Yuhanna ibn Mansur ibn Sarjun; "Chrysorrhoas", the golden stream, for his eloquence; often called the last of the Greek Fathers
HonouredSaint, honoured from the late 8th century. Doctor of the Church, declared by Pope Leo XIII in 1890. Byzantine tradition: 4 December. Syro-Malabar calendar: 4 December. Latin Church: 4 December, optional memorial (from 1890 to 1969 it was 27 March). Syro-Malankara calendar: 4 December.
His life

Sources. Only one full Life of John survives, written in Greek in the 10th century. The Catholic Encyclopedia judges it historically very unsatisfactory, short of detail and inclined to legend. A few earlier notices, his own writings and Arab historians fill the gaps. Many dates are therefore uncertain.

A Christian family of Damascus. John was born in Damascus, probably about 675. His family was Christian, Greek-speaking and Arabic-named, and one of the leading families of the city. His grandfather, Mansur ibn Sarjun, had been a senior financial official under the Byzantine Empire and, according to some sources, took part in the surrender of Damascus to the Arab armies in 635. His father, Sarjun ibn Mansur, served the new rulers. Benedict XVI says John took up early in life the post of treasurer of the caliphate, "perhaps already held by his father". Scholars discuss how high these offices were; John never mentions them in his own writings.

Christians under the Umayyads. From 661 Damascus was the capital of the Umayyad caliphs, who ruled from Spain to Central Asia. Most of their subjects in Syria were still Christians, and for decades the government kept Greek-speaking Christian officials and Greek records. Christians kept their churches and worship, paid a special tax, and lived under the protection and the limits of the law of the time. John grew up in this meeting place of Greek, Syriac and Arab cultures, as Benedict XVI notes.

His teacher Cosmas. According to the Life, his father ransomed a learned monk from Sicily named Cosmas, who had been taken captive, and made him tutor to John and to another boy he had adopted, also named Cosmas. John is said to have studied grammar, logic, mathematics, music, astronomy and theology. The younger Cosmas became his lifelong companion, a great hymn writer, and later Bishop of Maiuma near Gaza.

Leaving the court. At about the end of the 7th century, the caliphs began to change the government's language from Greek to Arabic, and the place of Christians in the service of the state grew harder. About 700, or a few years later, John gave up his position. Benedict XVI says he was "dissatisfied with life at court". With Cosmas he entered the monastery of St Sabas, Mar Saba, in the desert gorge of the Kidron, some miles from Jerusalem. He never left it, says the Pope, for the rest of his life.

The legend of the hand. The Life tells that the Byzantine Emperor, to ruin John, forged a letter in his hand offering to betray Damascus, and sent it to the caliph. The caliph had John's right hand cut off. John prayed all night before an icon of the Mother of God, and in the morning his hand was restored. This is a tradition, and the Catholic Encyclopedia reports it as the biographer's story without endorsing it. It is behind the icon called the "Three-handed" Mother of God, linked with John in Eastern tradition.

Monk and priest. At Mar Saba John lived as a monk under obedience, in prayer and study. Patriarch John V of Jerusalem (705 to 735) ordained him a priest and made him a preacher in the holy places. Many of his homilies were given in Jerusalem, at Gethsemane, on Mount Tabor and at the shrines. His cell and later his tomb were shown at the monastery.

The icon crisis, from 726. In 726, by the usual dating, the Byzantine Emperor Leo III began to act against the veneration of icons, and in 730 he forbade it by law. Patriarch Germanus of Constantinople resigned rather than agree. Many monks and laypeople suffered. John, living outside the Empire under the caliphs, could write without fear of the Emperor. He wrote three discourses in defence of the holy images, probably over several years from about 726 to 730, though some scholars date them later. They became the classic defence of icons.

The great summary. Late in life he gathered his teaching into The Fount of Knowledge, dedicated to Cosmas, by then Bishop of Maiuma: a guide to philosophy, a book on heresies, and an exact exposition of the faith. It was the first summary of the whole Christian faith of its kind in the East.

Death. The tradition says he died on 4 December 749, at Mar Saba. The date is not certain, but he was dead before 754, since the council of that year speaks of him as dead. He was buried at the monastery. His relics were later taken to Constantinople, according to the New Catholic Encyclopedia.

Condemned, then vindicated. In 754, at Hieria near Constantinople, the Emperor Constantine V held a council of bishops of the Empire that condemned the veneration of icons. It cursed John by his family name, "Mansur", with insulting additions. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea, the seventh ecumenical council, restored the veneration of icons, honoured John among the defenders of the faith. Benedict XVI says his discourses on images were the grounds for "his rehabilitation and canonization".

The world he lived in

A new empire. In the 630s Arab armies took Syria, Palestine and Egypt from the Byzantine Empire. Damascus fell in 635 and Jerusalem in 637 or 638. The Umayyad dynasty made Damascus its capital from 661 until 750. Under the Caliph Abd al-Malik (685 to 705) the Dome of the Rock was built in Jerusalem, and Arabic became the language of government. John's whole life was lived under Muslim rule.

Christians in that world. The Christians of Syria and Palestine were many and divided among themselves: Chalcedonians like John, the Syriac Orthodox (whom he called Jacobites), the Church of the East, and others. They lived under a Muslim government that allowed their worship. Benedict XVI describes John as a man at the meeting point of Christian Greek and Syriac culture and the spreading culture of Islam.

John and Islam. John was one of the first Christian writers to describe Islam. In the chapter on the "Ishmaelites" at the end of his book On Heresies, he treats it, as he saw it, as a new teaching that had arisen near the Christian faith, and he argues against it from the Christian faith in Christ as Son of God. The authorship of that chapter is debated by scholars. He wrote as a theologian of his own time, in a sharp style common in that age's writings on every faith, including other Christians. The Catholic Church today, with the Second Vatican Council, "regards with esteem the Moslems", who adore the one God, honour Jesus as a prophet and honour his Virgin Mother Mary (Nostra Aetate 3). Christians and Muslims lived side by side in John's Damascus, as they do there today.

Icons in the Byzantine Empire. Outside the caliphate, in the Christian Empire, a different storm broke. Some emperors and bishops came to think that images of Christ and the saints were idols, against the commandment of Exodus 20:4. Icons were removed or destroyed, and those who honoured them were punished. The movement, called iconoclasm, which means "breaking of images", lasted, with a pause, from 726 to 843, when the Feast of the Triumph of Orthodoxy marked its end.

Mar Saba. The Great Lavra of St Sabas, founded in 483, clings to the cliffs of the Kidron valley. It was a centre of learning and of liturgy, and its daily office shaped the worship of the Byzantine Churches. Monks still live there.

What he taught
  • Why Christians may make images. God the Father, who has no body, could not be pictured. But the Son of God became man: "the Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14). He who is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15) could be seen, touched and so also painted. To refuse images of Christ is, in the end, to doubt that he truly took flesh (Discourses on the Images 1, 2 and 3). The Catechism teaches the same (CCC 1159 to 1162, 2129 to 2132).
  • Worship and veneration are different. John distinguished latreia, adoration or worship, which is given to God alone, from proskynesis, honour or veneration, which may be given to holy persons and things for God's sake. As Benedict XVI puts it, the first "can only be offered to God". We do not adore the wood and paint of an icon. Following St Basil, he teaches that the honour given to the image passes to the one it shows (CCC 2132).
  • Matter is good. "I do not venerate matter, but the Creator of matter, who became matter for my sake and deigned to live in matter", he wrote, as quoted by Benedict XVI. Through matter came our salvation: the wood of the Cross, the tomb, the Gospel book, the Body and Blood of the Lord. "Do not, therefore, offend matter: it is not contemptible, because nothing that God has made is contemptible" (Discourses on the Images, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Images teach and lift up the heart. Icons are books for those who cannot read and a meadow for the eyes. The Catechism quotes him: "The beauty of the images moves me to contemplation, as a meadow delights the eyes and subtly infuses the soul with the glory of God" (CCC 1162, citing Discourses on the Images 1, 27).
  • The saints and their relics. The saints are friends of God, filled with his grace, and share in Christ's Resurrection. So their relics and images may be honoured. The Pope notes this line of his thought.
  • The whole faith in order. In The Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith he sets out the faith of the Fathers on God, the Trinity, creation, angels, man, providence, Christ, Mary, the sacraments and the last things. He was not trying to be original; he wanted to pass on what the Church had received. The Catechism cites this book several times: on the fall of the angels (CCC 393), on the soul and body of Christ in death (CCC 626), on Christ seated at the right hand (CCC 663), and on the Holy Spirit in the Eucharist (CCC 1106).
  • The Eucharist. He teaches that the bread and wine become truly the Body and Blood of Christ by the action of the Holy Spirit, as Christ took flesh from the Virgin by the Spirit (Exposition 4, 13; cited in CCC 1106).
  • Prayer. His short definition of prayer is the one the Catechism uses: "Prayer is the raising of one's mind and heart to God or the requesting of good things from God" (CCC 2559 and 2590, citing Exposition 3, 24).
  • Mary, Mother of God. He defends the title Mother of God (Theotokos) against those who would call her only Mother of Christ (Exposition 3, 12). In three homilies for 15 August, preached at Gethsemane where tradition placed the tomb of the Mother of God, he speaks of her falling asleep and of her body taken up into heaven. Pope Pius XII quoted him in defining the Assumption in 1950 (Munificentissimus Deus 21 to 22).
  • God's love and wonder. Benedict XVI highlights his words on creation as the work of God's goodness, and on God's "great sea of love" for man. Faith, like philosophy, begins in wonder; the Incarnation is "the only thing really new under the sun".

The Church has not judged any of his teaching to be in error. Nicaea II praised him; Leo XIII made him a Doctor.

How he taught

He gathered. John saw himself as a collector of the Fathers, not an inventor. He said, in substance, that he would say nothing of his own, but would gather what the best teachers had said. His books draw on St Athanasius, St Basil, the two Gregories, St John Chrysostom, St Cyril of Alexandria, Dionysius the Areopagite, St Maximus the Confessor and others, and set them in order.

He used philosophy. He began his great work with a guide to the terms of Aristotle and Porphyry, so that the reader could think clearly about nature, person, substance and will. For this he is often seen as a forerunner of the Scholastic method. Peter Lombard and St Thomas Aquinas used the Latin translation of his Exposition, made by Burgundio of Pisa about 1150.

He wrote for ordinary people. His discourses on images were meant to be read aloud and passed from hand to hand. He quotes the Fathers at length so that readers could see that the faith was old, not new.

He sang. He was, above all for the East, a poet. His canons, long hymns of nine odes, are still sung in the Byzantine Churches. The Easter Canon, which opens with "the day of Resurrection", is sung at the night of Pascha and through Easter week. Tradition also links him with the Oktoechos, the book of hymns in eight tones, and with hymns of the funeral service, though scholars debate how much is his.

He preached on feasts. His homilies on the Transfiguration, on Holy Saturday, on the birth and the falling asleep of the Mother of God, show a preacher steeped in Scripture and the liturgy.

His works

The Fount of Knowledge

Philosophical Chapters (Dialectica), on the terms of logic and philosophy. Authentic.
On Heresies, a list of a hundred or so heresies, mostly from St Epiphanius, with new chapters at the end, including those on Islam and on iconoclasm, whose authorship is debated.
The Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (De fide orthodoxa), a hundred chapters, later split into four books in the Latin West. His most important work. Authentic.

On images

Three Discourses against Those who Attack the Holy Images, about 726 to 730, by the usual dating. Authentic.

Against errors

Works against the Nestorians, against the Jacobites (written for Peter, Metropolitan of Damascus), on the two wills in Christ against the monothelites, against the Manichees, and a dialogue with a Muslim attributed to him, whose authorship is uncertain.
Elementary Introduction (Institutio elementaris) and short professions of faith.

Spiritual

On Fasting, On the Eight Spirits of Evil, On Virtues and Vices, and prayers.
Sacred Parallels (Sacra Parallela), a large collection of sayings from Scripture and the Fathers on virtues and vices. He may have compiled it, or helped.

Scripture

Commentary on the Letters of St Paul, mostly drawn from St John Chrysostom and St Cyril of Alexandria.

Homilies

About thirteen genuine homilies, including On the Transfiguration, On Holy Saturday, On the Withered Fig Tree, On the Nativity of the Mother of God, and three Homilies on the Falling Asleep (Dormition) of the Mother of God. A homily on the Annunciation survives only in a Latin version of an Arabic text. Some praises of saints are lost or doubtful.

Hymns

Canons for Easter, Christmas, the Theophany, the Ascension, Pentecost, the Transfiguration, the Dormition and other feasts; hymns in the Oktoechos, by tradition; funeral hymns, by tradition.

Disputed

Barlaam and Josaphat, a Christian retelling of the story of the Buddha: once thought to be his; many scholars give it to St Euthymius of Athos, others still to John.
On Those Who Have Died in the Faith: thought spurious by Bellarmine and others.

A short taste

"I do not venerate matter, but the Creator of matter, who became matter for my sake and deigned to live in matter." (Discourses on the Images, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 6 May 2009)

"Prayer is the raising of one's mind and heart to God or the requesting of good things from God." (Exposition 3, 24, as quoted in CCC 2559)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 defined that holy images may be set up and venerated, and that the honour paid to an image passes to the one it shows. It gave the faith John had defended the authority of an ecumenical council (CCC 1160 to 1161, 2131).

Leo XIII declared him a Doctor of the Universal Church in 1890.

Pius XII, defining the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary in 1950, quoted his homily and called him an outstanding herald of this tradition (Munificentissimus Deus 21).

St John Paul II, for the twelfth centenary of Nicaea II, wrote the Apostolic Letter Duodecimum saeculum on 4 December 1987, his feast day, on the veneration of icons.

Benedict XVI gave a General Audience on him on 6 May 2009. He calls him "a great Doctor in the history of the Universal Church", explains his distinction between worship and veneration, and dwells on his teaching on matter and the Incarnation. God, says the Pope, drawing on him, "desires to repose in us".

The Catechism cites him in eight paragraphs (CCC 393, 626, 663, 1106, 1159, 1162, 2559, 2590), and gives the teaching of Nicaea II on icons (CCC 1160 to 1161, 2131).

Why he matters today

Our churches and homes. Every icon in an Eastern church, every statue and picture in a Latin one, the holy pictures in a Kerala home, all rest on the truth he defended: God became visible in Jesus Christ, and so we may show him.

The body and the world are good. He says that nothing God has made is contemptible. In a time that can despise the body or worship it, he teaches us to honour it as the place where God chose to dwell.

Faith that thinks. He shows that faith needs clear thought and good order, and he gave the Church its first full summary of doctrine. He shows also that a theologian can be a poet and a man of prayer.

Living among other faiths. He was a Christian in a Muslim city, an official of a Muslim government, and later a monk under its rule. He held firmly to his faith and argued for it, but he lived and worked with his neighbours. Christians in many lands, including India, live with neighbours of other faiths; the Church today asks us to do so with esteem and in truth.

Easter joy. Each Easter night the Byzantine Churches still sing his canon. Thirteen centuries later, his voice still leads the Church's joy.

Sit with this

  1. When I look at an icon, a crucifix or a holy picture, do I let it lead me to the one it shows?
  2. Do I treat my body, and the world God made, as good and worthy of respect?
  3. How do I speak about my faith with neighbours who believe differently: with truth, and with esteem?
