St Augustine of Hippo

TimeBorn 13 November 354; baptized at Easter 387; bishop from 395 or 396; died 28 August 430
WhereBorn at Thagaste (today Souk Ahras, Algeria); studied at Madauros (Algeria) and Carthage (Tunisia); taught at Rome and Milan (Italy); bishop of Hippo Regius (today Annaba, Algeria)
What he wasTeacher of rhetoric, convert, monk, priest, bishop, preacher, theologian, philosopher and writer
LanguageLatin; he read Greek only with difficulty and knew a little Punic
Known asDoctor of Grace; the greatest Father of the Latin Church, in Benedict XVI's words; author of the Confessions and The City of God
HonouredSaint. One of the four great Doctors of the Latin Church, declared by Pope Boniface VIII in 1298. Latin Church: 28 August (memorial), the day after his mother St Monica (27 August). Byzantine tradition: 15 June, as "Blessed Augustine". The Syro-Malabar lectionary has proper readings for St Augustine on 28 August. Patron of the Augustinian family of religious and of theologians.
His life

Thagaste. Augustine was born on 13 November 354 at Thagaste, a small town in the Roman province of Numidia, in North Africa. His father Patricius, a town councillor of modest means, was a pagan who became a catechumen and was baptized before he died, about 371. His mother Monica was a fervent Christian, now honoured as a saint. As a baby he was signed with the cross and received the salt of the catechumens. He had a brother, Navigius, and a sister whose name is unknown, who was widowed and later led a community of women.

The name of Christ. He later wrote that he had drunk in the name of Christ "with his mother's milk" and that no book could fully win him if that name was missing (Confessions 3, 4, 8, in substance). Once, as a boy, he fell very ill and asked for baptism; when he recovered it was put off, as was common then.

The pears. He studied at Thagaste and at Madauros. At sixteen he spent an idle year at home while his father saved for his studies. With friends he stole pears from a neighbour's tree, not because he wanted them, but for the thrill of doing wrong together. Years later he spent a whole book of the Confessions (book 2) on this small theft, to understand how the heart can love evil for its own sake.

Carthage. From 370 or 371 he studied rhetoric at Carthage, capital of Roman Africa. He lived a wild student life, then settled with a woman whose name he never gives, to whom he was faithful for about fifteen years. In 372 she bore him a son, Adeodatus, "given by God". He mastered Latin but never learned Greek well, a lack he regretted.

Cicero's call. At nineteen, in 373, he read Cicero's Hortensius, a book now lost, which urged the love of wisdom. "This book changed my feelings", he wrote; he longed "for the immortality of wisdom" (Confessions 3, 4, 7, as quoted by Benedict XVI). He turned to the Bible, but its plain style and its stories of wars disappointed him.

Nine years a Manichee. He then joined the Manichees, a movement that called itself Christian and promised a purely rational religion. They taught that the world comes from two powers, one good and one evil, and that evil in us is not really our own doing. Augustine was only a "hearer", never one of the "elect". He stayed about nine years. His mother wept and prayed. A bishop she begged to talk to him told her: "It is not possible that the son of these tears should perish" (Confessions 3, 12, 21, in substance).

Teacher. He taught grammar at Thagaste and then rhetoric at Carthage, with great success. A close friend died, and Augustine was crushed with grief (Confessions 4, 4, 9). Slowly he lost faith in the Manichees. When their famous bishop Faustus came to Carthage, about 383, he found a pleasant speaker who could not answer his questions.

Rome and Milan. In 383, aged twenty-nine, he sailed for Rome, leaving his mother on the shore at night by a trick. He fell ill there, then taught, but his students cheated him of their fees. In 384, with the help of the prefect Symmachus, a pagan, he won the post of official professor of rhetoric at Milan, the imperial city.

Ambrose. At Milan he went to hear Bishop Ambrose, at first to study his style. Ambrose received him "as a father". Through Ambrose's preaching he learned to read the Old Testament as a journey toward Christ, and his old objections fell away. He saw Ambrose and his people resist the court in 386 by prayer and song. He read books of the Platonists, which showed him that God is spirit and that evil is not a substance but a lack of good. Yet they could not show him the way to God. He found that way in St Paul.

The struggle. Monica had followed him to Milan. She arranged a marriage for him, and the mother of Adeodatus was sent back to Africa, vowing never to know another man. Augustine was torn apart by this (Confessions 6, 15, 25). He could not break with his habits. The old priest Simplician told him how Victorinus, a famous Roman teacher, had publicly confessed Christ. A visitor, Ponticianus, told him of St Antony of Egypt and of two officials at Trier who had left everything for God. Augustine felt ashamed. He prayed: "Grant me chastity and continence, but not yet" (Confessions 8, 7, 17, in substance).

"Take and read", 386. In the late summer of 386 (Benedict XVI gives 15 August; others say September), weeping in a garden in Milan, he heard a child's voice from a nearby house chanting "tolle, lege", "take and read". He opened the letters of Paul and read: "let us live honorably as in the day, not in reveling and drunkenness, not in debauchery and licentiousness, not in quarreling and jealousy. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires" (Romans 13:13 to 14). "Instantly", he wrote, the light of peace flooded his heart (Confessions 8, 12, 29). He ran to tell his mother, and she rejoiced.

Cassiciacum and baptism. He resigned his post and went with Monica, Adeodatus and friends to a country house at Cassiciacum, north of Milan, near Lake Como. There they prayed the Psalms, read Scripture and held the philosophical dialogues that became his first books. In Lent 387 he enrolled for baptism, and at the Easter Vigil of 24 to 25 April 387 Ambrose baptized him, with Adeodatus and his friend Alypius.

Ostia, 387. On the way home to Africa they waited at Ostia, the port of Rome. Leaning at a window looking onto a garden, mother and son spoke of the life of the saints, and together they were lifted for a moment toward God (Confessions 9, 10, 23 to 25). A few days later Monica fell ill. She told her sons not to worry about where she was buried: only "remember me at the altar of the Lord" (9, 11, 27, in substance). She died aged fifty-six. Augustine was thirty-three.

Thagaste and Hippo. After some months in Rome he returned to Africa in 388. He sold his inheritance and lived with friends in community at Thagaste, in prayer and study. Adeodatus died there, still a young man. In 391 Augustine visited Hippo Regius, a port city, to meet a man thinking of the monastic life. While he was in church, the people seized him and asked Bishop Valerius to ordain him a priest. He wept, but accepted. Valerius gave him a garden near the church for a monastery and, against African custom, asked him to preach.

Bishop. In 395 or 396 Valerius had him ordained as his co-bishop, and Augustine soon succeeded him. For about thirty-five years he led the Church of Hippo. He lived in common with his clergy in the bishop's house, in poverty. He preached several times a week, often on several days running. He judged disputes between Christians every morning, a heavy duty of bishops then. He cared for the poor, the orphans and the widows, and ransomed captives. He went to councils at Carthage almost every year. Ten of his community became bishops.

The Donatists. The great problem of African Christianity was the Donatist schism. Since 312 the Donatists held that sacraments given by unworthy clergy were not valid, and that they alone were the pure Church. In many towns they outnumbered Catholics. Bands called Circumcellions attacked Catholic clergy; Augustine himself escaped an ambush. For years he sought dialogue and wrote even a popular song against the schism. In 411, at the Conference of Carthage, 286 Catholic and 279 Donatist bishops met, and the imperial commissioner Marcellinus ruled for the Catholics. Augustine at first rejected force in matters of faith. After the violence and the many returns to unity under imperial laws, he came to accept that the state could press schismatics, while always pleading that no one be put to death (Letter 100).

The Pelagians. From 411 the British monk Pelagius and his follower Celestius taught that human beings can be good by their own will, that Adam's sin harmed only Adam, and that grace only helps what nature can do. Augustine saw that this emptied the Cross. In book after book he taught that we need God's grace to begin, to continue and to finish any good work. African councils condemned Pelagianism, and Pope Innocent I confirmed them early in 417. Later that year Augustine told his people, in substance, that the case was closed (Sermon 131, 10). Pope Zosimus confirmed the condemnation in 418. He went on debating for years with Julian of Eclanum.

The fall of Rome, 410. When the Goths sacked Rome, pagans said the city fell because it had left the old gods. Refugees came to Africa. Augustine comforted them: the world grows old, but Christ is always young (Sermon 81, 8, in substance). And from 413 to 426 he wrote The City of God in answer.

Last years. On 26 September 426 he presented the priest Heraclius to his people as his successor, and they acclaimed him. Augustine spent his remaining years revising his works in the Retractations and finishing others. In 429 the Vandals crossed from Spain into Africa. In May or June 430 they besieged Hippo. Augustine fell ill with fever in the third month. He asked for the penitential psalms to be written out in large letters on the wall, and he read them weeping. He died on 28 August 430, aged seventy-five. He left no will, says his friend Possidius, because as a poor man of God he had nothing to leave, but he asked that the church library be carefully kept.

His body. At an unknown date his body was taken to Sardinia, and about 725, according to tradition by the Lombard king Liutprand, to Pavia in northern Italy. It rests in the Basilica of San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro. Benedict XVI prayed there on 22 April 2007.

The world he lived in

Roman Africa, the coast of today's Tunisia and Algeria, was rich in grain and olive oil, full of towns, and deeply Christian. It was the land of Tertullian and St Cyprian. It had hundreds of bishops, some of small villages. Latin was spoken in the towns and Punic in the countryside.

Augustine lived through the last years of the Western Empire. He was a child when the Emperor Julian tried to bring back paganism, and a bishop when Rome fell to the Goths. He died as the Vandals, Arian Christians, were taking Africa. A year after his death Hippo fell.

Christians were divided. The Donatists were a rival Church in the same towns. The Manichees were active in secret. Pelagian ideas spread among serious, ascetic Christians. And pagans of culture still said that Christianity was for the simple. Augustine had to speak to all of them.

He also stood between two worlds of thought: the philosophy of the Greeks, which he knew through Latin writers and through Plotinus and Porphyry in translation, and the Bible. He brought them together, always putting faith first.

What he taught
  • Made for God. "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you" (Confessions 1, 1, 1, as quoted by Benedict XVI; also CCC 30). The deepest human longing is for God.
  • God within. "Do not go outside; return into yourself; truth dwells in the inner man" (On True Religion 39, 72, as quoted by Benedict XVI). God is "more inward than my most inward part and higher than the highest element within me" (Confessions 3, 6, 11).
  • Faith and reason. "I believe in order to understand; and I understand, the better to believe" (Sermon 43, 7, 9, quoted in CCC 158). Faith and reason are "the two forces that lead us to knowledge" (Against the Academics 3, 20, 43).
  • The Trinity. One God, three Persons, equal and eternal. He looked for traces of the Trinity in the human mind: memory, understanding and will; or the lover, the beloved and love (On the Trinity). He taught that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from one source, a teaching the Latin Church has kept (compare CCC 246 to 248).
  • Evil is a lack. Against the Manichees: God made all things good. Evil is not a thing but the absence of a good that should be there. Moral evil comes from the misuse of created will.
  • Grace. Human beings, wounded by original sin, cannot save themselves. God's grace goes before us, heals our will and makes us able to love. Yet grace does not destroy our will; it sets it right. "God created us without us: but he did not will to save us without us" (Sermon 169, 11, 13, quoted in CCC 1847). This is why he is called the Doctor of Grace.
  • The Church, body of Christ. Christ and his members form "the whole Christ". "Let us rejoice then and give thanks that we have become not only Christians, but Christ himself" (Homilies on John 21, 8, quoted in CCC 795). Christ "prays for us as our priest, prays in us as our Head, and is prayed to by us as our God" (On the Psalms 85, 1; CCC 2616).
  • The sacraments. Against the Donatists: it is Christ who baptizes, so a sacrament is valid even when given by an unworthy minister. "The word comes to the element, and it becomes a sacrament" (Homilies on John 80, 3). To the newly baptized at the Eucharist: "Be what you see, and receive what you are" (Sermon 272, in substance).
  • Mary. Mary is virgin in conceiving, virgin in giving birth, ever virgin (Sermon 186, 1; CCC 510). She conceived Christ in her heart by faith before she conceived him in her womb (Sermon 215, 4, in substance). Because of the honour of the Lord, he would not speak of sin when speaking of her (On Nature and Grace 36, 42, in substance).
  • Two loves, two cities. "Two loves have made two cities: love of self, even to contempt of God, the earthly city; love of God, even to contempt of self, the heavenly city" (The City of God 14, 28, in substance). History is God's patient work, in which the two are mixed until the end.
  • Love. "Love, and do what you will" (Homilies on 1 John 7, 8). He means that a heart truly filled with the love of God will choose rightly, not that anything is allowed. "Our weight is our love": love carries us where we go (Confessions 13, 9, 10, in substance).
  • Prayer is desire. Prayer is the longing of the heart, and "prayer is the encounter of God's thirst with ours" (CCC 2560, after Augustine). When we ask, God widens our heart to receive.
  • Peace by peace. "It is a higher glory still to stay war itself with a word, than to slay men with the sword" (Letter 229, 2, as quoted by Benedict XVI). He also set out the conditions under which war could be just, ideas later developed by others.
  • A shepherd with his people. "For you I am a bishop, with you I am a Christian" (Sermon 340, 1, quoted in Lumen Gentium 32).
How he taught

He preached to simple people. He left the imperial court to speak to dockworkers and farmers in a port town. Benedict XVI notes that Augustine was ready to use simpler, even ungrammatical Latin so that all could understand. His sermons were taken down by shorthand writers as he spoke. Almost six hundred survive, of perhaps three or four thousand.

He prayed his theology. The Confessions are written as a long prayer to God. Even his hardest books turn to praise.

He loved dialogue. He held public debates with Manichees, Donatists and Arians, and wrote hundreds of letters answering questions from bishops, officials, monks, women and young people.

He used every gift. He used rhetoric, philosophy, music, numbers, the memory, even puns. He wrote On Christian Teaching to show how all learning can serve the reading of Scripture.

He taught catechists. In On Catechizing the Uninstructed he tells a weary deacon to teach with joy, to tell the whole story of salvation, and to adapt to each listener.

He corrected himself. At the end of his life he reread all his books and wrote the Retractations, noting what he would now say differently. Benedict XVI calls it "a teaching of sincerity and intellectual humility".

His works

Possidius, his friend, listed more than a thousand writings, counting each sermon and letter. Benedict XVI notes over three hundred letters and almost six hundred homilies that survive today. The main works, by kind:

Confessions and Retractations

Confessions, thirteen books, 397 to 400: his life to his mother's death (books 1 to 9), memory and the present (10), time and creation (11 to 13).
Retractations, two books, about 426 to 427: a review of his writings.

Early dialogues (386 to 391)

Against the Academics; On the Happy Life; On Order; Soliloquies; On the Immortality of the Soul; On the Greatness of the Soul; On Music; On the Teacher, a dialogue with Adeodatus; On the Choice of the Will (De libero arbitrio), three books.

Great doctrinal works

The City of God, twenty-two books, 413 to 426.
On the Trinity, fifteen books, about 399 to 419 or later.
On Christian Teaching, four books, begun about 396, finished about 426.
Enchiridion (handbook) on faith, hope and love, about 421.
On Faith and the Creed (393); On True Religion; On the Usefulness of Believing; On Catechizing the Uninstructed.

Scripture

Expositions of the Psalms, on all 150 psalms, many preached.
Homilies on the Gospel of John, 124; Homilies on the First Letter of John, 10.
The Literal Meaning of Genesis, twelve books, and two earlier works on Genesis.
The Lord's Sermon on the Mount; The Harmony of the Gospels; Questions on the Heptateuch; short works on Romans and Galatians.

Against the Manichees

On the Morals of the Catholic Church; Against Faustus, thirty-three books; Against Fortunatus and Against Felix, records of public debates; On the Nature of the Good; and others.

Against the Donatists

Psalm against the Party of Donatus, a popular song; On Baptism, seven books; Against the Letters of Petilian; Summary of the Conference of 411; and others.

On grace, against the Pelagians

On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins (412); On the Spirit and the Letter (412); On Nature and Grace (415); On the Grace of Christ and Original Sin (418); On Marriage and Concupiscence; Against Julian, six books; an Unfinished Work against Julian, left at his death; On Grace and the Will (De gratia et libero arbitrio), On Rebuke and Grace, On the Predestination of the Saints and On the Gift of Perseverance (426 to 429).

Moral and monastic

On the Good of Marriage; On Holy Virginity; On the Good of Widowhood; On Lying and Against Lying; On the Work of Monks; On the Care of the Dead; On Patience.
The Rule of St Augustine, a short rule for community life. Most scholars accept it as his, though some questions of its form are debated.

Letters and sermons

Letters: over 300, including 29 found by Johannes Divjak and published in 1981.
Sermons: almost 600, including 26 found at Mainz by François Dolbeau in 1990.
Many sermons once printed under his name are not his.

Lost and doubtful

Lost: his first book, On the Beautiful and the Fitting, written at Carthage, and some early works.
Not his: the Te Deum; the story that he and Ambrose composed it at his baptism has no good foundation.

A short taste

"Late have I loved you, beauty so old and so new: late have I loved you." (Confessions 10, 27, 38, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 30 January 2008)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave five General Audiences on him, on 9, 16 and 30 January and 20 and 27 February 2008, more than on any other Father. He called him "the greatest Father of the Latin Church" and said that when he read him he felt him to be "a man of today: a friend, a contemporary". He spoke of his three conversions: to Christ in 386; to the service of others when he was made a priest; and the daily conversion of asking forgiveness to the end. On 22 April 2007 he prayed at his tomb in Pavia, and there symbolically handed over his first encyclical Deus Caritas Est, saying he owed much of it to Augustine. His second, Spe Salvi, is also deeply Augustinian.

St John Paul II wrote the Apostolic Letter Augustinum Hipponensem on 28 August 1986, for the sixteenth centenary of his conversion.

Pius XI wrote the encyclical Ad salutem on 20 April 1930, for the fifteenth centenary of his death.

St Paul VI said that "all the thought-currents of the past meet in his works" and flow into the whole later tradition (4 May 1970, as quoted by Benedict XVI).

Pope Leo XIV, elected in 2025, is a member of the Order of St Augustine, one of the religious families that live by his Rule.

Councils and the Catechism. The Council of Carthage of 418 and the Second Council of Orange in 529, approved by Pope Boniface II, drew heavily on his teaching on grace. The Council of Trent used him often. The Catechism quotes him more than any other Father, for example CCC 30, 158, 510, 795, 1847, 2560 and 2616.

Where the Church has not followed him. His authority is very great, but the Church does not take every opinion of his as her teaching. Some later readers pushed his teaching on grace and predestination into errors the Church condemned, as with Jansenism. On infants who die without baptism, Augustine held that they share a mild punishment; the Church today entrusts them to God's mercy and hopes for their salvation (CCC 1261). And while he accepted that the state could press schismatics, the Church now teaches that no one may be forced to believe (Dignitatis Humanae 2 and 10). These are told here plainly and without blame; he himself asked his readers to correct him where he was wrong.

Why he matters today

He is the Father of the restless heart. Anyone who has searched, wandered and longed, who has tried every pleasure and every idea, can find his own story in the Confessions.

He shows that conversion is a whole life, not one moment. He was converted in a garden, again when he was made a priest, and again every day.

He gives hope to mothers and fathers who pray for children far from God. St Monica's tears were heard.

He shows that the mind and faith are friends. He thought hard about time, memory, language, evil and history, and found that faith opens reason rather than closing it.

He teaches that the Church is a field of wheat and weeds, not a club of the pure, and that unity is worth great patience.

And he points beyond every earthly city to the city of God, where "we shall rest and we shall see, we shall see and we shall love, we shall love and we shall praise" (The City of God 22, 30, in substance).

Sit with this

  1. Where is my heart restless? What am I asking things to give me that only God can give?
  2. Is there a "not yet" in my life that God is waiting for me to turn into "now"?
  3. Who prays for me as Monica prayed for her son? For whom do I pray like that?
