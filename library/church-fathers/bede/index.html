St Bede the Venerable

TimeBorn 672 or 673; died on the evening of 25 May 735, which by the reckoning of the day from sunset was already 26 May, Ascension Day
WhereThe twin monastery of St Peter at Wearmouth and St Paul at Jarrow, in the kingdom of Northumbria (today Sunderland and Jarrow, Tyne and Wear, north-east England)
What he wasBenedictine monk and priest, teacher, Bible scholar, historian, scientist of the calendar
LanguageLatin; he also taught and translated in Old English
Known asThe Venerable Bede (Beda Venerabilis); "the father of English history"
HonouredSaint. Doctor of the Church, declared by Pope Leo XIII on 13 November 1899. Latin Church: 25 May (optional memorial; from 1899 to 1969 it was 27 May). Also remembered by the Church of England on 25 May. His tomb is in the Galilee Chapel of Durham Cathedral.
His life

What we know. Almost all we know of Bede's life comes from a few lines he wrote at the end of his Ecclesiastical History, in 731, and from a letter by his disciple Cuthbert describing his death. He says he was born "in the territory of the said monastery", on its lands near the mouths of the rivers Wear and Tyne. He was then in his fifty-ninth year, so he was born in 672 or 673. He says nothing of his parents. Historians guess from his friendships with noblemen that his family was of some standing.

Given to the monks at seven. "At the age of seven I was, by the care of my relations, given to the most reverend Abbot Benedict, and afterwards to Ceolfrid, to be educated" (Ecclesiastical History 5, 24, tr. in the Catholic Encyclopedia). Abbot Benedict was St Benedict Biscop, a nobleman who had become a monk. He had travelled to Rome several times and founded the monastery of St Peter at Wearmouth in 674. He brought back books, pictures, relics, glaziers and masons, and the archcantor John of St Peter's in Rome to teach the Roman chant. In 682 Ceolfrith founded a sister house, St Paul's at Jarrow, a few miles north. The dedication stone of its church, dated 23 April 685, still survives. Bede probably moved there as a boy.

The plague of 686. A plague struck the monastery. An early Life of Ceolfrith says that only the abbot and one small boy were left who could sing the Office. The two kept the full round of psalms going, weeping, until others could be trained. Most historians think the boy was Bede, then about fourteen.

Deacon and priest. "In my nineteenth year I was admitted to the diaconate, in my thirtieth to the priesthood, both by the hands of the most reverend Bishop John", that is, St John of Beverley, then Bishop of Hexham, "at the bidding of Abbot Ceolfrid" (5, 24). That gives about 692 and about 702.

A life of prayer and books. "From that time I have spent the whole of my life within that monastery, devoting all my pains to the study of the Scriptures, and amid the observance of monastic discipline and the daily charge of singing in the Church, it has been ever my delight to learn or teach or write" (5, 24). He made short visits to other monasteries, to Lindisfarne and to York, but he seems never to have gone further. The monastery library, built up by Benedict Biscop and Ceolfrith, may have held about two hundred books, a great collection for the age.

Teacher and writer. He began with schoolbooks: on Latin verse and on the figures of speech in the Bible, about 701 to 703. Then came works on time and the calendar, and commentaries on books of the Bible, one after another. He wrote for his brothers and pupils, "for my own use and that of my brethren", as he put it. By the end he had written more than sixty books.

Accused of heresy, 708. In On Times (703) Bede had worked out from the Hebrew text, as Jerome translated it, that fewer years had passed between Creation and Christ than the common reckoning held. At a feast in the presence of Bishop Wilfrid, some monks who had been drinking accused him of heresy. Bede heard of it and answered at once in a careful Letter to Plegwin, asking that it be read to the bishop. He calls his accusers "rustics". Nothing more came of it.

The great Bible. Under Abbot Ceolfrith the monastery made three complete Latin Bibles of the Vulgate. One survives, the Codex Amiatinus, now in Florence, the oldest surviving complete Bible in the Latin Vulgate. Benedict XVI says that Bede worked on its text. In 716 Ceolfrith set out to carry it to Rome as a gift for the Pope, but he died on the way at Langres in France. Bede wrote of him in his Lives of the Abbots.

The great History, 731. At the request of King Ceolwulf of Northumbria, Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People in about 731. He gathered letters and records with care. A priest of London, Nothhelm, brought him copies of letters of St Gregory the Great from the papal archives in Rome. Abbot Albinus of Canterbury and others sent him information. He names his sources in the preface, a thing rare in his age.

Last letter. In 733 he visited his former pupil Egbert at York. In 734 he wrote him a long letter, the Letter to Egbert, now a bishop and soon the first Archbishop of York. In it he asks for more bishops and better priests, warns against false monasteries set up by laymen to gain land and escape service to the king, and asks that every Christian learn the Creed and the Lord's Prayer, in English if they knew no Latin. He says that he himself had translated them for unlearned priests.

His death, 735. Cuthbert, later Abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow, wrote an account of it to a friend. Some weeks before Easter Bede grew weak and short of breath. He kept teaching, singing the psalms and giving thanks. Cuthbert writes: "I never saw with my eyes or heard with my ears anyone return thanks so unceasingly to the living God" (tr. in the Catholic Encyclopedia). He often repeated the words of Hebrews 10:31 on falling into the hands of the living God. He sang the antiphon of the Magnificat of the Ascension, and he wept. He was working on two things to the end: an English translation of St John's Gospel, and extracts from Isidore's On the Nature of Things, saying, as Cuthbert reports, that he would not have his boys read what was false or labour without profit after his death.

On the Wednesday before the Ascension, after a morning of dictation, he gave out the little treasures in his box, some pepper, napkins and incense, to the priests of the house, and asked them to pray and offer Masses for him. In the evening the boy Wilberht said, "There is still one sentence, dear master, which is not written down." When it was done, Bede said, "Thou hast spoken truth; it is finished" (tr. in the Catholic Encyclopedia). He asked to be held facing the place where he used to pray. On the floor of his cell he sang the Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, and at the last word he died. It was 25 May in our reckoning, already 26 May, the Ascension, by the custom of counting the day from sunset. He was buried at Jarrow.

After death. Cuthbert's letter includes five lines of Old English verse on the soul's departure, known as "Bede's Death Song". Whether Bede composed it or only recited it is debated. In the early eleventh century his bones were taken to Durham and laid with St Cuthbert. In 1370 they were moved to a shrine in the Galilee Chapel of Durham Cathedral. The shrine was destroyed in 1541, at the Reformation, but the bones were buried again in the chapel, where his tomb is today.

The world he lived in

Bede was born less than a century after Christianity reached the English. In 597 Pope St Gregory the Great sent St Augustine of Canterbury with about forty monks to Kent, in the south. Missionaries from Rome reached Northumbria in 625. After a pagan reaction, King Oswald invited monks from the Irish monastery of Iona, and St Aidan founded Lindisfarne in 635. The English Church therefore had two parents, Roman and Irish, with different customs.

The sharpest difference was the date of Easter. In 664, at the Synod of Whitby, King Oswiu of Northumbria chose the Roman reckoning. Some Irish and British churches kept the older way for decades. For Bede, a common Easter was a sign of one Church, and it is a thread through his whole History.

England was not one kingdom but several: Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Wessex, Kent and others, often at war. Northumbria in Bede's youth was a centre of learning and art, the age of the Lindisfarne Gospels and of great stone crosses. In 669 Pope Vitalian sent Theodore of Tarsus, a Greek monk, as Archbishop of Canterbury, with the African abbot Hadrian. Their school taught Greek and Latin and helped to organise the dioceses.

The native Britons, who were Christians long before the English came, had suffered conquest by them. Bede's sources, especially the British writer Gildas, judged the Britons harshly, and Bede repeats that judgement. Modern historians note this bias. They also note that he says little about Mercia and Wessex, the rivals of his own Northumbria.

Elsewhere, the Muslim conquests had reached Spain. English missionaries such as St Willibrord and St Boniface were preaching on the Continent. Bede died a generation before Charlemagne, whose scholars would copy and spread his books.

What he taught
  • Christ is the key to all Scripture. Benedict XVI says Bede first "listens to exactly what the text says" and then reads it in the light of Christ, so that the Old and New Testaments are "one" Scripture. He calls the harmony of the two the concordia sacramentorum. The tent that Moses pitched and the temple of Solomon are images of the Church, "built on Christ and on the Apostles with living stones" (On the Tabernacle, On the Temple, as summarised by Benedict XVI).
  • A Church of all peoples. As foreign craftsmen helped to build Solomon's temple, so teachers of many nations build the Church. Bede gladly counted the Irish and the English among them. The Church, the Pope says, "is not restricted to one specific culture".
  • Time counted from Christ. Bede did not invent the counting of years from the Incarnation; it came from the monk Dionysius Exiguus. But Bede used it through his History and his work on time, and it spread through Europe from his books. Benedict XVI says that he "gave us this calendar that interprets history starting from the Incarnation of the Lord".
  • One Easter for one Church. His Reckoning of Time (725) explained how to find the date of Easter and extended the Easter tables. Benedict XVI says it "became the reference text for the whole Catholic Church".
  • The Holy Spirit at work in history. His histories, following the Acts of the Apostles, show the Spirit leading the Church through councils, saints and missions. He records the first six Ecumenical Councils and the faith they defined.
  • The sacraments make us Christ. In the line of Cyril, Ambrose and Augustine, Bede teaches that Christian initiation makes each believer "not only a Christian but Christ". Each time a soul receives and keeps the Word of God, "in imitation of Mary", Christ is conceived anew. When the newly baptised receive the Easter sacraments, the Church, in a daring phrase that Benedict XVI recalls, becomes in a sense "Mother of God", bringing forth children by the Holy Spirit.
  • Mary's song. In his homily on the Visitation he explains the Magnificat as the song of a soul that magnifies the Lord because all it has comes from him (Homilies on the Gospels 1, 4). The Church still prays with this homily in Advent.
  • Mercy that chooses. On the call of St Matthew he wrote that Jesus saw the tax collector miserando atque eligendo, "by having mercy and by choosing" (Homilies 1, 21). Pope Francis took these words as his motto.
  • Frequent Communion. He knew many lay people, men and women, married and single, "capable of irreproachable conduct who, if appropriately guided, will be able every day to receive Eucharistic communion" (Letter to Egbert, as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • Every state of life has its task. Benedict XVI draws out his counsel: scholars must present God's word attractively and keep "Catholic simplicity"; pastors must preach first, using also images, processions and pilgrimages; monks must not keep the Gospel to themselves; parents exercise in their homes "the priestly office as pastors and guides"; all must reproduce in life "what they celebrate in the liturgy".
  • Nature read with care. In The Reckoning of Time he explains why days grow longer and shorter on a round earth, and he links the tides to the moon, noting that high tide comes at different times along the same coast. He corrected his sources when he found them wrong.
  • Scripture above all books. "Holy Scripture is above all other books not only by its authority because it is Divine, or by its utility because it leads to eternal life, but also by its antiquity and its literary form" (On Figures of Speech, tr. in the Catholic Encyclopedia).
How he taught

A teacher first. He wrote schoolbooks on grammar, spelling, verse and figures of speech, using Christian poets as examples. His books were meant to be used in a classroom and in the choir.

He gathered from the Fathers. His commentaries draw on Augustine, Jerome, Ambrose and Gregory the Great, and he was one of the first to name these four together as the great teachers of the Latin Church. He added his own explanations where they had not written.

He named his sources. He marked in the margins which words came from which Father, and asked copyists to keep the marks. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls this "sense of literary property" remarkable for the age.

He wrote clearly. Modern scholars praise his Latin as clear and easy to read. Charles Plummer, his editor, called it "clear and limpid".

He wrote history to teach. His stories of kings and bishops are meant to give examples of good and evil, and he tells of many miracles. He believed, with Gregory the Great, that wonders can lead to a deeper faith. He also told the reader where his information came from, so the reader could judge.

He used the people's language. He translated the Creed and the Lord's Prayer into English for priests who knew little Latin, and at his death he was putting St John's Gospel into English. Cuthbert says he was "learned in our songs". These translations are lost.

His works

At the end of the History (5, 24) Bede left his own list of his works. Most survive. Dates are approximate.

History and lives

Ecclesiastical History of the English People, five books, about 731. About 160 medieval manuscripts survive. King Alfred's circle translated it into Old English.
Lives of the Abbots of Wearmouth and Jarrow.
Life of St Cuthbert, in verse and, later, in prose.
Life of St Felix, a prose form of the poems of Paulinus of Nola.
A revised Passion of St Anastasius, from a poor Latin translation of the Greek.
Martyrology, a list of saints by day. Genuine, but so much added to later that his part is uncertain.
On the Holy Places, on Jerusalem, drawn from Adomnán of Iona.

Time and nature

On the Nature of Things, modelled in part on Isidore's book.
On Times, 703, with a short chronicle.
The Reckoning of Time (De temporum ratione), 725, with the Greater Chronicle to 725 as chapter 66.
Letter to Plegwin, 708, his defence.
Letter to Wicthed, on the date of Easter.

Old Testament

Commentaries on Genesis; On the Tabernacle; On the Temple; on Samuel; Thirty Questions on the Books of Kings; on Ezra and Nehemiah; on Tobit; on Proverbs; on the Song of Songs; on the Canticle of Habakkuk; and notes on other books.

New Testament

Commentaries on Mark and on Luke; on the Acts of the Apostles, with a later Reconsideration (Retractatio); on the Seven Catholic Epistles; on the Apocalypse; and a Collection on the Apostle, passages of St Augustine arranged in the order of Paul's letters. A commentary on Matthew printed under his name is not his.

Homilies, verse, letters

Homilies on the Gospels, fifty in two books, for feasts and seasons; a few of the printed ones are doubtful.
Verses on the Day of Judgement; a book of hymns and a book of epigrams, partly recovered by modern scholars.
Letters, including the Letter to Egbert, 734.
Bede's Death Song, in Old English. Disputed.
A Penitential under his name is probably not his.

Schoolbooks

On the Art of Verse (De arte metrica).
On Figures of Speech in Scripture (De schematibus et tropis).
On Spelling (De orthographia).
Lost: his English translations of the Creed, the Lord's Prayer and part of St John's Gospel.

A short taste

"It has been ever my delight to learn or teach or write." (Ecclesiastical History 5, 24, tr. in the Catholic Encyclopedia)

"I beseech you, O good Jesus, that to the one to whom you have graciously granted sweetly to drink in the words of your knowledge, you will also vouchsafe in your loving kindness that he may one day come to you, the Fountain of all wisdom." (The closing prayer of the History, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 18 February 2009)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

"Venerable". The title was attached to his name within a few generations of his death. Alcuin and Paul the Deacon used it, and a council at Aachen in the ninth century called him "venerable and admirable doctor of modern times". A later legend says an angel finished his epitaph with the word; there is no early evidence for it. Benedict XVI says that Pope Sergius I already called him so when he wrote to his abbot in 701 asking that Bede come to Rome; historians debate whether that letter refers to him.

Boniface and Alcuin. St Boniface, the apostle of Germany, asked several times for copies of Bede's works for his missionaries. Alcuin, who studied at York in the school of Bede's pupil Egbert, spread his books through the empire of Charlemagne. Benedict XVI recalls that Notker of St Gall compared Bede to a new sun risen in the West.

Dante placed him in the heaven of the Sun among the great teachers, beside St Isidore of Seville and Richard of St Victor (Paradiso 10).

Doctor of the Church. In 1859 Cardinal Wiseman and the English bishops asked the Holy See to declare him a Doctor. On 13 November 1899 Pope Leo XIII did so and extended his feast to the whole Church, on 27 May. Since 1969 it has been kept on 25 May. For more than a century he was the only Doctor of the Church born in England, until St John Henry Newman was declared a Doctor on 1 November 2025.

Benedict XVI gave a General Audience on him on 18 February 2009. He praised his reading of Scripture with Christ as its key, his love of the Church's history and liturgy, and his counsel to each state of life. He ended with a prayer that today too there may be people of Bede's stature "to keep the whole continent united".

Pope Francis took his motto, miserando atque eligendo, from Bede's homily on the call of St Matthew, which the Liturgy of the Hours reads on that Apostle's feast, 21 September.

The liturgy. On his memorial the Office of Readings gives Cuthbert's letter on his death. Beda College in Rome, which prepares older men for the priesthood, bears his name.

Why he matters today

He shows that a hidden life can change the world. Bede hardly left his monastery, and yet he gave Europe its way of counting years, its account of how the English became Christian, and a library of commentaries read for centuries.

He joins prayer and work in a simple rhythm: the Office in choir, then the classroom and the desk, then the Office again. His learning never left the liturgy.

He is a model for anyone who writes or teaches: careful with sources, honest about where knowledge comes from, clear in language, and eager to put the faith into the people's own words.

And his death is one of the most beautiful in Christian memory. He kept working, gave away his small treasures, finished the sentence, and sang the Glory be. Few of us will be given so peaceful an end, but all of us can learn to give thanks "so unceasingly to the living God".

Sit with this

  1. Where is my monastery, the small place God has given me? Can I find there my delight "to learn or teach or write", or simply to serve?
  2. When I read the Bible, do I look for Christ in it, as Bede did?
  3. What "one sentence" have I still to finish, a reconciliation, a thanks, a gift, before my day ends?
