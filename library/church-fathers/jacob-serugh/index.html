Mar Jacob of Serugh, Flute of the Holy Spirit

TimeBorn about 451. Died 29 November 521, according to the Syriac sources (some give 520)
WhereBorn at Kurtam on the Euphrates. Studied at Edessa (today Şanlıurfa, Turkey). Served and died in the district of Serugh, at Batnan (today Suruç, Turkey, near the Syrian border)
What he wasPoet, preacher and teacher. Chorepiscopus (a bishop or senior priest for the villages) at Haura, then bishop of Batnan of Serugh from 519
LanguageSyriac
Known as"The Flute of the Holy Spirit and the Harp of the believing Church". Also Jacob of Sarug, Mar Jacob, Mor Ya'qub
HonouredA saint in the Churches of the West Syriac tradition, Catholic and Orthodox, and in the Maronite Church. The usual day is 29 November. The Syro-Malankara Catholic calendar for 2026 to 2027 lists him as "Mar Jacob of Sarug" at the end of November. He is not a Doctor of the Church
His honour, honestly Jacob is venerated as a saint by Churches that were later divided over the Council of Chalcedon (451): the Syriac Orthodox Church, and also Churches in full communion with Rome, such as the Maronite, Syriac Catholic and Syro-Malankara Churches. The scholar Philip Forness notes that both Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian traditions have received him as a saint. His day is 29 November, the day of his death in the Syriac sources. In the first centuries there was no canonisation process: saints were honoured by the worship of the Churches, and that is how Jacob is honoured. He is not, as far as we have found, named in the Roman Martyrology, and we have found no papal catechesis on him. This page uses the words of the West Syriac tradition, as the Syro-Malankara Church does.
His life

Birth. Jacob was born about 451, the year of the Council of Chalcedon, at Kurtam, a village on the river Euphrates in the district of Serugh, not far from Harran. The region was then part of the Roman Empire, close to its frontier with Persia. His family were Syriac speaking Christians. Later stories, written down long after his death in a metrical Life by Sa'id bar Sabuni (died 1095), tell of a childhood touched by grace. We cannot test them, and we do not repeat them as history.

At Edessa. He studied at the famous School of Edessa, the great Syriac centre of learning. He remembered it himself in a letter to the monks of Mar Bassus: forty five years earlier, he says, he had lived in Edessa "in the study of the divine scriptures". There he learned the Bible and the methods of its teachers. Scholars notice in his homilies traces of the biblical interpretation that came from Theodore of Mopsuestia and was taught at Edessa, even though Jacob later took a different path in theology.

Priest and teacher of the villages. He became a chorepiscopus, a pastor for the country churches, at Haura in the district of Serugh. We do not know the year. A chronicle written at the time, known as the Chronicle of Pseudo-Joshua the Stylite, calls him "the honoured Jacob, the periodeutes", a visiting pastor, and says that he wrote many homilies and sent letters to the cities when a plague of locusts struck. During the war that the Persian king Kavad waged inside the Roman frontier in 502 to 503, he wrote to encourage his people.

Bishop. In 519, at about sixty seven years of age, he was chosen bishop of Batnan of Serugh. Brock's entry in the Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage says that Paul, bishop of Edessa, consecrated him. He served for only two years or so. A manuscript copied two years after his death already calls him "bishop of Batnae".

Death. The Syriac tradition gives the day of his death as 29 November 521, and that is the day on which his Churches remember him. Some sources give 520. He died at Batnan, in today's Suruç.

A life of words. Jacob of Edessa, writing about two centuries later, credits him with 763 homilies in verse. Bar Hebraeus in the thirteenth century says about 760, and adds that seventy scribes worked to copy what he dictated. These numbers come from later writers, but the size of what survives, at least 380 verse homilies, shows that they are not far wrong.

The world he lived in

The Syriac Church of the Roman East. Jacob lived in the Syriac speaking provinces of the Roman Empire, between the cities of Edessa, Harran and Antioch. Syriac is a form of Aramaic, close to the speech of Jesus and his apostles. A rich Christian culture had grown there, with its own Bible (the Peshitta), its own poetry and its own schools. St Ephrem had died at Edessa about eighty years before Jacob was born. Jacob honoured him and wrote a verse homily about him.

A Church divided over words about Christ. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 taught that Christ is one Person in two natures, divine and human. Many Christians in Egypt and Syria held that this way of speaking weakened the unity of Christ, and they preferred the phrase of St Cyril of Alexandria, "one incarnate nature of God the Word". For a time the Emperor Zeno's decree of union, the Henotikon (482), tried to keep peace by not insisting on Chalcedon. Jacob accepted that peace. Under Emperor Justin, from 518, pressure to accept Chalcedon returned, and bishops who refused it were driven out. Jacob's own later letters show that he did not accept the Council's formula.

Two schools of thought in one city. Edessa was home to teachers of both kinds. The followers of Theodore of Mopsuestia spoke strongly of the two natures in Christ. Among them was Narsai, who left Edessa for Nisibis in Persia. Jacob belonged to the other side, which spoke above all of the one Christ. The two men were near contemporaries and were the two great verse preachers of their age. Later writers call Jacob equal to Narsai and second only to Ephrem.

War, famine and pastoral care. The frontier with Persia was not peaceful. In 502 to 503 the Persian army entered the Roman provinces. Locusts and famine struck. A pastor of the villages had to comfort frightened people, and Jacob's letters do this. He also wrote, between 518 and 521, to the Christians of Himyar in southern Arabia, who were suffering for their faith. It is a rare witness to the Church in Arabia before Islam.

What he taught
  • Approach the mystery with love and wonder. Jacob prefers the way of St Ephrem: symbol, image and paradox, rather than sharp definitions. Brock notes that he chose this poetic way of theology over the analytic approach of the arguments after Chalcedon. God is to be adored, not dissected. Many of his homilies open with a prayer to Christ asking for the gift of speech, as in his homily on the death of the Virgin.
  • One Christ, God made man. A phrase that returns again and again in his writing is Christ's "miracles and sufferings": the same Son of God works the miracles and bears the sufferings. The Word truly became flesh, was born of Mary and died on the cross. Scholars point to four homilies as the key to his thought: on the Council of Chalcedon, on the Faith, on "The Lord will raise up a prophet", and on the revelation that Simon (Peter) received (compare Matthew 16:16 to 17).
  • Mary, the Mother of God. He wrote several homilies on Our Lady: on the Blessed Virgin, on the Annunciation, on the Visitation, on the Nativity and on her death and burial. He praises her holiness, her humility and her faith, and the wonder that the Lord of all dwelt within the pure Mother. Mary Hansbury's translation, On the Mother of God (1998), gives four of them in English. The Kerala priest James Puthuparampil has written a full study of this Marian teaching.
  • The Old Testament speaks of Christ. He reads the Scriptures as full of signs and types of Christ. He preached on the Veil of Moses (Exodus 34:29 to 35), on the Red Heifer (Numbers 19), and on the Chariot that Ezekiel saw (Ezekiel 1). In each, the hidden meaning points to Christ and the Church.
  • Creation is good and speaks of God. His homilies on the six days of creation, called the Hexaemeron, are the first Syriac work of their kind on the opening of Genesis.
  • The saints are a school of holiness. He wrote verse homilies on saints such as Ephrem, Simeon the Stylite and George. His homily on Ephrem is an early witness to the tradition that Ephrem taught choirs of women to sing his hymns.
  • The pastor stands with his people. His letters during war and famine, and his letter to the persecuted Christians of Himyar, show a bishop who comforted the frightened and the suffering. He calls them to trust and endurance.
  • The Church's prayer. Much of his verse passed into the prayer of the West Syriac Churches. Brock lists among the texts attributed to him a baptismal rite used by the Maronite Church, petitions (bo'woto) and many hymns for Passion Week drawn from his homilies. Three anaphoras (Eucharistic prayers) also carry his name, though the attributions are uncertain.

The question of Chalcedon, with respect

Jacob stood in the tradition that did not accept the Council of Chalcedon. For a long time he said little about the quarrel. When Paul of Edessa pressed him in letters, he openly criticised Chalcedon and the language of "two natures", and he wrote a homily lamenting the Council. Scholars have argued about his teaching for about two hundred and fifty years. Joseph Simonius Assemani of the Vatican Library, in 1719, counted him among the orthodox writers. Letters found later, in the nineteenth century, showed his non-Chalcedonian position plainly. Philip Forness has traced these debates.

The Catholic Church today speaks of this division with care. On 23 June 1984 Pope St John Paul II and the Syriac Orthodox Patriarch Moran Mor Ignatius Zakka I Iwas signed a common declaration. They said that the later confusions and schisms between their Churches "arose only because of differences in terminology and culture", and that they confess the same faith in Christ. They even allowed their faithful, when they cannot reach a priest of their own Church, to receive Penance, the Eucharist and the Anointing of the Sick from the other's priests. The Catholic Church and the Oriental Orthodox Churches continue a theological dialogue together. So Jacob can be read by Catholics as a great witness to the one Christ, true God and true man, whose words came before that long road to understanding.

Where care is needed

Jacob speaks of Mary as pure and holy in the language of poetry. He does not speak in the terms of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception, defined in 1854 (CCC 490 to 492), and his words should not be pressed for or against it. His homilies were also written in an age of sharp arguments, and in places he speaks harshly of those who differed. Readers today follow the Church's call to respect all, and in particular the teaching of the Second Vatican Council in Nostra Aetate 4 that the Passion of Christ cannot be charged against all Jews, then or now.

How he taught

The verse homily. His chief form is the memra, a homily in verse, meant to be recited or chanted before the people. He wrote it in lines of twelve syllables, in couplets. This metre became so linked with him that the Syriac tradition calls it after him. The longest that survives runs to about 1,400 lines.

Other forms. He also wrote madroshe (teaching hymns sung with a refrain), sugyotho (dialogue songs, where two voices answer each other, often with an alphabet acrostic), and six prose homilies for the great feasts, called turgome. A passage of his prose homily on the Resurrection appears in the Hudra, the prayer book of the East Syriac Church, which shows how widely he was read.

Prayer before preaching. He often begins by turning to Christ and asking for light. In his homily on the death and burial of the Virgin, he opens with a long chain of petitions to the Son, who "in Your love inclined heaven and descended to earth" (Hansbury's translation). The preacher speaks only after he has prayed.

Telling the story. He loves to retell the Bible story, slowly, with the feelings of each person and the words they might have said. He lets Mary, the angel, Elizabeth or the prophets speak. This draws the listener into the scene. Then he draws out the hidden meaning, the type that points to Christ.

Images and paradox. Like Ephrem, he holds opposites together: the Infinite is held in a small womb, the Lord of all is wrapped in swaddling clothes, the Life dies and gives life. Such paradoxes lead the mind to adore rather than to argue.

His works

Verse homilies (memre)

Said to number 763; at least 380 survive, and over 200 are published. Among them: on the Chariot that Ezekiel saw; on the Veil of Moses; on the Red Heifer; on the six days of creation (Hexaemeron); on the Blessed Virgin; on the Annunciation; on the Visitation; on the Nativity; on the death and burial of the Virgin; on the Council of Nicaea; on the Council of Chalcedon; on the Faith; on Ephrem; on Simeon the Stylite; on George; and many on the Gospels and the feasts

Prose

Six festal homilies (turgome) for the great feasts; about 43 letters, some incomplete, including letters to Paul of Edessa, to the monks of Mar Bassus and to Bessas; the Letter to the Christians of Himyar (518 to 521); Lives of Daniel of Galash and of Hannina (unpublished)

Hymns and dialogue songs

Madroshe and sugyotho; some attributed works are not certainly his

Liturgy attributed to him

Three anaphoras (edited by Codrington, 1951); a Maronite baptismal rite; petitions (bo'woto) and Passion Week hymns, many drawn from his homilies. The attributions are uncertain

Not his. Some works once given his name are not by him, among them the Syriac Song of Alexander.

Editions and translations. Paul Bedjan published a large selection of the verse homilies in five volumes (Paris, 1905 to 1910); Sebastian Brock added a sixth volume (Gorgias Press, 2006). Gunnar Olinder edited the letters. Roger Akhrass and Imad Syryany published 160 more homilies in 2017. Ancient translations exist in Armenian, Arabic, Coptic, Georgian and Ethiopic. In English: Mary Hansbury, On the Mother of God (St Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1998); Thomas Kollamparampil CMI, Jacob of Serugh: Select Festal Homilies (Rome and Bangalore: Dharmaram, 1997), the work of a Syro-Malabar scholar; and the ongoing bilingual series of his metrical homilies from Gorgias Press. In 2018 only about a fifth of the published homilies had been put into English.

A short taste

"Son, who in Your love inclined heaven and descended to earth; and put on a body and became man from the daughter of David!" Homily on the death and burial of the Virgin, opening lines, translated by Mary Hansbury, On the Mother of God (1998).

"Lord of mankind, who wanted to become human in the flesh, and rested upon and dwelt within the pure mother." The same homily, in the same translation.

What the Popes and the Church say of him

The Popes. We have not found a catechesis of Benedict XVI or of another Pope given to Jacob, nor a papal document that quotes him. If one is found, it will be added.

The Catholic Eastern Churches. The Maronite Church has a long love for him; his work shaped its liturgy, and Maronite scholars such as Assemani at the Vatican Library did much to make the Syriac writers known in the West. The Syriac Catholic Church honours him, as does the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, whose official calendar (Panchangam) for 2026 to 2027 lists "Mar Jacob of Sarug" at the end of November. These Churches pray the West Syriac Office, which has carried his verse for centuries.

The Syriac Orthodox Church calls him a saint, "the Flute of the Holy Spirit and the Harp of the believing Church", and keeps his memory on 29 November.

In Kerala. Two Kerala scholars have done important work on him: Thomas Kollamparampil CMI, with his translation of the festal homilies (1997), and James Puthuparampil, with his study of Jacob's Marian thought, published by the St Ephrem Ecumenical Research Institute, Kottayam (2005), and later by Gorgias Press.

The ecumenical road. The 1984 common declaration of Pope St John Paul II and Patriarch Ignatius Zakka I Iwas, which built on the 1971 declaration of St Paul VI and Patriarch Ignatius Jacob III, allows Catholics to read Jacob as a brother in the one faith in Christ.

Why he matters today

Theology that sings. Jacob shows that faith can be taught in verse, prayed aloud and remembered by heart. A homily can be a hymn.

The Bible read slowly. He teaches us to stay with a single scene, to listen to each person in it, and to find Christ hidden in the Old Testament.

Love for Our Lady. His homilies on Mary are among the most beautiful in the whole Church. They are close to the heart of Malankara and Malabar Christians alike.

A bridge between divided Christians. He is honoured both by Churches in communion with Rome and by the Syriac Orthodox Church. In Kerala, where both are neighbours, he can help us to see how much we share.

A pastor in hard times. In war, famine and persecution he wrote to comfort his people. Pastors and parents today can learn from him how to speak hope.

Sit with this

  1. Before I speak about God, do I first ask him for light, as Jacob did?
  2. Which Bible scene could I read slowly this week, listening to each person in it?
  3. What in Mary's humility do I most need?
