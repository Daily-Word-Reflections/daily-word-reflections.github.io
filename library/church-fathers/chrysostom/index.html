St John Chrysostom

TimeBorn about 349 (some say 347), died 14 September 407
WhereAntioch in Syria (today Antakya, Turkey); the hills near Antioch; Constantinople (today Istanbul, Turkey); exile at Cucusus in Lesser Armenia (today Göksun, Turkey); died at Comana in Pontus (near Tokat, Turkey)
What he wasMonk, priest, preacher, Bishop of Constantinople, reformer, friend of the poor, exile
LanguageGreek
Known asJohn of Antioch; "Chrysostom", Greek for "golden mouth", a name later generations gave him for his preaching; "the Almoner", for his care of the poor
HonouredSaint. Doctor of the Church, one of the four great Eastern Doctors recognized by St Pius V in 1568. Patron of preachers (St Pius X, 1908). Latin Church: 13 September (before 1969 it was 27 January). Byzantine tradition: 13 November; 27 January, the bringing of his relics to Constantinople; and 30 January with St Basil and St Gregory Nazianzen as the Three Holy Hierarchs. Syro-Malabar calendar: 13 September, among the feasts of the Oriental tradition, with proper readings. Syro-Malankara calendar: 13 November.
His life

A widow's son. John was born at Antioch, one of the great cities of the Roman East, about 349. His father, Secundus, an officer in the army, died soon after his birth. His mother, Anthusa, was left a widow at about twenty and chose not to marry again. Benedict XVI notes that she gave him a deep Christian faith. She also sent him to the best schools. He studied rhetoric under Libanius, the most famous pagan teacher of his day. The story goes that Libanius, asked on his deathbed who should succeed him, said it would have been John, "if the Christians had not stolen him".

Baptism and study of Scripture. The Bishop of Antioch, St Meletius, drew him toward the Church. He was baptized in 368 and became a reader in 371. He joined a school for young ascetics led by Diodore, later Bishop of Tarsus, who taught him to read the Bible closely, word by word, in the way of the school of Antioch: the plain sense first, and then its lessons for life.

In the hills. He longed for the life of a monk. While his mother lived he stayed at home, praying and fasting. Then, Benedict XVI tells us, he spent four years among the hermits in the hills near Antioch, and two more alone in a cave, learning the Gospels and the letters of St Paul almost by heart. His health broke under the hard life, and about 378 or 379 he came back to the city.

Deacon and priest. Meletius ordained him a deacon in 381. In 386 Bishop Flavian ordained him a priest and made him a preacher in the main church of Antioch. For twelve years he preached there, often several times a week. He spoke against the Arians, on the martyrs, on the feasts, and above all on the Scriptures, book by book.

The statues, 387. In 387 a new tax drove the people of Antioch to riot. They pulled down the statues of the Emperor Theodosius and his family. The city waited in terror for punishment. Flavian set out to plead with the Emperor. It was Lent, and day after day John preached to the frightened crowds, calling them to repentance and trust in God. These are the Homilies on the Statues. "If we do not console you, where will you ever be able to find consolation?" he asked them, as Benedict XVI recalls. The Emperor pardoned the city.

Bishop of Constantinople, 398. In 397 the Bishop of Constantinople, Nectarius, died. The court wanted the famous preacher. John was taken from Antioch almost secretly, so the people would not stop him, and on 26 February 398 he was consecrated Bishop of the imperial capital by Theophilus, Patriarch of Alexandria, who had hoped to place his own candidate.

The reformer. He began at once. He sold the costly furnishings of the bishop's house and lived simply. He built hospitals and cared for the poor, so that people called him "the Almoner". He called the clergy to discipline, sent away monks who wandered the city, and spoke openly against the luxury of the rich. He made the liturgy beautiful and drew the people into it. He sent missionaries to the Goths. Through his envoys he also helped end the long separation between the Church of Antioch and the Church of Rome, as Benedict XVI notes in his letter of 2007.

Enemies. His plain speaking made enemies at court, among some clergy and among rich women whose vanity he mocked. The Empress Eudoxia took his sermons against luxury as attacks on herself. In 401 he went to Ephesus and deposed several bishops who had bought their office. Then a group of monks from Egypt, condemned by Theophilus, came to Constantinople to ask for his help. Theophilus saw his chance.

The Synod of the Oak, 403. In 403 Theophilus gathered a group of bishops at a villa called "The Oak" near Chalcedon. They condemned John on many charges and the Emperor Arcadius sent him into exile. But the sources tell of a misfortune in the palace that same night, perhaps an earthquake, and the frightened Empress called him back within days. The people met him with joy.

The second exile, 404. Peace did not last. When a silver statue of the Empress was set up near the cathedral with noisy games, he protested. At Easter 404 soldiers broke into the baptisteries during the Easter Vigil and scattered the newly baptized. In June 404 John was taken away for good. He was sent to Cucusus, a lonely town in the mountains of Lesser Armenia. His faithful followers, called "Johannites", were persecuted.

Letters from exile. From Cucusus he wrote many letters, some of the most moving of the ancient Church. Seventeen are to the deaconess St Olympias, a widow who served the poor of Constantinople and suffered for her loyalty to him. He wrote also to Pope Innocent I, who believed him innocent and broke communion with those who had condemned him, but who, as Benedict XVI says, was powerless to help him.

Death, 407. Because visitors kept coming to him in Cucusus, the government ordered him moved to Pityus, a bleak place on the eastern shore of the Black Sea. Old and ill, he was forced to walk in sun and rain. He never reached it. Near Comana in Pontus, in the chapel of the martyr St Basiliscus, he received Communion and died on 14 September 407, the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross. His last words, as his friend Palladius recorded them, were: "Glory be to God for all things" (Palladius, Dialogue on the Life of John Chrysostom 11, as quoted by Benedict XVI).

Home again. In 438 the Emperor Theodosius II, son of Eudoxia, had his body brought back to Constantinople on 27 January, and honoured him in the Church of the Holy Apostles. Benedict XVI says that his relics were taken to Rome in 1204 and rest in St Peter's Basilica. On 27 November 2004 St John Paul II gave a large part of them to the Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I.

The world he lived in

Antioch was the third city of the Empire, a rich, mixed city of Greeks, Syrians and Jews, with theatres, races and a large Christian population. It was here that the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). The Church of Antioch was proud and divided. For years it had rival bishops, and Rome and Alexandria supported a different one from the one John served.

Constantinople was the new capital, the city of the emperor, full of officials, soldiers, monks and courtiers. Its bishop was close to power and often caught in its quarrels. The Emperor Arcadius was weak, and his wife Eudoxia, his ministers and the eunuch Eutropius held much of the real power.

In the Church, the great crisis over the divinity of Christ was ending. The Council of Constantinople in 381 had confirmed the faith of Nicaea. John was a pastor, not a speculative theologian, but Benedict XVI notes that he faithfully handed on this tradition, and that he is a reliable witness to the Church's faith in his time.

It was also a time of great wealth beside great poverty. In both cities John saw rich houses, silks and silver, while beggars lay at the church doors. This contrast shaped his preaching more than anything else.

What he taught
  • The Eucharist. He taught clearly that the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ, and that Christ himself does it through the priest: "It is not man that causes the things offered to become the Body and Blood of Christ, but he who was crucified for us, Christ himself" (On the Betrayal of Judas 1, 6, in CCC 1375).
  • The Eucharist and the poor. No one taught more strongly that the altar and the poor belong together. "You have tasted the Blood of the Lord, yet you do not recognize your brother" (Homilies on 1 Corinthians 27, 4, in CCC 1397). In substance: do you want to honour Christ's body? Then do not ignore him when he is naked; do not honour him here in silk and neglect him outside in the cold (Homilies on Matthew 50, 4).
  • Our goods belong also to the poor. "Not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life. The goods we possess are not ours, but theirs" (On Lazarus 2, 5, in CCC 2446). Benedict XVI calls him "one of the great Fathers of the Church's social doctrine". He hoped for a city shaped by the Gospel, where all would be brothers and sisters with equal dignity, slave and poor included.
  • The priesthood. In On the Priesthood he speaks of the greatness of the priest's office and of its danger. Priests have received a power given neither to angels nor to archangels, the power to forgive sins (CCC 983). The Lord made care for his flock the proof of love for him (CCC 1551).
  • Marriage and the family. He calls the Christian home "a Church in miniature", as Benedict XVI recalls. He gives a husband words to say to his wife: "I have taken you in my arms, and I love you, and I prefer you to my life itself" (Homilies on Ephesians 20, 8, in CCC 2365). Whoever runs down marriage also lessens the glory of virginity (CCC 1620).
  • Bringing up children. He compares the stages of life to a sea crossing and says that early childhood is the time to shape a child's soul. Children should learn the sign of the cross and God's law early, impressed on them "as on a wax tablet" (as quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • The lay faithful. Through Baptism every Christian shares in Christ's office as priest, king and prophet, and each is responsible for the salvation of others. Benedict XVI singles this out as most timely for today.
  • Prayer anywhere. "It is possible to offer fervent prayer even while walking in public or strolling alone, or seated in your shop, while buying or selling, or even while cooking" (CCC 2743). What matters is not the number of words but the fervour of the heart (CCC 2700). "Nothing is equal to prayer" (On Anna 4, 5, in CCC 2744).
  • God who comes down to us. In his homilies on Genesis he speaks of God's "condescension", his coming down to our level: Scripture is like a letter God writes to us, and the Incarnation is his greatest coming down (Benedict XVI). Man was created for God, who did not spare his own Son for him (Sermons on Genesis 2, 1, in CCC 358).
  • Envy and unity. "We fight one another, and envy arms us against one another" (CCC 2538). Rejoice in your brother's progress, and God is glorified (CCC 2540).
  • Praying for the dead. "Let us not hesitate to help those who have died and to offer our prayers for them" (Homilies on 1 Corinthians 41, 5, in CCC 1032).
  • Gentleness in correcting error. Benedict XVI recalls his teaching that "nothing is more effective than moderation and kindliness" when correcting those who err in the faith.
How he taught

The preacher above all. He was the greatest preacher of the Greek Church. Crowds came to hear him, applauded, and sometimes pressed so close that he left the sanctuary to preach from the reader's desk in the middle of the church. Stenographers took down his sermons as he spoke.

Through the Bible, book by book. He did not preach on a few favourite verses. He went through whole books, Genesis, Matthew, John, Acts and all the letters of St Paul, verse by verse. Each homily usually explains the text first and then turns to practical life: money, the theatre, swearing, anger, how to treat servants.

Paul's friend. He loved St Paul above all writers. Seven of his sermons are in praise of Paul alone, and his commentaries on Paul's letters are still among the best ever made.

Plain and vivid. He speaks directly to his listeners, asks them questions, scolds them and encourages them. He uses pictures from the street, the market, the farm, the harbour and the home. His Greek is clear and beautiful.

Teaching those to be baptized. His instructions for those preparing for Baptism are, Benedict XVI notes, especially valuable. They show how the Church of his time formed new Christians.

His works

Homilies on the Old Testament

Genesis: 67 homilies, and nine shorter sermons.
Psalms: about 58 homilies on selected psalms.
Isaiah: a commentary on the first chapters, and six homilies on Isaiah 6.
Anna (five), David and Saul (three), and others.

Homilies on the New Testament

Matthew: 90 homilies, about 390.
John: 88 homilies, about 389.
Acts: 55 homilies.
Letters of Paul: homilies or commentaries on all of them, including Romans (32), 1 Corinthians (44), 2 Corinthians (30), Galatians, Ephesians (24), Philippians (15), Colossians (12), Thessalonians, Timothy, Titus, Philemon and Hebrews (34, published after his death from notes).
In Praise of St Paul, seven sermons.

Other sermons

About 700 homilies in all are judged authentic, according to Benedict XVI. Besides those above:
On the Statues, Lent 387.
On the Incomprehensible Nature of God, twelve, against the Anomoeans.
Instructions for those to be baptized.
On Lazarus and the rich man, On Repentance, On the Devil.
Feast sermons, including Christmas (386), and sermons on the martyrs.
Two on Eutropius, the fallen minister who fled to the church for refuge (399).
Eight sermons against Judaizing Christians, 386 to 387; see below.

Treatises

About seventeen treatises, mostly from his early years:
On the Priesthood, six books, a dialogue with his friend Basil.
On Compunction, two books.
To Stagirius, three books.
Against the Opponents of the Monastic Life, three books.
Comparison of a King and a Monk.
On Virginity; To a Young Widow.
On Vainglory and the Education of Children.
No One Can Harm the Man Who Does Not Harm Himself, from exile.

Letters

About 240 letters (Benedict XVI counts 241), almost all from exile, 404 to 407.
Seventeen letters to Olympias.
Two letters to Pope Innocent I.

Liturgy, and works not his

Divine Liturgy of St John Chrysostom, the usual Eucharistic liturgy of the Byzantine Churches. Its anaphora is linked with him by ancient tradition; scholars think he probably revised an older text rather than composing it all.
Many sermons were later copied under his famous name and are not his. The best known is the Unfinished Commentary on Matthew (Opus imperfectum), long read as his and now known to be by another author.

A short taste

"Glory be to God for all things." (His last words, from Palladius, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 26 September 2007)

"The goods we possess are not ours, but theirs." (On Lazarus 2, 5, as quoted in CCC 2446)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI gave two General Audiences on him, on 19 and 26 September 2007, for the sixteenth centenary of his death, and wrote a Letter on the same anniversary dated 10 August 2007. He calls him one of the great Fathers of the Church's social teaching, praises his care for the poor, his reform of the clergy, his teaching on marriage and on the laity, and his efforts for unity between East and West. He reminds theologians to return to the Fathers.

St Pius V counted him among the four great Doctors of the East in 1568. St Pius X named him patron of preachers in 1908. St John XXIII, Benedict XVI recalls, named him patron of the Second Vatican Council.

St John Paul II gave a large part of his relics, with those of St Gregory Nazianzen, to Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I on 27 November 2004.

The Catechism quotes him often: on creation (CCC 358), on forgiveness and the priesthood (CCC 983, 1551), on prayer for the dead (CCC 1032), on the Eucharist (CCC 1375, 1397), on marriage and virginity (CCC 1620, 2365), on the poor (CCC 2446), on envy (CCC 2538, 2540), on prayer (CCC 2700, 2743, 2744) and on the Our Father (CCC 2768, 2784).

His sermons against Judaizing Christians. In 386 and 387, as a young priest at Antioch, John preached eight sermons aimed at members of his own flock who went to the synagogue, kept the Jewish fasts and feasts, and sought cures there. He wanted them to hold fast to the Church. But in these sermons he spoke of the Jewish people and their worship in harsh and insulting language, in the fierce style of ancient rhetoric. In later centuries these words were used to feed contempt for Jews, with grievous results. Here a saint was not at his best, and the Church does not make those words her own. The Second Vatican Council taught that the Jews "should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God", and that the Church "decries hatred, persecutions, displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone" (Nostra Aetate 4). In 1998 the Holy See's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews admitted, in substance, that wrong and unjust readings of the New Testament about the Jewish people had circulated among Christians for too long, and that the Council had rejected them completely (We Remember: A Reflection on the Shoah, III). We honour John's holiness, his love of the poor and his courage, and we receive his teaching as the Church receives it, without repeating his harsh words about the people to whom God first spoke and whose gifts and calling are irrevocable (Romans 11:29).

Why he matters today

He shows that worship and justice cannot be separated. The one who receives Christ at the altar must meet him in the poor. Few have said this more forcefully, and his words still reach the comfortable.

He shows the power of the Word preached well. He took the Bible seriously, explained it plainly and applied it to real life. Preachers still learn from him.

He shows the cost of honesty. He would not flatter the powerful, and it cost him his see, his health and his life. Yet he died thanking God for everything.

He shows that families and lay people are at the heart of the Church: the home as a little Church, parents as the first teachers, every baptized person as priest, king and prophet.

And his own failure in the sermons at Antioch reminds us that even the saints spoke with the limits of their age, and that the Church, guided by the Spirit, grows in understanding and in repentance.

Sit with this

  1. Do I see Christ in the poor as truly as I believe he is present at the altar?
  2. What do I possess that, in Chrysostom's words, is not mine but theirs?
  3. Can I say, in what is happening to me now, "Glory be to God for all things"?
