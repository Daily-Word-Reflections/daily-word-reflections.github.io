St Ambrose of Milan

TimeBorn about 340 (some say 339, a few say 334 or 337); bishop from 374; died 4 April 397
WhereBorn most probably at Trier (Germany), where his father governed the Gauls; brought up in Rome (Italy); governor and then bishop at Milan (Italy)
What he wasRoman lawyer and governor, then Bishop of Milan; preacher, teacher of the newly baptized, writer of hymns, adviser and critic of emperors
LanguageLatin; he read Greek well
Known asThe bishop who baptized St Augustine; father of the Ambrosian rite and of Latin hymn singing
HonouredSaint. One of the four great Doctors of the Latin Church, so declared with St Jerome, St Augustine and St Gregory the Great by Pope Boniface VIII in 1298. Latin Church: 7 December, the day of his ordination as bishop (memorial). Byzantine tradition: 7 December. The Syro-Malabar lectionary has proper readings for St Ambrose on 7 December. Patron of Milan.
His life

A Roman family. Ambrose was born about 340, most probably at Trier on the Moselle, where his father, also called Ambrose, was Prefect of the Gauls. That office governed what is today France, Britain and Spain, with part of North Africa. Trier, Arles and Lyon have all claimed to be his birthplace. The family was Christian and noble, and counted a martyr, St Soteris, among its ancestors. He was the youngest of three children. His sister Marcellina was about ten years older, and his brother Satyrus was close to him all his life.

The bees. His secretary Paulinus, who wrote his Life at the suggestion of St Augustine, tells a story from his infancy. A swarm of bees settled on the baby's face in his cradle and went in and out of his mouth, then flew away high into the air. His father took it as a sign of future eloquence (Paulinus, Life). This is why Ambrose is often shown with a beehive and is a patron of beekeepers.

Rome. After his father's death, about 354, his mother took the children to Rome. There, Marcellina received the veil of a consecrated virgin from Pope Liberius, and she lived as a nun in the family house. Ambrose was well educated in literature, rhetoric and law, and he learned Greek, a rare gift in the Latin West of that time.

A rising official. He practised law at the court of the praetorian prefect Anicius Probus, who took him onto his council. In about 370 he was made consular governor of Aemilia and Liguria, with his seat at Milan, then one of the capitals of the Empire. According to Paulinus, Probus sent him off with the words "Go, and act not as a judge but as a bishop" (Paulinus, Life, in substance). His fair and gentle rule made him well loved.

"Ambrose, bishop!", 374. For nearly twenty years Milan had been ruled by Auxentius, a bishop on the Arian side. When he died in 374, the city was divided and an election could turn violent. Ambrose went to the church to keep order and spoke for peace. Paulinus says that a child's voice cried "Ambrose, bishop!", and the whole crowd took up the cry. He was still only a catechumen, not yet baptized, as many adults were then. He tried to escape the choice, even by strange means that Paulinus records, and he went into hiding. The Emperor Valentinian I confirmed the election, and Ambrose gave way. He was baptized by a Catholic bishop, passed through the orders, and was ordained bishop eight days later, on 7 December 374. This is his feast day. (Some historians place these events in 373.)

A bishop who had to learn. His first act was to give away his wealth. He gave his money to the poor and his lands to the Church, keeping provision for his sister. Satyrus left his own career to look after the household. Ambrose then threw himself into study, because, as he wrote, he had to learn and teach at the same time (On the Duties 1, 1, 4). He read Scripture with the help of Origen, St Basil and the Jewish writer Philo, and he was guided by the priest Simplician, who later succeeded him.

An open door. St Augustine, who knew him in 384 to 386, describes him. Crowds of people with troubles kept him busy. In the few moments left he ate a little or read, and he read silently, with his eyes alone, which surprised Augustine (Confessions 6, 3, 3). Paulinus adds that when sinners confessed to him, he wept with them and spoke of their sins to God alone (Life 39). He urged his people to receive the Eucharist often: "Receive daily what profits you daily" (On the Sacraments 5, 4, 25, in substance).

Satyrus. About 378 his brother Satyrus died. Ambrose preached two funeral sermons for him, among the earliest Christian works of consolation, full of grief and hope.

The captives. After the Roman defeat at Adrianople in 378, many people were taken captive by the Goths. Ambrose had the sacred vessels of the Church broken and melted down to ransom them. When criticized, he answered that it is better to keep living souls for the Lord than gold, and that the Church has gold "not to store up, but to lay out" for those in need (On the Duties 2, 28, 136 to 137, in substance).

Against the Arians in the West. At the request of the young Emperor Gratian he wrote On the Faith, on the divinity of the Son, and later On the Holy Spirit. He secured a Catholic bishop for Sirmium. In 381 he led the Council of Aquileia, which removed the last two Arian bishops of the region, Palladius and Secundianus.

The Altar of Victory, 384. The pagan senator Symmachus, Prefect of Rome, asked the young Emperor Valentinian II to restore the Altar of Victory to the Senate house. Ambrose replied in two letters. He argued that a Christian emperor should not set up an altar to pagan gods. The Emperor did not restore it.

The basilicas, 385 to 386. The Empress Justina, mother of Valentinian II and a supporter of the Arians, demanded a church in Milan for Arian worship. Ambrose refused: a bishop cannot hand over the temple of God. In Holy Week of 386 soldiers surrounded the basilica where he and his people stayed day and night. To keep them strong, he taught them to sing hymns and psalms in alternating choirs, "after the manner of the eastern regions", says Augustine (Confessions 9, 7, 15). This was the beginning of the great tradition of hymn singing in the West. Augustine was there, and his mother Monica was among the most watchful. In the end the court gave way.

The martyrs, June 386. Soon after, while preparing to dedicate a new basilica, Ambrose had the ground dug before the shrine of Saints Nabor and Felix and found the bodies of two martyrs, Gervasius and Protasius. Augustine and Ambrose both record healings, including that of a blind man known in the city (Confessions 9, 7, 16). The relics were placed under the altar of the new basilica, the one now called Sant'Ambrogio.

Augustine. The young African teacher of rhetoric had come to Milan in 384. Ambrose received him "as a father" (Confessions 5, 13, 23). Augustine listened at first only for the style, and slowly the content won him. At the Easter Vigil of 24 to 25 April 387 Ambrose baptized him, with his son Adeodatus and his friend Alypius. The old story that the two sang the Te Deum in turn at that baptism has no good foundation.

Envoy to a usurper. In 383 the general Maximus had the Emperor Gratian killed and took the West beyond the Alps. Ambrose went twice to his court at Trier on behalf of the young Valentinian II. On the second visit he refused communion with the bishops who had pressed for the execution of the heretic Priscillian, the first person put to death for heresy by a Christian ruler, and he was sent away.

Callinicum, 388. Christians at Callinicum on the Euphrates burned a synagogue. The Emperor Theodosius ordered the local bishop to rebuild it at his own cost. Ambrose protested strongly, in a letter and in a sermon before the Emperor, until the order was withdrawn. Historians, and many Catholic writers today, judge his stand here severely. The Church now teaches respect for the Jewish people and their worship (Nostra Aetate 4), and this episode is not part of what makes him a saint. It is told here honestly, as a wound and a warning.

Thessalonica, 390. After a riot at Thessalonica in which an imperial officer was killed, Theodosius ordered a punishment, and thousands of citizens were massacred in the circus; Theodoret gives seven thousand. Ambrose withdrew from Milan so as not to meet the Emperor, and wrote him a private letter "with my own hand", calling him to repentance and saying he would not offer the Sacrifice in his presence until he had done penance. Theodosius laid aside his royal robes and did public penance in church. Augustine praises his "religious humility" (City of God 5, 26). The later picture of Ambrose barring the doors of the cathedral comes from Theodoret and is thought to be much dramatized.

Last years. In May 392 the young Valentinian II died suddenly in Gaul, probably murdered, while Ambrose was on his way to baptize him. In his funeral sermon Ambrose taught that the desire for baptism had washed him. The usurper Eugenius favoured the pagan party. Ambrose left Milan rather than meet him. Theodosius defeated Eugenius in 394 and died at Milan in January 395, with Ambrose at his side. Ambrose preached his funeral sermon: "I loved him".

Death, 397. When Ambrose fell ill, the general Stilicho, fearing for Italy, sent leading citizens to beg him to pray for his life. Paulinus records his famous answer, which Augustine greatly admired: "I have not lived among you in such a way that I am ashamed to live; nor do I fear to die, because we have a good Lord" (Paulinus, Life 45; Possidius, Life of Augustine 27; in substance). On Good Friday afternoon he lay praying with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross. In the night Bishop Honoratus of Vercelli, roused by a voice, came down and gave him the Body of the Lord. Having received it, he died, before dawn on Holy Saturday, 4 April 397 (Paulinus, Life 47). He was not yet sixty. He was buried, as he wished, in his basilica beside Gervasius and Protasius. In 835 Bishop Angilbert II placed the three bodies together under the altar, where they were found in 1864. They rest in the crypt today.

The world he lived in

In the late fourth century Milan was an imperial capital. The court lived there; soldiers, officials, Gothic advisers and ambassadors filled the streets. Ambrose stood very close to power. He was a Roman of the governing class, and he knew how emperors thought.

The Empire had become Christian only recently. Constantine had ended persecution in 313. Pagan senators in Rome still kept the old rites. Theodosius would make Nicene Christianity the faith of the Empire in 380. In such a world it was new and hard to say what a bishop owed to the emperor and what the emperor owed to God. Ambrose put it simply: "The emperor is within the Church, not above the Church" (Sermon against Auxentius 36).

The Arian question was not over in the West. Many Goths in the army were Arian Christians, and the Empress Justina favoured them. Nicaea had confessed that the Son is of one being with the Father. Ambrose held to that faith and spent much of his life defending it.

It was also a time of disaster. The defeat at Adrianople in 378, where the Emperor Valens died, opened the Balkans to the Goths. Refugees and captives were everywhere. The Church had to feed, ransom and shelter people as well as preach.

Ambrose belonged to a generation of great bishops: St Basil in the East, with whom he corresponded, St Damasus and then Siricius in Rome, and St Martin in Gaul. Through his preaching the treasures of Greek Christian writing passed into Latin.

What he taught
  • "Christ is everything to us." His best-known lines: "Christ is everything to us. If you want to heal your wound, he is the doctor; if you are burning with fever, he is the spring; if you need help, he is strength; if you fear death, he is life; if you desire heaven, he is the way; if you are in darkness, he is light" (On Virginity 16, 99, in substance; quoted by Benedict XVI).
  • The Son is God. In On the Faith (378 to 380) he defends Nicaea: the Son is eternal, of the same being as the Father, not a creature. In On the Holy Spirit (381) he teaches, drawing on St Basil and Didymus, that the Spirit is God with the Father and the Son.
  • The Eucharist. His catecheses to the newly baptized teach clearly that the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ by the word of Christ. "Be convinced that this is not what nature has formed, but what the blessing has consecrated" (On the Mysteries 9, 50; quoted in CCC 1375). Christ's word, which could make what was not, can change what is.
  • Baptism and penance. In the Church, he says, "there are water and tears: the water of Baptism and the tears of repentance" (Letter 41, 12, quoted in CCC 1429). Against the Novatians, who said that grave sins after baptism could not be forgiven by the Church, his On Penance defends the Church's power to forgive all sins.
  • Mary, model of the Church. Mary is virgin and mother, as the Church is. He calls her a "type of the Church" (Commentary on Luke 2, 7), a teaching taken up by the Second Vatican Council (Lumen Gentium 63). He firmly taught her lasting virginity.
  • Where Peter is. "Where Peter is, there is the Church" (On Psalm 40, 30). He valued communion with the Church of Rome and spoke of it as the source of unity for all.
  • The goods of the earth. "You are not making a gift of what is yours to the poor; you are giving back something of what is his. For what was given for the use of all, you have taken for yourself alone" (On Naboth 12, 53, in substance). St Paul VI quoted this in Populorum Progressio 23. Ambrose taught that the earth was made for all.
  • Church and emperor. The emperor is a son of the Church. In matters of faith bishops judge emperors, not emperors bishops (Letter 21, in substance). He used this with courage, as at Thessalonica.
  • Virginity. He wrote more on consecrated virginity than any Latin writer before him. St Jerome wrote that in his books for his sister Ambrose had set out all that belongs to the praise of virgins (Jerome, Letter 22, 22, in substance). He also defended marriage as good and holy.
  • Reading Scripture. He taught people to see Christ in the whole Old Testament. The patriarchs are models of life, and the events of Israel point to Christ and the sacraments. Benedict XVI credits him with bringing lectio divina, prayerful reading of Scripture, into the Latin West.
How he taught

He preached first, and wrote later. Almost all his books began as sermons. Notes were taken as he spoke, and he later revised them into treatises. That is why they are lively and practical, full of exhortation.

He read Scripture in more than one sense. He respected the plain sense of the text, then looked for the deeper meaning that points to Christ and to Christian life. In this he learned from Origen and from St Basil, whose homilies on creation he adapted for his own Hexameron. St Jerome noted the debt.

He taught the newly baptized. In Easter week he explained to the new Christians what had happened to them: the opening of the ears, the anointing, the font, the white garments, the altar. These "mystagogical" catecheses remain models of how to teach the sacraments.

He made the people sing. His hymns are short, strong and clear: eight stanzas of four lines each, easy to remember. Augustine quotes four of them as his: Aeterne rerum conditor (Maker of all things), Deus creator omnium (God, creator of all), Iam surgit hora tertia (Now the third hour rises), and Intende qui regis Israel, often sung as Veni redemptor gentium (Come, Redeemer of the nations). Many later hymns written in his style are called "Ambrosian".

He taught by his life. Benedict XVI stresses that what moved Augustine was less the splendour of the sermons than the witness of a bishop and a Church that prayed and sang "as one", ready to die rather than give up their faith.

He adapted the best of the ancients. His On the Duties of Ministers follows the plan of Cicero's On Duties, but fills it with Scripture and with Christian aims: the virtues seen through the examples of the patriarchs and of Christ.

His works

On the Old Testament

Hexameron, six books of sermons on the six days of creation, after St Basil.
On Paradise; On Cain and Abel; On Noah; On Abraham, two books; On Isaac, or the Soul; On the Good of Death; On Jacob and the Happy Life; On Joseph; On the Patriarchs; On Flight from the World.
On Elijah and Fasting; On Naboth, on greed; On Tobit, against usury; On the Plea of Job and David; Apology of the Prophet David (a second apology is doubtful).

Psalms and Gospel

Commentaries on Twelve Psalms (1, 35 to 40, 43, 45, 47, 48, 61).
On Psalm 118 (119), twenty-two sermons on the long psalm.
Commentary on the Gospel of Luke, ten books, his only surviving New Testament work.

Doctrine and sacraments

On the Faith, five books, to the Emperor Gratian.
On the Holy Spirit, three books.
On the Mystery of the Lord's Incarnation.
On the Mysteries, to the newly baptized.
On the Sacraments, six books, notes of his Easter sermons; once doubted, now generally accepted as his.
On Penance, two books, against the Novatians.
Explanation of the Creed, a short talk, generally accepted.

Moral and on virginity

On the Duties of Ministers, three books.
On Virgins, three books, to his sister Marcellina.
On Virginity; On Widows; On the Training of a Virgin; Exhortation to Virginity.
On the Fall of a Consecrated Virgin: its authorship is debated.

Sermons for occasions

On the Death of his Brother Satyrus, two books.
On the Death of Valentinian (392) and On the Death of Theodosius (395).
Sermon against Auxentius, on not handing over the basilicas (386).

Letters and hymns

Letters: about ninety survive, mostly official, in ten books with others outside the collection. They are a main source for the history of his time.
Hymns: four are certainly his (named above); some others are probably his; many more are only in his style. The Te Deum is not his, though it was long called "the Ambrosian hymn"; many scholars link it with Niceta of Remesiana.

Lost, and not his

Lost: On the Sacrament of Rebirth, on baptism, quoted by Augustine; and a book On Philosophy, against those who said Christ learned from Plato. Some scholars think these two were one work.
Not his: the commentary on Paul's letters known as "Ambrosiaster"; the Te Deum. A Latin version of Josephus called "Hegesippus" is sometimes linked to him, without certainty.

A short taste

"Omnia Christus est nobis! To us Christ is all!" (On Virginity 16, 99, as quoted by Benedict XVI, General Audience, 24 October 2007)

"Be convinced that this is not what nature has formed, but what the blessing has consecrated." (On the Mysteries 9, 50, as quoted in the Catechism, CCC 1375)

What the Popes and the Church say of him

Benedict XVI spoke of him at the General Audience of 24 October 2007. He began with Ambrose's death, arms stretched out like the cross, and called it his "last catechesis". He showed him as the one who brought lectio divina to the Latin West, and as the bishop whose life, more than his words, opened the way for Augustine. He ended with "Omnia Christus est nobis!"

St John Paul II wrote the Apostolic Letter Operosam diem on 1 December 1996 for the sixteenth centenary of his death, at the request of Cardinal Carlo Maria Martini of Milan, who opened an Ambrosian Year.

St Paul VI quoted his words on the poor in Populorum Progressio 23 (1967).

Boniface VIII named him with Jerome, Augustine and Gregory as the four great Doctors in 1298. His statue, with Augustine, Athanasius and John Chrysostom, holds up the Chair of Peter in St Peter's Basilica, Bernini's work.

The Second Vatican Council quotes him on Mary and the Church (Lumen Gentium 63). The Catechism quotes him many times, for example on the Eucharist (CCC 1375) and on repentance (CCC 1429).

The Ambrosian rite. The Archdiocese of Milan keeps its own Latin liturgy, named after him. It is still used in most of the archdiocese and some neighbouring areas. How much of it goes back to Ambrose himself is uncertain, but it bears his name and his hymns.

His honest critics. He is not admired for everything. His stand at Callinicum is rightly criticized. Some of his sharp words against heretics and pagans belong to his age. The Church honours him for his faith, his courage for the poor and the captive, his care of souls and his holiness of life.

Why he matters today

He shows that faith can make a public man a pastor. Ambrose did not stop being a Roman of great ability when he became bishop; he put all of it at the service of his people.

He spoke truth to power, privately first and then openly, and he called even an emperor to penance. Yet he did it so that the man could be healed, and he loved Theodosius to the end.

He reminds us that church gold is for people. In a world of refugees and captives, his melted chalices still preach.

He taught people to sing their faith. Every hymn sung in church owes him something.

And he is a teacher of the sacraments. His Easter sermons can still be read slowly by anyone preparing for baptism, confirmation or first communion, or by any Christian who wants to know what happened at their own baptism.

Sit with this

  1. If Christ is "everything to us", what in my life would I name him today: doctor, spring, strength, life, way or light?
  2. Is there anything in my house or my parish that I keep "to store up" which should be "laid out" for those in need?
  3. Do I read Scripture so that it shapes how I live, as Ambrose taught his catechumens?
