Faith and Revelation: sixteen questions

How do we know God at all? What has he told us, where do we find it, and what does it mean to believe? Short answers in plain language, each with the paragraph of the Catechism of the Catholic Church where you can read further.

Why do people search for God? CCC 27

Because God made us for himself. The desire for God is written in every human heart, and God never stops drawing us to himself. Only in God do we find the truth and happiness we keep looking for.

Can we know God by reason alone? CCC 36–38

Yes. The Church teaches that God can be known with certainty from the created world by the natural light of human reason. But in practice our minds meet many obstacles, so we need God to show us even some things that reason could reach. Above all we need him to show us what reason could never find by itself.

What is Revelation? CCC 50–51

Revelation is God making himself known. Out of pure goodness he has shown us who he is and his loving plan for us: that through Christ, the Word made flesh, we may come to the Father in the Holy Spirit and share in the divine nature.

How did God reveal himself? CCC 53–64

Step by step, by deeds and words that belong together. He spoke to our first parents, made a covenant with Noah, called Abraham, and formed Israel as his people through Moses and the prophets. In this way he prepared humanity, like a patient teacher, for the coming of his Son.

Is anything still to come after Christ? CCC 65–66

No new public revelation is to be expected before Christ returns in glory. Christ is the Father's one, perfect and final Word; in him God has said everything. Yet the Church keeps growing in her understanding of this Word down the centuries.

What about private revelations, such as apparitions? CCC 67

Some private revelations have been recognised by the Church, but they do not belong to the deposit of faith. They do not improve or complete what Christ revealed. At best they help people live it more fully in a certain time, and no Catholic is obliged to believe them.

How has Revelation come down to us? CCC 75–83

Christ entrusted his Gospel to the Apostles, who handed it on by their preaching and example and in writing. It reaches us in two ways that flow from one source: Sacred Tradition and Sacred Scripture. Both are received and honoured with the same devotion and reverence.

Who explains the Word of God with authority? CCC 85–86, 95

The living teaching office of the Church, the Magisterium: the bishops in communion with the successor of Peter, the Bishop of Rome. The Magisterium is not above the Word of God but serves it, and teaches only what has been handed on. Tradition, Scripture and the Magisterium are so joined that none of them stands without the others.

Who wrote the Bible? CCC 105–107

God is its author, and so are the human writers. God inspired men who used their own powers and gifts as true authors, so that they wrote what he wanted written. For this reason the books of Scripture teach firmly, faithfully and without error the truth God wished to give us for our salvation.

How should we read Scripture? CCC 109–114

First find what the human writer meant, paying attention to his time, his culture and the kind of writing he used. Then read it in the Spirit in which it was written, in three ways: keep in view the unity of the whole Bible, read it within the living Tradition of the Church, and see how each truth of faith fits with the others.

What are the senses of Scripture? CCC 115–118

There is the literal sense, the meaning of the words, on which all the others rest. Then there is the spiritual sense in three forms: allegorical (how an event points to Christ, as the crossing of the Red Sea points to Baptism), moral (how it guides our life) and anagogical (how it points to heaven).

How many books are in the Bible? CCC 120–123

Seventy-three. The Church discerned the list, called the canon, by the apostolic Tradition: forty-six books of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. The Old Testament is God's Word too, and Christians honour it.

What is faith? CCC 142–144, 150

Faith is our answer to God who speaks. It is a personal bond with God and, together with it, an assent to the whole truth he has revealed. Abraham is the model of this obedience of faith, and the Virgin Mary its most perfect example.

Is faith a gift or a human act? CCC 153–155, 160

Both. Faith is a gift of God, a virtue he pours into us, and we can believe only with the help of his grace. Yet believing is truly human and goes against neither our reason nor our liberty. No one may be forced to embrace the faith.

Can faith and science contradict each other? CCC 159

No real contradiction is possible, because the same God reveals the mysteries of faith and gives us the light of reason. Truth cannot contradict truth. Honest research, done in a truly scientific way and with respect for moral laws, never truly conflicts with faith.

Why do we say both "I believe" and "We believe"? CCC 166–167

Faith is personal, but no one believes alone. We received the faith from others and must hand it on. "I believe" is the faith of the Church professed by each person, above all at Baptism; "We believe" is the faith professed by the bishops in council and by the assembly gathered in worship.

Sit with this

  1. God spoke first, and faith is my answer. Where is he speaking to me now?
  2. Do I read Scripture as a letter from someone who loves me?
  3. Who handed the faith on to me, and to whom am I handing it on?
