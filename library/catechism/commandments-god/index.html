The Ten Commandments, 1 to 3: love of God

The first three commandments tell us how to love God with all our heart, soul and mind: to worship him alone, to honour his name, and to keep his day holy. The commandments are given in Exodus 20:1 to 17 and Deuteronomy 5:6 to 21. Each answer points to the paragraph of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, so you can read further.

Do the Ten Commandments still bind Christians? CCC 2053, 2068, 2072

Yes. When the rich young man asked what he must do, Jesus first told him to keep the commandments, and then invited him to follow him. Following Christ does not abolish the commandments; it fulfils them. The Council of Trent taught that Christians, even when justified, are still bound to keep them. Because they express our basic duties to God and neighbour, they are unchanging and oblige always and everywhere; they are engraved by God in the human heart.

Why are they split into three and seven? CCC 2055, 2066–2067

Jesus summed up the whole Law in two commandments: love God, and love your neighbour as yourself. The first three commandments concern love of God and the other seven love of neighbour, just as the Law was given on two tablets. The Catechism follows the numbering of St Augustine, which became traditional in the Catholic Church. The Greek Fathers divided them slightly differently, and that division is found in the Orthodox Churches and Reformed communities; the content is the same.

What does the first commandment ask of us? CCC 2084, 2086–2087, 2093

"I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery; you shall have no other gods before me" (Exodus 20:2 to 3). God first reminds his people that he brought them out of slavery, and then asks to be served alone. The first commandment embraces faith, hope and charity: we believe God, trust him, and love him above everything and all creatures for his sake. Our first duty toward God is to believe in him and bear witness to him.

How can we sin against faith, hope and love? CCC 2088–2094

Against faith: wilful doubt, neglect of revealed truth, heresy, apostasy and schism. Against hope: despair, which stops hoping for God's forgiveness and help, and presumption, which hopes to be saved without God's help or forgiven without conversion. Against love: indifference, ingratitude, lukewarmness, spiritual sloth (acedia), and hatred of God, which comes from pride. Involuntary difficulty in believing is not the same as wilful doubt.

How do we worship God? CCC 2096–2102

By adoration, the first act of the virtue of religion: acknowledging God as Creator and Saviour, as Mary did in the Magnificat. By prayer, without which we cannot keep the commandments. By sacrifice, which is true only when it comes from the heart; the only perfect sacrifice is Christ's on the Cross, and we unite our own offering to it. And by keeping the promises and vows we make to God, as in Baptism, Confirmation, Marriage and Holy Orders.

What is superstition? CCC 2110–2111

Superstition is a distortion of religious feeling. It can even creep into the worship of the true God, when we give a kind of magical power to certain practices, as if prayers or sacramental signs worked by their outward performance alone, without the inner dispositions they require. Faith trusts a Person, not a technique.

Is idolatry only a thing of the past? CCC 2112–2114

No. Idolatry means treating as God what is not God. Besides worship of other gods or of demons, a person can make an idol of power, pleasure, race, ancestors, the state or money. Jesus said that no one can serve God and wealth (Matthew 6:24). Worship of the one God gathers our life into unity; idolatry scatters it.

Are horoscopes, fortune-telling and charms wrong? CCC 2115–2117

Yes. A Christian trusts the future to God's providence and gives up unhealthy curiosity about it. The Catechism rejects all forms of divination, including horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, omens and lots, clairvoyance and mediums, because they seek power over time and over others. Magic and sorcery, which try to tame hidden powers, are gravely contrary to the virtue of religion, even when used to restore health. The Catechism also calls the wearing of charms reprehensible. This does not forbid sensible planning or medicine.

What are tempting God, sacrilege and simony? CCC 2118–2122

Tempting God means putting his goodness or power to the test, as Satan tried to make Jesus do. Sacrilege is profaning or treating unworthily the sacraments and other liturgical actions, or persons, things and places consecrated to God; it is especially grave against the Eucharist. Simony is buying or selling spiritual things. No one should be refused the sacraments because of poverty.

What does the Church say about atheism and agnosticism? CCC 2123–2128

Atheism, which denies God, is a sin against the virtue of religion, though a person's guilt can be much lessened by intentions and circumstances. The Second Vatican Council admits that believers can share responsibility for atheism when they neglect their faith or hide the face of God by their lives. Agnosticism takes many forms. It can include a real search for God, but it can also become indifference or practical atheism.

Does the first commandment forbid images and icons? CCC 2129–2132

The commandment forbade making images of God, who had shown no form. Yet God himself allowed images that pointed to salvation, such as the bronze serpent and the cherubim. Because the Son of God became man and was seen, the Second Council of Nicaea in 787 defended the veneration of icons of Christ, the Mother of God, the angels and the saints. The honour given to an image passes to the person it shows. It is respectful veneration, not the adoration due to God alone.

What about people of other religions? CCC 2104–2106

Everyone is bound to seek the truth about God and to hold to it once found. This goes together with sincere respect for other religions, which often reflect a ray of the truth that enlightens all people. No one may be forced to act against conscience in religious matters, nor prevented from acting according to it, in private or in public, within due limits. This right rests on the dignity of the human person.

What does the second commandment ask? CCC 2142–2149

"You shall not make wrongful use of the name of the Lord your God" (Exodus 20:7). God's name is holy, and we keep it in loving adoration. We bear witness to it by confessing our faith without fear. The commandment forbids every improper use of the names of God, Jesus Christ, the Virgin Mary and the saints. Blasphemy, speaking against God with hatred, reproach or defiance, is directly opposed to it. Breaking a promise made in God's name also misuses it.

May a Christian take an oath? CCC 2150–2155

An oath calls God to witness that what we say is true. A false oath, or perjury, makes God a witness to a lie and is a grave lack of respect for him. Jesus taught his disciples to let their yes be yes and their no be no (Matthew 5:33 to 37). The Church has understood this as not excluding oaths for grave and right reasons, such as in court. An oath may be refused when an unjust authority demands it, and must be refused when it serves purposes contrary to human dignity.

Why is a Christian's name important? CCC 2156–2159

Baptism is given in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and there the Christian receives his name in the Church, often the name of a saint who becomes a model and a helper in prayer. We begin our day and our prayers with the Sign of the Cross. God calls each one by name, and every person's name deserves respect, because it stands for the person.

What does the third commandment ask, and why Sunday? CCC 2168–2176, 2180–2186

"Remember the sabbath day, and keep it holy" (Exodus 20:8). God gave Israel the sabbath as a day of rest and of remembrance of creation and of freedom from Egypt. Jesus rose on the first day of the week, so for Christians Sunday, the Lord's Day, fulfils the sabbath and announces our eternal rest in God. On Sundays and holy days of obligation the faithful take part in the Eucharist and avoid work that hinders worship, joy, works of mercy and rest. In the Eastern Catholic Churches the faithful keep this day by the Divine Liturgy, called the Holy Qurbana in the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches, or, where their own Church provides, by the celebration of the divine praises (CCEO can. 881).

Sit with this

  1. What in my life competes with God for first place: money, approval, comfort, a person?
  2. Do I trust the future to God, or do I look for control through luck, charms or worry?
  3. How do I speak God's name and Mary's name, at home, at work, when I am angry?
  4. What would make next Sunday truly the Lord's Day for me and my family?
