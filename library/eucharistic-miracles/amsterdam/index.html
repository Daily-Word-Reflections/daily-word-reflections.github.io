Eucharistic miracles · Netherlands · 1345

Amsterdam: the Host in the Fire and the Silent Walk

Church's position The bishop of the time accepted the devotion early. Jan van Arkel, Bishop of Utrecht, granted a 40-day indulgence to visitors on 19 October 1346, and a charter of his auxiliary bishop dated 30 November 1346 speaks of the Host. Rome later granted indulgences, including a plenary indulgence on 23 June 1844 for the fifth centenary and an indulgence for the Silent Walk on 13 January 1917. The Bishop of Haarlem approved the statutes of the Walk on 24 December 1917. There is no modern declaration that the event was supernatural, and no one is obliged to believe it. The Host itself no longer exists.
  • Place today The old chapel stood between Kalverstraat and Rokin and is gone. The devotion lives at the Begijnhof Chapel, Amsterdam, and in the streets on the night of the Walk.
  • Date 15 March 1345, in the last days of Lent
  • What is kept No Host. A wooden chest linked to the miracle, old paintings and documents; a column of the chapel stands on the Rokin.
  • Feast The Miracle Feast begins on the Wednesday after 12 March, unless Easter is early. The Silent Walk (Stille Omgang) is on the following night of Saturday to Sunday.

The account

A sick man and a hearth

Amsterdam in 1345 was a young town of traders on the Amstel. Its parish church was St Nicholas, today called the Oude Kerk, the Old Church.

According to tradition, a man lay gravely ill in a house on the Kalverstraat and feared he would die. He sent for a priest, who brought him Holy Communion as viaticum, the food for the last journey. After the priest had gone, the man was sick and vomited. The rule of the time was that such remains be burned out of reverence, so they were thrown on the fire in his hearth. Sources do not agree on whether it was his wife, his caretaker or a maid who did it.

The next morning, the woman raking the fire found the Host unharmed in the ashes. A later version says it hung in the air among the flames, and that she took it out with her bare hands.

The Host that came back

She put it in a small chest and took it to the priest at St Nicholas. The next day, the story goes, it was back at the house. This happened again, three times in all. Then the priest and clergy carried it to the church in solemn procession. People took the returns as a sign that the house itself should be a holy place. That is how the procession came to be remembered, and how later generations understood the route from the Old Church to the Kalverstraat.

A Dutch reference says that the returning Host is not in the oldest text and was added over time.

Who was the man?

Many devotional books call him Ysbrand Dommer, and one recent article even gives his age as 65. The scholar Peter Jan Margry, who wrote the standard work and the entry in the Dutch national pilgrimage database, says the man's identity is unknown. He rejects the name, which goes back to a suggestion by the nineteenth-century writer J. A. Alberdingk Thijm.

The earliest witnesses

The documents here are unusually early for a story of this kind, which is one reason historians take the beginnings of the cult seriously, whatever they think of the event.

31 March 1345The bailiff of Amstelland and the city council confirm the story, according to the Meertens entry. Devotional accounts place a report on Easter Sunday, which that year fell on 27 March.

1378A charter of Count Albrecht of Holland gives the oldest version of the story itself: a gravely ill man vomited the Host into the hearth, and it was not consumed by the fire.

What is known

A chapel rose on the Kalverstraat within two years. The bishop gave an indulgence. By 1378 the story was in a count's charter. For more than two centuries this was one of the great pilgrim places of the Low Countries. All this is documented. The Walk's own history since 1881 is fully documented too, with figures year by year.

What is less certain

The event rests on the telling. Some medieval sources give 1342, but R. Post argued in 1955 that 1345 is nearly certain. Some devotional accounts say 12 or 13 March, or 16 March, against the usual 15 March. The name of the man is not known. The return of the Host, the Host hanging in the fire and a cure of the sick man come from later tellings. One account says a Pope confirmed the veneration in 1504 without naming him, and a Dutch reference mentions a jubilee letter of John Paul II in 2004. Neither has been confirmed from a primary text.

There can be no scientific test here, because the Host has been gone since the sixteenth century.

The history after 1345

The Holy Place, 1346 to 1578

The house was made into a chapel called the Heilige Stede, the Holy Place. A road from the village of Sloten was laid out for pilgrims and is still called the Heiligeweg, the Holy Way. Fire was a danger in a timber city. The chapel burned in the city fire of 13 April 1421, was rebuilt, and burned in part on 25 May 1452, in the worst fire in Amsterdam's history. Popular accounts say the Host was again found unharmed. A third chapel was in use by 1456 and was enlarged in 1457 and again about 1498.

The feast was kept on the Wednesday after 12 March, the old day of St Gregory, and at Corpus Christi. A market ran for two weeks around the March feast. Pilgrims bought small badges to pin on as proof of their journey. For the Holy Year of 1500, people of Holland and Zeeland could gain the Roman jubilee indulgence at the chapel. Historians think the pilgrims helped make the young town rich and well known; it became a city known as a place of miracle as well as of trade.

Maximilian of Austria came in 1484. According to the stories he had been ill and had vowed the pilgrimage if he recovered. In 1489 he allowed Amsterdam to bear a crown over its arms, and the imperial crown is still seen there today, on the tower of the Westerkerk. One modern writer points out that the city's loans for his wars may have counted as much as his gratitude. Later his grandson, the Emperor Charles V, is named among the pilgrims. The Reformation and the Alteration of 1578

In the sixteenth century this shared devotion became a dividing one, as Charles Caspers shows. In the image-breaking of 1566, accounts differ. One says the chapel was badly damaged. Another says it was spared because women defended it. The standard history discusses the crucial part the community of sisters played in protecting the Holy Place.

In May 1578 came the change Dutch history calls the Alteration: the Catholic city government was replaced by a Protestant one. The chapel was stormed on 29 May. The last Host kept there was probably lost at about that time. Ash from the fire of 1345 was still there in 1578, according to the diary of Brother Wouter Jacobsz, who lived through those years. The public procession was banned and public Catholic worship ended.

The chapel passed to the Reformed Church and became the Nieuwezijds Kapel, the Chapel of the New Side. In 1624 the chapel master was ordered to remove the hearth. From 1579 the chest linked to the miracle was held at the Burgerweeshuis, the city orphanage, and the historian Jan Wagenaar traced it there in 1762.

Hidden and remembered, 1578 to 1800

Catholics in the Dutch Republic worshipped in hidden churches, within limits the authorities tolerated. Among the beguines of the Begijnhof the devotion found a home. Their courtyard kept its Catholic life, and from about the second quarter of the seventeenth century the chapel of Sts John and Ursula there became the centre of the devotion.

Pastor Leonardus Marius, there from 1631, was its great promoter, with his Amstelredams eer ende opcomen (1639) and a book of devotions, Getyden (1640). In 1645 the poet Joost van den Vondel, a Catholic convert and the greatest name in Dutch letters, wrote for the third centenary. In 1665 a larger hidden church was made from the sacristy and a nearby house. One devotional account says a monstrance moved there was stolen soon after.

Others kept the old road alive in another way. Catholics walked the former procession route privately and in silence, on their own, through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. On 16 December 1651 a woman of 91, Agatha Loen, stated before a priest the route she remembered from before the Alteration: Kalverstraat, Dam, Nieuwendijk, Ramskooi, Nieuwebrug, Warmoesstraat, Nes, Langebrug, and back. That statement is why the old way could be found again. Jubilees were kept in 1745 as well.

Revival and the demolition of the chapel

Catholics regained the right to worship openly in 1795, but the constitution of 1848 kept a ban on outdoor processions. The ban stood in law until the constitutional revision of 1983.

In 1840 the Begijnhof pastor N. J. A. Steins Bisschop began to revive the cult, and he obtained a plenary indulgence for the fifth centenary of 1845. A. J. Pluym published the first scholarly study that year, in a war of pamphlets between Catholic and Protestant writers. In 1871, 14,000 Hosts were given out on the feast. Rector B. H. Klönne led a further revival from the 1880s and founded the Miracle Guild, about 700 strong, in 1892.

The old chapel stood until the twentieth century, used for Reformed worship. Its Reformed owners decided to sell the land, and demolition began in 1908, despite protests from Catholics and from people of culture. A new Protestant chapel of 1912 and shops took its place. To Catholics the loss was painful, and it is remembered so. A sculpted stone now marks the place, and in 1988 a column of the old chapel was raised on the Rokin; it was put up again in April 2018 after metro works.

The Silent Walk

How it began, 1881

In 1880 a Catholic of Amsterdam, Joseph Lousbergh, came by an old text describing the medieval route. With his friend Carel Elsenburg he decided, in the Moses and Aaron Church in 1881, to walk it every year in silent pilgrimage. Because a procession was against the law, they carried nothing and said nothing aloud. There were no banners, no vestments and no hymns. A walk like that broke no rule.

It began very small: twelve walkers in 1885. Adriaan Apol joined in 1884 and Henri de Veer in 1889. Rector Klönne gave open support in 1887. The first group from outside came from Haarlem in 1886, and the name "stille omgang" came into use in 1892. Lousbergh died in 1914. A formal board followed in February 1915. Elsenburg's son, grandson and great-grandson all later chaired the society. Margry describes it as a re-enactment reduced to the barest form, in keeping with the shy and careful Catholicism of the Netherlands, yet also a quiet stage for Catholics seeking their place in Dutch life.

The great years and the decline

Its growth surprised everyone. Local societies, as many as about 300 across the century, organised pilgrimages from towns and villages all over the country. For decades the night walk was for men, and women had a separate walk early in the morning. Women were admitted to the night walk only in 1965.

No walk was held from 1941 to 1945, under the German occupation. Amsterdam hosted the 27th International Eucharistic Congress in 1924. For the sixth centenary, kept in 1946, about 56,000 people gathered in the Olympic Stadium.

From the 1960s the numbers fell sharply, in step with the changes in Dutch Catholic life. In the 1980s a new chairman stressed the spiritual and ecumenical side of the Walk, and a policy paper was issued in 1985. The jubilee of 1995 had a strong ecumenical character. The Meertens entry now speaks of a tradition in transition. In 2024 one group walked all the way from Utrecht.

In 2005 another step came. For the 150th year of Our Lady's Church on the Keizersgracht, a public Sacrament procession went over the canals to the Begijnhof, the first of its kind in the city since 1578. Over a thousand joined the next year's procession, led by Bishop Jozef Punt of Haarlem. The silence of the Walk has remained as well.

How it is walked today

Pilgrims first go to Mass in one of the city's churches, and the Blessed Sacrament is exposed in several places through the night. The Archdiocese of Utrecht, for example, begins with Mass in the Basilica of St Nicholas near Central Station, followed by silent adoration. Then they walk, group after group through the night, for about an hour.

The route starts at the Spui and goes along the Kalverstraat and the Nieuwendijk to the Prins Hendrikkade, back by the Warmoesstraat and the Nes, then by the Langebrugsteeg, and past the place of the old chapel. It passes the nightlife of the old centre. No one prays aloud or sings, and no one wears vestments or carries a religious sign. Five points on the route mark a new intention for prayer. The city customarily asks bars along the way to close their curtains and keep the music down.

There is something moving in this picture: people walking in silence past Saturday night in the middle of a great city, because a Host was once found in the ashes of a house. Each year has its intention. In 2026 it was that God may reach many through the Eucharist and that we may not reject him.

How the Church has honoured it

  • Bishops of Utrecht. The indulgence of 1346, and a charter of an auxiliary bishop the same year. One city account names the auxiliary as Nythardus.
  • Rome in 1500. The jubilee indulgence available at the chapel to people of Holland and Zeeland.
  • Rome in 1844 and 1917. A plenary indulgence for the fifth centenary, and an indulgence for the Walk.
  • Local bishops. Haarlem approved the Walk's statutes in 1917. In 2025 the Basilica of St Nicholas, on the Walk's route, became a co-cathedral for the Diocese of Haarlem-Amsterdam under Bishop Jan Hendriks. Bishops regularly take part in the night.

The fruits

  • A city's memory. The miracle has a window in the official Canon of Amsterdam. The Heiligeweg and Mirakelbrug keep the name. The city's 750th year, in 2025, recalled it again.
  • Art. Jacob Cornelisz van Oostsanen's woodcut (1518) and his painted panels, now in fragments at the Amsterdam Museum (between 1505 and 1518), a painting of about 1555, and many prints.
  • Literature. Vondel's jubilee poem of 1645. Anton van Duinkerken's verses for the sixth centenary. J.-K. Huysmans also wrote about the Walk.
  • A way of being Catholic. Margry argues that the quiet, careful walk shaped a Dutch way of living the faith among others, and even inspired later silent marches of mourning in the Netherlands. A reviewer of his book adds a caution, that ordinary daily life between Catholics and Protestants was often peaceful, and the conflicts should not be made to tell the whole story.

If you visit

The Begijnhof is a quiet courtyard off the Spui in the centre of Amsterdam. Its Catholic chapel keeps the devotion, with daily devotional Masses in Miracle Week, Wednesday to Saturday. Jacob Cornelisz's panels belong to the Amsterdam Museum; ask whether they are on show. The column stands on the Rokin. To walk, come to Amsterdam on the Saturday night after the Miracle Feast begins; the 2026 walk fell on 21 March. Trains are recommended over cars. Registration and dates are given each year by the society at stille-omgang.nl and by Dutch dioceses.

What the Church teaches

Our faith rests on Christ's own words and not on miracles. At every Mass and every Qurbana, Christ is truly present in the Eucharist, with or without any sign (CCC 1374 to 1376). Amsterdam has a lesson of its own here. The Host of 1345 has been gone for centuries, and people still walk, because the Christ they honour was never in that one Host alone. He is on the altar of the next church they pass. No one is obliged to believe this event, and walking in silence does not require it.
