Eucharistic miracles · Spain · about the 14th century

O Cebreiro

Church's position Long veneration in the church where the event is said to have happened. Papal bulls of Innocent VIII (1487) and Alexander VI (1496) are named in connection with O Cebreiro. Most sources tie them to the transfer of the monastery to San Benito of Valladolid. One historian writes that the miracle received papal approval in 1487, but the text of the bull has not been checked. No modern judgment by the Holy See was found. No Catholic is obliged to believe it.
  • Place today O Cebreiro, Pedrafita do Cebreiro, province of Lugo, Galicia, Spain; on the Camino Francés
  • Date no year was recorded; tradition says about 1300 or the 14th century
  • Kept chalice and paten, and the reliquary given by the Catholic Monarchs, in the church of Santa María a Real
  • Days relics carried on Corpus Christi, 15 August and 8 September; pilgrimage of Os Milagros on 8 and 9 September

The account

A monastery on a mountain pass

O Cebreiro stands at about 1,293 metres, where the pilgrim road to Santiago enters Galicia. It is the first Galician village on the French Way. It is known for its snow, wind and fog, and for the old round thatched houses called pallozas. Medieval hospices were often built at just such hard points on the road.

The church of Santa María is very old. Its beginning is usually put in the ninth century, and one tradition gives 836. The monastery is mentioned with more certainty in 883, in a privilege of King Alfonso III. In 1072 King Alfonso VI granted the house and its hospital to monks from the abbey of St Gerald at Aurillac in France. Its documents are few, since fire and war destroyed much of them. Fires are recorded in 1450 and 1641, and the buildings were burned in the Peninsular War.

The winter Mass, according to tradition

The story that follows is the one told at O Cebreiro today. On a bitter winter day, snow was falling hard and the wind was almost beyond bearing. A Benedictine priest went to say Mass in the side chapel, thinking no one would come. His faith had grown cold, and he no longer truly believed that Christ was present in the Sacrament.

One man did come. A peasant from the hamlet of Barxamaior climbed the mountain through the storm. The tradition calls him Juan Santín. The priest looked down on him in his heart. In the local telling, he thought or said that it was a great deal of trouble for a little bread and wine.

At the consecration, the Host became visible flesh on the paten, and the wine became blood in the chalice, spilling over and staining the corporal. The priest's eyes were opened. The peasant's faith was rewarded.

A second detail has been loved at O Cebreiro ever since. The wooden image of Our Lady in the church is said to have bowed her head at that moment, and to have stayed so. People call her the Virgin of the Holy Miracle. The carving is Romanesque, and it has been restored and much altered.

The priest and Juan Santín are said to lie buried in the chapel, side by side. Their names and the location of their tombs are a matter of tradition.

When did it happen?

No one knows. The sources give the winter of 1300, about 1300, the early fourteenth century, the fourteenth century, and even the early thirteenth. No year was ever recorded, and all of these are later estimates. One Spanish article places the priest in the fourteenth century with a question mark, and that question mark is honest.

The earliest written witnesses

According to the historian Mathew Kuefler, the first recorded mention of the miracle is tied to the visit of King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella in 1486. That is about 180 years after the usual date. Queen Isabella and the king were on pilgrimage to Compostela, heard the story, and left a precious reliquary of glass. The sources call it either a reliquary or two glass vessels. They found the house in decline, and arranged for it to pass from Aurillac to the Benedictines of San Benito at Valladolid.

The bulls of 1487 and 1496 followed. Then, in the early seventeenth century, the Benedictine chronicler Antonio de Yepes wrote of the two small vessels, one with blood and one with flesh, and said he had himself "visto y adorado", seen and adored, this holy mystery. Yepes is the classic written account. He is a witness to what the church held and venerated in his day, and not to the event.

What is known

The monastery is documented from 883, and was given to Aurillac in 1072. The Catholic Monarchs came in 1486. Yepes saw and venerated the relics in the early seventeenth century. The chalice and paten are real medieval pieces, dated to the twelfth century, and the chalice bears two Latin inscriptions. Cult and procession continue today.

What is less certain

The year of the event, the names of priest and peasant, and the bowing of the statue all rest on later tradition. There is no witness from the time. If the event happened about 1300, a chalice from the twelfth century could well have been in use then, so its age does not decide the matter. Kuefler doubts the event took place at all. He sees its shape as close to the story of Bolsena. That is one scholar's reading, and other Catholic writers take the tradition as well founded. No scientific test of the relics was found.

The chalice and the "Holy Grail of Galicia"

Travel writers often call the chalice "the Holy Grail of Galicia". It is not the cup of the Last Supper, and no one should think so. It is a twelfth-century Romanesque chalice. Its two inscriptions are read by Kuefler as "In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and of the Blessed Virgin Mary" and "By this covenant is consecrated that through which life is obtained for all".

Kuefler offers an attractive explanation of how the name came about. The monks' patron was St Gerald of Aurillac, a nobleman and layman of the early tenth century, known for treating his peasants justly. In the Occitan speech of Aurillac he was Sant Guiral, and in Galicia Sant Gral. In Galician the Grail is Sant Grial. Pilgrims shown a chalice in a church linked with "Sant Gral", a saint they did not know, could easily have made the mistake. This is a proposal and not a proof.

The written link between the Grail and O Cebreiro is modern. Kuefler finds it first in the Galician poet Ramón Cabanillas, in his poem "O cabaleiro do Sant Grial" of 1926. Tourist pages often say that the miracle inspired Wagner's opera Parsifal of 1882. No evidence for this was found.

The chalice and the arms of Galicia

The arms of Galicia show a gold chalice and a silver Host. Popular accounts say the arms come from O Cebreiro. The Xunta de Galicia's own account does not say so. It traces the earliest known form to an armorial of the fifteenth century, where the sign was a ciborium, and says the chalice with the Host appeared in the late sixteenth century. The official form was set by a law of 1984. This is a shared love of the Eucharist, and no direct link with O Cebreiro was documented.

The fruits

Pilgrims fed for the road

For centuries the monks' hospice sheltered people crossing the mountain. The story of Juan Santín suits such a place. It honours a poor man who climbed through snow to be at Mass, and it warns a priest against a tired heart. Either figure can speak to a pilgrim today.

The monastery's end and Don Elías

The monks left after the suppressions of the nineteenth century. The sources give 1853, 1854 and 1858 for the end of the old life of the house, and the church resumed as a parish in about those years.

Elías Valiña Sampedro was born in 1929 near Sarria and ordained on 21 September 1957. He took a doctorate at the Pontifical University of Salamanca in 1965, with a thesis on the road to Santiago. As parish priest he worked for running water, electricity and schooling, and he rebuilt the church, whose restoration is dated from 1962. He was the leading figure in the revival of the Camino. He published guides from 1971, and is remembered above all for marking the road with yellow arrows, painted with the help of volunteers. He died on 11 December 1989 and is buried in the chapel of St Benedict in the church. One account says there were about 5,000 pilgrims in 1990 and nearly half a million a year by 2023, all across the Camino and not only at O Cebreiro.

Feasts and the people

The relics are carried on Corpus Christi, on 15 August and on 8 September. The pilgrimage called Os Milagros, on 8 and 9 September, draws large crowds from the surrounding country. It honours the image of Santa María and the relics of the monastery. Galician food is served in a large tent.

If you visit

O Cebreiro lies about 71 kilometres from Lugo, by road through Pedrafita do Cebreiro. Walkers reach it on the Camino Francés by the steep climb from the Bierzo side. See the chalice, paten and reliquary in the Chapel of the Holy Miracle, the image of Our Lady, and the tomb of Don Elías. A palloza nearby has been made into an ethnographic museum. Bring warm clothes in any season, and check the hours of the church.

What the Church teaches

Our faith rests on Christ's own words, and not on miracles. Christ is truly present in the Eucharist at every Mass and Qurbana, with or without any sign (CCC 1374 to 1376). The priest of the story was wrong before anything was seen to change, and Juan Santín was right. No one is obliged to believe in this miracle. Anyone may take its lesson.
