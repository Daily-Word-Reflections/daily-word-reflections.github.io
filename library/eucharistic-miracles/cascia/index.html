Eucharistic miracles · Italy · 1330

Cascia

Church's position Long veneration. Devotional accounts say a local bishop examined the event, that Popes gave indulgences, and name Pope Boniface IX in 1401, but the documents themselves have not been checked and the bishop is not named. A formal recognition of the relic is reported in 1687. No judgment of the Holy See on the miracle was found. No Catholic is obliged to believe it.
  • Place today Basilica of St Rita, Cascia, province of Perugia, Umbria, Italy
  • Event 1330, near Siena; relic brought to Cascia by Blessed Simone Fidati
  • Kept a page of a priest's breviary marked with blood, in a monstrance of 1930
  • Procession Corpus Christi

The account

Why a relic from Siena is at Cascia

The miracle of Cascia did not happen at Cascia. By all the better accounts it happened near Siena in Tuscany. Cascia keeps it because of one man. Blessed Simone Fidati, an Augustinian friar and well-known preacher, was a son of the town, and he brought it home.

The priest and the breviary, according to tradition

The tradition does not name the priest. In 1330 a gravely ill peasant near Siena asked for Communion. The priest took a consecrated Host. Instead of carrying it in the proper small vessel, he slipped it between the pages of his breviary, the book of the daily prayer of the clergy, and set out. Some tellings stress his carelessness, others say he had lost his reverence and was going through his duties by habit.

He came to the sick man's house and heard his confession. When he opened the book to give Communion, the Host, he found, had turned to blood. It had stained the two pages between which it lay. Some accounts speak of a clot and some of living blood. One says that the Host was gone and the marks were left.

The priest was shaken. He went to Siena, to the Augustinian house, to Father Simone, who was known as a holy and wise confessor. He told him all that had happened. Father Simone heard his confession and absolved him. He asked to keep the two pages.

Perugia and Cascia

Here we have firm ground. The standard Italian biographical dictionary, the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani, says in its article on Fidati by Enrico Menestò that when he returned from Siena in 1330, he stopped in Umbria, first at Perugia and then at Cascia, and that in these two cities he left a relic of a Eucharistic miracle that had happened near Siena. It does not tell the story of the miracle or say whether it happened. It records the relics.

Devotional accounts add that one page was left in Perugia and the other went to the Augustinian house at Cascia. One says that this was decided by a meeting at Perugia. It is also said that only the Cascia page survives today. What became of the Perugia page is not clear.

The earliest written witnesses

The oldest evidence named in the sources is the Communal Statutes of Cascia of 1387. These are said to order that each year on Corpus Christi the authorities and people should gather and carry the relic through the town, starting from the church of St Augustine. If so, then 57 years after the event the relic was already the town's civic treasure, which is early for a relic of this kind.

A recognition of the relic in 1687 is also reported, citing a very old book of the Augustinian house which described the miracle. The date of that book is not given. In 1930 the historical documents were published for the sixth centenary. A study by Adolfo Morini, La reliquia del "Corpus Christi" di Cascia: storia e documenti, is listed in library catalogues; it is not used here.

Blessed Simone Fidati

Simone was born at Cascia to a noble family, sometime between 1280 and 1295. He first studied the natural sciences. A holy man, probably the Franciscan spiritual Angelo Clareno, turned him to the things of God, and he probably joined the Augustinian Hermits in his home town about 1310. He refused academic posts and spent about 27 years preaching in central Italy, at Rome, Perugia, Gubbio, Foligno, Siena, Florence and Pisa.

In Florence, about 1332 to 1333, he founded a house for women who had turned from a life of sin, and later a second house for women. He wrote L'ordine della vita cristiana in 1333, a guide to Christian life in Italian. In 1338 in Rome he began his main work, De gestis Domini Salvatoris, a long reflection on the Gospels. About thirty Latin letters, a spiritual rule, sermons and some Italian poems also survive. He criticised the government of Florence, and its Priors forbade him to preach.

He fell ill in Rome in 1347 and died on 2 February 1348 in the time of the great plague, most likely in Florence. His body was later brought to Cascia. Pope Gregory XVI recognised his cult on 23 August 1833, which is why he is called Blessed. After the suppression of the religious houses in 1866 his remains were moved to the collegiate church of St Mary. In 1948, the centenary of his death, they were placed in the new church of St Rita. He is honoured on 2 February, and the Augustinians keep 16 February.

What is known

Simone Fidati is a well documented person. A leading modern reference work accepts that he left a relic of a Eucharistic miracle at Perugia and Cascia in 1330. Cascia's statutes of 1387 are reported to order a Corpus Christi procession with it. The relic is shown and venerated at the Basilica of St Rita today, and carried in procession.

What is less certain

The priest is unnamed and his story comes from devotional retellings. Whether the event was at Siena or in a village nearby differs. The role of the bishop and the 1401 act of Boniface IX were seen only in secondary accounts. Exactly where in the basilica the relic is kept varies between sources. No scientific test of the page was found, and none is reported here.

The "face" in the stain

Some accounts say that when a lamp is held behind the page, the stain shows the profile of a bearded man, and that some see the face of Christ. This is a personal impression, and not a statement of the Church or a finding of any study. People see faces in many patterns. Nothing depends on it.

How the Church has honoured it

1387The town's statutes, as reported, order the procession on Corpus Christi.
1401Pope Boniface IX is said to grant an indulgence like that of the Portiuncula. The Umbrian tourist board also says that Boniface IX recognised the cult. The act itself has not been checked.
1687A formal recognition of the relic is reported.
1833Gregory XVI confirms the cult of Simone Fidati. This concerns the man and not the miracle.
1930Sixth centenary: a Eucharistic Congress of the diocese of Norcia at Cascia, a new monstrance, and publication of the documents.
1947, 1955The new church is completed in 1947. Pope Pius XII raises it to a minor basilica in 1955.
1948The remains of Fidati are moved to the new church.
1988The lower basilica is inaugurated in April.

The fruits

The fruit of Cascia is a quiet one. Its first lesson is for priests: the story warns against the handling of holy things as routine, and shows a confessor who received a frightened man and gave him pardon. It also speaks of the care that is owed to the Sacrament when it is carried to the sick.

The relic also became part of the life of the town. Each year on Corpus Christi it is carried in procession. Beside the windows around the high altar showing Bolsena, Lanciano, St Anthony and the donkey, St Clare and the Multiplication of the Loaves, one window shows an open book with two red marks. Most pilgrims come to Cascia for St Rita, who lived and died in the Augustinian monastery that stands beside the basilica, and whose body rests in the basilica.

If you visit

Cascia is a small mountain town in Umbria, near the Monti Sibillini, in the province of Perugia. The basilica is at Viale Santa Rita 13, and one guide gives its hours as 7:00 to 18:30 every day; check before you travel. The upper church, with St Rita's chapel, was built between 1937 and 1947. Most sources place the relic in the lower chapel, with the remains of Blessed Simone close by, under the altar of the Consolation chapel according to one account. Ask at the sanctuary for the Eucharistic relic by name, the Corpus Christi.

What the Church teaches

Our faith rests on Christ's own words, and not on miracles. Christ is truly present in the Eucharist at every Mass and Qurbana, with or without any sign (CCC 1374 to 1376). Every Host carried to a sick person is the Lord, whether a book is stained or not. No one is obliged to believe in the miracle of Cascia. Anyone may let it teach reverence.
