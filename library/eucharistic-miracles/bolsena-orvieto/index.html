Eucharistic miracles · Italy · 1263

Bolsena and Orvieto

Church's position Long veneration, and warm words from Popes, but no formal judgment that we could find. We found no decree of any Pope or bishop declaring the event a miracle after an inquiry. The bull Transiturus of Urban IV (11 August 1264) gave the whole Church the feast of Corpus Christi, and it does not name Bolsena. Later Popes have spoken of the event in their own ways: Paul VI at Orvieto in 1964 spoke of it as a "story", "delightful and sacred", that carries the echo of medieval tales; St John Paul II at Orvieto in 1990 called it a "miraculous sign"; Benedict XVI in 2010 called it "the Eucharistic miracle" of Bolsena of 1263. None of these binds anyone. No Catholic is obliged to believe it.
  • Places today Bolsena, province of Viterbo, Lazio; Orvieto, Umbria; about 20 km apart
  • Date According to tradition, summer 1263
  • What is kept The corporal, in Orvieto Cathedral; stained altar stones, in the Basilica of St Christina, Bolsena
  • Feast Corpus Christi, with processions in both towns

The account

What the tradition tells

Everything in this section is told according to tradition. The oldest written form we know of comes from well after the event, as the next section explains.

In the summer of 1263 a priest from beyond the Alps was on pilgrimage to Rome. He is known as Peter of Prague. The Vatican's stamp office, in its text for the 750th anniversary, calls him a Bohemian. Paul VI, speaking in 1964, called him "a German priest, whom we will call Peter of Prague". The name itself comes from the later tradition.

He was troubled by doubt. The doubt is usually described as doubt about the real presence of Christ in the consecrated Host. The Vatican's text says that his doubts of faith had put his vocation in crisis, and that he went to Rome to quiet them. He prayed at the tomb of St Peter and found his faith strengthened.

On the way home he stopped at Bolsena, a small town on the eastern shore of the lake of the same name. He celebrated Mass in the church of St Christina, a young martyr honoured there. During the Mass, at the consecration or just after, blood began to come from the Host. It fell on the corporal, the square of white linen on which the Host and chalice rest, and on the stones of the altar.

The priest was deeply shaken. The tradition says he wrapped everything in the corporal and the sacred linens and left the church. When he had recovered, he went to nearby Orvieto, where Pope Urban IV was then living with his court, and told him what had happened.

The Pope sent a bishop to look into it. The Vatican's anniversary text names him as Bishop Giacomo. He brought the relics back to Orvieto. The tradition ends with a great scene: the Pope and his whole court go out to meet the procession, and thousands of the faithful fill the streets as the corporal is carried into the city.

Then, the story says, Urban IV gave the feast of Corpus Christi to the whole Church, and asked St Thomas Aquinas, who was then at Orvieto, to compose its prayers.

The earliest witnesses, and how late they are

This is the part most retellings leave out. It matters, because it shows what we can and cannot say.

  • 1264, the bull. Transiturus is the document nearest in time. It speaks of the Eucharist and of the new feast, and it refers discreetly to the mystical experiences of St Juliana, as Benedict XVI noted. It does not mention Bolsena.
  • After 1317, a lost document. According to the art historian G. Freni, summarised by the reference guide Key to Umbria, the story emerged or re-emerged at Orvieto early in the fourteenth century, based on a document written after 1317. That document is now lost.
  • 1337 to 1338, the enamels. The earliest surviving telling of the story in pictures is on the great reliquary made for the corporal at Orvieto.
  • 1357 to 1364, the frescoes. The walls of the chapel in the cathedral show eight scenes of the miracle, with Latin explanations written in 1362.
  • About 1563, a copy. The earliest surviving copy of the lost document dates from about this year.
  • 1573 and 1601, the inscriptions. That copy lies behind an inscription of 1573 at Bolsena, which no longer survives, and four inscriptions of 1601 to 1602 still on the wall of the chapel at Orvieto.

So more than fifty years lie between the event and the first written account we know of, and about seventy before the first that we can still see. That does not show the event did not happen. Many true things were written down late. It does mean that the details of the story, such as the words of the priest and the scene of the meeting, cannot be checked.

The feast did not begin at Bolsena

The feast of Corpus Christi was already nearly twenty years old in Liège before anyone is said to have seen blood at Bolsena.

St Juliana of Cornillon was born near Liège, in present-day Belgium, in 1191 or 1192. As a girl of sixteen she had a vision, repeated many times: the full moon with one dark band across it. She came to understand that the moon was the life of the Church on earth, and that the dark band was a missing feast in honour of the Eucharist. She kept this to herself for years, then confided in two other women, one of whom was Blessed Eva, a recluse of Liège.

Bishop Robert of Thourotte of Liège, after some hesitation, accepted the proposal. In 1246 he ordered the feast for his diocese, to begin the next year. He died that October, and the canons of St Martin at Liège were the first to celebrate it. Juliana suffered much opposition in her later years and died at Fosses in 1258.

Here the two stories meet. Jacques Pantaléon, a Frenchman, had been archdeacon of Liège and had known the feast there. In 1261 he became Pope as Urban IV. Paul VI recalled this link when he spoke at Orvieto. The papal court had taken refuge at Orvieto, and from there, on 11 August 1264, Urban IV issued Transiturus, which ordered the feast for the whole Church on the Thursday after Trinity Sunday and attached indulgences to it.

He died on 2 October of the same year, and the feast spread slowly. Clement V ordered it again at the Council of Vienne in 1311. John XXII urged it, and Benedict XVI gives 1317 as the year it was re-established for the whole Church. From then on it took root everywhere, and with it the procession of the Blessed Sacrament.

The honest way to put it is this. The feast came from Liège, by way of a woman's prayer and a bishop's decision, and a Pope who knew them. Bolsena may have helped to move that Pope. The documents do not say that it did. Benedict XVI, telling the story in 2010, presents both Juliana and Bolsena as part of what led Urban IV to act.

The relic: from Bolsena to its chapel at Orvieto

We do not know where the corporal was kept in its first decades. Benedict XVI says that the Pope ordered it to be kept at Orvieto. It was probably venerated in the church of Santa Maria Prisca, and from about 1297 in the new cathedral. Clear records begin in the 1330s.

In 1338, under Bishop Beltramo Monaldeschi, a Dominican, the bishop, the archpriest and the canons commissioned a reliquary from the Sienese goldsmith Ugolino di Vieri and his companions. It is made of silver gilt and enamel, and its shape copies the front of the cathedral. Eight enamels tell the story of Bolsena. Others show the Passion and the early life of Christ. The earliest surviving reference to the Orvieto celebration also dates from 1338: a public holiday and a procession with the corporal in its reliquary.

Then the cathedral built a chapel for it, at the end of the left transept, in 1350 to 1356. Nicolò da Siena designed the marble tabernacle from 1358. Ugolino di Prete Ilario and his helpers painted the walls from 1357, and he signed his work on 8 June 1364. Lippo Memmi painted the Madonna dei Raccomandati that is also in the chapel, dated 1339 by one guide and probably before 1320 by another, an image of Our Lady sheltering the members of a confraternity.

The corporal stays in the tabernacle today, now in a lighter reliquary used for the yearly procession. The old reliquary of Ugolino di Vieri is shown near the entrance of the chapel.

What is known

A linen corporal has been venerated at Orvieto at least since the 1330s. Its reliquary of 1337 to 1338 and its chapel of 1350 to 1356 still stand. Stones are venerated at Bolsena, and a chapel for them was begun there in 1693. Urban IV was living at Orvieto in 1263 and 1264, and he issued Transiturus there on 11 August 1264. The feast came first from Liège. St Thomas Aquinas was at Orvieto at the time. Raphael painted the event in the Vatican for Julius II.

What is less certain

No written account of the event is known from before 1317, and the first one is lost. The bull does not name Bolsena, and historians debate how far the event moved the Pope, though many popular accounts make it the direct cause. The priest's name and country come from later tradition. We found no scientific study of the corporal or the stones, so nothing can be said about what the stains are. Sources also differ on small things. The Vatican's stamp text says the bull recognised the miracle, but the bull's silence is noted by historians. One art-history guide dates the bull to September 1263, and the Catholic Encyclopedia to 8 September 1264; Paul VI and Benedict XVI give 11 August 1264, and we follow them. Scholars such as Gy and Torrell accept St Thomas as author of the office, while Simon Tugwell judged it very unlikely. The first stone of the cathedral was laid in 1290: the stamp text links its building to the miracle, while John Paul II, in his homily there, did not tie it directly to the miracle or the feast.

The fruits

  • A feast for the whole Church. Whatever part Bolsena played, Corpus Christi is now kept across the Latin Church, and its procession has become one of the most public acts of Catholic faith.
  • Prayers we still sing. The office attributed to St Thomas gave the Church Pange lingua, with its closing Tantum ergo, and Lauda Sion. Benedict XVI calls these texts masterpieces in which theology and poetry meet.
  • A cathedral and a chapel. Orvieto Cathedral is one of the great buildings of Italy, and the Chapel of the Corporal keeps a large medieval cycle of paintings on the Eucharist.
  • Art. Ugolino di Vieri's enamels, the frescoes at Orvieto, Raphael's fresco in the Vatican, and at Bolsena Francesco Trevisani's altarpiece of 1704.
  • Two living processions. At Orvieto the corporal is carried through the city, and on 7 June 2026 more than 400 people in historical costume walked with it. At Bolsena a procession of about 3 km passes over carpets of flower petals, the Infiorata, made by volunteers who gather the flowers the day before. It is said to be over two hundred years old. One local account dates the first solemn procession with the relics to 1881, and others give earlier dates in the nineteenth century.

If you visit

Orvieto stands on a rock in south-west Umbria. It has its own railway station, and a funicular links the station to the old town. The Chapel of the Corporal is in the left transept of the cathedral. The relic is kept in the tabernacle and is shown at set times, especially around Corpus Christi, so ask the cathedral before you travel.

Bolsena is about 20 km to the south-west, on the eastern shore of the lake. In the Basilica of St Christina, see the Chapel of the Miracle, begun in 1693, which keeps the stones. From it you go down to the Grotto of St Christina, where the Altar of the Miracle stands under an early medieval canopy on four columns. Its stone is honoured there. A tradition about St Christina's footprints belongs to a stone beneath the altar table. The Renaissance front of the church was commissioned by Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, later Pope Leo X.

In the Vatican, the fresco is in the Raphael Rooms of the Vatican Museums, in the Stanza of Heliodorus.

What the Church teaches

Our faith rests on Christ's own words, and not on miracles: "this is my body" (Mark 14:22; see John 6:51 to 58). The Eucharist is the real presence of Christ at every Mass and Qurbana, with or without any sign (CCC 1374 to 1376). What the priest at Bolsena is said to have seen adds nothing to what is on the altar every day. A corporal at Orvieto is honoured because it points to that gift, and no one is obliged to believe that this story happened as it is told. If you are ever troubled by doubt, as he was, do what he did: go on praying, and go on going to Mass.
