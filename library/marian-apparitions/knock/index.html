Marian apparitions · Ireland · 1879

Our Lady of Knock

Approval status No formal decree on the supernatural origin of the apparition was found. Two commissions named by the Archbishops of Tuam, in 1879 and 1936, judged the testimony of the witnesses trustworthy. The Holy See has honoured the shrine: St John Paul II came as a pilgrim in 1979 and gave it the Golden Rose, and on 19 March 2021 Pope Francis raised it to an international shrine of special Eucharistic and Marian devotion. These are signs of trust and pastoral approval of the devotion. They are not a definition, and no Catholic is bound to believe the apparition.
  • Witnesses 15 official witnesses, from a child of about 5 to a woman of about 75
  • Date Thursday 21 August 1879, once only, from about 8 in the evening for about two hours
  • Place South gable of the parish church, Knock, County Mayo, Ireland
  • Feast 17 August on the national calendar of Ireland; 21 August is kept at the shrine as the anniversary
What happened

The place. In 1879 Knock was a small country parish in the west of Ireland. St John Paul II called it, in his homily there, a once obscure bogside village. The parish church of St John the Baptist dated from 1828. The people were poor, and many spoke Irish at home while their children were being schooled in English. 1879 was a year of potato blight in Mayo, reported as one of the worst in the area since the Great Famine of the 1840s. The parish priest since 1867 was Archdeacon Bartholomew Cavanagh, then about 58, who preached in both Irish and English.

The first sight. The evening of Thursday 21 August was very wet. The priest's housekeeper, Mary McLoughlin, aged about 45, said that at about seven or a little later, while it was still light, she walked past the church and saw figures and an altar at the gable. She took them for statues that the Archdeacon had ordered from Dublin or somewhere else, and she walked on to the home of her friend Margaret Byrne, a widow.

At about eight she was going back with Margaret's daughter Mary Byrne, who was in her twenties. The sources give her age as 26 or 29. Mary Byrne stopped short at the sight of three life-sized figures. She did not take them for statues. She ran home to tell her family. Word passed from house to house, and others came.

The watching. Fifteen people later gave evidence: men, women, boys and small children. They stood or knelt in the rain for up to two hours and said the Rosary. They saw the figures standing a little out from the wall and a foot or two above the ground, in a bright light. The figures did not flicker and they did not speak. Several witnesses said that the ground beneath the figures stayed dry in the pouring rain, though the wind was blowing from the south, toward the gable. When it was over, the gable was dark and the ground there was wet.

Patrick Hill, a boy of 11 by most lists and 13 by another, gave the fullest account. He went close enough, he said, to see the lettering in the book that St John was holding. Little John Curry, about five, had to be lifted up to look, and he spoke afterwards of the lights and of the babies he had seen, by which he seems to have meant the angels. Bridget Trench, about 75, who had no English, went up to embrace the feet of Our Lady. She told the commission that her arms met only the wall, though she had seen the figures plainly. A farmer about half a mile away reported seeing a great globe of golden light around the gable.

The priest. After about an hour Mary McLoughlin hurried to tell the Archdeacon. For reasons the accounts do not make clear, he stayed at home. Some sources say that he thought little of her report. The people were disappointed. In the following months, though, he kept careful notes of the cures people reported, and he sat on the inquiry.

The first inquiry. On 8 October 1879, about six weeks later, Archbishop John MacHale of Tuam set up a commission of inquiry. Its members included Canon Ulick Bourke, a noted Irish scholar, Canon James Waldron, and Archdeacon Cavanagh himself. The depositions of 15 witnesses were taken in the following months. The commission looked only at the event of 21 August and not at later reports. It found no natural explanation it could offer and no sign of fraud, and it judged the testimony, taken as a whole, "trustworthy and satisfactory". The depositions also show differences in detail: for example on whether St John wore a mitre, whether he was preaching, and on the exact times.

The second inquiry. In 1936 Archbishop Thomas Gilmartin of Tuam named a second commission, to hear those who were still alive. Patrick Byrne, then 71, confirmed his evidence. Mary Byrne, by then Mrs Mary O'Connell and aged 86, confirmed hers under oath from what proved to be her deathbed, saying that she was clear about all she had stated. She died about six weeks later. John Curry, living in New York, gave evidence before a special inquiry there in 1937. The commission found the witnesses upright and trustworthy.

The witnesses afterwards. They were ordinary people and went on as such. None founded anything or claimed a mission. John Curry lay for decades in an unmarked grave on Long Island. On 13 May 2017 his remains were laid in a churchyard in Lower Manhattan, after a Mass at which Cardinal Timothy Dolan of New York presided. Archdeacon Cavanagh died in 1897 and is buried in the church at Knock.

The main messages

At Knock there are no words at all. Nobody spoke, and no witness reported a message or a secret. So the "message" is what was seen. Here it is, as the witnesses described it.

Our LadyIn the centre, in a large white cloak fastened at the neck, with a bright crown and a rose where it met the brow. Her hands were raised to the height of her shoulders or a little higher, and her eyes were lifted to heaven, deep in prayer.
St JosephOn her right, in white, with his head bowed toward her and, as Patrick Hill noticed, his hands joined in prayer.
St JohnOn her left, dressed as a bishop according to several witnesses, with a book in his left hand and his right hand raised as if teaching. Mary Byrne recognised him because he looked like a statue she had seen in the chapel at Lecanvey, near Westport.
The altarA plain altar a little to the left of St John, toward the centre of the gable.
The LambA lamb standing on the altar, with a large upright cross behind it.
AngelsAngels hovering around the Lamb.

In one line

Our Lady stands in silent prayer beside the Lamb on the altar, and teaches us to look where she looks.

Nothing new in the faith

Altar, Lamb and cross speak of Christ's sacrifice and of the Mass (see Jn 1:29; Rev 5). Nothing here adds to the Gospel. The Church has given no official reading of each detail, so the meanings offered in books and sermons are reflections and not teaching.

Pope Francis gave a reflection of his own in 2021: at Knock the Virgin says nothing, and yet her silence is, in his words, "the most expressive language" given to us. He spoke of silence before the love of Jesus, the Lamb sacrificed for all.

How the Church sees it
JudgmentThe Archdiocese of Tuam holds the competent authority. Its two commissions (1879 and 1936) found the witnesses trustworthy. A formal episcopal decree on supernatural origin, of the kind given at Lourdes or Beauraing, could not be found in the sources checked. One reference work files Knock among the "traditionally approved" apparitions. Under the 2024 norms of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, the Church usually gives a pastoral conclusion in such cases and does not declare a supernatural origin.
FeastOur Lady of Knock was placed on the national calendar of Ireland in 2011, on 17 August, because 21 August belongs to St Pius X. It is not on the General Roman Calendar. At Knock the anniversary is kept on 21 August, when the novena ends.
Before 1979Reference works record that Pope Pius XII blessed a Knock banner in 1945, that the image was crowned in the Marian Year 1954, that Pope St John XXIII sent a candle in 1960, and that Pope St Paul VI blessed the foundation stone of the new church in June 1974. These come from secondary sources, and they do not agree on the date of the crowning.
St John Paul IIOn 30 September 1979, for the centenary, he came to Knock as a pilgrim before a crowd reported at over 450,000. He called Knock the goal of his journey to Ireland. He spoke to the sick, celebrated Mass, gave a candle and the Golden Rose, raised the new church to a basilica and knelt at the gable wall.
Pope FrancisOn 26 August 2018, during the World Meeting of Families, he prayed in silence in the Apparition Chapel and led the Angelus there. He also begged forgiveness there for abuse in the Church in Ireland. On 19 March 2021 he raised the national shrine to an international shrine of special Eucharistic and Marian devotion, in a video message played in a chapel closed to pilgrims during the pandemic. Archbishop Michael Neary of Tuam, custodian of the shrine, had asked for this.
HealingsThe first reported cure was of a deaf girl, Delia Gordon, within about two weeks. Archdeacon Cavanagh recorded 637 reported cures by October 1880. These were reports, not judgments of the Church. The one case the Church has formally acknowledged is that of Marion Carroll of Athlone, who had been brought to Knock on a stretcher, with multiple sclerosis and failing sight, in September 1989, and who stood up after the blessing of the sick with the Blessed Sacrament. On 1 September 2019, after 30 years of inquiry by the medical bureau, Bishop Francis Duffy and Archbishop Neary stated that the Church acknowledged her healing as having no medical explanation. Some Catholic news reports called this a miracle.

Other explanations. Some writers have offered natural explanations. David Berman in 1979 argued for a magic lantern, citing letters that he himself described as hearsay. Melvin Harris suggested a lantern and a mirror. Joe Nickell in 2017 pointed to differences between the witnesses and to the evening sun, suggesting light reflected from a nearby building. None of these has been shown to be what happened. Neither has the Church staked anything of the faith on the answer. The honest position is the one the 1879 commission reached: the people who gave evidence were believed to be telling the truth about what they saw.

The fruits
  • Pilgrims. About 5,000 came for the anniversary in 1883, and about 120,000 in the summer of 1939. Recent reports give one million to 1.5 million visitors a year, though none gives a counted figure for a named year. The rector, Fr Richard Gibbons, estimated about 150,000 pilgrims at the National Novena of 2025.
  • Confession. In 2025 the shrine had 16 full-time chaplains hearing confessions daily, from morning to evening. Fr Gibbons calls the confessionals the engine room of the shrine.
  • The Eucharist. Adoration and the blessing of the sick with the Blessed Sacrament have a central place. Ireland's National Eucharistic Congress met there on 25 and 26 June 2011, with an estimated 13,000 people.
  • The National Novena. Begun in 1977, and kept each August without a break since then.
  • The laity. The Knock Shrine Society was founded in 1935 by Judy and Liam Coyne. Each Irish diocese makes a yearly pilgrimage.
  • The Irish abroad. Pope Francis recalled the emigrants, priests and lay people, who kept the Rosary and this devotion alive wherever they went.
If you visit

Knock is in County Mayo in the west of Ireland. Ireland West Airport Knock, about 19 km away near Charlestown, was built through the efforts of Monsignor James Horan, parish priest from 1967 to 1986, who also led the building of the basilica. See the old parish church and its gable, the glass Apparition Chapel with Lorenzo Ferri's figures, and the Basilica of Our Lady, Queen of Ireland, with its large Apparition mosaic. The shrine also has a museum, and the novena falls in August. Its dates shift a little from year to year (13 to 21 August in 2026), so check before you travel.

Read it rightly

Apparitions, even those the Church honours, are private revelations. They add nothing to the faith, which was given once in Christ, and no Catholic is obliged to believe them. Their role is to help us live the Gospel more fully in a certain time (CCC 66 to 67). Knock has the special grace of saying nothing new, because it says nothing at all: it simply shows the Lamb, and Our Lady at prayer.
