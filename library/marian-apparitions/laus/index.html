Marian apparitions · France · 1664 to 1718

Our Lady of Laus

Approval status Approved by the local bishop, Jean-Michel di Falco Léandri of Gap and Embrun. At Mass at Laus on 4 May 2008 he said: "I recognize the supernatural origin of the apparitions." Some sources date the decree 5 May. It followed a diocesan commission and contact with the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, and the Apostolic Nuncio to France, Fortunato Baldelli, attended. Under the 2024 norms the Church now usually gives a pastoral conclusion rather than such a declaration, but this one was made under the earlier practice.
  • Seer Venerable Benoîte Rencurel, shepherdess, aged 16 at the start (born 16 September 1647; some sources say 17)
  • Dates May 1664 until her death in December 1718, 54 years
  • Place today Notre-Dame du Laus, Saint-Étienne-le-Laus, Hautes-Alpes, France
  • Feast A pilgrim guide gives 1 May for the shrine; not in the general calendar
What happened

The girl and the valley

Laus lies in the French Alps, not far from the town of Gap, about 900 metres above the sea. The name comes from an old word for a lake. In 1666 the hamlet had about twenty households.

Benoîte Rencurel was born on 16 September 1647 in Saint-Étienne d'Avançon, now Saint-Étienne-le-Laus. Her father died when she was seven, and left the family in debt. There was no school, and she never learned to read or write. At about twelve she began to keep sheep for two employers. She was known for the Rosary, which she prayed in the fields.

May 1664: St Maurice and the Beautiful Lady

In May 1664, after hearing a homily from her parish priest, Benoîte longed to meet the Mother of God. The shrine's history says that St Maurice then appeared to her near a ruined chapel dedicated to him, and told her that her wish would be granted. He sent her to a valley above the village, the Vallon des Fours, where plaster was made from gypsum.

There she saw a Beautiful Lady holding a child, on a rock. Benoîte, a shepherd girl with little to give, offered to share her bread. The Lady only smiled. For about four months the Lady came daily. At first she was silent. Then she began to teach the girl, as a mother teaches. People could see Benoîte speaking, but could not hear the Lady.

One of Benoîte's employers doubted her and came to listen. She overheard a warning that her soul was in danger, and that she must do penance. Moved, she went back to the sacraments. It was the first of many conversions.

29 August 1664: "I am Dame Mary"

Benoîte finally asked her name. The shrine gives her answer: "I am Dame Mary, the Mother of my very dear Son." She added that she wished to be honoured in the parish, but not in that place. Then, for a month, the visits stopped.

29 September 1664: Pindreau, and the road to Laus

At Pindreau, across the valley, a bright light drew Benoîte to a ruin, and there was the Lady. The girl cried out to ask why she had been left so long. Mary told her to go to Laus, where she would find a small chapel by its sweet scent, and that there she would speak with her very often.

The next day Benoîte found it, the humble chapel of Bon-Rencontre, Our Lady of the Good Encounter, poor and neglected. Mary stood on the altar. Then she spoke the words that define the place: "I have asked my Son for this place for the conversion of sinners, and He has granted it to me."

She asked for a larger church, and for a house for priests, so that people could come for confession. She said that the oil of the lamp burning before the Blessed Sacrament would heal those who applied it with faith in her intercession.

1665: the vicar general asks for a sign

News spread through the winter, and by spring 1665 pilgrims were coming. On 14 September 1665 the diocese's vicar general, Antoine Lambert, arrived. He did not believe, and meant to close the chapel. He questioned Benoîte, who answered calmly. Then he told her to ask for a sign. During his stay a local woman, Catherine Vial, was cured after a novena of a leg illness that had crippled her. Lambert changed his mind. On 18 September 1665 the diocese authorised the pilgrimage, the building of a church, and resident priests.

The church and the mission

On 7 October 1666 the first stone was laid, by the priest Pierre Gaillard according to one account. The church went up by 1669, built around the old chapel, which remains inside it to this day. Pilgrims came in great numbers. In 1672 Benoîte took a small house close by. She became a Dominican tertiary, wore a veil, and was called Sister Benoîte.

Her work was simple and hard. Those who knew her said she could read hearts, and she used this gift to help pilgrims make a good confession. Sinners came, priests heard them, and many went home changed. Gaillard wrote of confessors who sent penitents away very happy.

The suffering Christ

From autumn 1669 she reported visions of Christ crucified at a wayside cross near Avançon, five in all up to 1684. The place is now the Chapel of the Precious Blood. Accounts add that from July 1673 she bore the marks of the Passion, and suffered what they call a mystical crucifixion, week after week for some years, from Thursday evening to Saturday morning. These are the claims of her witnesses. They are told here as such.

Twenty years of opposition

Later priests at Laus treated her very differently. Several accounts speak of chaplains influenced by Jansenism, a severe movement of that age condemned by Rome, who distrusted her and the crowds. She was pressed to leave and to enter a convent. Some accounts say she was kept close to her house for about fifteen years and allowed to Mass only on Sundays. Others say she followed Mass through a small window of the church gallery. Dates and details vary. The pattern does not: about twenty years of slander and hardship, which she bore without bitterness.

In 1692 troops of the Duke of Savoy invaded the region. Benoîte and the priests fled to Marseille, and on returning they found the area devastated.

Peace came only near the end. In 1712, with a new bishop and new priests from Sainte-Garde, she was treated with honour again.

Her death

Benoîte received Communion on Christmas Day 1718 and died soon after. Most accounts give 28 December, a few give 25 December. She was 71. She lies buried before the main altar of the basilica, beneath a stone.

How we know: the Laus manuscripts

Benoîte could not write, so others wrote for her. Four men who knew her left about 1,800 pages of testimony: François Grimaud, a judge in the Avançon valley; Jean Peytieu; Pierre Gaillard, vicar general of Gap and her confessor; and François Aubin, a hermit and her confidant.

Aubin gathered the papers and handed them to the Fathers of Sainte-Garde. They were hidden during the French Revolution, and in 1824 an Oblate priest found them. An authentic copy was made in 1850 at the request of Bishop Depéry of Gap. Father De Labriolle published them in 1977. Their age and their witnesses are the strength of Laus, for we hear from people who stood beside her.

In one line

Laus is a place of mercy: Mary asks her Son for a house of confession, so that sinners may find God again.

Nothing new in the faith

The heart of Laus is the Gospel's call to repent, and the sacraments of Penance and the Eucharist. It adds no new teaching.

How the Church sees it

In her lifetimeThe diocese allowed the pilgrimage, the church and the priests in September 1665, but this is not the same as a judgment on the apparitions.
JudgmentBishop di Falco Léandri, installed at Gap in 2003, wrote to the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith in June 2005. He set up a commission in November 2005, with theologians and an investigating judge among its experts, and it reported in January 2007. He announced the recognition at Mass in Laus on 4 May 2008, with some thirty cardinals and bishops expected and about 6,000 people reported present.
What it saidThe decree speaks of God's love for men, his infinite mercy and his appeal for conversion, as one report quotes it. Press reports called it the first recognition of apparitions in France since Lourdes. The bishop said pilgrims could come "in total confidence".
CrowningBlessed Pius IX granted a coronation of the shrine's statue, held on 23 May 1855. About 40,000 people attended, according to one source.
BasilicaThe church was named a minor basilica in the early 1890s. Sources give 1892 or 1893.
The seerHer cause opened in 1871 under Pius IX, who gave her the title Servant of God (some sources say 1872). The writings presented in her cause were approved on 11 March 1895 (some sources say 1896; she herself could not write). The cause was suspended in 1913. St John Paul II allowed it to resume in 1981. Benedict XVI declared her Venerable on 3 April 2009. She is not yet beatified.
HealingsThe shrine reports many testimonies of relief or healing with the oil. We found no medical bureau or formal list of recognised miracles as at Lourdes, so these are testimonies and not Church judgments.

The fruits

The first fruit is confession. Laus is still known as a place where people go back to the sacrament after many years. People have called it Refuge of Sinners.

The oil. A lamp burns in the basilica near the altar of Bon-Rencontre, before the Blessed Sacrament. The shrine is clear that this is ordinary oil. It is not a magic cure. It says that using it is an act of prayer and trust in God, who may give graces seen or hidden, of soul or body, as he wills. The sick may ask for it from the shrine.

The perfume. Benoîte's witnesses spoke of sweet scents at the chapel, and visitors still report them. This is reported experience, not a matter of faith.

Saints and writers. St Eugène de Mazenod and St Peter Julian Eymard were devoted to Laus. Eymard walked about 60 kilometres there as a boy of eleven. The philosopher Jean Guitton called it "one of the most hidden and also most powerful treasures" in the history of Europe.

Pilgrims. Reports from 2008 give about 120,000 a year. More recent guides give 150,000 to 170,000, and one gives 200,000. No count from the shrine itself could be checked.

If you visit

The shrine lies in the Hautes-Alpes, a short distance from Gap, and it is open all year with daily Mass, confession, Rosary and adoration, with lodging on the spot. Trains stop at Gap, and the regional tourism office says the shrine runs a shuttle from the station. Walks lead from the basilica to Pindreau, the Chapel of the Precious Blood and the Col de l'Ange. The Vallon des Fours lies about 10 to 15 minutes on foot from Benoîte's birthplace in Saint-Étienne.

Read it rightly

Even approved apparitions are private revelations. They add nothing to the faith, and no Catholic is obliged to believe them. Their role is to help us live more fully by the revelation already complete in Christ (CCC 66 to 67). What Laus asks of anyone is what the Gospel asks: come back to God, and go to confession without fear.
