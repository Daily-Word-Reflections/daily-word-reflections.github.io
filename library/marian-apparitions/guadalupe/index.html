Marian apparitions · Mexico · 1531

Our Lady of Guadalupe

Approval status Approved by the acts of the Holy See, and not by one decree on the events of 1531. No judgment by the bishop of that time survives. Pope Benedict XIV named her patroness of New Spain and granted a proper Mass and Office in 1754. Pope Leo XIII ordered the image crowned, which was done in 1895. St John Paul II canonised St Juan Diego at the basilica on 31 July 2002. Historians still debate the early documents, as this page explains.
  • Seer St Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin, born in 1474 according to the Vatican's biography, so about 57; his uncle Juan Bernardino also reported seeing her
  • Dates 9 to 12 December 1531, according to tradition
  • Place today Tepeyac hill, in the Gustavo A. Madero district of Mexico City
  • Feast 12 December; St Juan Diego on 9 December
What happened

The land and the man

In 1531 Mexico was ten years past the Spanish conquest of 1521. A young Church was at work among peoples who had lost much. The bishop in Mexico City was a Franciscan, Juan de Zumárraga.

The Vatican's biography says Juan Diego was born in 1474 at Cuautitlán, now part of Mexico City. His own name was Cuauhtlatoatzin, which it gives as "the talking eagle". It says he belonged to the Chichimeca people, and that he was baptised at about 50 by the Franciscan Peter of Ghent. He was a poor layman, and Tepeyac lay on his way to Mass and instruction in the city.

The story below follows the Nican Mopohua, a Nahuatl account whose title means "Here it is told". Its date and author are discussed further down. It is the voice of tradition. It is not a police report, and it should be read with love and with care.

Saturday, 9 December: the hill sings

Juan Diego was walking before dawn. At the foot of Tepeyac he heard singing from the top of the hill, like the song of beautiful birds. Then the singing stopped, and someone called him by name. He climbed up and saw a Lady whose clothes shone like the sun. The rock under her feet glittered. The thorny plants of that dry hill looked to him like emerald, turquoise and gold.

She spoke to him tenderly, in his own language. One English translation gives her first question as: "Juanito, the most humble of my sons, where are you going?" She told him she was the Virgin Mary, and asked for a house of prayer to be built on the plain, where she could show her love and help to all who came. He was to carry this request to the bishop.

The first refusal

He went to the bishop's house, waited, and gave the message. The bishop listened kindly but found it hard to believe, and told him to come back another day. That same day Juan Diego returned to the hill. He begged the Lady to send someone of standing, since he was nobody. She answered that she had many servants, but that it was her wish to send him. He was to go again in her name.

Sunday, 10 December: the bishop asks for a sign

After Mass he went back to the bishop, in tears. This time the bishop questioned him closely and asked for a sign that the Lady truly sent him. He also had men follow Juan Diego, but they lost sight of him near the bridge on the road to Tepeyac. On the hill the Lady told him to come the next day, and she would give him the sign.

Monday, 11 December: the sick uncle

Juan Diego did not come. His uncle, Juan Bernardino, was gravely ill with fever, and he spent the day caring for him. The old man asked him to fetch a priest, for he believed he was dying.

Tuesday, 12 December: the roses

Before dawn he set out for the city. Ashamed, he took another path around the hill, so that the Lady would not stop him. She came down and met him on that very path. He told her of his uncle. The answer she gave is the best-loved line of the whole story. One translation has: "Am I not here, who is your Mother?" She told him not to fear, and that his uncle was already healed.

She sent him to the top of the hill to gather flowers. It was December, and the hilltop was bare rock and thorn. There he found roses of Castile in bloom. He gathered them into his tilma, the plain cloak of a working man. She arranged them with her own hands, and told him to open the cloak before the bishop alone.

The bishop's servants kept him waiting. At last he stood before the bishop and unfolded the cloak. The roses fell to the floor, and on the cloth was the image of the Virgin, the image still kept today. The bishop knelt, and asked pardon for his slowness. He kept the cloak in his chapel.

The uncle's story

Juan Diego led the bishop's men to the place she had chosen, and then went home. His uncle was well. Juan Bernardino said that the same Lady had come to him and healed him, and that she wished to be called Holy Mary of Guadalupe. He gave his testimony to the bishop.

According to tradition the image was carried in procession to a small chapel at Tepeyac on 26 December 1531. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the first shrine to 1532, and the New Catholic Encyclopedia to about 1533.

What happened to Juan Diego

The Vatican's biography says that with the bishop's permission he lived as a hermit beside the chapel, caring for it and welcoming pilgrims. He died in 1548 and was buried in that first chapel.

The image on the cloak

The image is what anyone can see today, above the main altar of the basilica. Mary stands with her hands folded and her head gently bowed. Rays of light surround her. She wears a rose robe and a blue-green mantle with stars, and stands on a crescent moon held up by an angel. Readers of Scripture have often thought of the woman of Revelation 12:1.

The cloth measures about 1.72 by 1.07 metres. It is made of two pieces sewn together, and shows signs of having been trimmed. An old crown on her head, seen in older copies, disappeared in about 1887 or 1888. The historian David Brading argues that it was removed by human hands.

The cloth and the paint have been studied many times, and the results do not agree.

  • In 1666 seven painters told an inquiry that no artist could have painted such an image on so rough a cloth.
  • In 1751 to 1756 the painter Miguel Cabrera studied it and praised it in his book Maravilla Americana, finding several techniques in one picture.
  • In 1787 painters working for José Ignacio Bartolache called the fibre palm, and first noted human touch-ups.
  • In 1981 Philip Callahan and Jody Smith, using infrared, found no sketch beneath the main figure and some later additions.
  • In 1982 the restorer José Sol Rosales judged the cloth to be linen and hemp, and the work to be by human hands.
  • The cloth has also been called maguey, as the Catholic Encyclopedia does. So the very fibre remains disputed.

Popular books also claim figures reflected in her eyes. Critics answer that the eye tends to find shapes in small marks. None of this is Church teaching. Faith does not rest on a laboratory result, either way.

Two old dangers are often told. In 1785 or 1791 a worker is said to have spilled acid on the cloth. On 14 November 1921 a bomb hidden among flowers damaged the altar, yet the image was not harmed.

The early documents, and what historians debate

Guadalupe has real questions of history. An honest reader should know them, and need not fear them.

Zumárraga's silenceNo writing by Bishop Zumárraga that mentions the apparitions is known. Older witnesses spoke of a letter, but none has been found. The New Catholic Encyclopedia says it is probable he made no report.
The shrine was realDevotion at Tepeyac is firmly attested by the 1550s. Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar, a Dominican, promoted it and began a second church in 1556. The soldier and writer Bernal Díaz mentions it in 1568.
The 1556 quarrelOn 8 September 1556 the Franciscan Francisco de Bustamante preached against the devotion, and Montúfar opened an inquiry. Some witnesses then said an indigenous painter had made the image, perhaps one named Marcos.
SahagúnIn the late 1570s the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún complained that people came to Tepeyac calling on Tonantzin, an old Nahua title meaning "Our Mother". He feared confusion with the old worship.
The written storyThe story first appears in print in Spanish in Miguel Sánchez's book of 1648. In 1649 Luis Laso de la Vega published the Nican Mopohua in Nahuatl, in a work called Huei tlamahuiçoltica. The date 12 December appears in Sánchez, and not in the Nican Mopohua itself.
Who wrote itThe Vatican's biography names Antonio Valeriano, a Nahua scholar taught by the Franciscans, and gives 1556. Many Mexican historians agree. Others date it nearer to 1649. Most scholars credit the 1649 book as a whole to Laso de la Vega. No manuscript from the mid-1500s has been found.
Sworn testimonyInquiries took place in 1666 and 1723. The 1666 witnesses spoke 135 years after the events, and the New Catholic Encyclopedia values these inquiries mainly for showing how the tradition endured.
Doubts about Juan DiegoJuan Bautista Muñoz raised objections in 1794, and Joaquín García Icazbalceta in a private report of 1883. In 1996 the basilica's abbot, Guillermo Schulenburg, resigned after reports that he doubted Juan Diego's existence. The historian Stafford Poole, a priest, has argued that the evidence is too late.
The Church's reviewIn 1998 the Congregation for the Causes of Saints reopened the historical question through a special commission under Fidel González, Eduardo Chávez and José Luis Guerrero. It reported in favour in October 1998, and the work was published in 1999. Critics answered that it does not separate the age of the shrine from the age of the story.
Codex EscaladaThis parchment, published in 1995, shows an apparition and records Juan Diego's death in 1548. Its supporters date it to the 1500s. Others, among them Poole and Alberto Peralta, question whether it is genuine.
The nameA famous shrine of Our Lady of Guadalupe already stood in Extremadura in Spain. Since 1675 some writers have proposed a Nahuatl word behind the name, and none has won agreement.

In short: the devotion at Tepeyac is old and sure. The full story of 1531 comes to us in writing more than a century later, and scholars disagree on how far it reaches back. The Church has canonised Juan Diego after review, and that is an act of the Church which Catholics honour. A Catholic may still study the documents honestly.

The main messages

As given in the Nican Mopohua tradition.

9 DecShe tells Juan Diego who she is, and asks for a house of prayer at Tepeyac, where she will show her love and hear those who come. He is to tell the bishop.
9 Dec, laterShe insists that he, a poor man, is the one she sends.
10 DecShe promises the sign that the bishop has asked for.
12 Dec"Am I not here, who is your Mother?" She tells him not to fear, says that his uncle is healed, and sends him to gather the roses.
12 DecTo Juan Bernardino, according to his testimony: she heals him, and gives the name Holy Mary of Guadalupe.

In one line

A mother comes to the poor in their own tongue, and asks for a house where her Son may be found.

Nothing new in the faith

The story adds no doctrine. It shows what the Gospel already says: God lifts up the lowly (Luke 1:52), and Mary points to her Son (John 2:5).

How the Church sees it

JudgmentNo bishop's decree on the events of 1531 survives. Approval has come through patronage, liturgy, coronation and canonisation. These are formal acts of the Holy See, and they honour the devotion and its seer. As with every apparition, they oblige no one to believe it.
Benedict XIVBy the bull Non est equidem of 25 May 1754, he named her patroness of New Spain and granted a proper Mass and Office for 12 December.
Leo XIIIDecreed the coronation on 8 February 1887. The image was crowned in his name on 12 October 1895.
St Pius XLetter of 16 June 1910. Sources describe its reach differently, as Mexico or as Latin America. The shrine was made a basilica in 1904.
Pius XI, Pius XIIPatronage reached the Philippines in 1935. In a radio message of 12 October 1945, Pius XII called her Queen of Mexico and Empress of the Americas.
St Paul VISent a Golden Rose in 1966. In 1976 he gave the new building the title of minor basilica.
St John Paul IICame as a pilgrim in 1979, 1990, 1999 and 2002. In Ecclesia in America 11, given at Mexico City on 22 January 1999, he asked that her feast be kept across the continent on 12 December. He called her "Mother and Evangelizer of America". The Holy See's calendar added her memorial for the whole Latin Church in 2002.
The seerBeatified by St John Paul II on 6 May 1990 at the basilica. Canonised there on 31 July 2002. He called him "the first indigenous Saint of the American Continent". The miracle accepted was the recovery of Juan José Barragán Silva after a fall in May 1990, and sources differ on some details.
FrancisSent a Golden Rose in 2013. Prayed at the basilica on 13 February 2016, and there recalled her words: "Am I not your mother? Am I not here with you?"

The Church has also read Guadalupe as a model of how the Gospel enters a culture. In the canonisation homily, St John Paul II said Juan Diego kept his indigenous identity when he accepted the faith, and he called for justice and care for indigenous peoples.

The fruits

Tepeyac has been a place of pilgrimage for almost five centuries. Many thousands arrive on foot, some on their knees, and parishes come in groups.

  • The city government of Mexico City reported 12.5 million pilgrims for the December feast of 2022, and called it a record.
  • About 12 million were expected for 8 to 12 December 2024, and the district reported 12.1 million.
  • Catholic News Agency has called the basilica the most visited Marian shrine in the world. Reference works estimate about 20 million visits a year. These counts are estimates.
  • Her image is honoured far beyond Mexico, and she is patroness of the Americas.

If you visit

The shrine lies in the north of Mexico City, at the Villa de Guadalupe. The nearest Metro stop is La Villa-Basílica. The original image hangs in the round modern basilica, opened on 12 October 1976, and it holds about 10,000 people. Beside it stands the old basilica, begun in 1695 and opened in 1709, which has suffered from sinking in the soft ground. Paths lead up Tepeyac to a chapel on the hill. Dates around 12 December are the most crowded of the year.

Read it rightly

Even approved apparitions are private revelations. They do not belong to the deposit of faith, and no Catholic is obliged to believe them. Their role is to help us live more fully by the revelation already complete in Christ (CCC 66 to 67). Honour the Mother, go to her Son, and care for the poor whom she came to meet.
