Marian apparitions · France · 1846

Our Lady of La Salette

Approval status Approved by the local bishop, Philibert de Bruillard of Grenoble, in a formal doctrinal letter dated 19 September 1851. He judged that the apparition "bears within itself all the characteristics of truth" and authorised the devotion. His successor, Bishop Ginoulhiac, confirmed this. The approval covers the event of 1846 and its public message. It does not cover the later published "secret", which the Holy See condemned in 1923. This judgment came long before the 2024 norms of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, under which the Church now usually gives a pastoral conclusion and not a declaration of supernatural origin.
  • Seers Mélanie Calvat, just under 15; Maximin Giraud, 11
  • Date Saturday 19 September 1846, about 3 in the afternoon
  • Place A high pasture above La Salette-Fallavaux, Isère, France, in the diocese of Grenoble
  • Feast 19 September (in the calendar of the shrine and the Missionaries of La Salette)
  • Title Our Lady of La Salette, Reconciler of sinners

What happened

The two children and the place

La Salette in 1846 was a poor mountain parish of eight or nine scattered hamlets, with about 800 people, mostly small farmers. It lies in the French Alps, in the hills above the small town of Corps. France had been through revolution and war within living memory. The harvests of those years were bad. The Missionaries of La Salette record that a famine, begun in 1845, was by the winter of 1846 raging through Europe.

Mélanie Calvat was born on 7 November 1831 at Corps, the fourth of ten children of a stonemason and sawyer. The family was so poor that the children sometimes begged. She was hired out young to mind cows. In 1846 she worked for Jean-Baptiste Pra at Les Ablandins, a hamlet of La Salette. She had no schooling and little religious teaching, and could not read or write.

Maximin Giraud was born on 26 August 1835, also at Corps, the son of a wheelwright. His mother died when he was 17 months old. His stepmother reportedly took little interest in him. He spent his days in the streets with a dog and a goat, had not gone to school, and had hardly any catechism. He was at La Salette for only a few days in September 1846, lent to a farmer, Pierre Selme, to mind his cows.

Both children spoke the local dialect and very little French. They had barely met before that week.

Saturday 19 September 1846

The children took their cows up to the high pasture. In the afternoon they slept for a while. When they woke they went to look for the animals. Coming back down, they saw a globe of light in the hollow by a dry spring. Inside the light a woman was sitting on a stone with her face in her hands, weeping.

She stood up and spoke. The shrine's own account says she was first seated and in tears, and that she spoke to them for a long time, in French and in the dialect. She said: "Come near, my children, do not be afraid. I am here to tell you great news." The children went to her.

The light that surrounded all three, according to the shrine, came from a large crucifix on her breast, with a hammer on one side of the cross and pincers on the other. She had a heavy chain on her shoulders and roses beside it. Roses also circled her head, her waist and her feet. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls her dress "a strange costume"; the children called her only "a beautiful lady".

She kept weeping as she spoke. When she had finished she told them to make the message known to all her people. She then climbed a short steep slope and disappeared into the light.

Each child later said that she had also told them a secret, which the other could not hear. The earliest accounts of the event do not mention this. Documents from 12 October 1846 onward do.

The first days

That evening the children told their employers. Mélanie's mistress, the widow Pra, believed they had seen the Blessed Virgin and told them to go to the parish priest. They went the next morning, a Sunday. The priest, Father Perrin, wept, took notes and spoke about it in his sermon.

The same Sunday evening, with Mélanie present and Maximin already taken back to Corps, three men (Baptiste Pra, Pierre Selme and Jean Moussier) wrote down the words. This "Pra report" survives only in a copy made in February 1847, but it agrees with other very early documents. Father Jean Stern, the historian of La Salette, has published these papers in full. He points out that the two children each remembered part of the message better than the other, and that their accounts came to agree more closely as they told them together.

Word spread fast. On the first anniversary, 19 September 1847, at least 30,000 pilgrims climbed the mountain, according to the Missionaries' history. People spoke of healings and of a spring that now ran where it had been dry.

The bishop's inquiries, 1846 to 1851

Bishop Philibert de Bruillard of Grenoble was personally convinced by about November 1846, but he wanted firm evidence before judging. He asked for reports in late 1846 from professors of the major seminary and from canons of his cathedral. The canons asked for a wider study. So two men, Canon Orcel and Canon Rousselot, carried out a new inquiry on the spot from July to September 1847.

In November and December 1847 sixteen senior priests met at the bishop's house to study their report. Most concluded that the apparition was authentic. A cure at Avallon on 21 November 1847 was examined by the local archbishop, Mellon Jolly of Sens, who on 4 May 1849 recognised it as miraculous, according to the sources on Mélanie's life. The bishop allowed Rousselot's report to be published, with his own letter of approval.

Opposition, and the incident at Ars

La Salette was attacked from the start. Anticlerical papers mocked it. More painful was the opposition among priests, especially in the dioceses of Grenoble and Lyon. Cardinal de Bonald of Lyon, the bishop's own metropolitan, was unconvinced and favoured the critics.

The hardest blow came in September 1850. Maximin, then 15, was taken to Ars by two supporters, against the bishop's wishes, to ask St John Vianney about his vocation. After the meeting the holy Curé told several people that the boy had admitted he had seen nothing. Maximin denied this in writing that November. He said there had been a misunderstanding, and his explanations did not always match. The Curé stood by what he had heard, though he wrote that if the fact was the work of God, man would not destroy it. Father Stern, who favours the apparition, thinks the boy, fond of teasing people, may simply have tried to fool him. For years the Curé's doubt troubled him. According to those who knew him, including his helper Catherine Lassagne, he later came to believe in La Salette again. Historians still weigh this episode.

Bishop de Bruillard, for his part, did not think the children could have made up the whole event. He went ahead.

The judgment of 1851, and what followed

The bishop's doctrinal letter carries the date 19 September 1851, the fifth anniversary. It seems to have been adjusted after remarks from Cardinal Lambruschini in Rome, and it was published in November, which is why some reference books, including the Catholic Encyclopedia, give 16 November 1851. Its words are given under "How the Church sees it".

In 1852 de Bruillard resigned. On 1 May 1852 he announced a shrine on the mountain and a body of priests to serve it, the Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette. The first stone of the church was laid on 25 May 1852. The critics took the arrival of a new bishop, Jacques Ginoulhiac, as their chance to renew their attacks. He instead held a new inquiry and confirmed the decision. The shrine dates this confirmation to 1855, and the Missionaries' history records a statement of 1853. He is remembered for saying that the mission of the shepherds was finished, and that of the Church was beginning.

Two priests, Abbé Deléon and Abbé Cartellier, went on to claim that the "beautiful lady" was really a former nun, Mademoiselle de La Merlière, who had played a trick on the children. She sued for defamation and lost, on 2 May 1855 and again on appeal on 6 May 1857. The story has no serious support.

What became of the two seers

The bishop's judgment did not depend on the later lives of the children, and those lives were not easy. Fame came to them young. They were questioned, flattered and used by people with causes of their own.

Maximin never settled. He tried the seminary at Grenoble and elsewhere, worked in a nursing home, studied medicine without finishing, and worked in a pharmacy. In 1865 he went to Rome and joined the Papal Zouaves, the Pope's volunteer soldiers. In 1866 he published a short profession of faith in the apparition. He lost money in a business that used his name to sell liqueur. He returned at last to Corps, poor and helped by the priests of the shrine. He made a final pilgrimage up the mountain in November 1874 and died at Corps on 1 March 1875, aged 39. The Catholic Encyclopedia says he died "a holy death". He never denied the apparition in public.

Mélanie entered the Sisters of Providence at Corenc and took the veil in October 1851. Bishop Ginoulhiac did not admit her to profession, judging her not ready. She went to the Carmel at Darlington in England in 1855 and took temporary vows in 1856. In 1860 she was released and returned to the continent. She lived with the Sisters of Compassion at Marseille and in Greece, then for about seventeen years at Castellammare near Naples. She moved between Italy and France, kept writing, and tried to found a new religious order with its own rule, which she said had come from the apparition. Bishops did not accept this. She spent her last months unknown at Altamura in southern Italy, going daily to Mass in the cathedral, and was found dead in her home in December 1904. Sources give the 14th or the 15th. She has not been beatified. An often repeated report that St Pius X spoke warmly of her is not a judgment of the Church, which has made none on her holiness.

The main messages

Everything was said on one afternoon, 19 September 1846. The words below follow common English translations of the early accounts. The wording varies a little between versions.

The greeting"Come near, my children, do not be afraid. I am here to tell you great news."
Her sorrowIf her people will not submit, she says she must let go the arm of her Son, which is so heavy she can no longer hold it. She has long suffered for them and prays without ceasing.
Sunday and the NameThe seventh day is not kept, and carters cannot swear without using her Son's name. These two things, she says, are what make his arm so heavy.
The harvestShe speaks of the rotting potatoes of the past year and warns of a spoiled harvest, of famine, and of the sufferings of children. Here she changes from French to the local dialect, so that the children will understand.
The promiseIf people are converted, "the rocks will become piles of wheat", and the potatoes will seem to have sown themselves.
PrayerShe asks the children if they say their prayers well. When they admit they hardly do, she tells them to pray at night and in the morning, and at least an Our Father and a Hail Mary when they have no time.
Mass and LentOnly a few old women go to Mass in summer; others work on Sundays; in Lent people go to the butcher's "like dogs".
A homely proofShe reminds Maximin of a day at Coin, when his father showed him spoiled wheat and gave him bread. He had forgotten it, and now he remembered.
The charge"Well, my children, you will make this known to all my people." She says it twice.

In one line

A weeping Mother calls her people back to Sunday, to reverence for God's name, and to daily prayer, and promises mercy to those who turn.

Nothing new in the faith

The message repeats the Commandments and the Gospel's call to repent. St John Paul II summed it up as penance, perseverance in prayer and faithfulness to Sunday. It teaches no new doctrine and gives no date for anything still to come.

How the Church sees it

JudgmentBishop de Bruillard wrote: "We judge that the Apparition of the Blessed Virgin to the two cowherds... bears within itself all the characteristics of truth, and that the faithful have grounds for believing it indubitable and certain." He authorised the devotion and preaching on it. He rested this on the study of the facts, on cures, and on the spiritual fruits, especially conversions.
Who inquiredSeminary professors and canons (1846), Canons Orcel and Rousselot (1847), a conference of sixteen priests (late 1847), a special commission on the Ars affair (1850), and Bishop Ginoulhiac's later inquiry.
The Holy SeeThe judgment is the bishop's. Rome did not rule on the dispute at the time. The Holy Office later protected the devotion by name: its decree of 21 December 1915 says that it does not forbid devotion to the Blessed Virgin under the title of Reconciler, commonly called "of La Salette".
PopesLeo XIII granted a canonical coronation of the image on 21 August 1879, the year the church was made a minor basilica. For the 150th anniversary St John Paul II wrote to Bishop Louis Dufaux of Grenoble (6 May 1996), calling La Salette "a message of hope". He wrote that her tears help us grasp the gravity of sin and the faithful love of her Son.
Feast19 September, kept at the shrine and by the Missionaries. French reference works report a decree of the Congregation for Divine Worship of 18 March 2016 placing it in the calendar for France as an optional memorial. The decree itself has not been checked here.
The seersNeither has been canonised or beatified. Approval of an apparition is not a judgment on the later life or later statements of its seers.
The "secrets": an honest account

This is the most disputed part of La Salette, and it needs plain words.

1851. On Bishop de Bruillard's advice, and after pressure from Cardinal de Bonald, each child wrote down their secret. Both were sent to Pius IX and were handed over in July 1851. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports two opposite accounts of how the Pope reacted, and it is not known which is right. In 1999 Father Michel Corteville reported finding texts of 1851 in the Vatican archives. The Church has not given any judgment on them.

1879. Mélanie published a much longer "secret" at Lecce in Italy, with the imprimatur of its bishop, Salvatore Zola. It had harsh attacks on clergy and frightening predictions. A bitter quarrel followed about whether this was the same text she had sent in 1851. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports the view of "wise and prudent persons" that the later text was a work of imagination, shaped by her reading of apocalyptic books. In 1880 the cardinal secretary of the Holy Office wrote to a French bishop that the Holy Office was displeased and wished copies withdrawn, though it is not clear that this was a formal condemnation. Most defenders of the text were censured by their bishops.

1915. The Holy Office, under Benedict XV, forbade all the faithful to write about or discuss the so-called "Secret of La Salette", in any of its forms or its application to present or future times, with penalties. In the same decree it protected the devotion itself.

1923. By decree of 9 May 1923, approved by Pius XI, the Holy Office condemned a 1922 reprint of Mélanie's text and told bishops to take copies out of the hands of the faithful (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 15, pages 287 to 288). The Index of Forbidden Books was later abolished, but that did not turn this text into an approved one.

What this means for us. The Church approved the public message of 1846. She has never approved the published "secret". Nobody should build fear, dates or predictions, or attacks on the Pope and the clergy, on it. Groups of prophecy enthusiasts, some with political aims, used Mélanie's name in her lifetime and after. They do not speak for La Salette.

Other questions people raise

The words of the message. In 1928 the Jesuit scholar Hippolyte Delehaye argued that the message resembles the old "letters from heaven", popular pamphlets about Sunday. He pointed to the words about "six days" and "the seventh", which sound strange on Mary's lips. The Church's approval does not make every phrase a dictated word: the message reaches us through two unschooled children and the first people who wrote it down. Such questions are one reason the Church binds no one to believe in an apparition.

The "arm of my Son". This is an image, not a teaching that Mary holds back an angry Christ. Jesus is the one Saviour and his love does not need persuading. St John Paul II reads the image the right way: for the one who walks humbly, her Son's arm will not press down to condemn, but will take the outstretched hand. Mary, in this reading, is a mother who prays and weeps with us.

The fruits

  • Pilgrims. At least 30,000 on 19 September 1847 (Missionaries' history). Today the numbers are less certain: French reference works give about 300,000 a year, while one Catholic newspaper report says more than a million. The shrine's own pages give no figure that we could find.
  • A confraternity. A confraternity of Our Lady Reconciler was authorised in the diocese. By 1849, 15,000 people had joined, according to the Missionaries.
  • The Missionaries of Our Lady of La Salette. Founded by Bishop de Bruillard in 1852 and now a religious congregation serving in about 25 countries. They had to leave France for a time under the anticlerical laws, and Bishop Caillot recalled them to the shrine in 1943.
  • In India. La Salette priests from India, trained in the Philippines, opened a mission in Kerala in 1988. India became a province of the congregation in 2006. There is a shrine of Our Lady of La Salette at Kayakunnu in Wayanad, and another at Kodaikanal in Tamil Nadu.
  • The sisters and laity. The Sisters of Our Lady of La Salette began in 1871, and there are lay people associated with La Salette spirituality.
  • Conversions. Bishop de Bruillard and St John Paul II both point to changed lives, a return to the sacraments and to Sunday Mass, as the chief fruit. This matters more than any wonder.
  • Shrines elsewhere. These include Attleboro, Massachusetts (the United States national shrine), Gdańsk in Poland and Oliveira de Azeméis in Portugal.

If you visit

The shrine stands at about 1,800 metres in the French Alps, above the town of Corps in Isère, and is reached by a mountain road from Corps. Statues mark the place of the apparition, near the basilica. The shrine offers Mass, confession and lodging for pilgrims. Mountain weather can change plans, so check hours and lodging with the shrine first, at lasalette.cef.fr.

Read it rightly

Approved apparitions are private revelations. The Catechism teaches that no new public revelation is to be expected before Christ's return, and that private revelations do not improve or complete the deposit of faith. They may help people live it more fully in a certain time (CCC 66 to 67). No Catholic is obliged to believe in La Salette. Its stern words are a call to conversion, not a timetable of disasters, and the mercy it promises is the mercy of the Gospel.
