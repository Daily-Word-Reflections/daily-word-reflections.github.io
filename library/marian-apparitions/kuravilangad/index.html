Marian apparitions · India · local tradition, date disputed

Our Lady of Kuravilangad

Marth Mariam, the Muthiyamma of Kuravilangad

Approval status A local tradition, with no formal judgment. No Church authority is known to have inquired into the apparition or ruled on it. The church honours Our Lady under this tradition with the full knowledge of its pastors, and it is a major pilgrim church of the Syro-Malabar Church. That honours the place and the devotion. It does not confirm the event, its date, or the claim that it was the first apparition of Mary in the world.
  • Seers Unnamed children tending flocks
  • Dates Given variously: 105, 335 or 345 AD; no written record of the time
  • Place today Kuravilangad, Kottayam district, Kerala; Syro-Malabar Eparchy of Palai
  • Great days Moonnu Nombu, the three-day fast before Lent, with the Kappalottam
What is told

This page is written with care, because the tradition is dear to many Kerala families and because love for it does not need claims it cannot bear. It tells the story as the tradition tells it, then says what history can and cannot show. The words of the Saint Thomas Christians are used here on purpose: Marth Mariam (Lady Mary, in Syriac), Muthiyamma (a Malayalam name of tender respect, the grandmother or elder mother), Moonnu Nombu (the three-day fast), madbaha (the sanctuary), Kappalottam (the running of the ship). In the Malayalam page the Syro-Malabar words for our Lord and for the Qurbana are used as well, because they are this church’s own.

The families from Palayur

According to the tradition of the Saint Thomas Christians, the apostle came to Kerala in AD 52 and founded a church at Palayur, baptising families remembered as Kalli, Kalikavu, Pakalomattom and Sankarapuri. From there, it is told, some of these families travelled south by way of Ettumanoor and settled near Kuravilangad, in the beginning of the second century according to one telling.

The children and the spring

Some children were tending their flock on the low hills of what was then forest. The heat, hunger and thirst drove them to look for water and roots to eat, and they lost their way. They called on God for help. In the empty forest a woman stood before them, holding a child with a cross in his hand. In the telling on the parish’s own document, as reported, she took up stones which became bread for them. Then she scratched the ground with her fingers, and a spring of water rose up. When they had eaten and drunk, they looked for her and she was gone.

They reached home at dusk and told their parents, who did not believe it. The next time the elders went back with the children to the place, and there, in a dry season, was a spring of clear water. The Blessed Virgin, carrying the infant Jesus, appeared again and told them to build a church in her name at that spot. Then she vanished. So the church was built on the hill and named for Marth Mariam. Some tellings speak of three boys. The spring still flows, and people still draw from it. They say it has never dried.

Nothing else is told of the children: no names, no later lives, no message beyond the request for a church. A manuscript of Fr Vichensa de Maria, cited in a history of the church by George, is said to tell of another appearance of the Muthiyamma; that account could not be read for this page.

How old is it? What can and cannot be shown

Here it is best to be plain. Four dates are in circulation, and they do not agree.

105 ADThe date of foundation in the parish’s tradition, and the one repeated by many writers and directories. It rests on tradition alone. No source of that age is known.
335 ADVery often given for the apparition, and the sign at the spring in 1995 said “4th century”. An inscription dated AD 337 is reported on the old Mani Malika, the bell house. It is reported by a Thomas Christian history website, and no scholarly publication of that inscription could be found.
345 ADThe Kerala government’s tourism pages say the church was built then and consecrated by Mar Joseph, who is said to have come with Knai Thoma. This too is tradition, and is told of the Knanaya migration.
About 1160The Niranam Grandhavari, a chronicle written around 1773, is reported as placing the coming of the families to Kuravilangad much later. One historian, M. Kurian Thomas, therefore reads “335” or “the 4th century” as the Malayalam Era, not the Christian era. M.E. 335 would be about AD 1160. This chronicle is itself much later than what it tells, and was written within another Thomas Christian community with concerns of its own.

What is well attested. The Portuguese account of Archbishop Menezes’s journey, the Jornada (printed 1606), reports according to a local historian who gives its pages that the archbishop laid the foundation stone of a new church at Kuravilangad in 1599, and that the old church was very dark and in the old style. So in 1599 a church already stood here, old enough to need replacing. In 1657 the papal envoy Sebastiani described a very beautiful church of St Mary Major here, with a chapel of St Sebastian and a fine granite cross. A bell with a Syriac inscription, “Mother of God”, is reported as cast in 1584. These are the firmer points. Their witness is that this was already an old, important Marian church when Europeans first wrote of it.

What cannot be shown. That an apparition happened, on any date. No known document from before the 16th century mentions it. The earliest form of the story has not been traced in any study available for this page. A devotional article says that Menezes himself recorded an appearance of Mary here in 1599, but no such passage could be found, and it is not repeated here as fact. Very little writing survives from the earlier centuries of the Thomas Christians, so silence proves little either way. A church’s long memory deserves respect, but it is not the same as a record.

“The first apparition in the world.” This phrase is often used, and printed signs at the church have made the point. It cannot be established. Spain’s tradition of Our Lady of the Pillar, for one, is set in the first century, and it too is a tradition. No one can rank such memories by date. It is fairer to say that Kuravilangad holds one of the oldest Marian traditions in India, and that the people who kept it were Christians long before Europeans reached Kerala. A writer in the Catholics and Cultures study of the church noted what the claim does for the faithful here: it shows that the faith is no foreign import. That part is true, whatever the date of the spring.

The main messages

To the childrenNo words are remembered. She feeds the hungry and gives water to the thirsty.
To the eldersShe asks that a church be built in her name at the spring.

In one line

A mother finds lost and hungry children, feeds them, and asks for a house of prayer.

Nothing new in the faith

There is no message to believe here. The tradition points to the Child in her arms, the cross in his hand, and the worship of God in his Church.

How the Church sees it

JudgmentNo inquiry or judgment is known. The tradition is one of long custom, older than any of the Church’s modern procedures. Under the 2024 norms of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, the Church in any case does not usually declare events supernatural.
StandingThe parish belongs to the Syro-Malabar Eparchy of Palai, and was made a forane church on 25 July 1950. It is known today as the Major Archiepiscopal Marth Mariam Archdeacon Pilgrim Church. A report in January 2018 spoke of the special status given to the shrine, but that report could not be read for this page, and the decree itself was not found. Please ask the parish for the exact text and date.
FeastNo separate liturgical feast of the apparition could be confirmed. The parish’s great feast in January honours St Sebastian, and the great days of the Muthiyamma’s church are those of Moonnu Nombu.
The church’s place in historyKerala Tourism calls this the birthplace of five archdeacons, the native heads of the Thomas Christians. After the Coonan Cross Oath of 1653 the community was divided. Archdeacon Thomas became Mar Thoma I, first in the line of the Malankara bishops. Chandy Parambil, in Latin Alexander de Campo, who had been vicar here, was consecrated on 1 February 1663 as bishop of the Catholics, called by one history the first known Indian bishop. He governed from Kuravilangad and died on 2 January 1687. His tomb is in the madbaha. The families of both men have been discussed by historians, and details remain disputed. The other Christians who trace their line to these same men are our brothers and sisters.
Nidhirikal Mani KathanarFr Emmanuel Nidhiri (1842 to 1904), vicar here, was a leader of Catholic renewal and of efforts toward Christian unity, and first editor of the daily Deepika. He began the parish’s English school in 1894. K. R. Narayanan, later President of India, studied there.

The fruits

Moonnu Nombu. Three weeks before the Great Fast, on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday, the churches of the Syriac tradition keep the Rogation of the Ninevites, recalling Jonah (Jon 3). The fast came to the Thomas Christians from the Church of the East, where it is said to have begun during a great plague in the sixth century. It is kept in many Kerala parishes, and Kuravilangad is its best-known home. More than a hundred thousand people are reported to come, by one estimate. No official count was found.

The Kappalottam. During the fast a wooden ship about forty feet long, with masts, rigging and wooden sailors, and with a figure of Jonah, is carried on men’s shoulders down the great steps and through the crowd. They run with it and rock it as if on a stormy sea. The right to do this is held by the men of Kadappoor, once a place of sailors, who are said to have given the ship. A people who lived by the sea’s trade are remembered in the way they keep this fast.

Good neighbours. Kerala Tourism records the memory that the Ettumanoor Mahadeva temple gave a paddy field for the rice cooked for the feast, along with a hanging lamp and a great brass vessel, and that the festival’s elephants date from then. It is a tradition worth keeping for its own sake.

What the church holds. The bell of 1584 with its Syriac words; three great bells brought from Germany in 1911; murals on either side of the madbaha, of the Baptism of Christ and of Moses with the Commandments; a painted stone image of Our Lady with the Child; an open-air granite cross with lamps around its foot. Sources disagree on the date of the cross, whether 1575 or between 1638 and 1657, and on its height. The church itself was rebuilt in the 1950s, finished in 1960 by Fr Thomas Manakkatt, who kept the old sanctuary.

If you visit

Kuravilangad is on the M.C. Road in Kottayam district, about 22 kilometres north of Kottayam town. Climb the steps to the church, pray in the old sanctuary and at the tomb of Mar Chandy, see the bells and the cross, and go down to the spring. Come in the three days of Moonnu Nombu for the crowds and the ship, and on an ordinary morning for the quiet. The date of the fast moves with Easter and usually falls between late January and February.

Read it rightly

Even apparitions that the Church has judged are private revelations. They add nothing to the faith and no Catholic is obliged to believe them, though they may help us live the Gospel more fully (CCC 66 to 67). A Kerala Catholic may love this tradition, and may also ask what can be proved, with no disloyalty in either. Our faith in St Thomas’s Lord, and our love for his Mother, do not hang on the age of a spring. What Kuravilangad surely shows is a people who have called on Marth Mariam for many centuries, and have brought their children to her Son.
