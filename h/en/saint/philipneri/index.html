St Philip Neri

Priest, founder of the Congregation of the Oratory · Feast: 26 May

Open in the Daily Word app

Word for the day

“Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, Rejoice.” Philippians 4:4

Life

Philip Neri was born in Florence on 21 July 1515, the son of a notary, Francesco Neri. His mother died when he was young. He was a cheerful, obedient boy whom people called good Pippo. He learned much from the Dominican friars of San Marco. At about eighteen he was sent to work with a relative in business at San Germano, near Monte Cassino, but he soon felt that God was calling him elsewhere, and around 1534 he went to Rome with almost nothing.

In Rome he became tutor to the sons of a Florentine, who gave him a small room and food. He studied philosophy and theology for a time, then sold his books and gave the money to the poor. For about thirteen years he lived as a layman, praying long hours, often at night in the catacombs of St Sebastian, and spending his days in the streets and shops, talking with young men and drawing them to God and to the sacraments. At Pentecost in 1544, while praying in the catacombs, he had an experience of the Holy Spirit that he spoke of only near the end of his life. In 1548, with his confessor, he founded a confraternity to care for poor pilgrims and the sick, which served thousands in the Holy Year of 1550.

On 23 May 1551 he was ordained priest. He lived with a community of priests at San Girolamo della Carità and spent long hours in the confessional. Young men came to his room in the afternoons for prayer, reading, conversation and music. When the room became too small, a hall was built over the church for them, and it was called the Oratory. Philip also led walking pilgrimages to the seven churches of Rome, with prayer, singing and a meal in the open air. In 1575 Pope Gregory XIII approved the Congregation of the Oratory, communities of secular priests living together without vows, and gave them the church of Santa Maria in Vallicella, which they rebuilt as the Chiesa Nuova.

Philip was sought out by poor people and by cardinals, by artists and musicians, and by several popes. His disciple Caesar Baronius, later a cardinal, wrote the great history of the Church at his direction. Philip was known for his humour, which he used to keep himself and others humble, and for his deep joy in God. He died in Rome in the early hours of 26 May 1595, after the feast of Corpus Christi. He was canonised on 12 March 1622, together with Ignatius of Loyola, Francis Xavier, Teresa of Avila and Isidore the Farmer. He is often called the second apostle of Rome. St John Henry Newman brought his Oratory to England in 1848.

Stories

The feathers (legend)

A popular story tells that a woman confessed to Philip that she often gossiped. For penance he told her to pluck a chicken while walking through the streets, and then to come back. When she returned, he told her to go and gather up all the feathers. She said it was impossible, for the wind had scattered them. So it is with words spoken against others, he answered.

The fire of Pentecost (tradition)

Philip's early biographers say that in 1544, while he was praying to the Holy Spirit in the catacombs, a ball of fire seemed to enter his mouth and come to rest in his heart. From then on he felt a burning in his chest and a trembling of love. After his death, the doctors who examined his body reported that two of his ribs were raised over the heart.

The hat he would not wear (tradition)

Philip's friends tell that Pope Gregory XIV and later Pope Clement VIII wished to make him a cardinal. He would not accept and turned the offer aside with a joke. He preferred to remain a simple priest in his little room among the young.

Homily

1. Rejoice in the Lord

Christian joy is a gift of the Spirit (Philippians 4:4).

2. A friend to the young

He met them in the streets and led them to God.

3. Holy humour

He laughed at himself and stayed humble.

Dear friends, St Paul writes, “Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, Rejoice” (Philippians 4:4). Some people think that saints must be gloomy. St Philip Neri shows us the opposite. He was one of the most joyful men of his time, and he drew thousands of people to God by that joy.

Philip came to Rome as a young man with nothing. The city was full of wealth and also of corruption, and many had grown cold in faith. Philip did not complain about the times. He went into the streets, the shops and the squares, and talked with young people. He invited them to pray, to go to confession, to visit the sick in the hospitals. He made holiness attractive.

His joy did not come from an easy life. It came from long hours of prayer, from the Holy Spirit who filled his heart, and from his love for the Eucharist and for confession. He also had a great sense of humour, which he used against his own pride. When people praised him, he would do something foolish to make them laugh. A humble heart is a joyful heart.

Today many young people are searching for meaning. They need priests, parents and teachers who listen to them and show them that following Christ brings joy. At this Holy Mass / Holy Qurbana, let us pray for the young people of our parish, for confessors, that they may be patient and kind like Philip, and for ourselves, that we may rejoice in the Lord always. Amen.

Prayer

O God, who raised St Philip Neri to the glory of holiness through the joy of your Holy Spirit, grant through his prayers that we may serve you with cheerful hearts, bring young people to your love, and find in the sacrament of mercy the peace that you alone can give. Amen.
