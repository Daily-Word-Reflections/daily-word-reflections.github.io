Pope St Gregory VII

Pope, reformer of the Church · Feast: 25 May

Open in the Daily Word app

Word for the day

“You love righteousness and hate wickedness.” Psalm 45:7

Life

Hildebrand was born around 1020 at Sovana in Tuscany, in a family of modest means. As a boy he was sent to Rome to be educated, and he grew up close to the monastery of St Mary on the Aventine, where an uncle is said to have been abbot. He became chaplain to Pope Gregory VI and went with him into exile in Germany in 1046. When Pope Leo IX began the reform of the Church in 1049, he brought Hildebrand back to Rome, and for more than twenty years he served five popes as a trusted adviser and administrator.

The Church of that time was weakened by two great evils. Many church offices were bought and sold, which was called simony, and many clergy did not keep celibacy. Behind both lay the custom by which kings and nobles chose bishops and abbots and invested them with ring and staff, as if they were officials of the crown. The reforming popes worked to restore the holiness of the clergy and the right of the Church to choose her own pastors. In 1059 a decree reserved the election of the pope to the cardinals, to protect it from the pressure of Roman families and of the emperor.

When Pope Alexander II died in April 1073, the clergy and people gathered at his funeral acclaimed Hildebrand as pope. He took the name Gregory. In his synods he forbade simony and lay investiture and called the clergy to a chaste and holy life. This brought him into conflict with the young King Henry IV of Germany. In 1076 bishops loyal to Henry declared the Pope deposed, and Gregory in turn excommunicated the king. In January 1077 Henry came to the castle of Canossa in northern Italy and stood for three days at the gate as a penitent, until Gregory absolved him.

The peace did not last. In 1080 Gregory excommunicated Henry again, and Henry had a rival pope elected. In 1084 Henry entered Rome and Gregory took refuge in the Castel Sant'Angelo. He was rescued by the Normans of southern Italy, but their soldiers plundered the city, and the Romans turned against the Pope. Gregory withdrew to Monte Cassino and then to Salerno, where he died on 25 May 1085. His last words were taken from the psalm: I have loved justice and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile. His name was placed in the Roman Martyrology in 1584, and in 1728 his feast was extended to the whole Latin Church.

Stories

The snow at Canossa (history)

In January 1077 Henry IV crossed the Alps in winter and came to the castle of Canossa, where the Pope was staying as the guest of Countess Matilda of Tuscany. For three days the king stood at the gate in the clothes of a penitent. Matilda and Abbot Hugh of Cluny pleaded for him. On the third day Gregory received him, absolved him and gave him Communion.

A letter to a Muslim king (history)

In 1076 Gregory wrote to al-Nasir, the Muslim ruler of a kingdom in North Africa, to thank him for kindness to Christians and for releasing some captives. He wrote that Christians and Muslims believe in and confess one God, though in different ways. The Second Vatican Council cited this letter in its declaration Nostra Aetate.

The cry at his election (history)

At the funeral of Alexander II in the Lateran in 1073, the people suddenly cried out that Hildebrand must be pope. Gregory later wrote that he was carried off by force and that he was filled with fear at the burden laid on him.

Homily

1. Love of justice

He loved righteousness (Psalm 45:7).

2. A holy priesthood

He called the clergy back to their first love.

3. The cost of fidelity

He died in exile, but not defeated.

Dear friends, the psalm says of the king anointed by God, “You love righteousness and hate wickedness” (Psalm 45:7). Pope St Gregory VII made those words his own as he lay dying in exile at Salerno. He had spent his life for the reform of the Church, and at the end it seemed that he had lost everything.

Gregory lived in a time when church offices were bought and sold, when many priests lived carelessly, and when kings treated bishops as their servants. He saw that the Church could not be the light of the world if her shepherds were chosen for money or power. So he worked, first as adviser to other popes and then as pope himself, to make the clergy holy and to let the Church choose her own pastors.

It brought him great suffering. He was opposed by an emperor, by bishops and finally by the people of Rome. Yet at Canossa, when the king came as a penitent, Gregory did not refuse him mercy. Firmness and mercy belong together in a good shepherd. We too face the temptation to make the Church a place of power, money or politics. Gregory reminds us that the Church belongs to Christ and exists for the salvation of souls.

Gregory died thinking he had failed. But the reform he began renewed the Church for centuries, and the Church now honours him as a saint. Fidelity is never wasted, even when we do not see its fruit. At this Holy Mass / Holy Qurbana, let us pray for our Holy Father and our bishops, for priests, that they may live holy lives, and for ourselves, that we may love justice and keep faith when it is hard. Amen.

Prayer

O God, who gave Pope St Gregory VII the strength to work for the holiness and liberty of your Church, grant through his prayers that our pastors may be faithful and pure of heart, that the Church may serve you without fear, and that we may love justice even when it costs us dearly. Amen.
