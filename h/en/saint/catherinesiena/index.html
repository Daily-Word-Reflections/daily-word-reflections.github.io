St Catherine of Siena

Virgin, Doctor of the Church, patron of Italy and of Europe · Feast: 29 April

Open in the Daily Word app

Word for the day

“I came to bring fire to the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled!” Luke 12:49

Life

Catherine Benincasa was born in Siena, in Italy, on 25 March 1347, one of the youngest of the many children of Giacomo, a wool dyer, and his wife Lapa. According to her confessor and biographer, Blessed Raymond of Capua, she saw the Lord in a vision when she was about six, and soon afterwards promised her virginity to God. When her family wanted her to marry, she resisted firmly, even cutting off her hair. At last her father gave way.

At about the age of sixteen she was received among the Mantellate of Siena, women of the Dominican Third Order who lived in their own homes, wearing the white habit and black mantle. For some years she lived almost hidden in a small room of the family house, in prayer and penance, and she went out mainly to church. In 1367, as she later told Raymond, she experienced a mystical marriage with Christ. From then on she understood that her love for him must be lived out among people: she nursed the sick, cared for the poor and the dying, especially when plague struck Siena in 1374, and visited prisoners.

A group of disciples gathered around her, priests, religious and lay people, who called her mother. She could not write in her youth, so she dictated hundreds of letters to popes, kings, queens, soldiers, nuns and ordinary families. Some 380 survive. She worked to make peace between quarrelling cities and families. In 1376 she went to Avignon in France, where the popes had lived for about seventy years, and urged Pope Gregory XI to return to Rome, the see of Peter. He did so in January 1377. She called the Pope “sweet Christ on earth”, yet she spoke to him and to the clergy with great frankness about the reform of the Church.

In 1377 and 1378 she dictated the book now called the Dialogue, a conversation between the soul and God about truth, love, the Blood of Christ and the bridge that leads to him. When the Great Western Schism broke out in 1378, she supported Pope Urban VI and moved to Rome to pray and work for unity. Worn out, she died there on 29 April 1380, aged thirty-three. She is buried in the church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva. Pope Pius II canonised her in 1461. Pius XII named her a patron of Italy in 1939, St Paul VI declared her a Doctor of the Church in 1970, one of the first two women so honoured, and St John Paul II named her a patron of Europe in 1999.

Lessons

Prayer that goes out

Her hidden years of prayer sent her out to the sick and the poor.

Love for the Church

She loved the Church enough to work and suffer for its renewal.

Courage in truth

She spoke the truth humbly and clearly, even to popes.

The cell of self-knowledge

She taught that knowing God and knowing ourselves go together.

Stories

The young man on the scaffold (history)

In a letter to Raymond of Capua Catherine describes how she visited Niccolò di Toldo, a young man condemned to death in Siena, who was in despair. She prayed with him, he received Communion, and she promised to be with him at the end. She stood by him at the execution and received his head into her hands, and she writes that he died calling on Jesus.

The ring no one else could see (tradition)

Raymond of Capua writes that at the end of Carnival in 1367 Christ appeared to Catherine with Our Lady and the saints and espoused her to himself with a ring, which ever afterwards she alone could see. Raymond records it as she told it to him.

The wounds at Pisa (tradition)

At Pisa in 1375, after receiving Communion, Catherine is said to have received the wounds of Christ, at her own prayer hidden from others, and seen only after her death. The account comes from Raymond, who was present at Pisa.

Words of the saint

If you are what you ought to be, you will set the whole of Italy on fire.
St Catherine of Siena, from a letter to one of her disciples, in substance
You, eternal Trinity, are a deep sea: the more I enter you, the more I find, and the more I find, the more I seek you.
St Catherine of Siena, Dialogue 167, in substance

Homily

1. Fire on the earth

Jesus came to kindle a fire (Luke 12:49).

2. From the cell to the street

Her prayer became service and peacemaking.

3. Love for the Church

She spoke the truth because she loved.

Dear friends, Jesus once said, “I came to bring fire to the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled!” (Luke 12:49). St Catherine of Siena let that fire catch hold of her. She was a dyer's daughter, without schooling, who died at thirty-three. Yet she is a Doctor of the Church, a teacher for the whole world, and a patron of Italy and of Europe.

Her fire began in a small room. For years she lived in her father's house in silence and prayer. She called it the cell of self-knowledge: in the light of God she saw her own weakness, and in her own weakness she saw how great his mercy is. We too need such a room, a corner of our home or a quiet time each day, where God can teach us who he is and who we are.

But Catherine did not stay in the room. Love sent her out to nurse the plague-stricken, to sit with a condemned young man until his last breath, to make peace between enemies, and to travel to Avignon to beg the Pope to return to Rome. She loved the Church as a mother. Because she loved it, she spoke plainly about what needed to be healed, but she never walked away from it. That is a lesson for us when we are hurt by the faults of people in the Church.

In these weeks of the Resurrection, the risen Lord breathes his Spirit of fire on us. At this Holy Mass / Holy Qurbana, Catherine would point us to the Blood of Christ, which she loved to speak of, poured out for us. Let us ask her to obtain for us a burning love for Jesus, a humble knowledge of ourselves, and the courage to serve and to heal. Amen.

Prayer

O God, who set St Catherine of Siena on fire with love of you and of your Church, grant through her prayers that we may know ourselves in the light of your goodness, serve the poor and the sick with gentle hands, and work with courage and humility for the unity and holiness of your Church. Amen.
