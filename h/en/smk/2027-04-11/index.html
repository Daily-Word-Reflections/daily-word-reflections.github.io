Word for the day

“Jesus said to them, “Come and have breakfast.”” John 21:12

Reflection

After the Resurrection Peter says, “I am going fishing,” and the others go with him. They fish all night and catch nothing. At dawn a stranger on the shore says: “Cast the net to the right side of the boat, and you will find some.” The net fills so full they cannot haul it in. The beloved disciple says: “It is the Lord!” Peter jumps into the sea to reach him.

On the shore Jesus has already made a charcoal fire, with fish and bread. “Come and have breakfast.” The risen Lord of glory cooks for his tired friends. He meets them in their ordinary work, in their failure, and feeds them. Every Holy Qurbono is this breakfast on the shore: the risen Lord takes bread and gives it to us.

Then, beside another charcoal fire, Jesus heals Peter's wound. Three times Peter had denied him by a fire in the high priest's courtyard; three times Jesus asks: “Simon son of John, do you love me?” Three times Peter answers: “Lord, you know that I love you.” And three times Jesus entrusts him: “Feed my lambs… tend my sheep… feed my sheep.” Failure is not the end; love restores and sends.

Moses sang at the sea: “The LORD is my strength and my might, and he has become my salvation.” Wisdom says: “God did not make death.” Paul preaches in Antioch: “God raised him from the dead.” The risen Christ turns empty nights into full nets, and denials into love.

Homily outline

Theme: “It is the Lord!”

After the Resurrection the disciples go back to fishing on the lake. All night they catch nothing. At dawn a stranger tells them where to cast the net, and they recognise him. He has prepared breakfast for them.

1. The night of empty nets

  • Peter says “I am going fishing”: back to the old life.
  • “That night they caught nothing”: our efforts without the Lord bear little fruit.
  • The Psalmist knew it: unless the Lord builds the house, the builders labour in vain.
“Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain.” Psalm 127:1
“Simon Peter said to them, “I am going fishing.” They said to him, “We will go with you.” They went out and got into the boat, but that night they caught nothing.” John 21:3

Apply: Begin each workday this week with a short prayer offering your work to God.

2. At his word, abundance

  • At dawn Jesus stands on the shore; they do not recognise him.
  • They obey: “Cast the net to the right side”, and the net is full.
  • The beloved disciple sees with the eyes of love: “It is the Lord!”
“The LORD is my strength and my might, and he has become my salvation; this is my God, and I will praise him.” Exodus 15:2
“He said to them, “Cast the net to the right side of the boat, and you will find some.” So they cast it, and now they were not able to haul it in because there were so many fish. That disciple whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It is the Lord!”” John 21:6–7

Apply: Where have you failed recently? Try again, this time at his word and with prayer.

3. Come and have breakfast

  • The Risen Lord has lit a fire and prepared bread and fish.
  • He takes the bread and gives it to them: the gesture of the Eucharist.
  • “Taste and see that the Lord is good”: in the Qurbono he still feeds tired disciples.

Apply: Come to Holy Qurbono this week not as a duty but as an invitation from a friend.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, after Easter Peter says, “I am going fishing,” and six others go with him. They work all night and catch nothing. Many of us know such nights: hard work with nothing to show for it, prayers that seem unanswered, plans that fail.

At dawn a stranger calls from the shore: “Cast the net to the right side.” They do, and the net is full. John cries, “It is the Lord!” and Peter jumps into the water. On the beach Jesus has a fire going, with fish and bread. “Come and have breakfast.” The risen Lord cooks for his tired friends. That is our God: he meets us in our failures and feeds us.

After breakfast, beside the fire, Jesus asks Peter three times: “Do you love me?” Three times, because three times Peter had denied him by another fire. Jesus does not scold him; he lets love heal the wound. “Lord, you know that I love you.” “Feed my sheep.” Peter, forgiven, is made the shepherd of the Church.

Whatever your failures, Jesus asks you the same question today: “Do you love me?” Answer him honestly. And let your love become service: feed his lambs, in your family, your parish, your work. Every Holy Qurbono he says again: “Come and have breakfast.” Amen.

For children

Children, the disciples went fishing all night and caught nothing. In the morning a man on the beach said: “Throw your net on the right side!” And the net was full of fish!

Jesus asks you too: “Do you love me?” What will you say? Let's say: “Jesus, I love you!”

Prayer

Risen Lord, you met your disciples after a night of failure, filled their nets and fed them on the shore. Feed us at your table, heal our denials with your love, and send us to care for your flock. Lord, you know that we love you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Second Sunday after Qyomtho the Church's table is again rich. The Evening Gospel (John 21:15–19) gives the Lord's threefold question to Peter, “Do you love me?”, and his command, “Feed my sheep.” Four Old Testament lessons follow: the song of Moses at the sea (Exodus 15:1–8); the word of Wisdom that “God did not make death” (Wisdom 1:12–16); the priests with their trumpets going round the walls of Jericho (Joshua 6:8–14); and the one who comes from Edom, “mighty to save” (Isaiah 63:1–6). In Acts 13:26–41 Paul preaches at Antioch in Pisidia that God raised Jesus, and that “he whom God raised up experienced no corruption.”

We study three of these closely. Exodus is the lesson nearest to the Gospel: both take place at the sea, and in both the Lord saves as night turns to morning (Exodus 14:24, 27). Ephesians closes with a call to pray at all times and a blessing of peace, love and grace. John shows the risen Lord on the shore at daybreak, the great catch, and the breakfast he has prepared. The line runs from the sea that covered the enemy to the sea that gives up a net full of fish, and its key words are sea, daybreak and It is the Lord!

Exodus 15:1–8 · I will sing to the Lord

Setting.

Israel has just crossed the sea on dry ground, and the Egyptian chariots that followed them have been covered by the returning waters (14:21–31). Chapter 14 ends, “So the people feared the Lord and believed in the Lord and in his servant Moses” (14:31). Their first answer to salvation is a song.

The Song of the Sea is counted among the oldest poems in the Bible, and many scholars think that its language is older than the prose around it. Miriam takes up its opening words with the women and their tambourines (15:20–21). The reading gives the first part of the song, which tells of the victory at the sea; the second part tells of God leading his people to his holy dwelling (15:13–18).

Structure.

  • v. 1: Moses and Israel sing: horse and rider thrown into the sea.
  • vv. 2–3: the Lord is my strength, my salvation, my God; the Lord is a warrior.
  • vv. 4–5: Pharaoh's chariots sink like a stone.
  • vv. 6–7: your right hand, O Lord, shatters the enemy.
  • v. 8: at the blast of your nostrils the waters stood up in a heap.
  • ¶ The song moves from telling the story (vv. 1–5) to speaking to God directly (vv. 6–8). Israel first says what happened, and then turns to the one who did it.

Key words.

  • yeshû‘āh, salvation: “He has become my salvation” (v. 2). The word means rescue, deliverance, victory. The name Jesus (Yeshua) comes from the same root and means “the Lord saves” (CCC 430).
  • ’îsh milḥāmāh, warrior: Literally “a man of war” (v. 3). Israel did not fight at the sea; the Lord fought for them, as Moses had said: “The Lord will fight for you, and you have only to keep still” (14:14).
  • rûaḥ, blast, breath: “At the blast of your nostrils” (v. 8). The same Hebrew word means wind, breath and spirit. The strong east wind of 14:21 is here the very breath of God, like the wind from God that swept over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2).

Verse by verse.

“I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea.” (15:1)

These are also the words of Miriam's refrain (15:21). The first great song in the Bible is a song of thanksgiving for salvation.

“The Lord is my strength and my might, and he has become my salvation;” (15:2)

A note on the text. The Hebrew word rendered “might” (zimrāt) can also mean “song”, and many translations read “my strength and my song”. Isaiah 12:2 and Psalm 118:14 repeat this line, and it became one of Israel's great confessions of faith.

“The Lord is a warrior; the Lord is his name.” (15:3)

The battle belongs to God, not to Israel. The name of the Lord, made known to Moses at the bush (3:14–15), is now known by what he has done.

“At the blast of your nostrils the waters piled up, the floods stood up in a heap; the deeps congealed in the heart of the sea.” (15:8)

The poem paints the sea as a wall that stands still at God's breath. The deep, which the old stories of the nations pictured as a power fighting the gods, obeys the Lord without a struggle.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the crossing of the sea as a figure of Baptism. Paul says that our ancestors “were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:2), and the Catechism teaches that the crossing of the Red Sea, the liberation of Israel from slavery in Egypt, announces the liberation brought about by Baptism (CCC 1221). The Book of Revelation shows those who have conquered standing beside a sea of glass and singing “the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb” (Revelation 15:3). So the song of Exodus becomes the Church's own Easter song: the Lord has passed through death, and his people come up out of the water singing.

Ephesians 6:18–24 · Pray in the Spirit at all times

Setting.

Ephesians ends its teaching on Christian life with the armour of God (6:10–17): truth, righteousness, the gospel of peace, faith, salvation and “the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God”. Our reading follows straight on. Prayer is not one more piece of armour; it is the way all of it is worn.

The letter presents Paul as a prisoner (3:1; 4:1; 6:20). Many scholars think that Ephesians was written in Paul's name by a disciple, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture among the letters of Paul. Verses 21–22 are almost word for word the same as Colossians 4:7–8, and in both letters Tychicus is the messenger.

Structure.

  • v. 18: pray in the Spirit at all times; keep alert; persevere in prayer for all the saints.
  • vv. 19–20: pray for me, an ambassador in chains, that I may speak boldly.
  • vv. 21–22: Tychicus will bring news and encourage you.
  • vv. 23–24: peace, love with faith, and grace to all who love the Lord.

Key words.

  • en panti kairō, at all times: Kairos is time as occasion, the moment at hand. The call is to pray in every situation, whatever the hour brings.
  • parrēsia, boldness: Open, confident speech, the speech of one who has nothing to hide. In Acts it is the mark of the apostles after Easter (Acts 4:13, 31). Paul asks for it as a gift of prayer, not as a natural talent.
  • presbeuō en halysei, an ambassador in chains: An ambassador was protected by his office; this one is in chains. The paradox is the gospel's own: the King's envoy is a prisoner, and yet “the word of God is not chained” (2 Timothy 2:9).

Verse by verse.

“Pray in the Spirit at all times in every prayer and supplication.” (6:18)

Prayer “in the Spirit” is prayer that the Spirit stirs and carries (Romans 8:26). It is not limited to set hours, though set hours teach us how to pray at all times.

“keep alert and always persevere in supplication for all the saints.” (6:18)

“The saints” are all the baptized. Christians pray not only for themselves but for the whole Church.

“Pray also for me, so that when I speak, a message may be given to me to make known with boldness the mystery of the gospel,” (6:19)

The apostle asks the people to pray for him. The shepherd needs the prayers of the flock.

“Grace be with all who have an undying love for our Lord Jesus Christ.” (6:24)

A note on the text. The last Greek words are literally “in incorruption”. The NRSV joins them to love, “an undying love”; the Malayalam POC joins them to the gift, “grace and eternal life”. Both readings are possible, and both are true: love for the risen Lord shares in his life that does not decay.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 18 in its teaching on praying constantly, and explains that this is not a matter of many words but of a heart turned toward God at all times (CCC 2742–2745). It also shows that the first Christian communities lived the prayer of intercession intensely, and that the apostle both prayed for them and asked their prayer for his preaching (CCC 2636). Our Antiochene tradition lives this rhythm in its seven times of prayer, which begin in the evening and run through the night and day, so that the whole day is offered.

John 21:1–14 · It is the Lord!

Setting.

John's Gospel seems to end at 20:30–31, with the words “these are written so that you may come to believe”. Chapter 21 follows as an epilogue. Many scholars think that it was added by the evangelist or by his disciples, who speak in 21:24 (“we know that his testimony is true”). No ancient manuscript of the Gospel lacks it, and the Church receives it as part of the inspired Gospel.

The scene moves from Jerusalem back to Galilee, to the Sea of Tiberias, John's name for the Sea of Galilee (6:1). Seven disciples are there, Peter first and Thomas second, and they have gone back to the work they knew. Earlier in the Gospel Peter denied the Lord beside a charcoal fire (18:18); here he will meet him beside another.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: seven disciples; Peter goes fishing; that night they catch nothing.
  • vv. 4–6: at daybreak Jesus stands on the beach: “Cast the net to the right side”; the net is full.
  • vv. 7–8: the beloved disciple says, “It is the Lord!”; Peter jumps into the sea.
  • vv. 9–11: a charcoal fire, fish and bread; Peter hauls ashore a hundred and fifty-three large fish, and the net is not torn.
  • vv. 12–14: “Come and have breakfast”; Jesus gives them bread and fish; the third appearance.

Key words.

  • phaneroō, show, reveal: The passage begins and ends with this verb (21:1, 14). It is John's word for Jesus making his glory known, as at Cana (2:11). The risen Lord is not found by searching; he shows himself.
  • paidia, children: The word a father uses for his little ones, or an older man for younger friends. The first word of the risen Lord to tired fishermen is tender.
  • anthrakia, charcoal fire: This word appears only twice in the New Testament: here, and at the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter denied Jesus (18:18). The Lord himself lights the fire beside which Peter will be healed.

Verse by verse.

“They went out and got into the boat, but that night they caught nothing.” (21:3)

In John night is more than a time of day (3:2; 13:30). Without the Lord the work of the night is empty.

“Just after daybreak, Jesus stood on the beach; but the disciples did not know that it was Jesus.” (21:4)

As with Mary Magdalene at the tomb (20:14) and the two on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:16), the risen Lord is not recognized at once. He is the same, yet he is known only when he gives himself to be known.

“That disciple whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It is the Lord!”” (21:7)

Love sees first; Peter acts first. The beloved disciple recognizes him, and Peter throws himself into the sea to reach him. The Church needs both.

“full of large fish, a hundred fifty-three of them; and though there were so many, the net was not torn.” (21:11)

The Greek verb for “torn” is the one from which our word “schism” comes. It is the same verb the soldiers used of Jesus' seamless tunic: “Let us not tear it” (19:24). The net that holds so many and does not break is a picture of the one Church.

“Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish.” (21:13)

These are the same actions as at the feeding of the five thousand beside this same sea (6:11). The risen Lord feeds his own with his own hands.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that the risen Jesus showed his disciples that he was not a ghost by inviting them to touch him and by sharing their meal, and it cites this chapter (CCC 645). St Augustine, preaching on this Gospel, saw in the hundred and fifty-three fish all who are saved by grace: 153 is the sum of all the numbers from 1 to 17, and 17 joins the 10 of the Law to the 7 of the Spirit (Tractates on John 122, in substance). St Jerome reports that the naturalists of his day counted a hundred and fifty-three kinds of fish, so that the catch stands for every people (Commentary on Ezekiel, on 47:9–12, in substance). The Fathers differ in the detail but agree on the meaning: the Church's net is for all, and it holds.

Questions people ask

Why did the disciples not recognize Jesus at first?

The Gospels describe the same thing more than once (John 20:14; Luke 24:16). The risen body of Jesus is truly his own body, which bears the wounds, but it now has the new properties of a glorious body: it is not limited by space and time, and he appears how and when he wills (CCC 645). Recognition comes as a gift, through a word, a sign or a shared meal. That is still how we recognize him.

Is the number 153 meant to be a code?

John gives the number as a fact, the kind of thing fishermen count, and it suggests the memory of an eyewitness. The Fathers were also sure that it carries a meaning, and they offered several explanations; none of them is certain. What John himself underlines is that the net, though so full, was not torn. The fullness and the unity of the Church are the point.

The song of Moses calls the Lord a warrior and rejoices that Pharaoh's army was drowned. How should Christians pray it?

The song celebrates God's rescue of a people of slaves from a power that would not let them go. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the books of the Old Testament contain “some things which are incomplete and temporary” (Dei Verbum 15) and are to be read in the light of Christ. In Christ the true enemy is revealed: sin and death, which he overthrew by his own death and Resurrection. This is why the Fathers read this song as a song of Baptism. It is not a song against any people.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

On this Second Sunday after Qyomtho the Evening Gospel already gives the scene that follows today's breakfast: Peter, who denied the Lord three times, is asked three times, “Do you love me?”, and is told, “Feed my sheep.” The Catechism sees in these words the risen Lord confirming the charge he had given to Peter (CCC 553). For the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church these words have a special warmth: in 1930 Mar Ivanios and those who came with him entered full communion with the successor of Peter, while keeping the whole Antiochene heritage. The breakfast on the shore is also an image of every Holy Qurbono: the risen Lord prepares the table, takes the bread and gives it to his own.

For study and teaching

  • The disciples worked all night and caught nothing. Where am I working hard without listening for the Lord's word from the shore?
  • Moses and Israel answered their rescue with a song. What has the Lord done for me this season of Qyomtho that deserves my thanks aloud?
  • Ephesians asks us to pray “for all the saints”. For whom in the Church, near or far, will I pray by name this week?
  • Who in my parish or family is like the beloved disciple, quick to say, “It is the Lord!”? How can I learn to recognize him in the ordinary work of my day?
