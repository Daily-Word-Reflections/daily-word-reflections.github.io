Word for the day

“If a house is divided against itself, that house will not be able to stand.” Mark 3:25

Reflection

When Jesus casts out demons, some scribes say he does it by the ruler of demons. Jesus answers: “If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand. And if a house is divided against itself, that house will not be able to stand.” Satan divides; Christ unites. Wherever the Spirit is at work, he builds communion: in hearts, families, parishes and between Churches.

Jesus then gives a solemn warning: “Truly I tell you, people will be forgiven for their sins and whatever blasphemies they utter; but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit can never have forgiveness.” The Church teaches that this is not a sin too great for God's mercy, but the refusal to accept that mercy: calling the Spirit's work evil and so closing the door through which forgiveness comes. In the evening Gospel Jesus adds: “by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned.”

St Paul tells the Ephesians that Christ “is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Jews and Gentiles, once far apart, are now “citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.” Isaiah had promised: “Peace, peace, to the far and the near, says the LORD; and I will heal them.”

The first Christians, threatened by the authorities, did not divide or despair. They prayed together: “Lord, look at their threats, and grant to your servants to speak your word with all boldness.” “When they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered together was shaken; and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and spoke the word of God with boldness.” United prayer brings the Spirit; the Spirit brings courage.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, when Jesus drove out demons, his enemies said he was working with Satan. Jesus answered: “If a house is divided against itself, that house will not be able to stand.” The devil's work is division: between husband and wife, parents and children, neighbours, parishes, even Churches. The Spirit's work is communion.

Then Jesus warns about the sin against the Holy Spirit, which can never be forgiven. People sometimes fear they have committed it. But the Church teaches: God's mercy has no limits. This sin is to refuse that mercy to the end, to call the Spirit's work evil and close the door. If you are worried that you have committed it, that very worry shows you have not. And Jesus adds: “by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned.” Our tongues can build or destroy.

St Paul says: Christ “is our peace; he has broken down the dividing wall of hostility.” Through his blood those who were far off have been brought near. We who were strangers are now members of God's household, built on the apostles with Christ as the cornerstone.

When the first Christians were threatened, they did not divide; they prayed together, and the place was shaken, and they were filled with the Holy Spirit. Let us do the same. This week take one step to heal a division: a phone call, an apology, a visit. Guard your words. And pray for the unity of all the children of St Thomas. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Seventh Sunday after Pentecost the readings are about division and peace. In the evening Gospel (Matthew 12:30–37) Jesus says that whoever does not gather with him scatters, and that “by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned.” Leviticus 16:29–34 sets out the Day of Atonement, the one day in the year on which Israel's sins were cleansed. In 1 Samuel 4:18–22 the ark is taken, old Eli dies, and a newborn child is named Ichabod, “The glory has departed from Israel.” In Hosea 5:1–5 the prophet says that Israel's deeds do not permit them to return to their God. In Acts 4:23–31 the believers pray under threat, the place is shaken, and “they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and spoke the word of God with boldness.”

We study three readings more closely. Isaiah 57:14–21 shows the high and holy God who dwells also with the contrite, and who says, “Peace, peace, to the far and the near.” Ephesians 2:11–22 answers that promise: Christ “is our peace”, has broken down the dividing wall, and makes of Jews and Gentiles one household of God. In Mark 3:20–30 the scribes accuse Jesus of working by Beelzebul, and he answers that a house divided against itself cannot stand. Today is also Family Day in the Church. The key words are house, peace and the Spirit.

Isaiah 57:14–21 · Peace, peace, to the far and the near

Setting.

These verses belong to the last part of the Book of Isaiah (chapters 56–66), which many scholars think was addressed to the community that had returned from exile in Babylon and found a hard life in a ruined land. The people are discouraged. There are quarrels about worship and about who belongs to God's people (chapter 56 speaks of foreigners and eunuchs welcomed to the Lord's house). The Church receives the whole book as the word of God.

Today's passage is the Lord's answer to a people who feel far from him. He opens with a cry to clear the road (v. 14), then speaks of himself: the high and holy God who lives in eternity and who dwells also with the humble (v. 15). His anger was real, but it has an end (vv. 16–17). What follows is a promise of healing and peace (vv. 18–19), and a warning that the wicked will not find peace (vv. 20–21).

Structure.

  • v. 14: the call to build up the road and remove every obstruction from the people's way.
  • v. 15: the high and holy God who dwells with the contrite and humble.
  • vv. 16–17: he will not accuse for ever; the people's covetousness had brought his anger.
  • vv. 18–19: “I have seen their ways, but I will heal them”: peace to the far and the near.
  • vv. 20–21: the wicked are like a restless sea, and have no peace.

Key words.

  • šālôm, peace: In Hebrew, more than the end of war: wholeness, health and right relationship with God and one another. The prophet repeats the word, “peace, peace”, to show its fullness.
  • rāḥôq wĕqārôb, far and near: The far are those distant from God or from the land; the near are those close. Many interpreters take the far as the exiles scattered abroad and the near as the people in Jerusalem. Ephesians 2:17 reads the words of the Gentiles, who were far, and the Jews, who were near.
  • dakkā’, contrite: Literally “crushed”, from the verb meaning to crush. It describes the person whose pride has been broken and who has become open to God. God lives with such a person.

Verse by verse.

“prepare the way, remove every obstruction from my people’s way.” (57:14)

The command echoes Isaiah 40:3. The way home is to be made smooth for the people. The Church hears in this the work of the forerunner, John, who prepared the way of the Lord.

“I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with those who are contrite and humble in spirit,” (57:15)

The most striking verse of the passage. The Lord, whom heaven cannot contain, makes his home in the broken-hearted. It is the heart of the whole of Scripture's teaching about humility.

“I will not continually accuse, nor will I always be angry;” (57:16)

The Lord's anger is real, but it is not his last word. He recalls that he made the spirits and souls of his people and knows how frail they are (compare Psalm 103:9–14).

“I have seen their ways, but I will heal them;” (57:18)

God sees everything, and still chooses to heal. Mercy that knows the whole truth is what the word “grace” means.

“Peace, peace, to the far and the near, says the Lord; and I will heal them.” (57:19)

Peace is God's gift to all, whether they are close to him or far away, and it is joined to healing. This is the verse Ephesians takes up.

“There is no peace, says my God, for the wicked.” (57:21)

The passage ends with a sober warning, repeated at 48:22. Peace cannot be had apart from God, because it is his gift. The way back, however, is always open (v. 15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the promise of peace “to the far and the near” in the light of Christ. In Ephesians 2:17 Paul applies it to the preaching of Christ to Gentiles and Jews alike. The Catechism teaches that earthly peace is the image and fruit of the peace of Christ (CCC 2305). God's closeness to the humble is also the song of the Magnificat, where Mary says that God has looked on the lowliness of his servant (Luke 1:46–55).

Ephesians 2:11–22 · He is our peace

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter about the Church and the unity of Jew and Gentile in Christ. It is traditionally ascribed to Paul, writing from prison (3:1; 4:1); many scholars think it was written in Paul's name by a close disciple, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself. Either way the Church receives it as inspired Scripture. Most of the readers were Gentile believers.

In the first half of chapter 2 Paul has said that God has made us alive in Christ by grace. Now he turns to the community: the Gentiles who were once far from God's people have been brought near, and the old hostility between Jews and Gentiles has been ended in the cross. The “dividing wall” of verse 14 may recall the low stone barrier in the Jerusalem temple that kept Gentiles from the inner courts, with its inscription warning them not to go further. Paul uses it as a picture of the separation that Christ has taken down.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: remember what you once were: without Christ, strangers to the covenants of promise.
  • vv. 13–18: but now in Christ you are near: he is our peace; he has broken down the wall, made one new humanity, reconciled both to God through the cross, and given both access to the Father in one Spirit.
  • vv. 19–22: you are no longer strangers, but citizens and members of God's household, built on the apostles and prophets with Christ as the cornerstone, and growing into a holy temple.
  • ¶ The movement is from remembering to peace and then to building.

Key words.

  • mesotoichon tou phragmou, the dividing wall: The Greek words mean a partition and a fence. The barrier in question is the hostility between Jews and Gentiles. The cross puts that hostility to death (v. 16).
  • eirēnē, peace: “He is our peace” does not mean only that Christ brings peace; he himself is the peace between God and humanity, and between those who were divided.
  • oikeioi tou theou, members of the household of God: From oikos, house. The Gentiles are not guests in the house of God; they belong to the family. A household is the place where people eat, pray and are cared for together.

Verse by verse.

“without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise,” (2:12)

Paul begins by honouring Israel. The covenants and the commonwealth of Israel are the gifts of God that the Gentiles were not yet sharing. The Church does not leave Israel behind; she is grafted in (Romans 11:17–18).

“you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” (2:13)

The prophet's “far” and “near” (Isaiah 57:19) now have a name: the Gentiles who were far have been brought near. The nearness is not by their own effort but by the blood of Christ.

“he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall” (2:14)

One body, one household. Christ does not make Gentiles into Jews or Jews into Gentiles; he makes of both one new humanity (v. 15).

“reconcile both groups to God in one body through the cross,” (2:16)

Peace with one another begins with peace with God. The cross is the place where the two are reconciled, because there the hostility is put to death.

“you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God,” (2:19)

Citizens and family members. Both pictures are needed: a city with its rights, and a home with its love.

“with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.” (2:20)

The apostles and prophets are the foundation, but Christ is the stone that holds the corner together, so that the building does not fall apart.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this passage to describe the Church as God's building: the apostles are the foundation, Christ is the cornerstone, and the building has many names, among them the house and dwelling of God and the holy temple (CCC 756; compare Ephesians 2:19–22). The Church sees the “one new humanity” as the fruit of the cross, where Christ reconciles all things to God. At the same time the Church teaches that God's covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (Romans 11:29; CCC 121 and 839), and that Paul speaks here not of cancelling Israel's calling but of Gentiles being welcomed into it.

Mark 3:20–30 · A house divided against itself

Setting.

Jesus has just named the Twelve on the mountain (3:13–19) and now comes “home”, to the house at Capernaum (2:1). The crowd presses so hard that he and his disciples cannot even eat. Mark tells the story in a typical pattern: he begins one scene (v. 21, his family), breaks in with another (vv. 22–30, the scribes), and then returns to the first (vv. 31–35, his mother and brothers). The two scenes explain each other: some who are near him do not understand him, and others who should know better call his goodness evil.

The scribes who came down from Jerusalem are experts in the Law and part of the leadership; the accusation comes from them, not from the Jewish people as a whole. The crowd, the Twelve and Jesus himself are all Jews.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: the crowd; his family go out to restrain him, saying that he is out of his mind.
  • vv. 22–27: the scribes say he works by Beelzebul; Jesus answers with parables: a divided kingdom or house cannot stand; no one plunders a strong man's house unless he first binds the strong man.
  • vv. 28–30: every sin and blasphemy can be forgiven, but not blasphemy against the Holy Spirit; Mark explains that the scribes had said, “He has an unclean spirit.”

Key words.

  • exestē, he is out of his mind: Literally “he has stood outside himself”. People thought his zeal was madness. Mark never excuses the accusation; he wants the reader to see that those who love Jesus can fail to understand him.
  • ho ischyros, the strong man: Satan, who holds the world in his grip. Jesus is the stronger one who has come to bind him (compare Isaiah 49:24–25; Mark 1:7). Each exorcism is a piece of plunder taken from the strong man's house.
  • blasphēmia eis to pneuma to hagion, blasphemy against the Holy Spirit: Not a single careless word, but a settled refusal to recognise the work of God as God's, calling the Spirit's work evil. Mark's own explanation is in verse 30.

Verse by verse.

“He has gone out of his mind.” (3:21)

The “family” here translates a general phrase meaning “those from beside him”: relatives and neighbours. Mark does not say that his mother shared the view; in the Church's reading her faith is never in doubt.

“He has Beelzebul, and by the ruler of the demons he casts out demons.” (3:22)

His enemies cannot deny that the demons are driven out, so they say the power is evil. This is a fatal turn of mind: when what is good is called evil, there is nothing left to be healed.

“If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand.” (3:24)

Jesus answers with common sense. Evil does not fight itself. If the demons are being driven out, a stronger power is at work, and the kingdom of God has come.

“no one can enter a strong man’s house and plunder his property without first tying up the strong man” (3:27)

This is a picture of Jesus's whole ministry: he has entered the house of the strong man and is releasing those held captive.

“people will be forgiven for their sins and whatever blasphemies they utter;” (3:28)

The great generosity of the passage. Every sin, every blasphemy, can be forgiven. No sinner is outside God's mercy.

“whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit can never have forgiveness” (3:29)

This is not a sin that God refuses to forgive but one that closes the heart against forgiveness. A person who says that the Spirit's work is the devil's will not ask for the very mercy that the Spirit brings. Anyone who fears he has committed it shows by his fear that he has not.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that there are no limits to the mercy of God, but that anyone who deliberately refuses to accept his mercy by repenting rejects the forgiveness of his sins and the salvation offered by the Holy Spirit; such hardness of heart can lead to final impenitence (CCC 1864). The Church therefore reads verse 29 as a warning about a hardened heart and not as a sentence on those who are afraid. The strong man's house is read as the whole kingdom of Satan, which Christ has entered and is plundering through exorcisms, the forgiveness of sins and the cross (compare CCC 550). Here is the firm ground of the Church's unity: a house divided against itself cannot stand, and Jesus prays that his disciples be one (John 17:21).

Questions people ask

What is the “sin against the Holy Spirit”, and can I have committed it?

It is the sin of refusing, to the very end, the forgiveness that God offers. Mark explains it in verse 30: the scribes saw the Spirit's work in Jesus and called it unclean. The Catechism says that God's mercy has no limits, but that someone who deliberately refuses to repent shuts out forgiveness himself (CCC 1864). If you are afraid that you have committed it, that fear is a sign that your heart is still open to God. Go to the sacrament of Reconciliation and trust his mercy.

Does Ephesians say that the Jewish Law has been done away with?

No. The “law with its commandments and ordinances” in verse 15 refers to the observances that marked Israel off from the nations, which stood as a barrier to Gentiles being welcomed into God's people. Christ ends that barrier. He does not end God's covenant with Israel, which is never taken back, because “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29; CCC 839), nor the Law and the Prophets, which he came “not to abolish but to fulfill” (Matthew 5:17). Gentiles are welcomed into the covenant family, not into a replacement for it.

Did Jesus's family really think he was mad?

Mark reports what people were saying, and says that “those from beside him” went out to restrain him. It was a wide circle of relatives and neighbours who, like many others, did not yet understand him. Mark does not say that his mother believed it, and the Church honours her faith from the beginning. The story is a reminder that even those who love Jesus can fail to understand him at first, and that following him takes time.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam marks today as Family Day: pray for every family of the Church. Ephesians gives the Church her own name for herself: the household of God. In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost show the life of that household, gathered by the Spirit around the table of the Holy Qurbono. Before the anaphora the faithful exchange peace, and Christ's own gift, “he is our peace”, is put into their hands. The Catechism calls the Christian family a “domestic church” (CCC 2204): in every home the same Spirit is at work to make one body of those who were divided, and to make the house a place where Christ is at home.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus says that a house divided against itself, “that house will not be able to stand”. Where is there a division in my family or parish that I could help to heal this week?
  • God dwells “with those who are contrite and humble in spirit”. What would it mean for me to be broken open before God, and not hardened?
  • Christ has broken down the dividing wall. Is there a wall of suspicion or old hurt that I keep building up?
  • It is Family Day. How can my family be a small household of God: at prayer, at table and in our care for one another?
