Word for the day

“And he said to them, “Follow me, and I will make you fish for people.”” Matthew 4:19

Reflection

Today's Epistle comes from the most painful pages of St Paul's letters, where he has to defend his own ministry before the people he had loved and served. Rival preachers had come to Corinth, boasting of their gifts, and some of them were being paid for their teaching. They looked down on Paul because he had worked with his hands (Acts 18:3) and had asked nothing from the Corinthians. Paul answers with a sharp irony: “Did I commit a sin by humbling myself so that you might be exalted, because I proclaimed God's good news to you without payment?” He had accepted help from other churches, “I robbed other churches by accepting support from them in order to serve you,” and he lists the friends from Macedonia who supplied his needs, the Philippians who had always helped him (Philippians 4:15–16). So, he says, “I did not burden anyone,” and “I will continue to refrain from burdening you in any way.” We must read him rightly. Paul does not teach that the Church should never support her ministers; elsewhere he says plainly that the Lord commanded those who proclaim the gospel to get their living by the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:14). He is saying that he gave up this right at Corinth so that no one could say he preached for money. And he ends with the reason of the heart: “And why? Because I do not love you? God knows I do!”

Then Paul's tone turns grave: “For such boasters are false apostles, deceitful workers, disguising themselves as apostles of Christ. And no wonder! Even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light. So it is not strange if his ministers also disguise themselves as ministers of righteousness. Their end will match their deeds.” The danger he names is not crude evil but a polished counterfeit, a message that sounds beautiful and a preacher who looks holy, but who seeks his own glory or gain. How does a simple believer tell the difference? The Lord himself gave the test: “You will know them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:16). St John adds: “do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God” (1 John 4:1). The Church has always taught us to ask whether a teaching agrees with the faith handed down from the Apostles, which the bishops, in communion with the successor of Peter, are charged to guard, and whether the preacher bears the fruit of humility, love and service. This is not a call to suspicion of others; it is a call to be wise, for the simple believe everything, but the clever consider their steps (Proverbs 14:15). Paul himself is the pattern of the true apostle: he gives, he does not take; he works, he does not boast.

The Gospel shows us what the true call looks like at its beginning. “As he walked by the Sea of Galilee, he saw two brothers, Simon, who is called Peter, and Andrew his brother, casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen. And he said to them, ‘Follow me, and I will make you fish for people.’ Immediately they left their nets and followed him.” Then two more: “James son of Zebedee and his brother John, in the boat with their father Zebedee, mending their nets, and he called them. Immediately they left the boat and their father, and followed him.” These men had no theology degree, no fine speech, no fame. They had boats, nets and a father, and they had a call. Anyone who has watched the fishermen of Kerala mend their nets on the beach after the monsoon knows how much a net means to a family. They left what they had in order to follow him, and he promised them no income, only a new kind of work: to draw people toward God. The false apostles take; the true Apostles leave. Yesterday the Panchangam remembered St Benedict, the monk of Italy who began his Rule with the single word “Listen” and taught his monks to put nothing before the love of Christ (Rule, Prologue and chapter 4, in substance). He too heard “Follow me” and left what he had. The same call goes out to each of us today, in the plain places where we work and mend our nets.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Gospel today takes us to the shore of the lake. Jesus is walking, and he sees two brothers casting a net, and he says: Follow me, and I will make you fish for people (Matthew 4:19). They were not scholars or leaders. They were working people. And the Gospel says: Immediately they left their nets and followed him (Matthew 4:20). Then it happens again with James and John, who were mending their nets in the boat with their father.

Think of what they left: the boat, the nets, the income, the father who needed them. They could not see where this would lead. They only knew who was calling. And that is the heart of every vocation: not a clear plan, but a clear voice. Jesus calls each of us in the middle of our ordinary work. The question is what we are ready to leave behind: a habit, a grudge, a fear, the love of money.

In the Epistle, St Paul shows us what it is to follow Jesus to the end. He worked with his own hands and asked nothing from the Corinthians so that nobody could say he preached for gain (2 Corinthians 11:9). He serves, he does not take. But he warns us as well: Even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light (2 Corinthians 11:14). Not everything that shines is of God. The Lord gives us a simple test: you will know them by their fruits (Matthew 7:16): humility, love and service.

Yesterday the Panchangam remembered St Benedict, who began his Rule with the word Listen. Let us listen today to the voice that says: Follow me. Let us leave one net at his feet, and let us serve without asking for a reward. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what Jesus said to the fishermen? He said, Follow me! And they left their nets and boats right away, and went with him. They did not know everything about where they were going, but they loved Jesus and trusted him. Jesus calls you too. He does not call you to leave your family; he calls you to be kind, to pray, to help others and to listen to him. And St Paul tells us to serve people without asking for anything back, just like a good fisherman who shares his fish. This week, do one kind thing for someone without telling anyone, and say: Jesus, I want to follow you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you walked beside the Sea of Galilee and called fishermen to follow you, and they left their nets at once. Call us also, and give us hearts that can leave what holds us back. Make us servants like Paul, a burden to no one, glad to give and not to take. Keep us from the disguises of light and from every preaching that seeks its own glory, and keep your Church in the faith of the Apostles. By the prayers of St Benedict and all your holy servants, teach us to listen and to put nothing before your love. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about the kind of ministry that is true. In the Epistle Paul defends himself by pointing to his unpaid, costly service to Corinth, and warns against those who disguise themselves as apostles of Christ. In the Gospel Jesus walks by the Sea of Galilee and calls two pairs of fishermen, who leave their nets and boat and follow him at once. The key words are serving and following.

2 Corinthians 11:7–15 · Disguised as apostles of Christ

Setting.

This passage belongs to Paul's “fool's speech” (11:1 to 12:13), which began at 11:1 with the question of who may boast. Now Paul takes up a charge that seems to have been made against him in Corinth: that he did not accept support from the church there, and so showed that he did not really love them or that he was not a true apostle.

Paul had come to Corinth working at his trade as a tentmaker with Aquila and Priscilla (Acts 18:1–3). Other travelling preachers, who came later, took money from the community. Paul chose a different way, in order not to be a burden, and now has to defend that choice.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–9: Paul preached without payment, supported by gifts from Macedonia.
  • vv. 10–11: he will keep to this; it is not for lack of love.
  • v. 12: he will go on in order to deny an opening to those who boast.
  • vv. 13–15: such people are false apostles, disguised as apostles of Christ, as Satan disguises himself as an angel of light.

Key words.

  • esylēsa, I robbed, I stripped: A strong word for what soldiers do in taking spoil. Paul uses it with bitter irony: other churches, above all Philippi (Philippians 4:15–16), supported him so that he could serve Corinth, and he speaks as if he had plundered them.
  • pseudapostoloi, false apostles: The word appears only here in the New Testament. Scholars discuss who these people were and what exactly they taught, and the Church does not decide the question. Paul's concern is clear: they preached themselves, not the crucified Christ.
  • metaschēmatizō, to disguise, to change one's outward form: The same verb is used three times in verses 13–15. The danger is of something that looks like the truth and is not. A false teacher seldom looks false.

Verse by verse.

“I robbed other churches by accepting support from them in order to serve you.” (11:8)

Paul speaks sharply. The Corinthians, who were well off, had given him nothing, and poorer churches had supported him. He is not asking for repayment but showing the Corinthians what they are doing.

“I did not burden anyone,” (11:9)

Paul chose not to claim a right that he held (1 Corinthians 9:12–15). This does not mean that preachers may not be supported: Paul himself says that “those who proclaim the gospel should get their living by the gospel” (1 Corinthians 9:14).

“Because I do not love you? God knows I do!” (11:11)

Love is behind the whole defence. Paul does not wish to win an argument, but to win back the Corinthians.

“Even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light.” (11:14)

The Old Testament does not say this in so many words; it reflects Jewish thought of Paul's time, in which Satan is a deceiver. The point for Paul is that great spiritual brilliance is not proof of truth. The test is Christ crucified and the teaching handed down.

How the Church reads it.

The Church holds two truths together here. First, she teaches that the faithful have a duty to provide for the needs of the Church, so that her ministers may give themselves to serving (CCC 2043), and that Paul himself defended the worker's right to a living. Second, she values the example of Paul, who for the sake of the Gospel asked nothing. The warning about disguise is a call for discernment: the Church asks the faithful to test what they hear against the faith handed down from the apostles, as John also says, “do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits” (1 John 4:1). The Church also teaches that her ministers are servants and not masters of the faithful (Lumen Gentium 27).

Matthew 4:18–22 · Follow me, and I will make you fish for people

Setting.

After his baptism and the temptation in the wilderness, Jesus leaves Nazareth and settles in Capernaum on the shore of the Sea of Galilee (4:12–13). Matthew sees in this the fulfilment of Isaiah: “the people who sat in darkness have seen a great light” (4:15–16). Jesus begins to proclaim, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has come near” (4:17). Our passage then tells of the first disciples.

The fishermen of Galilee were working people who owned boats and nets, and sometimes employed hired hands (compare Mark 1:20). Jesus does not call scholars or priests but those who work with their hands. The scene is brief and bare. Four men are called, and they follow.

Structure.

  • v. 18: Jesus sees Simon called Peter and his brother Andrew, casting a net.
  • v. 19: the call: “Follow me”, and the promise: “I will make you fish for people”.
  • v. 20: they leave their nets at once and follow.
  • vv. 21–22: James and John, with their father Zebedee in the boat, are called, and follow.

Key words.

  • deute opisō mou, come after me, follow me: The words mean literally “come behind me”. In the world of the rabbis, students chose their teacher. Here the teacher chooses. To follow Jesus means to walk behind him, to go where he goes.
  • halieis anthrōpōn, fishers of people: Jesus takes the work that they already know and gives it a new purpose. They will draw people out of the waters of death into the life of the kingdom. In the Bible the sea is often a symbol of chaos and danger, so to draw people out of it is to save them.
  • euthys, immediately, at once: Matthew uses this word of the response to the call. It is not a calculation of gains and losses. The call is met with the whole life.

Verse by verse.

“Follow me, and I will make you fish for people.” (4:19)

The call has two parts: “follow me” and “I will make you”. Jesus does not ask them to be ready first. He promises that he himself will form them.

“Immediately they left their nets and followed him.” (4:20)

They left what they were holding. For fishermen the nets were their livelihood. The text does not hide that this is a real cost; it simply shows the call as stronger than the cost.

“Immediately they left the boat and their father, and followed him.” (4:22)

James and John leave the family business and their father. Jesus' call comes before even the family's claim, though not against it; in the same Gospel he teaches the commandment to honour father and mother (15:4). Zebedee is left, not rejected.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that from the beginning Jesus associated his disciples with his own life, shared the mystery of the Kingdom with them and gave them a share in his mission, his joy and his sufferings (CCC 787). The Church sees in this call the beginning of every vocation, to priesthood, to religious life, to marriage and to every Christian service; each begins with the Lord's “Follow me” and is answered willingly. The Catechism adds that parents must respect and encourage the vocation of their children (CCC 2232). The calling of Peter and Andrew, James and John is also read as the beginning of the apostolic ministry, which continues in the bishops, the successors of the apostles. The Syro-Malankara Church, which holds that the apostle Thomas, according to tradition, brought the Gospel to India, remembers that the apostles' mission began with a call by the lake and went out to the ends of the earth.

Questions people ask

Did the fishermen abandon their families by following Jesus?

The Gospel does not say that they stopped loving their families. It says that the call of Christ came first. Peter's mother-in-law is later healed in Peter's own house in Capernaum (Matthew 8:14–15), so Peter still had a home. Zebedee is left in the boat with the hired men (Mark 1:20), who could carry on the business. The call asks for a change in what comes first, not for a rejection of those one loves. Such a call is also lived in marriage and family, with the Lord first.

For study and teaching

  • Paul chose to give up what was his by right so that the Gospel would not be hindered. What have I the right to that I might give up for the sake of someone else's faith?
  • “Follow me, and I will make you.” What holds me back from answering Jesus' call, and what do I hold in my hands that he is asking me to put down?
