Word for the day

“Truly I tell you, unless you change and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.” Matthew 18:3

Reflection

The disciples ask: “Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?” Jesus calls a child, puts the child among them and says: “Truly I tell you, unless you change and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven. Whoever becomes humble like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven. Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me.” Children are not only the future of the Church; they show us now how to trust, to receive, to depend on the Father.

Jesus speaks with great seriousness about harm done to children: “If any of you put a stumbling block before one of these little ones who believe in me, it would be better for you if a great millstone were fastened around your neck and you were drowned in the depth of the sea… Take care that you do not despise one of these little ones; for, I tell you, in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father.” “It is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” Protecting and forming children is a holy duty of every family and parish.

St Paul gives the heart of what we teach them: “if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For one believes with the heart and so is justified, and one confesses with the mouth and so is saved… Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” Faith is believed in the heart and spoken with the lips. Sunday School exists so that children may learn both.

At Marah the water was bitter, and the people could not drink. God showed Moses a piece of wood; when it was thrown into the water, the water became sweet. Then God said: “I am the LORD who heals you.” The Fathers saw in that wood the Cross, which makes the bitter waters of life sweet. In Rome Paul, a prisoner, “welcomed all who came to him, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness.” Teachers of the faith carry on his work.

Homily outline

Theme: “Unless you become like children.”

Jesus places a child in the midst of his disciples and teaches that the humble are the greatest. In the 2025–26 lectionary this Sunday was Sunday School Day: a day to thank catechists and renew our duty to hand on the faith.

1. Become like children

  • The disciples ask who is the greatest; Jesus answers with a child.
  • Childlike faith trusts, depends on the Father, and receives everything as gift.
  • Like a child quiet at its mother's side, the soul rests in God.
“But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; my soul is like the weaned child that is with me.” Psalm 131:2
“Truly I tell you, unless you change and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven. Whoever becomes humble like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven.” Matthew 18:3–4

Apply: Spend five minutes in silent, trusting prayer each night before sleep.

2. Do not lose one of these little ones

  • Moses commanded parents to teach God's words at home, on the road, at rest and at rising.
  • The Father does not want even one little one to be lost.
  • Parents are the first catechists; Sunday school helps but cannot replace the home (CCC 2223–2226).
“Keep these words that I am commanding you today in your heart. Recite them to your children and talk about them when you are at home and when you are away, when you lie down and when you rise.” Deuteronomy 6:6–7
“So it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” Matthew 18:14

Apply: Thank your children's catechists, and pray with your children every evening.

3. Confess with your lips, be healed

  • God revealed himself as the Lord who heals.
  • Faith must be confessed openly: Jesus is Lord.
  • Children learn to confess Christ by seeing adults do it without shame.
“I will not bring upon you any of the diseases that I brought upon the Egyptians; for I am the LORD who heals you.” Exodus 15:26
“Because if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.” Romans 10:9

Apply: Make the sign of the cross before meals, even in public.

The kingdom belongs to the childlike, and children belong to the kingdom.

Let us become little before God and great in our care for the little ones.

Take-home resolution: Support Sunday school generously, and begin family preparation for the Fifteen Days' Fast next Sunday.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the disciples asked Jesus: “Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?” Jesus did not answer with a name. He called a child and stood the child among them: “Unless you change and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.” A child trusts, receives, asks, and is not ashamed of needing help. That is how we must stand before God.

Then Jesus speaks very strongly: whoever causes one of these little ones to stumble would be better off with a millstone around his neck in the depths of the sea. Their angels always see the Father's face. Children are precious to God. Every family and every parish must protect them, form them, and never give them scandal by our lives.

St Paul tells us what we must teach them: “if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.” That is the work of our Sunday School teachers, who give their Sunday mornings to teach our children the faith. Let us thank them and pray for them today.

At Marah the water was bitter; God showed Moses a piece of wood, and the water became sweet. The Fathers saw there the Cross, which makes the bitterness of life sweet. Let us teach our children to love the Cross and the Lord who heals. And next Sunday, 1 August, we begin the Fifteen Days' Fast in honour of our Mother's Assumption. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Tenth Sunday after Pentecost the readings speak of a God who heals and who draws near. In the evening Gospel (Luke 9:12–17) Jesus tells the apostles, “You give them something to eat”, and five loaves and two fish feed the crowd: “all ate and were filled”. In Exodus 15:23–27 the bitter water of Marah is made sweet, and the Lord says, “I am the Lord who heals you.” In Job 42:7–17 the Lord restores Job after he has prayed for his friends. Psalm 112 praises the one who fears the Lord and gives to the poor. In Isaiah 57:14–21 the Holy One who dwells on high also dwells “with those who are contrite and humble in spirit”, and promises, “I will heal them.” In Acts 28:23–30 Paul, a prisoner in Rome, spends a whole day explaining the kingdom of God to the Jewish leaders of the city, some of whom are convinced, and he goes on welcoming everyone who comes to him.

We study three readings more closely. At Marah the people find water that cannot be drunk, and God answers a complaint with a piece of wood and a promise of healing. Paul tells the Romans that the word of salvation is not far away, high in heaven or deep in the abyss, but “on your lips and in your heart”, and that the same Lord is generous to Jew and Greek alike. In Matthew, Jesus puts a child in the midst of the disciples who are arguing about greatness, warns against causing the little ones to stumble, and tells of the shepherd who goes after the one sheep that has gone astray. The key words are bitter and sweet, near and little ones.

Exodus 15:23–27 · Marah: the bitter water made sweet

Setting.

The Israelites have just crossed the sea and sung the Song of Moses (15:1–21). Three days later they are in the wilderness of Shur and have found no water (15:22). The song of victory is hardly finished when the first complaint is heard. The story is the first of several “wilderness” stories in Exodus and Numbers in which God tests his people and teaches them to trust (see 16:4; 17:1–7).

Marah is a Hebrew word meaning “bitter”. The place is not certainly identified today. In verse 25 the Lord shows Moses a piece of wood; it is cast into the water and the water becomes sweet. There “the Lord made for them a statute and an ordinance”, which means that the first lesson of the journey is also the first lesson of the law: listen to the voice of the Lord. The reading ends at Elim, a place of twelve springs and seventy palm trees, where God gives more than the bare minimum.

Structure.

  • v. 23: Marah: water that cannot be drunk, and a place named for its bitterness.
  • v. 24: the people complain against Moses.
  • v. 25: Moses cries to the Lord; the wood makes the water sweet; statute and test.
  • v. 26: if they listen and obey, they will not suffer the diseases of Egypt; “I am the Lord who heals you”.
  • v. 27: Elim, with its springs and palm trees.
  • ¶ The complaint (v. 24) and the cry (v. 25) are placed side by side: the people complain to Moses, and Moses cries out to the Lord.

Key words.

  • mārâ, bitter: The name of the place tells the whole story: the first stop after the sea is a place of bitterness. Israel will learn that God turns bitterness to sweetness, but only by teaching hearts to trust.
  • nissâhû, he put them to the test: The same word is used of Abraham in Genesis 22:1. A test in the Bible is not a trap but a school: it brings to light what is in the heart and makes it grow.
  • rōp̄ᵉ’ekā, your healer: Literally “the one who heals you”. God takes this title for himself. The healing here is of body, community and heart, and it is tied to listening to his voice.

Verse by verse.

“they could not drink the water of Marah because it was bitter.” (15:23)

Right after the great deliverance comes ordinary thirst. The life of faith does not remove difficulty, but it teaches us where to take it.

“the Lord showed him a piece of wood; he threw it into the water, and the water became sweet.” (15:25)

The cure comes from God, not from the wood. Moses must be shown the wood; he does not find it himself. The healing is a gift, received in obedience.

“there he put them to the test.” (15:25)

The same moment is both a gift and a test. God gives water and also asks for trust.

“I am the Lord who heals you.” (15:26)

The promise is conditional on listening, but it is mainly a revelation of who God is. He does not only punish; he heals.

How the Church reads it.

Several of the Fathers, among them Tertullian and Origen (in substance), saw in the wood that sweetened the water a figure of the cross, which turns the bitterness of the old letter and of death into sweetness. The Church has also heard in “I am the Lord who heals you” the voice of Christ the physician. The Catechism recalls that Christ’s compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a resplendent sign that God has visited his people (CCC 1503). The Church’s care for the sick, and her sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick, continue that healing. The passage also teaches how to meet complaint: not by silencing it, but by bringing it to God as Moses did, and then waiting to be shown the wood.

Romans 10:5–13 · The word is near you

Setting.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth, probably in the winter of AD 56–57, to a church he had not founded and hoped to visit on his way to Spain. Chapters 9–11 are about Israel, his own people, whom he loves so much that he would wish himself cut off from Christ for their sake (9:3). Just before our passage he says, “my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved” (10:1). He is not speaking against the Jewish people but as one of them, in grief and in hope.

Verses 5–8 set two ways of speaking side by side. Verse 5 quotes Leviticus 18:5, where Moses promises life to those who keep God’s commandments. Verses 6–8 take words from Deuteronomy 30:12–14, where Moses tells Israel that the commandment is not “in heaven” or “beyond the sea”, but “very near to you”. Paul hears in that nearness the nearness of Christ himself. Interpreters differ on exactly how Paul sets verse 5 against verses 6–8, but they agree that for him the word of God has come close in Christ and calls for faith.

Structure.

  • v. 5: Moses and the righteousness that comes from the law.
  • vv. 6–8: the righteousness that comes from faith: the word is near, on your lips and in your heart.
  • vv. 9–10: confessing with the lips and believing in the heart.
  • vv. 11–13: for everyone: Jew and Greek, the same Lord; whoever calls on his name will be saved.

Key words.

  • rhēma, word: Paul uses the word rhēma, the spoken word that is proclaimed. Faith “comes from what is heard” (10:17). The Gospel is announced, and the Church’s preaching is the way that the word comes near.
  • homologeō, I confess: To say the same as another, to speak in agreement. To confess that Jesus is Lord is to agree with God’s own word about him. The earliest Christian creed is this short cry.
  • kyrios, Lord: In the Greek Old Testament kyrios is the word used for the name of the God of Israel. To call Jesus “Lord” is to give him the honour due to God, and it is a work of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:3).

Verse by verse.

“The word is near you, on your lips and in your heart” (10:8)

Paul reads Deuteronomy 30:14 in the light of Christ. We do not have to climb to heaven or go down to the abyss to find him; he has come to us. The Church’s Gospel, sacraments and prayer are how the nearness is lived.

“if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.” (10:9)

Heart and lips go together. Faith is a gift of God received with the whole person, and it is expressed in public confession. For Catholics this faith is lived in the Church and sealed in Baptism; it is also the root of the works of love (compare CCC 1815).

“No one who believes in him will be put to shame.” (10:11)

Paul quotes Isaiah 28:16. Faith is never put to shame, because the one in whom we trust does not fail.

“there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; the same Lord is Lord of all and is generous to all who call on him.” (10:12)

The one Lord is the Lord of Jews and Gentiles. Paul does not set Jews aside: he teaches that Gentiles are welcomed into the same promise (see 11:1, 29, where he says that God has not rejected his people and that his gifts and call are irrevocable).

How the Church reads it.

The cry “Jesus is Lord” is the earliest confession of the Church. The Catechism teaches that the title Lord expresses the divinity of Jesus and that to call Jesus “Lord” is to confess his divine sovereignty (CCC 446–449). The same Catechism says that believing in Jesus Christ and in the One who sent him is necessary for obtaining salvation (CCC 161). In the Syriac tradition the Creed is said aloud by the whole people at the Holy Qurbono, so that heart and lips speak together, as Paul says. The passage is also read with Romans 11 in mind: the Church has never taught that God has rejected the Jewish people, and Nostra Aetate 4 recalls that God does not repent of the gifts he has made nor of the calls he issues.

Matthew 18:1–14 · Become like children; the sheep that went astray

Setting.

Matthew 18 is the fourth of the five great discourses of the Gospel, often called the discourse on the community or the Church. It begins with a question about rank (v. 1) and goes on to teach humility (vv. 1–5), care for the little ones (vv. 6–10), the search for the lost sheep (vv. 12–14), and then fraternal correction, forgiveness and the prayer of two or three gathered in his name (vv. 15–35). In Mark 9:33–37 the dispute about greatness arises on the road to Capernaum, and Luke 9:46–48 tells of it too.

In the ancient world a child had little status and no rights, and “the little ones” can mean children, but also all those who are small, humble or easily overlooked in the community, as the phrase “who believe in me” in verse 6 shows. The sayings about the hand, foot and eye (vv. 8–9) are typical Semitic exaggeration: they teach how seriously sin must be fought, not that the body is to be harmed. One note on the text: verse 11 (“For the Son of Man came to save the lost”) appears in many manuscripts but is not in the earliest ones, and the NRSV omits it; the sense is the same as Luke 19:10.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: who is the greatest? A child is placed in the midst; whoever welcomes one such child welcomes Christ.
  • vv. 6–9: woe to those who cause the little ones to stumble; renounce whatever causes sin.
  • v. 10: do not despise the little ones; their angels see the Father’s face.
  • vv. 12–14: the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine and goes after the one that has strayed; the Father does not want one of them lost.

Key words.

  • straphēte, you turn / you change: The Greek verb is “to turn around”, the same idea as the Hebrew word for repentance. To become like a child is not to become childish but to turn: from the question “who is greatest?” to trust and dependence on the Father.
  • skandalon, stumbling block: Originally the trigger of a trap. Here it is whatever makes a believer fall. The warning is very severe because the little ones belong to Christ.
  • planēthē, has gone astray: The same verb is used of the sheep that wanders. The shepherd’s search is not a punishment for the wanderer but a sign that the sheep is precious.

Verse by verse.

“unless you change and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.” (18:3)

The condition is humility. A child receives everything as a gift and does not keep a score of merits. Greatness in the kingdom is the opposite of climbing.

“Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me.” (18:5)

Christ makes himself one with the little ones. Welcome given to a child, to a poor person or to a new member of the parish is welcome given to him.

“Woe to the world because of stumbling blocks!” (18:7)

Jesus says that stumbling blocks are bound to come, but this does not excuse anyone. Anyone who leads a young believer into sin, by scandal or neglect, answers for it.

“in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father in heaven.” (18:10)

The little ones have heavenly guardians who stand in the Father’s presence. The text is the main biblical root of the teaching on guardian angels.

“it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” (18:14)

The passage ends with the will of the Father: not one lost. The shepherd acts out the Father’s will.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes “become like children” as the way to enter the kingdom: the Gospel shows that to become a child in relation to God is the condition for entering it (CCC 526). It also teaches that from infancy to death human life is surrounded by the watchful care and intercession of the angels, and that beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd (CCC 336, citing St Basil). Scandal is defined as an attitude or behaviour that leads another to do evil (CCC 2284), and the Church takes with great seriousness the duty to protect children and the vulnerable. St Gregory the Great, preaching on the lost sheep in another Gospel, hears in it Christ taking our nature on his shoulders (Homilies on the Gospels 34, in substance). The Church’s pastoral work is a continuation of that search.

Questions people ask

Marah’s bitter water was made sweet by a piece of wood. Was that a miracle or a natural event?

The text says that the Lord showed Moses the wood and that the water became sweet. Some scholars note that plants with such properties exist in the Sinai, and they may be right, but this does not take anything from the story. The point is that the people were saved by what God showed and what Moses did in obedience. The Church sees in it a sign of God’s power to turn bitter things sweet, and in the wood a figure of the cross.

Does Paul say that we are saved by words, by simply saying “Jesus is Lord”?

No. Paul joins the lips and the heart: the confession is the expression of a faith that comes from God and is lived. Elsewhere he writes that no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:3), and that faith works through love (Galatians 5:6). Jesus himself warns that not everyone who says “Lord, Lord” will enter the kingdom (Matthew 7:21). Faith, Baptism and a life of love belong together.

Why does Jesus use such strong words about cutting off a hand or an eye?

He is using a vivid Semitic way of speaking to say that nothing is worth more than life with God. No Christian teaching asks us to hurt the body. The “hand”, “foot” and “eye” stand for whatever we hold dear and which leads us to sin, such as a habit, a friendship, or a source of income. The Church asks us to break with it, by repentance and by the grace of the sacraments.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the Syro-Malankara tradition, as in the other Eastern Churches, children receive Baptism, Chrismation with the Holy Mooron and the Holy Qurbono together, so the child whom Jesus places in the midst is truly at the table of the Lord. This Sunday also falls in the long season after Pentecost, in which the Church reads the Scriptures steadily and hears them as the unfolding of the Spirit’s work in the life of the community. The evening Gospel of the loaves and fish prepares us for the Holy Qurbono, where Christ still says, “You give them something to eat”, and gives the apostles and their successors bread to set before the crowd. The cry “Kurielaison” (Lord, have mercy) in the Qurbono and the hours is the cry of the one who has found the water too bitter and calls on the name of the Lord, in the words of Romans 10:13.

For study and teaching

  • At Marah the people complained and Moses cried out to the Lord. When bitterness comes into my life, do I complain, cry out, or do both, and who helps me to see “the wood”?
  • Paul says that the word is near, on my lips and in my heart. What word of the Gospel has been closest to me this week, and have I confessed it aloud to anyone?
  • Jesus puts a child in the midst and says that whoever welcomes such a child welcomes him. Whom in my family or parish do I overlook as “too small”, and how can I welcome them?
  • The Father does not want one of the little ones lost. Is there someone who has strayed from the Church whom I can search for with patience, as the shepherd does?
