Word for the day

“for we walk by faith, not by sight.” 2 Corinthians 5:7

Reflection

St Paul is writing about confidence, and the odd thing is that he is writing from a place where nothing can be seen. “So we are always confident; even though we know that while we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight.” Anyone who has crossed a flooded paddy field at night along the narrow bund will know what he means. You cannot see the whole path; a small lamp shows the next step and the next, and you walk because you trust that the ground is there. Faith is that walk. Paul is not despising the body, which God made and will raise; he is saying that, as long as we live in it, we know the Lord by trust and not yet by sight, and that this is enough for courage. Because the end is the Lord, he gives life a single aim: “whether we are at home or away, we make it our aim to please him.” And he adds a sober reminder: “For all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive recompense for what has been done in the body, whether good or evil.” We do not meet a stranger there. We meet the Christ who loved us and gave himself for us.

The Gospel puts a picture beside the aim to please him: a fig tree planted in a vineyard, which the owner has watched for three years and found without fruit. “Cut it down! Why should it be wasting the soil?” In our own compounds the picture is easy to see: a jackfruit or mango tree that gets the best of the soil and the rain and never bears. The tree is every one of us, and the soil that it wastes is the time, the gifts and the grace that God has given. Jesus told this parable immediately after speaking of repentance, when he said that unless people repent they will all perish (Luke 13:1–5). The fruit that he looks for is a changed life: the fruit of the Spirit, which is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.

Then the gardener speaks, and the whole story turns on his voice: “Sir, let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it. If it bears fruit next year, well and good; but if not, you can cut it down.” The Church has long heard in these words the voice of Christ, who stands between us and judgment and asks for time. He does not ask the owner to ignore the barren tree; he promises to work: to dig around it, loosen the hard ground and feed it. And note what he feeds it with: the manure. God does not waste even the refuse of our lives, our failures and our humiliations; in his hands they become what makes us fruitful. The one more year is today, for “now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” In the lean, rainy weeks of Karkidakam our grandparents used to say that the tree you tend now feeds the house next year. Let us not waste the year that the Lord asks for us.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells us that we walk by faith, not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7). Think of crossing a flooded field at night with a small lamp. You see only the next step, and you walk because you trust that the ground is there. Faith is that walk. And Paul gives life a single aim: whether we are at home or away, we make it our aim to please him (2 Corinthians 5:9).

He adds a sober reminder: all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10). But the judge is the Christ who loved us and gave himself for us. The question is not how to escape him but how to be ready to meet him.

The Gospel gives a picture: a fig tree that has borne no fruit for three years. The owner says, Cut it down. The gardener says, Sir, let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it (Luke 13:8). Jesus is that gardener. He gives us time, and he digs and feeds us with his grace. Even the refuse of our lives he turns into nourishment.

So let us not waste the year he asks for us. Now is the acceptable time; now is the day of salvation (2 Corinthians 6:2). Let us take one step by faith today, and let us bear one fruit of the Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, do you have a tree at home, a mango or a jackfruit or a coconut? Think how long it takes for a tree to give fruit! A man in Jesus' story had a fig tree that gave no fruit for three years. He wanted to cut it down, but the gardener said, “Give it one more year. I will take care of it.” Jesus is like that gardener. He is patient with us, he helps us grow, and he wants us to bear good fruit: kindness, sharing and love. What good fruit will you grow this week?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the Gardener who asks for one more year for each of us. Give us the faith to walk when we cannot see, and the aim to please you in all things. Dig around our hardness, feed us with your grace, and make us bear the fruit of love, so that when we stand before your judgment seat we may hear: Well done. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s readings ask what we do with the time God gives us. Paul, in the middle of the weakness of the body, is confident because he walks by faith and wants only to please the Lord. In Luke, a gardener begs for one more year for a barren fig tree. The time we have is a mercy, and the mercy is meant to bear fruit. The key words are aim to please and one more year.

2 Corinthians 5:6–10 · We walk by faith, not by sight

Setting.

Paul has just spoken about the earthly “tent” of the body and the “building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens” (5:1). He writes after a time in which he was close to death (1:8–10), and he is thinking honestly about death and about what lies beyond it.

These verses are neither a wish for death nor a fear of it. Paul says that he is confident, even though he is away from the Lord while he lives in the body, and he would rather be at home with him. But his chief aim, whether he is alive or dead, is to please the Lord, for the Lord is also the judge.

Structure.

  • vv. 6–7: confidence now: away from the Lord in the body, but walking by faith.
  • v. 8: the preference: to be away from the body and at home with the Lord.
  • v. 9: the aim in both states: to please him.
  • v. 10: all must appear before the judgment seat of Christ.

Key words.

  • dia pisteōs … ou dia eidous, by faith, not by sight: The Greek word for “sight” can also mean “appearance”. We live by what God has said and promised, not by what we can see or feel.
  • philotimoumetha, we make it our aim: Literally, “we are ambitious” (from the love of honour). Paul turns the word of worldly ambition toward a single goal: to please the Lord.
  • bēma, judgment seat: A raised platform on which a judge sat. It stood in the public square at Corinth (Acts 18:12), so the readers knew exactly what Paul meant.

Verse by verse.

“we walk by faith, not by sight.” (5:7)

Faith is a way of walking: a step at a time, trusting God, even when the road ahead is not visible.

“we would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord.” (5:8)

Paul looks to death without fear, because of the Lord who waits there. He does not despise the body, which will be raised (see 4:14), but longs for closer union with Christ.

“we make it our aim to please him.” (5:9)

This is the centre of Christian life. We do not act in order to earn God’s love but to answer it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that death ends the time in which we can accept or reject the grace God offers us in Christ (CCC 1021), and that each person receives, at the moment of death, an eternal retribution in an individual judgment (CCC 1022). Judgment is not meant to terrify: the Church sees in the judgment seat of Christ the face of the one who died for us. At the Holy Qurbono we meet the Lord under signs, by faith and not by sight, and this is a foretaste of the day when we shall be at home with him.

Luke 13:6–9 · The barren fig tree

Setting.

The parable follows Jesus’ teaching on repentance after two tragic events: Pilate’s killing of Galileans in the temple and the fall of the tower of Siloam (13:1–5). Jesus warns, “unless you repent, you will all perish” (13:3, 5), and then tells this story to show what repentance is for: fruit.

In the Old Testament the fig tree and the vine often stand for Israel (Hosea 9:10; Joel 1:7; Micah 7:1). But Luke gives the parable to every hearer, not only to a nation: “unless you repent, you will all perish” is said to the whole crowd. The vineyard is the place of God’s care, and the three years and the extra year suggest patience that is real but not endless.

Structure.

  • v. 6: a man plants a fig tree in his vineyard and finds no fruit.
  • v. 7: his verdict: three years without fruit; cut it down.
  • v. 8: the gardener’s plea: leave it one more year; he will dig and manure.
  • v. 9: the open ending: if it bears fruit, well and good; if not, cut it down.

Key words.

  • karpos, fruit: The fruit of repentance, as John the Baptist taught (3:8). Fruit is not a show but what grows from a living root.
  • aphes, let it alone: The same word is the usual Greek word for “forgive” (as in the Our Father, 11:4). The gardener’s request is a request for forgiveness and mercy.
  • ampelourgos, vinedresser, gardener: The one who cares for the vineyard. The parable gives him a voice for the patient intercession that Christians have always heard in the prayer of Christ for us.

Verse by verse.

“he came looking for fruit on it and found none.” (13:6)

The owner’s search is reasonable. God looks for fruit because he has planted us and cared for us.

“let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it.” (13:8)

The gardener does not defend the barren tree. He asks for time and promises extra work. Mercy gives time, and it also takes trouble.

“If it bears fruit next year, well and good; but if not, you can cut it down.” (13:9)

Jesus does not tell us how the story ends, for it is our story. The ending is left to the hearer.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in the gardener the intercession of Christ, who “always lives to make intercession” for us (Hebrews 7:25). The Catechism teaches that Jesus calls us to conversion, which is at once a gift of grace and a task of daily life (CCC 1427–1428). The time we are given is therefore not neutral: it is time for fruit, shown in prayer, penance, the care of the poor and the peace we make. The parable should not be read as a threat. It is a gentle warning, spoken by someone who has dug around the roots of the tree and given it manure, and is willing to do so again.

For study and teaching

  • Paul’s chief aim is to please the Lord, whether in life or in death. What is the chief aim that drives my days, and what would change if it were his?
  • The gardener asks for one more year and promises to dig and to fertilise. Whose “one more year” has someone given me, and who needs my patience and my help to bear fruit?
