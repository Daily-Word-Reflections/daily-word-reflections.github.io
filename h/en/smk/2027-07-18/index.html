Word for the day

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 14:11

Reflection

At a dinner Jesus watches the guests choosing the places of honour and says: “When you are invited, go and sit down at the lowest place, so that when your host comes, he may say to you, ‘Friend, move up higher’; then you will be honoured in the presence of all who sit at the table with you. For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Humility is not thinking little of ourselves; it is letting God decide our place.

In the evening Gospel Jesus tells three small parables of the kingdom. A man sows good seed, but while everyone sleeps an enemy sows weeds among the wheat. The servants want to pull them up at once. The master says: “No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them. Let both of them grow together until the harvest.” God is patient. The Church on earth is a field of wheat and weeds; judgment belongs to God at the harvest, not to us now.

The kingdom is also like a mustard seed, “the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches”; and like yeast that a woman mixed into three measures of flour until all of it was leavened. God works through small and hidden things: a family Rosary, a catechism class, one honest worker in an office.

Paul reaches Rome as a prisoner, but believers walk out to meet him, and “on seeing them, Paul thanked God and took courage.” He reminds the Corinthians, who were taking one another to court, of what they had become: “you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God.” A humble and patient Church, aware of the grace she has received, will grow like the mustard seed.

Homily outline

Theme: “The lowest place and the hidden seed.”

At a dinner Jesus teaches humility; in the parables he shows how God's kingdom grows quietly, patiently, from small beginnings. In two weeks the Fifteen Days' Fast in honour of the Mother of God begins.

1. Take the lowest place

  • Guests rush for places of honour; Jesus tells them to sit at the lowest.
  • Humility is not thinking little of ourselves but seeing ourselves truly before God.
  • The greater one's position, the greater the need for humility.
“The greater you are, the more you must humble yourself; so you will find favor in the sight of the Lord.” Sirach 3:18
“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 14:11

Apply: This week let someone else have the honour, the credit or the last word.

2. Wheat and weeds: the patience of God

  • Good and evil grow together in the world and even in the Church until the harvest.
  • God is patient, giving time for repentance; judgement belongs to him.
  • Our task is not to uproot others but to be wheat.
“Through such works you have taught your people that the righteous must be kind, and you have filled your children with good hope, because you give repentance for sins.” Wisdom 12:19
“Let both of them grow together until the harvest; and at harvest time I will tell the reapers, Collect the weeds first and bind them in bundles to be burned, but gather the wheat into my barn.” Matthew 13:30

Apply: Replace one judgemental thought about someone with a prayer for them.

3. Mustard seed and yeast

  • The kingdom begins small and grows great; it works quietly like yeast.
  • Keeping God's law faithfully is Israel's wisdom before the nations; our faithful lives are a witness too.
  • Mary, the humble handmaid, is the perfect example of this hidden growth.
“You must observe them diligently, for this will show your wisdom and discernment to the peoples, who, when they hear all these statutes, will say, “Surely this great nation is a wise and discerning people!”” Deuteronomy 4:6
“The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in his field; it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.” Matthew 13:31–32

Apply: Begin now to plan how your family will keep the Fifteen Days' Fast from 1 August.

God's kingdom grows through the humble, the patient and the faithful in small things.

Let us take the lowest place, trust God's patience, and be yeast in the world.

Take-home resolution: Pray the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55) each evening this week.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Jesus is at a dinner and notices the guests rushing for the best seats. He says: Sit in the lowest place, and let the host call you higher. “For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” In families, parishes and offices there is often a quiet competition for the best seat. Jesus offers us freedom: let God decide your place.

In the evening Gospel a farmer's enemy sows weeds among his wheat. The servants want to pull them out at once. The master says: no, you will pull out the wheat too; let them grow together until the harvest. We are often impatient servants. We want to separate the good and the bad in the Church and in our families right now. But God is patient. Many who look like weeds today will become wheat tomorrow. Judgment belongs to him.

Then Jesus speaks of the tiny mustard seed that becomes a tree, and of the yeast hidden in flour. God's kingdom grows quietly: a mother's prayer, a catechism class on Sunday morning, a young person's honest word. Do not despise small beginnings.

St Paul reminds the Corinthians: “you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ.” Remember who you are. And remember: on 1 August the Fifteen Days' Fast begins, in honour of our Mother's Assumption. Let us prepare our hearts with humility. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: when you go to a party, don't run for the best chair! Sit in a simple place, and the host may say, “Come up here, friend!”

He also told a story about a tiny mustard seed that grew into a big tree where birds could build nests.

Small kind acts can grow big too! This week, let someone else go first.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you lifted up the humble and were patient with the field of wheat and weeds. Teach us to take the lowest place, to leave judgment to you, and to trust the small seeds of your kingdom. Wash, sanctify and justify us in your Spirit, and prepare us to keep the fast in honour of your Mother. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Ninth Sunday after Pentecost the readings ask what a holy community looks like and how it lives among others. In the evening Gospel (Matthew 13:24–35) Jesus tells three parables of the kingdom: wheat and weeds growing together until the harvest, a mustard seed that becomes a tree, and yeast hidden in three measures of flour. Sirach 13:1–7 warns against the company of the proud and of the powerful who use others. Psalm 22:1–11 begins with the cry that Jesus took on the cross, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”, and turns to trust: “In you our ancestors trusted”. Isaiah 24:1–5 describes the earth polluted by those who have “broken the everlasting covenant”. In Acts 28:11–22 Paul reaches Rome; when the brothers come out to meet him on the road, he “thanked God and took courage”, and he tells the leaders of the Jews that he is in chains “for the sake of the hope of Israel”.

We study three readings more closely. In Deuteronomy 4:1–6 Moses tells Israel that the statutes God has given them will show their wisdom to the nations. In 1 Corinthians 6:1–11 Paul tells a community of quarrelling believers that they have been “washed … sanctified … justified”, and so should live as people who are no longer what they were. In the Gospel (Luke 14:7–11) Jesus watches the guests choosing the best places, and tells them that “all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” The key words are wisdom, holiness and humility.

Deuteronomy 4:1–6 · This great nation is a wise and discerning people

Setting.

Deuteronomy is Moses’ farewell, spoken on the plains of Moab, with the Jordan before the people and the promised land on the other side. In chapters 1–3 he recalls the forty years in the desert. In chapter 4 he turns from the past to the future with the words “So now, Israel”: what the people have seen of God’s works must now become obedience.

The word translated “statutes and ordinances” is the Law that God gave at Horeb (Sinai). To Moses these are not a burden but the people’s life: “so that you may live”. The chapter goes on to say that no other nation has a God so near, or laws so just (4:7–8).

Structure.

  • v. 1: listen to the statutes and ordinances, so that you may live and take the land.
  • v. 2: add nothing, take nothing away; keep the commandments.
  • vv. 3–4: the lesson of Baal of Peor: those who followed him were destroyed; those who held fast to the Lord are alive today.
  • vv. 5–6: Moses teaches them to keep the commandments, which will show their wisdom to the peoples, who will say, “Surely this great nation is a wise and discerning people!”
  • ¶ The passage moves from obedience, to the memory of a failure and a rescue, to witness before the nations.

Key words.

  • šᵉma‘, listen, give heed: The command is the first word of the great prayer of Israel, “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4). In the Bible to “hear” means to listen and to obey. Faith begins with listening.
  • ḥuqqîm ûmišpāṭîm, statutes and ordinances: A pair of words for God’s whole instruction: the decrees and the judgments that shape life in the covenant. The Hebrew word tôrâ, “Law”, means more precisely “teaching”, a father’s instruction to his children.
  • dābaq, to hold fast, to cling: The verb used in Genesis 2:24 of a man who “clings” to his wife. Israel is called to cling to the Lord with a love that does not let go. Verse 4 contrasts them with those who left him for Baal.
  • ḥokmâ ûbînâ, wisdom and understanding: Wisdom is the skill of living well, and understanding is the discernment to see what God is doing. Moses says that Israel’s observance of the Law will make these visible to other nations.

Verse by verse.

“So now, Israel, give heed to the statutes and ordinances that I am teaching you to observe, so that you may live” (4:1)

Obedience is the road to life. The Law is not given to make the people anxious but so that “you may live”, in the land and in the friendship of God.

“You must neither add anything to what I command you nor take away anything from it” (4:2)

The command protects God’s word from human changes. Jesus, quoting Isaiah, warns against replacing God’s commandments with human traditions (Mark 7:8–9). The Church teaches that the deposit of faith is handed on whole: its understanding can grow, but its content cannot be altered (CCC 66, 94).

“while those of you who held fast to the Lord your God are all alive today.” (4:4)

The reference is to Numbers 25, when many Israelites joined in the worship of the Baal of Peor and a plague broke out. Moses reminds the survivors that their life was a gift linked to their loyalty.

“You must observe them diligently, for this will show your wisdom and discernment to the peoples” (4:6)

Israel’s mission is public. Its way of life is meant to be seen, and to draw the respect of other peoples. The nations do not see a conqueror but a community that has been taught by God.

“Surely this great nation is a wise and discerning people!” (4:6)

This is the answer Moses hopes to hear from the nations. The prophets later foresee them coming to learn the Lord’s ways in Zion (Isaiah 2:2–3).

How the Church reads it.

The Church receives the Law of Moses as God’s gift to Israel and as a preparation for the Gospel: the Catechism calls the Old Law “the first stage of revealed Law”, which Christ did not abolish but fulfilled (CCC 1961–1964; Matthew 5:17). The wisdom that Moses speaks of finds its fullness in Christ, in whom “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3). Paul’s teaching about the Church as a holy people among the nations, which we hear today in the Epistle, continues the same call: to live so that others may see God’s wisdom in the way we live.

1 Corinthians 6:1–11 · You were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus about AD 54–55. After dealing with divisions (chapters 1–4) and a case of grave immorality (chapter 5), he turns to another scandal: believers were taking each other to court before the city’s magistrates. Corinth was a busy Roman colony where legal disputes were common, and the courts favoured the rich and well connected.

Paul does not ask whether the law of the land is wrong. He asks what it says about the community of the baptized if it cannot settle its own quarrels. His answer is short: the Church is a holy people, and the life of holiness has begun in Baptism.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the shame of seeking justice from unbelievers, when the saints will judge the world, and even angels.
  • vv. 4–6: surely a wise person in the community can decide between believers.
  • vv. 7–8: lawsuits among believers are already a defeat; better to be wronged than to wrong.
  • vv. 9–10: a warning: wrongdoers will not inherit the kingdom of God.
  • v. 11: “And this is what some of you used to be. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified.”
  • ¶ The passage moves from a practical matter to the baptismal grace that makes the community holy.

Key words.

  • hagioi, the saints, the holy ones: Paul’s name for all the baptized, not for a few heroes. It means those set apart for God. To be called “saints” is the community’s starting point, not its prize.
  • apelousasthe, you were washed: A word for washing the body, used here for Baptism (see also Acts 22:16; Titus 3:5). The Greek verb can be read as “you had yourselves washed”: they came to Baptism of their own will, and received the grace.
  • hēgiasthēte … edikaiōthēte, you were sanctified … you were justified: Paul puts holiness and justification together, in the order of Baptism. They are God’s gift, “in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God”: the whole Trinity is at work.

Verse by verse.

“Do you not know that the saints will judge the world?” (6:2)

The Scriptures speak of the faithful sharing in God’s judgment (Daniel 7:22; Wisdom 3:7–8; Matthew 19:28). If they are destined for that, Paul asks, how can they be unable to settle a small quarrel?

“I say this to your shame.” (6:5)

Paul speaks sharply because the matter is serious. He wants the Corinthians to see how far their conduct is from what they are.

“In fact, to have lawsuits at all with one another is already a defeat for you. Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded?” (6:7)

These words echo the teaching of Jesus about turning the other cheek (Matthew 5:39–40). Paul does not ask believers to be silent about justice; he asks them to put the bond of the community above the winning of every dispute.

“Do you not know that wrongdoers will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived!” (6:9)

The list that follows names serious sins: idolatry, adultery, theft, greed, drunkenness, abuse, robbery, and sexual sins. Paul’s purpose is not to condemn certain people but to warn that these acts close the door of the kingdom unless the person turns from them. The Church teaches respect, compassion and sensitivity towards every person, and that every sign of unjust discrimination is to be avoided (CCC 2358).

“And this is what some of you used to be. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God.” (6:11)

This is the centre of the passage. “Used to be” says that no one is bound for ever by what he or she has been. Baptism washes, sanctifies and justifies, by the grace of the Trinity, and not by anything the Corinthians had done.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes the three verbs of verse 11 as a description of Baptism: the sacrament is called “the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit” (CCC 1215), and justification is the work of grace, which cleanses us from sin and gives us God’s own righteousness (CCC 1987). The Church has also always taught that those who fall into grave sin after Baptism may be reconciled by the sacrament of Penance, which the Fathers called “a laborious kind of baptism” (CCC 980, 1446). Paul’s warning in verses 9–10 and his comfort in verse 11 belong together: the warning is serious because the grace is real. The call to settle quarrels within the community has its source in Jesus’ own advice (Matthew 18:15–17), and the Church carries on this work of reconciliation and justice through her own tribunals and pastors.

Luke 14:7–11 · Those who humble themselves will be exalted

Setting.

Jesus has been invited to eat on the sabbath in the house of a leader of the Pharisees (14:1). The invitation shows that he continued to eat with Pharisees, as Luke tells in other places (7:36; 11:37). Before the meal he heals a man suffering from dropsy and asks whether it is lawful to heal on the sabbath (14:2–6). Then, as the guests find their places, Jesus notices how they choose the seats of honour, and tells them a parable.

The advice has a long root in Israel’s wisdom: “Do not put yourself forward in the king’s presence … for it is better to be told, ‘Come up here,’ than to be put lower in the presence of a noble” (Proverbs 25:6–7). Jesus takes the old counsel and deepens it. In the next verses (14:12–14) he turns from the guests to the host, and says to invite the poor and the sick.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the occasion: Jesus sees how guests choose the places of honour.
  • vv. 8–9: do not take the best seat at a wedding feast; the host may ask you to give it up, and then you will begin with shame to take the lowest.
  • v. 10: take the lowest place, so that the host will say, “Friend, move up higher”, and you will be honoured.
  • v. 11: the saying: those who exalt themselves will be humbled, those who humble themselves will be exalted.

Key words.

  • prōtoklisiai, places of honour, first couches: At a formal Greek or Roman dinner, guests reclined on couches, and the places nearest the host were the most honoured. The word names the competition for status that is as old as the table.
  • tapeinoō, to humble, to make low: The word comes from tapeinos, “low”. In the Bible humility is not self-contempt but the true place of a creature before God, and of a person before others. Mary says that God “has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant” (1:48).
  • hypsoō, to exalt, to lift up: The verb is used of God’s reversals in Scripture. In the passive form, “will be exalted”, Jesus speaks of God’s act without naming God: it is God who raises up and God who lowers (see 1:52).

Verse by verse.

“When he noticed how the guests chose the places of honor, he told them a parable.” (14:7)

Jesus watches. The parable begins from ordinary life, and its first lesson is to see ourselves as we are when we come to a gathering.

“When you are invited by someone to a wedding banquet, do not sit down at the place of honor” (14:8)

The wedding banquet is a frequent image in the Gospels for the kingdom of God (Matthew 22:1–14). The lesson on the guests’ seats is also a lesson on how to take our place before God.

“But when you are invited, go and sit down at the lowest place, so that when your host comes, he may say to you, ‘Friend, move up higher’” (14:10)

Jesus does not tell us to pretend to be small in order to win a better place. The point is that the host decides, and that those who come humbly receive more than they expected.

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” (14:11)

This saying appears in several places (Luke 18:14; Matthew 23:12). The Lord’s own life fulfils it: he “humbled himself”, and God “highly exalted him” (Philippians 2:8–9).

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer: “Man is a beggar before God” (CCC 2559). St Teresa of Avila said that humility is truth (in substance): to see ourselves as God sees us, and no greater or smaller. Many preachers have heard in this Gospel an invitation to the banquet of the Eucharist, where no one holds a reserved place by merit, and where the Lord himself serves (Luke 22:27). The Church also looks to Mary as the model of this humility, whom God lifted up (Luke 1:48, 52), and whose Assumption we shall soon celebrate.

Questions people ask

Does Paul say that Christians should never go to court?

No. Paul is speaking of lawsuits between believers before unbelieving judges, and of the scandal of a community that cannot reconcile itself. He asks that quarrels be settled within the Church, in the spirit of Jesus’ own advice (Matthew 18:15–17). The Church has its own law and tribunals, and it respects the just laws and courts of civil society. When justice requires it, to seek it is not a sin. The aim is that Christians should first look for reconciliation, as brothers and sisters, before looking for victory.

What does it mean that “the saints will judge the world”?

The Scriptures say that God’s people will share in his judgment at the end (Daniel 7:22; Wisdom 3:7–8; Matthew 19:28; Revelation 20:4). Paul’s argument is simple: if we are called to take part in the great judgment, we should be able to settle small disputes. The Church teaches that the final judgment belongs to Christ alone (CCC 679), and that we share in it by his grace, not by our own power.

Is it wrong to want to do well, or to accept honour?

No. Jesus does not condemn work well done or honour rightly given; he condemns the struggle for the first place, and the habit of measuring ourselves by it. True humility is truth: to know that every gift comes from God and to use it for others. The proud person is always afraid of being found out, and the humble person is at ease being himself or herself.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost show the Church that the Holy Spirit has gathered, and teach her how to live. Today’s lesson is humility. We are the “saints” of Corinth, washed in Baptism, and we come to the table of the Lord as guests, not as owners. The Fast of the Assumption (Shunoyo Nombu) begins on 1 August, in preparation for the feast of the Assumption of Mary, Mother of God, on 15 August. The humble servant of the Lord, whom God has lifted up, is the model we are given. As we prepare for the fast, we may ask for the grace of her humility, so that we too may be found among those whom God exalts.

For study and teaching

  • Moses says that Israel’s way of life should show wisdom to the nations. What does my family’s or parish’s way of life show to our neighbours?
  • Paul says, “And this is what some of you used to be.” What would it mean for me to live as one who has been washed, sanctified and justified, and not as the person I used to be?
  • Is there a quarrel in my family or parish in which I could choose to be wronged, for the sake of peace?
  • Where do I look for the best seat, in the home, in the parish, at work? What would the lowest place look like there?
