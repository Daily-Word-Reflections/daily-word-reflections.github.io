Word for the day

“Do not, therefore, abandon that confidence of yours; it brings a great reward.” Hebrews 10:35

Reflection

Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews asks its readers to remember: “recall those earlier days when, after you had been enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings.” The early Christians often called Baptism “enlightenment.” St Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, explains that the washing of Baptism is called illumination, because those who receive it are enlightened in their understanding (First Apology 61). In the Easter season the Church thinks especially of those baptized at Easter, and of our own Baptism. The first readers of this Letter had paid a price for that light. They had been “publicly exposed to abuse and persecution”; they had stood by others who suffered; they “had compassion for those who were in prison,” and they “cheerfully accepted the plundering” of their possessions, “knowing that you yourselves possessed something better and more lasting.” Now, years later, some were growing tired. So the Letter says: “Do not, therefore, abandon that confidence of yours; it brings a great reward. For you need endurance.”

The risen Lord is our reason for endurance. “In a very little while, the one who is coming will come and will not delay; but my righteous one will live by faith.” The Letter ends the passage with a sentence of courage: “we are not among those who shrink back and so are lost, but among those who have faith and so are saved.” In the Gospel Jesus shows that faith is needed not only under persecution but in the daily life of the community. “Occasions for stumbling are bound to come, but woe to anyone by whom they come!” He speaks with great seriousness about anyone who causes “one of these little ones to stumble,” words that the Church today hears as a solemn call to protect children and the vulnerable in every parish and home. Then he asks for something that seems impossible: “if the same person sins against you seven times a day, and turns back to you seven times and says, ‘I repent,’ you must forgive.” The apostles' reply is honest: “Increase our faith!” They understood that only faith can forgive like that.

Jesus answers: “If you had faith the size of a mustard seed, you could say to this mulberry tree, ‘Be uprooted and planted in the sea,’ and it would obey you.” The mulberry tree had deep and stubborn roots, like a resentment that has grown in us for years. A small faith in the risen Lord can uproot it. How do we live this today? First, remember your own enlightenment: take out your baptismal certificate, find the date, and thank God for the light you received. Second, look for one person whom you have stopped forgiving, perhaps someone who keeps hurting you in the same way, and ask the Lord for faith enough to forgive once more, while also asking wisely for the change that true repentance requires. Third, be a guardian of the little ones: let no word or example of yours make a child stumble. And when you feel tired in faith, make the apostles' prayer your own, especially before the Holy Qurbono: “Lord, increase our faith.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews says: recall those earlier days when, after you had been enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings (Hebrews 10:32). The early Church called Baptism enlightenment.

Those first Christians had suffered, been mocked, lost their possessions, and still rejoiced, knowing they had something better and more lasting. Now some were growing tired. So the Letter says: do not, therefore, abandon that confidence of yours; it brings a great reward (Hebrews 10:35).

In the Gospel Jesus asks us to protect the little ones and to forgive, even seven times a day. The apostles reply: Increase our faith! They knew that only faith can forgive like that. Jesus answers that faith the size of a mustard seed can uproot a mulberry tree, the deep roots of an old resentment.

Let us thank God today for our Baptism, forgive one person once more, and pray before the Holy Qurbono: Lord, increase our faith. Amen.

For children

Children, a mustard seed is tiny, smaller than a grain of rice. Jesus said that if our faith is even that small, it can pull up a big tree! When is it hard for you to forgive your brother, sister or friend? Ask Jesus: “Lord, give me more faith so that I can forgive.” Then go and make peace.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you enlightened us in Baptism and called us to share in your victory. When we grow tired, do not let us abandon our confidence; give us endurance to do the will of God and receive what you have promised. Make us guardians of the little ones, and give us hearts that forgive again and again. Increase our faith, that even a mustard seed of it may uproot the bitterness that has grown in us. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Wednesday asks how faith lasts. Hebrews tells tired Christians to remember the early days of their faith, when they suffered with joy, and not to throw away their confidence now. In Luke the apostles hear what forgiveness asks, seven times in a day, and they do the right thing: they ask for more faith. Jesus answers that the question is not how much faith, but whether it is real.

Hebrews 10:32–39 · Do not abandon your confidence

Setting.

After the warning of vv. 26–31 comes encouragement. The readers had once endured public insult; some of them had been imprisoned, and others had lost their property. The letter gives no names or dates. Its closing greeting from “those from Italy” (13:24) suggests a link with Rome, and many scholars connect these sufferings with troubles faced by Christians there in the first century, but certainty is not possible.

Structure.

  • vv. 32–34: remember the former days.
  • vv. 35–36: do not abandon your confidence; you need endurance.
  • vv. 37–39: the word of the prophet (Isaiah 26:20 and Habakkuk 2:3–4), and “we are not among those who shrink back”.

Key words.

  • phōtisthentes, enlightened: Also used at 6:4. Early Christians called Baptism “enlightenment”; St Justin Martyr uses this name in the second century (First Apology 61).
  • hypomonē, endurance: Not passive waiting but steady staying power under a heavy load.
  • hypostolē, shrinking back: Drawing back in fear. It is the opposite of parrēsia, the confidence of v. 35.

Verse by verse.

“But recall those earlier days when, after you had been enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings,” (10:32)

Memory is a remedy for weariness. What God did in us at the beginning is a pledge of what he will do.

“you cheerfully accepted the plundering of your possessions, knowing that you yourselves possessed something better and more lasting.” (10:34)

Their joy did not come from loss itself but from knowing what could not be taken away.

“but my righteous one will live by faith.” (10:38)

Habakkuk 2:4, quoted in its Greek form. Paul also uses it (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11). Here faith means faithful perseverance while waiting for the Lord.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith can be lost, and that to persevere in it to the end we must nourish it with the word of God and beg the Lord to increase our faith (CCC 162). The Church has always seen in passages like this one a call to remember: the anniversaries of Baptism, the witness of those who suffered before us, and above all the memorial of the Lord in the Holy Qurbono.

Luke 17:1–6 · Increase our faith

Setting.

Jesus is on the long journey to Jerusalem that Luke describes from 9:51 onward. He has just told the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (16:19–31). Now he turns to his disciples with short teachings: on causing others to stumble, on correcting and forgiving, and on faith.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: causing others to stumble.
  • vv. 3–4: rebuke, and forgive without limit.
  • vv. 5–6: “Increase our faith!” and the mustard seed.

Key words.

  • skandalon, occasion for stumbling: Originally the trigger of a trap or an obstacle that makes someone fall. Here, anything that leads another person into sin.
  • epitimēson, rebuke: To correct, to warn seriously. Forgiveness in this passage does not mean pretending that nothing happened.
  • sykaminos, mulberry tree: Usually identified with the black mulberry, a tree with deep, strong roots. To tell it to be planted in the sea is a deliberate impossibility.

Verse by verse.

“It would be better for you if a millstone were hung around your neck and you were thrown into the sea than for you to cause one of these little ones to stumble.” (17:2)

“Little ones” are not only children but the weak and those new in faith.

“If another disciple sins, you must rebuke the offender, and if there is repentance, you must forgive.” (17:3)

The Greek says “your brother”. Correction is part of love, and forgiveness answers repentance.

“The apostles said to the Lord, “Increase our faith!”” (17:5)

Faced with a command they cannot keep alone, they pray. Jesus' answer shows that even a little real faith, faith in God and not in ourselves, can do what seems impossible.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that there is no limit or measure to this forgiveness, which is divine in its very nature (CCC 2845). It admits that it is not in our power to stop feeling an offence or to forget it, but the heart that offers itself to the Holy Spirit turns injury into compassion (CCC 2843). The apostles' prayer, “Increase our faith”, is the right response to every command of Christ that seems too great for us.

For study and teaching

  • What do I remember of the early days of my faith, or of a time when God was very close? How can that memory help me now?
  • Is there someone I must forgive “seven times”? Can I make the apostles' prayer my own today?
