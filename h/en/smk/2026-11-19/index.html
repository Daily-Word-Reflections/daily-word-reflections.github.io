Word for the day

“The greatest among you will be your servant.” Matthew 23:11

Reflection

Paul turns to the Christian home. Children are to obey their parents “in the Lord” and honour father and mother, the first commandment with a promise. And fathers are told something revolutionary for their time: “do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.” Authority in a Christian family is a service of love, not a tool of control.

Jesus criticises leaders who “do not practice what they teach”, who tie heavy burdens on others and love the best seats and titles. Among his disciples it must be different: one teacher, one Father, one instructor, the Messiah. “The greatest among you will be your servant.” Whether at home or in the Church, the one who serves most is greatest in God's sight.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul turns to the Christian home: children, obey your parents in the Lord; honour your father and mother, the first commandment with a promise. And he tells fathers something revolutionary for the time: do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord (Ephesians 6:1–4).

In the Gospel Jesus warns against leaders who say but do not do. The greatest among you will be your servant; all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and all who humble themselves will be exalted (Matthew 23:11–12).

Faith is first learnt at home, more by example than by words. Parents, let your children see you pray and forgive. Children, thank your father and mother today. A family that walks humbly is a small picture of God's Kingdom. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about authority as service. Paul tells children to honour their parents and tells fathers not to crush their children. Jesus warns against leaders who load burdens on others and love titles: among his disciples, the greatest is the servant.

Ephesians 6:1–4 · Children and parents in the Lord

Setting.

These verses belong to the “household code” of Ephesians (5:21–6:9), which addresses wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. The whole code is governed by its first line: “Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ” (5:21). Children are addressed directly: they were present when the letter was read in the assembly.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: children: obey and honour, with the promise of the commandment.
  • v. 4: fathers: do not provoke; bring up in the Lord.

Key words.

  • hypakouete, obey: Literally “listen under”: obedience begins with listening.
  • paideia kai nouthesia, discipline and instruction: Formation by example and correction, and formation by word. Both are “of the Lord”: parents form children for Christ, not for themselves.

Verse by verse.

“Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right.” (6:1)
“this is the first commandment with a promise:” (6:2)

Exodus 20:12 and Deuteronomy 5:16. The commandment to honour parents is the first that carries a promise of blessing.

“And, fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.” (6:4)

Roman law gave the father almost unlimited power over his children. Paul sets a limit to that power in the name of Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that children owe their parents respect, gratitude and, while they live at home, obedience (CCC 2214–2217), and that parents are the first ones responsible for the education of their children (CCC 2223). St John Paul II called the parents' right and duty to educate their children “essential” and “irreplaceable” (Familiaris Consortio 36).

Matthew 23:1–12 · The greatest among you will be your servant

Setting.

Matthew 23 opens Jesus' last public discourse in the Temple. Matthew wrote for a Church living in tension with the synagogue leaders of his time, and that tension shows. Yet Jesus' words are not aimed at a people. They warn every religious leader, the Church's own shepherds first of all, against saying without doing.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: do what they teach, not what they do.
  • v. 4: heavy burdens on others.
  • vv. 5–7: religion for display.
  • vv. 8–10: one Teacher, one Father, one Instructor.
  • vv. 11–12: the servant is the greatest.

Key words.

  • kathedra, seat: “Moses' seat” means the authority to teach the Law; stone seats of honour have been found in ancient synagogues. Our word “cathedral”, the church of the bishop's chair, comes from the same word.
  • phylaktēria / kraspeda, phylacteries / fringes: Small boxes with Scripture verses worn for prayer (Deuteronomy 6:8) and tassels on the cloak (Numbers 15:38–39). Jesus himself wore the fringe (9:20). He criticises not the custom but the display.
  • diakonos, servant: The word from which “deacon” comes. Authority in the Church is measured by service.

Verse by verse.

“therefore, do whatever they teach you and follow it; but do not do as they do, for they do not practice what they teach.” (23:3)

Jesus respects the teaching office even when its holders fail. The word of God keeps its value even on unworthy lips.

“And call no one your father on earth, for you have one Father” (23:9)
“The greatest among you will be your servant. All who exalt themselves will be humbled, and all who humble themselves will be exalted.” (23:11–12)

The whole passage is summed up here, in Jesus' own life: he came “not to be served but to serve” (20:28).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that ministry in the Church is by its nature service: those who receive it become servants of Christ (CCC 876). St Gregory the Great called himself “servant of the servants of God”, a title the popes still use.

Questions people ask

If Jesus says “call no one father”, why do we call priests “Father”?

Jesus forbids putting any human being in the place of God, the source of all fatherhood. He does not forbid the word: Paul writes, “in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel” (1 Corinthians 4:15). A priest is called father because he serves the fatherhood of God, not because he replaces it.

For study and teaching

  • Ephesians 6:4 says “do not provoke your children to anger.” What in my words or habits provokes anger at home?
  • Where am I tempted to “say and not do”?
