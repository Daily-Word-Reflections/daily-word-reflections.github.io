Word for the day

“You also are to testify because you have been with me from the beginning.” John 15:27

Reflection

The Epistle today takes us into one of the sharpest moments of the early Church. At Antioch, Peter had been eating with Gentile believers, and then, when visitors came from Jerusalem, he drew back, as if the table of Christ had two classes of guests. Paul opposed him to his face, and in today's verses he gives the reason: “We ourselves are Jews by birth and not Gentile sinners; yet we know that a person is justified not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ. And we have come to believe in Christ Jesus, so that we might be justified by faith in Christ, and not by doing the works of the law, because no one will be justified by the works of the law.” Paul is not speaking against good works, nor against the law God gave to Israel. He is saying that no one, Jew or Gentile, can stand before God by a record of achievements. We are made righteous because Christ has loved us and given himself for us, and we receive that gift by faith. That is why the table cannot be divided: everyone who sits there sits by the same grace. In the Holy Qurbono the priest and the newest catechumen, the old family and the migrant worker, come forward with the same empty hands.

Then Paul adds a warning: “But if I build up again the very things that I once tore down, then I demonstrate that I am a transgressor.” He means the walls that Christ pulled down between people, and the old self-reliance that grace pulled down in us. It is possible to be set right by God and then quietly to begin building again: to start keeping accounts of our virtues, to look down on those who pray less or give less, to think that our place at the altar is earned. The Christian life is not a building of our own that God inspects at the end; it is a life received. St Augustine put his whole experience of grace into one short prayer: give what you command, and then command what you will (Confessions 10.29, in substance). Everything good we do is first his gift, and our part is to receive it and not to block it.

The Gospel comes from the Last Supper. Jesus promises: “When the Advocate comes, whom I will send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who comes from the Father, he will testify on my behalf. You also are to testify because you have been with me from the beginning.” There are two witnesses here, the Spirit and the disciples, and they speak together. The Spirit does not testify instead of us; he testifies through us, in our words and our lives. Today the Church remembers one in whom that double witness was very plain: St Mariam Thresia of Puthenchira, near Thrissur. Born in 1876, she gave herself from childhood to prayer, and as a young woman she went with a few companions to the homes of the sick, the dying and the poor, and to families torn by quarrels and debts, without asking which church or which community they belonged to. In 1914 she founded the Congregation of the Holy Family to carry on this work, and she died on 8 June 1926. Pope Francis canonized her on 13 October 2019. She was a daughter of the Syro-Malabar Church, and our Panchangam keeps her memory with gladness, for the saints belong to the whole Church. Her works were many, but she never thought she had earned anything by them: she was simply letting the Spirit testify to Jesus through her hands. In this month when families settle into a new school year and the monsoon keeps people indoors, she reminds us that the first place of witness is the home.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul says today: we know that a person is justified not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ (Galatians 2:16). That is good news for every one of us. We do not stand before God with a list of our achievements. We stand before him because Christ loved us and gave himself for us.

Paul also warns us: if I build up again the very things that I once tore down, then I demonstrate that I am a transgressor (Galatians 2:18). Grace pulled down our pride and the walls between us. Let us not build them up again by counting our good deeds or looking down on others.

In the Gospel Jesus promises the Holy Spirit: he will testify on my behalf. You also are to testify because you have been with me from the beginning (John 15:26–27). The Spirit speaks, and we speak; the Spirit works, and we work. Today we remember St Mariam Thresia of Puthenchira. She went into the homes of the sick, the poor and the troubled, and the Spirit testified to Jesus through her hands.

So let us receive God's grace with humility, and let us carry it into homes, beginning with our own. A visit, a meal, a prayer at a sickbed, a quarrel healed: this is how the Spirit testifies today. Amen.

For children

Children, do we have to earn God's love by being very good? No! God loves us first, and Jesus gave his life for us. That is a gift. Because we have received it, we want to share it. Today we remember St Mariam Thresia from Kerala. She loved Jesus so much that she went to visit sick people and poor families, and helped them. When you help your mother at home, or share with a friend who has nothing, you are telling people about Jesus without even using words.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you loved us and gave yourself for us, and by faith in you we are made righteous before the Father. Keep us from building again the walls you have pulled down and the pride you have broken in us. Send us the Spirit of truth, who comes from the Father, to testify to you in our words and our lives. Through the prayers of St Mariam Thresia, who carried your love into the homes of the sick, the poor and the troubled, make our families holy, peaceful and open to all who need us. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of witness. Paul, in the sharpest quarrel of his ministry, bears witness to the heart of the Gospel: we are made right with God not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ. In the Gospel Jesus promises the Spirit of truth, who will testify on his behalf, and tells the disciples, “You also are to testify.” Today the Church remembers St Mariam Thresia, whose whole life was that kind of testimony. The key words are justified and testify.

Galatians 2:15–18 · Justified through faith in Jesus Christ

Setting.

Paul wrote to the churches of Galatia, in central Asia Minor, probably in the mid-50s. Teachers had come there insisting that Gentile Christians must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses in order to belong fully to God's people. In chapter 2 Paul tells how, at Antioch, Peter used to eat with Gentile believers but drew back when “certain people came from James” (2:12), and how Paul opposed him “to his face” (2:11). Today's verses begin as Paul's words to Peter and flow into his teaching to the Galatians.

Structure.

  • v. 15: “We ourselves are Jews by birth”: Paul speaks as a Jew to a Jew.
  • v. 16: justified not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ, said three times over.
  • v. 17: an objection: is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not.
  • v. 18: to build up again what one has torn down is to become a transgressor.

Key words.

  • dikaioō, to justify: To declare and make righteous, to set a person in right relationship with God. For Paul it is God's act, received in faith. The Council of Trent teaches that justification is not only the forgiveness of sins but also the sanctification and renewal of the inner person (Decree on Justification, ch. 7).
  • erga nomou, works of the law: The deeds the law of Moses requires. In this letter Paul has in view above all those that marked off Jews from Gentiles: circumcision, the food laws, the feasts (2:12; 4:10; 5:2). His point is not that the law is bad; in Romans 7:12 he calls it holy. His point is that no one becomes righteous before God by keeping it.
  • pistis Iēsou Christou, faith in Jesus Christ: The Greek can mean “faith in Jesus Christ” or “the faithfulness of Jesus Christ”, and scholars discuss which Paul intends here. The two belong together: Christ's faithfulness to the Father, even to the cross, is what our faith rests on.

Verse by verse.

“yet we know that a person is justified not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ.” (2:16)

“We know”: Paul presents this as ground he shares with Peter, not as his own invention. The verse ends with an echo of Psalm 143:2: “no one living is righteous before you.”

“is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not!” (2:17)

If Jewish believers who eat with Gentiles are counted “sinners” by the standard of the law, has Christ led them into sin? Paul refuses the conclusion with his strongest “no”.

“But if I build up again the very things that I once tore down, then I demonstrate that I am a transgressor.” (2:18)

What was torn down is the law used as a wall between Jew and Gentile at Christ's table (compare Ephesians 2:14). To build it again, as Peter's withdrawal did, would be to say that faith in Christ was not enough.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches with Paul that justification is God's gift, merited for us by the Passion of Christ and given in Baptism (CCC 1987; 1992). No one can merit the first grace of forgiveness and justification; moved by the Holy Spirit, we can then, by grace, do works that are fruitful for eternal life (CCC 2008; 2010). The Council of Trent says that we are justified by faith because faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation and root of all justification (Decree on Justification, ch. 8). In 1999 the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, confessing together that by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit on our part, we are accepted by God.

John 15:26–27 · The Spirit of truth will testify

Setting.

Still at the Last Supper, Jesus has just warned the disciples that the world will hate them as it hated him (15:18–25). Into that warning he sets the promise of a witness: the Advocate. The next verses speak of being put out of the synagogues and of persecution (16:1–4). The testimony of the Spirit and the testimony of the disciples belong to the time of trial.

Structure.

  • v. 26: the Advocate, sent by Jesus from the Father, the Spirit of truth who comes from the Father, will testify to Jesus.
  • v. 27: the disciples also testify, because they have been with him from the beginning.

Key words.

  • ekporeuetai, comes from, proceeds from: The Creed of Constantinople (381) took up this word: the Spirit “proceeds from the Father”. The Eastern tradition stresses that the Father is the first source; the Latin tradition adds “and the Son” (Filioque). The Catholic Church teaches that the two ways of speaking, rightly understood, are complementary and do not affect the identity of the one faith (CCC 246–248).
  • martyrēsei, will testify: A courtroom word: to bear witness. From the same root comes the word “martyr”: one who bears witness to Christ with his life.
  • ap' archēs, from the beginning: From the start of Jesus' ministry. When the apostles choose a successor for Judas, he must be one who was with them from the baptism of John (Acts 1:21–22). The apostolic witness rests on having been with the Lord.

Verse by verse.

“the Spirit of truth who comes from the Father, he will testify on my behalf.” (15:26)

Jesus sends the Spirit “from the Father”: the Son and the Father act together in the sending, and the Spirit's whole work is to point to Jesus.

“You also are to testify because you have been with me from the beginning.” (15:27)

There is one testimony in two voices, the Spirit within and the Church without. Peter says the same before the council in Jerusalem: “we are witnesses to these things, and so is the Holy Spirit” (Acts 5:32).

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these verses her own mission. She is apostolic because she is built on the witness of the apostles and carries it on, guided by the Spirit (CCC 857). Pope St Paul VI wrote that people today listen more willingly to witnesses than to teachers, and if they do listen to teachers, it is because they are witnesses (Evangelii Nuntiandi 41).

Questions people ask

Did Paul think the law of Moses worthless, or the Jewish people rejected?

No. Paul speaks here as a Jew (2:15), and elsewhere he calls the law “holy and just and good” (Romans 7:12). His argument is about how anyone, Jew or Gentile, is made right with God: by grace, through faith in Christ. Of his own people he writes that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29), and the Church teaches the same (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Mariam Thresia (1876–1926) of Puthenchira, foundress of the Congregation of the Holy Family. She went into the homes of the poor and the sick, cared for the dying and worked to bring peace to divided families. A daughter of the Syro-Malabar Church, she was canonised by Pope Francis on 13 October 2019, and her memory is kept with love by many in Kerala. Her life shows what Jesus meant by “you also are to testify”: a witness given less in words than in a whole life spent for others.
