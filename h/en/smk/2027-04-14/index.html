Word for the day

“So I say to you, Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” Luke 11:9

Reflection

Christ is risen! In these weeks after Qyomtho, the newly baptized and all of us are tempted to settle down: the feast is over, Lent is behind us, life goes on as before. Hebrews refuses to let us stop. “Therefore let us go on toward perfection, leaving behind the basic teaching about Christ, and not laying again the foundation: repentance from dead works and faith toward God, instruction about baptisms, laying on of hands, resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment.” The foundation is precious: repentance, faith, Baptism, the laying on of hands, the hope of the resurrection. But a foundation is laid in order to build. The Christian who received the risen Lord at Easter is called to grow, not merely to stay where he was. Then comes a hard warning about those who “have once been enlightened, and have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit,” and then fall away. The Church has never read this as if there were no forgiveness for sins after Baptism; she has always held that there is no sin so grave that the Church cannot forgive it, for those who repent (Catechism 982). The warning is against a deliberate turning away that refuses to come back: that heart, “holding him up to contempt,” closes the door on itself. The remedy is in the image that follows: “Ground that drinks up the rain falling on it repeatedly, and that produces a crop useful to those for whom it is cultivated, receives a blessing from God.” Easter is rain. Let it bear fruit.

Today the Panchangam commemorates Mar Shemun Bar Sabbae, known in the West as St Simeon bar Sabbae, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, the twin royal city of the Persian Empire on the Tigris. In the fourth century, under King Shapur II, the Christians of Persia were suspected of siding with Rome. According to the ancient accounts, Shemun was ordered to collect a heavy double tax from his people and to bring them to worship the sun. He refused: he would not burden his poor flock, and he would worship only the God who made the sun. He was put to death with many bishops, priests and faithful, on a Good Friday, around 341 to 344, at the start of a long persecution. The historian Sozomen tells that an old court official named Usthazanes, who had once been a Christian and had given way under pressure, was moved by the bishop's courage, confessed Christ again, and died a martyr before him. There is Hebrews 6 in a single life: a man who had fallen away, given grace to return, and ground that at last bore fruit. The Syriac Churches have kept the memory of the Persian martyrs with love, and our own liturgy belongs to the same Syriac tradition in which their story was first told.

The Gospel gives the secret of such strength: persistent prayer. Jesus tells of a man who goes to a friend at midnight and says, “Friend, lend me three loaves of bread; for a friend of mine has arrived, and I have nothing to set before him.” The friend inside does not want to get up, but “at least because of his persistence he will get up and give him whatever he needs.” Then the Lord says plainly: “So I say to you, Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” And he ends: “If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!” The greatest gift to ask for is the Holy Spirit, and the Church is now walking toward Pentecost. St Augustine said that God delays at times so that our desire may grow and become able to receive what he wants to give (in substance). Begin today to pray each day for the Holy Spirit, and keep knocking: for your family, for those who have fallen away, and for Christians who are persecuted today, as Mar Shemun's flock once was.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Hebrews says: let us go on toward perfection (Hebrews 6:1). The feast of Easter is not an end. The foundation of faith has been laid in us; now the Lord wants to build.

Ground that drinks up the rain falling on it repeatedly, and that produces a crop useful to those for whom it is cultivated, receives a blessing from God (Hebrews 6:7). Easter is rain on our lives. Let it bear fruit.

Today we remember Mar Shemun Bar Sabbae, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon in Persia. He refused to burden his poor people and to worship the sun, and he died a martyr on a Good Friday in the fourth century. An official who had denied Christ was moved by his courage, came back to the faith and died a martyr too.

Where does such strength come from? From prayer. Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you. How much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him! Let us ask every day until Pentecost. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Jesus told a story about a man who knocked on his friend's door at midnight and kept knocking until his friend gave him bread. Jesus says: keep asking God! He is a good Father, and he loves to give us the Holy Spirit. Every night this week, say this little prayer: Father, give me your Holy Spirit.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you have poured the rain of your grace upon us this Easter. Do not let us settle down, but lead us on toward perfection, and make our lives produce a crop that is useful to others. By the prayers of Mar Shemun Bar Sabbae and the martyrs of Persia, give courage to all Christians who suffer for your name, and bring back those who have fallen away. Teach us to ask, to search and to knock, and fill us with the Holy Spirit whom the Father gives to those who ask him. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Easter is not a place to stop. Hebrews urges believers to move on from the first lessons of faith toward maturity, and warns of ground that drinks the rain and yields only thorns. Luke tells of a friend who knocks at midnight and will not go away, and promises that the Father will give the Holy Spirit to those who ask. Growth comes from asking and receiving. The key words are perfection, rain and ask.

Hebrews 6:1–8 · Let us go on toward perfection

Setting.

Just before this passage the writer has scolded his hearers: by now they ought to be teachers, yet they still need milk and not solid food (5:11–14). So he resolves to lead them on. The six items he lists in 6:1–2 are the basics that a new Christian learned before and around Baptism.

Then comes one of the hardest passages in the New Testament, on those who have fallen away. It must be read with the verse that follows it: “Even though we speak in this way, beloved, we are confident of better things in your case” (6:9). The warning is meant to wake people up, not to drive them to despair.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: move beyond the foundation, which has six parts.
  • vv. 4–6: the danger of falling away after receiving so much.
  • vv. 7–8: a picture from farming: the same rain, two kinds of ground.

Key words.

  • teleiotēs, perfection, maturity: The state of the grown-up believer, able to take solid food. It is the same family of words used of Christ “made perfect” (5:9).
  • phōtisthentas, those who have been enlightened: Early Christians used “enlightenment” as a name for Baptism. St Justin Martyr says so in the second century, and the Catechism repeats it (CCC 1216). The list in vv. 4–5 describes the gifts of Christian initiation.
  • parapesontas, have fallen away: Not an ordinary sin of weakness, but a deliberate turning away from Christ after knowing him, as if crucifying the Son of God again (6:6).

Verse by verse.

“Therefore let us go on toward perfection,” (6:1)

The writer says “let us”: he includes himself. Growth in faith is a road all of us walk together.

“instruction about baptisms, laying on of hands, resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment.” (6:2)

The “laying on of hands” was linked with the gift of the Spirit after Baptism (see Acts 8:17), the root of what our Church gives in Holy Mooron.

“For it is impossible to restore again to repentance those who have once been enlightened,” (6:4)

Many Fathers, among them St John Chrysostom, read this as meaning that Baptism cannot be given a second time, not that repentance is closed (in substance). The person who refuses Christ cannot be brought back by human effort while he goes on refusing.

“Ground that drinks up the rain falling on it repeatedly, and that produces a crop useful to those for whom it is cultivated, receives a blessing from God.” (6:7)

The rain is the same for both fields. What differs is the crop. Grace is given generously, and it asks for a harvest.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always rejected the view that sins after Baptism cannot be forgiven. The Catechism says that there is no offence, however serious, that the Church cannot forgive, and that the door of forgiveness stays open to all who turn back (CCC 982). It also teaches that Baptism is given once and cannot be repeated, because it marks the soul with a seal (CCC 1272). The passage is therefore a call to perseverance, and the sacrament of Penance is the Lord's answer to those who fall.

Luke 11:5–13 · Ask, search, knock

Setting.

Right after giving the Lord's Prayer, Jesus teaches how to pray it. In a village of his day, guests arrived at any hour, and hospitality was a duty for the whole community. A family's bread was baked for the day, and at night the house was shut, with the family sleeping together in one room. A friend who knocked would wake everyone.

Luke alone has the parable of the friend at midnight. The sayings that follow (vv. 9–13) are found also in Matthew 7:7–11.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–8: the friend at midnight.
  • vv. 9–10: ask, search, knock.
  • vv. 11–13: from human fathers to the heavenly Father.

Key words.

  • anaideia, persistence: Literally “shamelessness”. The man at the door does not stand on dignity. Jesus praises a prayer that is not too proud to keep knocking.
  • aiteite, zēteite, krouete, ask, search, knock: Three verbs in the present tense, which in Greek suggests going on doing them: keep asking, keep searching, keep knocking.
  • pneuma hagion, the Holy Spirit: Where Matthew says the Father gives “good things” (Matthew 7:11), Luke says he gives the Holy Spirit. The best gift is God himself.

Verse by verse.

“Friend, lend me three loaves of bread;” (11:5)

He asks not for himself but for a guest. Prayer for others has a special place in this parable.

“at least because of his persistence he will get up and give him whatever he needs.” (11:8)

The argument is from the lesser to the greater. If a sleepy neighbour gives in, how much more will God, who does not sleep and is not annoyed by our asking.

“Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” (11:9)

The verbs are passive in the first and third lines: “it will be given”, “will be opened”. The giver is God.

“how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!” (11:13)

This is the promise of Pentecost, already spoken on the road to Jerusalem. In Qyomtho we ask for the Spirit day by day.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism groups three parables of Luke on prayer: the friend at midnight, the widow and the judge, and the Pharisee and the tax collector (CCC 2613). St Augustine, writing to the widow Proba, explains that God does not need to be told our wants; he wants our desire to grow through prayer, so that we may be able to receive what he is ready to give (Letter 130, in substance).

Questions people ask

Does Hebrews 6 mean that a Christian who falls into serious sin cannot be forgiven?

No. The Church teaches that every sin can be forgiven to one who repents (CCC 982), and the Lord gave the sacrament of Penance for those who fall after Baptism. Hebrews warns about a hardened turning away from Christ. As long as a person refuses him, no one can bring that person back; but God's door is never shut to the one who knocks.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Shemun Bar Sabbae (Simeon bar Sabbae), bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, the capital of the Persian empire, who was put to death with many companions under King Shapur II in the fourth century. The ancient accounts tell that he refused to force his people to pay a crushing double tax laid on Christians, and that an aged court official who had denied the faith repented on seeing him and died a martyr too. He is honoured by the Syriac Churches of East and West, and he is a living picture of Hebrews 6: the one who had fallen came back.
