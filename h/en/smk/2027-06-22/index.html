Word for the day

“for God loves a cheerful giver.” 2 Corinthians 9:7

Reflection

We are in the middle of the Apostles' Fast, which began last Wednesday and runs to the feast of Sts Peter and Paul. The Church has always kept three companions together in a time of fasting: prayer, fasting and almsgiving. Fasting without prayer becomes a diet; fasting without almsgiving can turn into a private achievement. Today's readings speak of the third companion. St Paul is gathering a collection among the Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem, and he does not ask the Corinthians to give out of pity or shame. He reaches for a farmer's picture: “The point is this: the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully.” In these weeks of the monsoon, the paddy fields of Kuttanad and the hill farms of the high ranges are being sown. No farmer throws a single handful on an acre and hopes for a full harvest. Seed that leaves the hand seems lost for a time in the wet earth; in truth it has only gone ahead to become the harvest.

Then Paul says something about the heart of the giver: “Each of you must give as you have made up your mind, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.” God does not need our money. What he wants is a heart that has become like his own, a heart that gives because it is glad to give. And Paul is not afraid of running dry: “And God is able to provide you with every blessing in abundance, so that by always having enough of everything, you may share abundantly in every good work.” The one who gives seed to the sower and bread for food will also keep the generous person supplied, so that the giving can go on. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this chapter, pointed out that Paul calls almsgiving a sowing so that no one should think of it as a loss: the seed seems thrown away, but it returns as a harvest, and the harvest is greater than the seed (Homilies on Second Corinthians, in substance). In the fast we give up a meal or a pleasure; the money and the time we save are meant for someone who has less.

The Gospel goes to the root: “Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal; but store up for yourselves treasures in heaven.” In the time of Jesus wealth was kept in fine clothes, which moths could eat, and in coins and metal, which rust could spoil, or which a thief could dig through a mud wall to take. Our wealth is kept in bank accounts and gold in a locker, but the question has not changed: what will last? Then comes the sentence that searches every heart: “For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” St Augustine told his people that the poor are like porters who carry our goods ahead of us to heaven: what we give them is not lost but sent on to the place where we hope to live for ever (Sermon 60, in substance). The Apostles whose fast we keep left their nets and boats and found a treasure that no moth could touch. Let us ask, during these days of the fast, where our treasure really is, and let one generous act move it a little closer to heaven.

Full homily

Dear friends, we are keeping the Apostles' Fast, and the Church reminds us that fasting has two companions: prayer and almsgiving. Today St Paul speaks of giving, and he uses the picture of a farmer: the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully (2 Corinthians 9:6). In these rainy weeks our farmers are sowing. Nobody sows a handful and hopes to fill a granary.

Paul goes on: each of you must give as you have made up your mind, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7). God does not ask for a gift squeezed out of us with a sigh. He looks for a heart that gives gladly, because it has learned from him. And he promises that the giver will not run dry: God is able to provide you with every blessing in abundance (2 Corinthians 9:8).

Jesus goes deeper still. Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal (Matthew 6:19). Store them up in heaven. And he tells us why it matters so much: for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also (Matthew 6:21). What we hold on to holds on to us.

So in these days of the fast, let us put aside what we save, a meal, a treat, a little spending, and give it with a smile to someone who needs it. That is seed sown for a harvest, and treasure sent ahead to heaven. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what a farmer does when the rain comes? He takes his seeds and throws them into the wet field. The seeds seem to disappear, but after some weeks green plants come up, and later there is a big harvest! St Paul says that when we share, it is like sowing seeds. What we give is not lost; it grows. And God loves people who share with a happy face. This week, share something with a smile: a snack, a pencil, a toy, or your time.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you became poor for our sake so that by your poverty we might become rich. In this Apostles' Fast teach us to sow bountifully and to give with a glad heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion. Take from us the fear that makes us hold on to what moth and rust will consume, and set our hearts on the treasure that is kept in heaven. Bless the hands that give and the hands that receive, and supply us with all we need, so that we may share abundantly in every good work. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about where we put what we have. Paul asks the Corinthians to give generously to the poor of Jerusalem, because “God loves a cheerful giver” and will never let the generous be the poorer for it. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells us to store up treasure in heaven, “for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” In the Apostles' Fast, fasting and almsgiving go together. The key words are sowing, cheerful and treasure.

2 Corinthians 9:6–11 · God loves a cheerful giver

Setting.

Chapters 8 and 9 of 2 Corinthians are about the collection that Paul was gathering among his Gentile churches for the poor Christians of Jerusalem (Romans 15:25–27; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4). At the meeting in Jerusalem, James, Cephas and John had asked him to “remember the poor”, which, he says, “was actually what I was eager to do” (Galatians 2:10). For Paul the collection was more than relief. It was a sign that Jews and Gentiles were one Church. Some scholars think chapter 9 was first a separate note, perhaps to the churches of Achaia around Corinth; others read the two chapters as one appeal.

Structure.

  • v. 6: the principle: sparing sowing, sparing harvest; generous sowing, generous harvest.
  • v. 7: give from the heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion.
  • vv. 8–9: God can give every grace in abundance, as the psalm says of the righteous.
  • vv. 10–11: the one who gives seed will multiply it, and the gift will become thanksgiving to God.

Key words.

  • hilaros, cheerful: The word that gives English “hilarious”, but here it means glad and willing. Verse 7 echoes the Greek text of Proverbs 22:8, which adds: “God blesses a cheerful and generous man.”
  • autarkeia, enough: Having what one needs. The philosophers used it for being content with little. Paul uses it for having enough so that one can share abundantly.
  • eulogia, blessing, bounty: In verse 6, “bountifully” is literally “with blessings”. A gift is a blessing to the one who receives it, and it calls down blessing on the one who gives.

Verse by verse.

“the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully.” (9:6)

Every farmer knows this. The seed thrown into the ground seems lost, but it is the only way to a harvest. Paul does not promise a financial return; the harvest he speaks of is the “harvest of your righteousness” (9:10).

“Each of you must give as you have made up your mind, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.” (9:7)

Paul will not force the Corinthians, though he has urged them strongly. A gift taken by pressure is not a gift. God looks at the heart of the giver more than at the size of the gift (Mark 12:41–44).

“He scatters abroad, he gives to the poor; his righteousness endures forever.” (9:9)

From Psalm 112:9, which describes the righteous person who fears the Lord. Generosity to the poor is part of what Scripture calls righteousness.

“You will be enriched in every way for your great generosity, which will produce thanksgiving to God through us;” (9:11)

The gift does not end with the poor who receive it. It turns into prayer: those who are helped give thanks to God. Almsgiving becomes worship.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls that from the very beginning Christians brought to the Eucharist, together with the bread and wine, gifts to share with those in need, and that this custom of the collection is inspired by the example of Christ, who became poor to make us rich (CCC 1351; 2 Corinthians 8:9). It also teaches that giving alms to the poor is a witness to fraternal charity and a work of justice pleasing to God (CCC 2462). In the Syriac tradition, as in the whole Church, fasting has always gone with almsgiving: what we deny ourselves is given to those who have less.

Matthew 6:19–21 · Where your treasure is

Setting.

In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus has just spoken of almsgiving, prayer and fasting, done in secret for the Father who sees in secret (6:1–18). Now he turns to possessions. Our three verses begin a short section that goes on with the eye as the lamp of the body (6:22–23) and the saying that no one can serve two masters, God and wealth (6:24). The thought was familiar to devout Jews: Sirach had said, “Lay up your treasure according to the commandments of the Most High” (Sirach 29:11), and Tobit taught that almsgiving lays up a good treasure (Tobit 4:9).

Structure.

  • v. 19: do not store up treasures on earth, where they are lost.
  • v. 20: store up treasures in heaven, where they are safe.
  • v. 21: the reason: the heart follows the treasure.

Key words.

  • thēsaurizete, store up: To heap up, to hoard. The noun is thēsauros, treasure or storehouse. Jesus does not condemn work or saving for one's family. He warns against making wealth the goal of life.
  • sēs kai brōsis, moth and rust: In the ancient world wealth was often kept in fine clothes, which moths eat, and in grain or metal, which decay. Brōsis means literally “eating”, anything that consumes.
  • kardia, heart: In the Bible the heart is not only the feelings. It is the centre of the person, where we think, decide and love.

Verse by verse.

“where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal;” (6:19)

“Break in” is literally “dig through”: houses were often built of mud brick, and a thief could dig through the wall. No earthly treasure is safe.

“but store up for yourselves treasures in heaven,” (6:20)

Treasure in heaven is laid up by what we give and do for God and for others, above all for the poor. Jesus says to the rich young man: “sell your possessions, and give the money to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven” (19:21).

“For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” (6:21)

Jesus does not say “where your heart is, there your treasure will be”, but the other way round. What we give our time and money to slowly shapes what we love.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse in teaching on the tenth commandment and the poverty of heart that Jesus asks of all his disciples (CCC 2544, 2551). Detachment from riches is necessary for entering the kingdom of heaven. St Augustine, preaching on this verse, calls the poor the porters who carry our goods ahead of us into heaven (Sermons, in substance). During the Apostles' Fast the Church invites us to look at our purse as well as our plate.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “God loves a cheerful giver.” Do I give to God and to the poor gladly, grudgingly, or only when I am pressed?
  • If someone looked at how I spend my time and money, where would they say my treasure is?
