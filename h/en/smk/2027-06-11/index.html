Word for the day

“Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” John 14:23

Reflection

Today is a family feast for our Church. On 11 June 1932, Pope Pius XI, by the apostolic constitution Christo Pastorum Principi, erected the hierarchy of the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, with the Archeparchy of Thiruvananthapuram under Archbishop Mar Ivanios and the Eparchy of Tiruvalla under Bishop Mar Theophilos. Less than two years earlier, on 20 September 1930 at Kollam, these two bishops and a small band of companions had made their profession of the Catholic faith and entered full communion with the Bishop of Rome, keeping the venerable West Syriac liturgy of their fathers. They left churches and properties behind; they brought with them the Holy Qurbono, the prayers of Mar Aprem and the faith of the Apostle Thomas. In 1932 the Church gave that small flock a home of its own: bishops, sees, a hierarchy, an ordered life within the one Catholic communion. In 2005 our Church was raised to the rank of a Major Archiepiscopal Church. We thank God today for all of it, and we remember with respect our brothers and sisters of the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite Churches, with whom we share the same roots, the same liturgy and the same fathers, praying together for the unity that Christ desires.

The readings the Panchangam gives for this day could hardly be more fitting. St Paul writes: “Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand.” Access and standing: the language of someone who has been brought into a house and given a place in it. And the source of it all is not human planning but divine love: “hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” St Augustine loved this verse above almost any other; he taught that the very love with which we love God is itself God's gift, poured out by the Spirit, so that whatever good we do is grace from beginning to end (On the Spirit and the Letter 5, in substance). Paul goes on to speak of reconciliation: “For if while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more surely, having been reconciled, will we be saved by his life.” The reunion of 1930 was a work of reconciliation, and Mar Ivanios understood it not as a victory over anyone but as a step towards the unity for which Christ prayed. The work is not finished. Every Christian division is a wound in the body of Christ, and the love poured into our hearts asks us to keep praying, to keep speaking kindly, and to keep the door open.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks at the Last Supper of the home that God makes with those who love him: “Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” Before this he has promised: “I will not leave you orphaned; I am coming to you,” and he has spoken of “another Advocate, to be with you forever.” A hierarchy is not, in the first place, an organisation chart. It is one of the ways the Lord keeps that promise: through bishops in communion with the successor of Peter, through the sacraments they celebrate and the faith they guard, the Holy Spirit makes sure that the flock is never left orphaned. St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, said that the proof of love is the display of works: we show that we love by keeping his word (Homilies on the Gospels 30, in substance). Our Church will celebrate this anniversary well, not with speeches only, but by being a home where the Father and the Son are glad to live: faithful to the word, generous to the poor, eager for unity, joyful in the liturgy.

The Panchangam also remembers two apostles today. Mar Barthulmai, St Bartholomew, is one of the Twelve whose names stand in every list of the apostles; many in the tradition identify him with Nathanael, of whom Jesus said: “Here is truly an Israelite in whom there is no deceit!” An ancient report preserved by Eusebius says that when the teacher Pantaenus travelled to India, he found Christians there who had a Gospel of Matthew left to them by Bartholomew (Church History 5.10); the tradition of the Churches places his martyrdom in Armenia. Mar Barnabas, a Levite of Cyprus, was called by the apostles “son of encouragement”. He sold his field for the poor, he took the newly converted Saul by the hand when everyone else was afraid of him, and he was sent to Antioch, where “the disciples were first called Christians”. It was in that church of Antioch, while they were worshipping the Lord and fasting, that the Holy Spirit said: “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.” Our liturgy comes to us from the Syriac tradition of Antioch, and next Wednesday we begin the Apostles' Fast. On this day of our Church, Barnabas teaches us to encourage, Bartholomew teaches us to be without deceit, and both teach us that a Church is built on the faith of the apostles.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is a family feast for our Church. On 11 June 1932 Pope Pius XI erected the hierarchy of the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, with the Archeparchy of Thiruvananthapuram and the Eparchy of Tiruvalla. Two years before, in 1930, Mar Ivanios, Mar Theophilos and a few companions had entered full communion with the Catholic Church, leaving buildings and properties behind, but keeping our beautiful liturgy and the faith of our fathers.

St Paul tells us today: since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 5:1). And he tells us the source of it all: God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us (Romans 5:5). Our Church was born from that love, and from a longing for reconciliation and unity.

In the Gospel Jesus promises: I will not leave you orphaned; I am coming to you (John 14:18). And again: we will come to them and make our home with them (John 14:23). Through our bishops in communion with the successor of Peter, through the sacraments and through the faith they guard, the Lord keeps that promise. Our Church is meant to be a home where God lives and where every one of us belongs.

Today the Panchangam also remembers two apostles. Mar Barthulmai, Bartholomew, one of the Twelve, whom many identify with Nathanael, the Israelite in whom there is no deceit. And Mar Barnabas, son of encouragement, who stood by Saul when others were afraid, and who was set apart by the Holy Spirit at Antioch while the church was praying and fasting.

So on this day let us thank God for our Church, pray for our Major Archbishop-Catholicos and our bishops, and pray for the unity of all Christians, especially our brothers and sisters of the other Malankara Churches. And let us be like Barnabas: people who encourage, who open the door, who make the Church a home. Amen.

For children

Children, today is like a birthday for our Malankara Catholic Church! In 1932 the Pope gave our Church its own bishops and its own dioceses, so that we would have a home in the big family of the Catholic Church. Jesus said: I will not leave you alone like orphans. We also remember two friends of Jesus: Bartholomew, who never told lies, and Barnabas, who always encouraged people. When a new child comes to your class or your Sunday school, be a Barnabas: smile, sit with them and help them feel at home.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you promised not to leave us orphaned, and in your love you gave our Syro-Malankara Church a home within the one Catholic communion. We thank you for Mar Ivanios, Mar Theophilos and all who walked with them, for the hierarchy erected in 1932, and for all our bishops, priests, religious and faithful. Pour your love into our hearts through the Holy Spirit, keep us faithful to your word, and make our Church a home where the Father and you are glad to dwell. Through the prayers of Mar Barthulmai, your apostle without deceit, and of Mar Barnabas, son of encouragement, heal the divisions among your disciples and bring all who call on your name into the unity you desire. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church keeps the Foundation Day of its hierarchy and remembers two apostles, Mar Barthulmai (St Bartholomew) and Mar Barnabas. The readings speak of a home given by grace. Paul says that through Christ “we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand”, at peace with God, with his love poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit. In the Gospel Jesus promises: “we will come to them and make our home with them.” The key words are access, love and home.

Paul speaks of what God has done: he has justified us and reconciled us to himself while we were still enemies. He also speaks of what follows: peace, and a hope that does not disappoint. Jesus speaks of the answer on our side: a love that keeps his word. Together the readings describe the Church as a household of God, received by grace, kept by love and filled with the Spirit.

Romans 5:1–11 · God's love poured into our hearts

Setting.

Romans 1–4 has shown that all have sinned and that God justifies by grace through faith, as he justified Abraham (ch. 4). Chapter 5 opens a new section of the letter: what life is like for those who have been justified. Paul begins with “Therefore”, gathering up all that went before, and the passage is full of “we”: Paul speaks as one of the believers, describing what all of them now share.

Two of Paul's words come from daily life. “Access” (prosagōgē) could describe being brought into the presence of a king, or drawing near to God in worship. “Reconciliation” (katallagē) belonged to diplomacy and family life: enemies, or estranged husband and wife, made one again. Paul uses both to say what Christ has done.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: justified by faith: peace with God, access to grace, hope of glory.
  • vv. 3–5: boasting even in sufferings: endurance, character, hope; the love of God poured out through the Spirit.
  • vv. 6–8: Christ died for the ungodly: the proof of God's love.
  • vv. 9–10: “much more”: saved from wrath, saved by his life.
  • v. 11: boasting in God, through whom we have received reconciliation.

Key words.

  • eirēnēn echomen, we have peace: Some ancient manuscripts read “let us have peace” (echōmen), others “we have peace” (echomen); the two forms differ by one vowel and sounded alike. Most modern translations, the NRSV among them, take “we have”; the Malayalam POC follows the reading “let us have peace”. Both say something true: peace is God's gift, and we are called to live in it.
  • kauchōmetha, we boast: Paul has excluded all boasting in ourselves (3:27). Now he names three right ways to boast: in the hope of sharing God's glory (v. 2), in sufferings (v. 3), and in God himself (v. 11).
  • ekkechytai, has been poured: The same verb root as in Joel's prophecy of the Spirit poured out on all flesh, which Peter proclaimed at Pentecost (Acts 2:17). The tense says the love was poured out once and remains.
  • katallagē, reconciliation: God is the one who reconciles; we are the ones reconciled. The change is not in God's love, which came first (v. 8), but in our state: from enemies to friends.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,” (5:1)

Peace here is not first a feeling but a new relationship: the war between the sinner and God is over, and Christ has made the peace.

“and hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” (5:5)

St Augustine quoted this verse more than almost any other: the very love with which we love God is itself God's gift, poured out by the Spirit (On the Spirit and the Letter, in substance).

“But God proves his love for us in that while we still were sinners Christ died for us.” (5:8)

God did not wait for us to become worthy. The love came first, and the Cross is its proof.

“much more surely, having been reconciled, will we be saved by his life.” (5:10)

“Much more” is an argument from the harder to the easier: if God did the hardest thing for enemies, he will surely finish what he has begun in friends. “His life” is the life of the risen Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in verse 5 the gift of the Holy Spirit as love itself: “God is Love and love is his first gift, containing all others” (CCC 733). Verse 8 stands at the heart of her teaching on the Cross: God's plan for us is one of love, prior to any merit on our part (CCC 604). The chain of verses 3–5 has long consoled those who suffer: trials borne with Christ become a school of hope. Hope here is not mere optimism; it is a theological virtue resting on God's faithfulness (CCC 1817–1818).

John 14:15–24 · We will make our home with them

Setting.

At the Last Supper Jesus has promised that he goes to prepare a place for the disciples in his Father's house (14:2–3). Now he turns the promise around: the Father and the Son will come and make their home with the one who loves him (14:23). The word for “dwelling places” in 14:2 and the word for “home” in 14:23 are the same Greek word, monē.

The question in verse 22 comes from “Judas (not Iscariot)”, called “Judas son of James” in Luke's lists of the Twelve (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13) and identified in tradition with Thaddeus. Many hoped that the Messiah would show himself openly to the whole world; Judas asks why the revelation will be to the disciples only.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: love shown in keeping the commandments; another Advocate, the Spirit of truth, who abides with them.
  • vv. 18–20: “I will not leave you orphaned”: they will see him and live.
  • v. 21: the one who loves is loved and receives Christ's self-revelation.
  • vv. 22–24: Judas' question; the Father and the Son make their home with the one who loves.

Key words.

  • allon paraklēton, another Advocate: “Another” implies that Jesus himself has been their first Advocate (compare 1 John 2:1). The Spirit carries on his presence and his work.
  • orphanous, orphaned: A disciple without his teacher was like a child without a father. Jesus promises that they will not be left so.
  • monē, home, dwelling: From the verb menein, to abide, which runs through these chapters (15:4–10). God's dwelling with his people, once in the tent of meeting and the Temple, is now in the heart of the believer and in the Church.

Verse by verse.

“If you love me, you will keep my commandments.” (14:15)

Love is the root, obedience the fruit. Jesus does not say “keep my commandments and I will love you”, but the reverse.

“I will not leave you orphaned; I am coming to you.” (14:18)

He comes at Easter, he comes in the Spirit, and he will come at the end. John holds the three comings together.

“because I live, you also will live.” (14:19)

Our life hangs on his. Because the risen Christ lives, death cannot hold those who belong to him.

“Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” (14:23)

This is the indwelling of the Holy Trinity in the soul of the just. The Catechism quotes this verse when it speaks of the final goal of God's whole plan: the entry of his creatures into the perfect unity of the Trinity, which begins even now (CCC 260).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this Gospel as the promise of God's indwelling: by grace the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit make their home in the baptised (CCC 260). The prayer of St Elizabeth of the Trinity, “O my God, Trinity whom I adore”, quoted in that same paragraph of the Catechism, is a prayer in the spirit of this verse. And the Church herself is the place where this home becomes visible: “a people made one with the unity of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit” (Lumen Gentium 4, quoting St Cyprian).

Questions people ask

Who was St Bartholomew, and is he the same as Nathanael?

Bartholomew appears in all four lists of the Twelve (Matthew 10:3; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:14; Acts 1:13). The name is Aramaic, Bar-Tolmai, “son of Tolmai”, which is why the Syriac Churches call him Mar Barthulmai. John's Gospel never names him, but it tells of Nathanael, brought to Jesus by Philip (John 1:45–49). Since the first three Gospels name Bartholomew next to Philip, many have identified the two, though this cannot be proved. Eusebius records an old report that Pantaenus of Alexandria found in “India” a Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew letters, left there by Bartholomew (Church History 5.10); which land was meant by “India” here is uncertain.

Was Barnabas an apostle?

He was not one of the Twelve, but Acts calls him and Paul “apostles” (Acts 14:14), and the Church honours him as an apostle. His name was Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, and the apostles called him Barnabas, “son of encouragement” (Acts 4:36). He sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet (4:37), stood by the newly converted Saul when others were afraid of him (9:27), and was sent to Antioch, where he is called “a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith” (11:24).

Does “justified by faith” mean that good works do not matter?

No. Paul says we are justified by faith, and in the same passage that God's love has been poured into our hearts (5:5). That love, given by grace, bears fruit in works. Jesus says the same from the other side: “Those who love me will keep my word” (John 14:23). Justification is the beginning of a new life in which, by grace, our good works become pleasing to God and merit eternal life (CCC 1987; 2008–2011).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

On 11 June 1932 Pope Pius XI, by the apostolic constitution Christo Pastorum Principi, erected the hierarchy of the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church: the Archeparchy of Thiruvananthapuram under Archbishop Mar Ivanios and the Eparchy of Tiruvalla under Bishop Mar Theophilos. On 20 September 1930 the two bishops and their companions had entered full communion with the Bishop of Rome, keeping the West Syriac liturgy of their fathers. In 2005 the Church was raised to the rank of a Major Archiepiscopal Church. Today's readings fit the day: a people given “access to this grace in which we stand”, and a home where the Father and the Son make their dwelling. We thank God for the bishops, priests, religious and faithful of our Church, and we pray, with respect and love for our brothers and sisters of the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite Churches, who share the same roots and the same liturgy, for the unity that Christ desires.

The two apostles remembered today link the day to the first preaching of the Gospel. Mar Barnabas was the bridge-builder of the early Church: he welcomed Saul when others held back, and at Antioch “when he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced” (Acts 11:23). That gift, to see grace at work and to rejoice in it, is one to ask for on this feast.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that suffering produces endurance, character and hope. Where have I seen that chain at work in my own life, or in the history of our Church?
  • “While we still were sinners Christ died for us.” How should this change the way I treat those I consider unworthy?
  • Jesus promises that the Father and he will make their home with those who love him. What kind of home does my heart offer them?
  • Barnabas was a “son of encouragement”. Whom can I encourage in faith this week?
