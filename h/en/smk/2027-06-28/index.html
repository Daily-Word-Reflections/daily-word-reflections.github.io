Word for the day

“Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved by him, a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth.” 2 Timothy 2:15

Reflection

This is the last weekday of the Apostles' Fast. Tomorrow we keep the feast of Sts Peter and Paul and the fast comes to an end. Today St Paul, writing from prison to his young disciple Timothy, gives advice that every teacher of the faith needs: “Remind them of this, and warn them before God that they are to avoid wrangling over words, which does no good but only ruins those who are listening.” Paul is not against careful thought; he is against the quarrelsome spirit that turns the word of God into a battlefield for pride. Then he gives the positive picture: “Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved by him, a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth. Avoid profane chatter, for it will lead people into more and more impiety.” The word translated “rightly explaining” describes cutting a straight road or a straight furrow: the teacher's task is to lay the word of truth out straight, so that others can walk on it. Every catechist, every parent who teaches a child to pray, every preacher on Sunday, is called to be such a worker.

The Panchangam today remembers a bishop who did exactly that: Mar Irenaeus, St Irenaeus of Lyons. He was born in Asia Minor in the second century, and as a young man he listened to St Polycarp of Smyrna, who had himself known the Apostle John. Years later Irenaeus wrote to a friend that he could still describe the place where Polycarp sat and talked, and how he told of his conversations with John and with others who had seen the Lord (in Eusebius, Church History 5.20, in substance). Irenaeus went to Gaul, served as a priest in Lyons and, after the martyrdom of Bishop Pothinus in the persecution of 177, became bishop there. In his day groups called Gnostics were wrangling over secret words and hidden teachings, claiming a knowledge higher than the faith of ordinary Christians. Against them Irenaeus wrote his great work Against Heresies. He did not answer with clever speculation; he pointed to the faith handed down openly from the Apostles in the churches through the succession of bishops, and above all to the Church of Rome, which he described as founded and organized by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul (Against Heresies 3.3.2, in substance), the Apostles whose feast we keep tomorrow.

His name means peaceful, and the historian Eusebius remarked that he lived up to it, for when a dispute about the date of Easter threatened to divide the churches, Irenaeus wrote to the Bishop of Rome urging patience and peace (Church History 5.24, in substance). In 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church with the title Doctor of Unity. Irenaeus also left us one of the most beautiful sayings of the early Church: the glory of God is a human being fully alive, and the life of a human being is the vision of God (Against Heresies 4.20.7, in substance). Tradition honours him as a martyr; he died around the year 202. Today too the Panchangam keeps the name day of Most Rev. Samuel Mar Irenaios. In our tradition a bishop receives a new name at his ordination, and the feast of the saint whose name he bears is kept as his name day. We pray today that he may be, like his patron, a teacher of the true faith and a servant of unity.

The Gospel shows where all this begins. Jesus says to the Apostles: “You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last, so that the Father will give you whatever you ask him in my name. I am giving you these commands so that you may love one another.” The Apostles did not apply for the job; they were chosen. The fruit they bore has lasted twenty centuries: John taught Polycarp, Polycarp taught Irenaeus, and the faith came down the generations, through the Apostle Thomas and his successors, to our own land and our own parish. The fruit that lasts is not wrangling but love. On this last day of the fast, let us thank God for those who handed on the faith to us, ask for the grace to explain it straight and live it with love, and prepare our hearts for tomorrow's feast of the two Apostles on whom, as Irenaeus taught, the Church of Rome was founded.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the last weekday of the Apostles' Fast, and tomorrow we celebrate Sts Peter and Paul. St Paul writes to Timothy: avoid wrangling over words, which does no good but only ruins those who are listening (2 Timothy 2:14). Instead, be a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth (2 Timothy 2:15). Quarrels about words destroy; the truth explained with love builds up.

Today we remember Mar Irenaeus. As a young man he listened to St Polycarp, who had known the Apostle John. He became bishop of Lyons and defended the faith against people who invented secret teachings. He told them: look at the faith handed down in the Church from the Apostles, and especially in the Church of Rome founded on Peter and Paul. His name means peaceful, and he worked for peace among the churches. We also pray today for our bishop Samuel Mar Irenaios on his name day.

In the Gospel Jesus tells the Apostles: You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last (John 15:16). The fruit has lasted: from John to Polycarp to Irenaeus, and down to us. And Jesus tells us what that fruit is: I am giving you these commands so that you may love one another (John 15:17).

So let us thank God for those who handed on the faith to us. Let us learn our faith well, so that we can explain it straight. Let us avoid quarrels that wound, and let us prepare our hearts for tomorrow's feast. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever played the game where you whisper a message from one person to the next? Sometimes the message gets mixed up! Jesus gave his message to the Apostles, and they passed it on very carefully. St Irenaeus learned it from Polycarp, who learned it from the Apostle John. And it came all the way to us, through our parents and teachers. Listen well when you learn about Jesus, so that you can pass the message on without mixing it up.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you chose your Apostles and appointed them to go and bear fruit that will last. Through the prayers of Mar Irenaeus, who received the faith from those who knew your Apostles and defended it with patience and peace, keep your Church in the true faith handed down to us. Make us workers who have no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth, and keep us from wrangling over words and from idle talk. Bless our bishop Samuel Mar Irenaios on his name day, and all the shepherds of your Church. As the Apostles' Fast ends, prepare our hearts to celebrate Sts Peter and Paul, and let us bear the fruit of love. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the word that is handed on and the fruit it bears. Paul tells Timothy to avoid quarrels about words and to be “a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth.” In John, Jesus reminds the disciples that they did not choose him; he chose them and appointed them to bear fruit that will last, and the fruit he names is love for one another. On the day the Panchangam remembers Mar Irenaeus, the readings fit a bishop who spent his life explaining the word of truth rightly. The key words are truth and fruit.

2 Timothy 2:14–16 · Rightly explaining the word of truth

Setting.

2 Timothy is written as Paul's last letter, from prison in Rome, to his beloved co-worker Timothy, who was leading the church at Ephesus. Many scholars think that the Pastoral Letters were written by a disciple in Paul's name after his death; others hold that they come from Paul himself near the end of his life. The Church receives them as inspired Scripture either way.

Just before our verses comes the hymn: “If we have died with him, we will also live with him” (2:11). Timothy must remind the community of this saving faith, and protect it from teachers who were spreading empty speculation; verses 17–18 name two of them, who claimed that the resurrection had already taken place.

Structure.

  • v. 14: remind them; warn them not to wrangle over words.
  • v. 15: be an approved worker who rightly handles the word of truth.
  • v. 16: avoid profane chatter, which leads into impiety.

Key words.

  • logomachein, to wrangle over words: Literally “to fight with words”. Not careful thought about the faith, which the Church has always needed, but quarrels for the sake of winning, which leave the listeners confused and divided.
  • ergatēs anepaischyntos, a worker who has no need to be ashamed: The image of a labourer whose work can bear inspection. Timothy's ministry is honest work done before God, not a performance before people.
  • orthotomounta, rightly explaining: Literally “cutting straight”, like a road cut in a straight line or a furrow ploughed true. The minister of the word keeps to the straight path of the truth and leads others along it.

Verse by verse.

“warn them before God that they are to avoid wrangling over words, which does no good but only ruins those who are listening.” (2:14)

The test of any argument about faith is what it does to the listeners. Quarrels that leave people bitter or lost are not the work of the Spirit.

“Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved by him, a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly explaining the word of truth.” (2:15)

This verse has become a motto for preachers and catechists. The first audience of every preacher is God.

“Avoid profane chatter, for it will lead people into more and more impiety,” (2:16)

The same warning appears at the close of 1 Timothy: “Avoid the profane chatter and contradictions of what is falsely called knowledge” (1 Timothy 6:20). Empty talk does not stay empty; it pulls people away from God.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the task of authentically interpreting the word of God has been entrusted to the living teaching office of the Church, which is not above the word of God but serves it, teaching only what has been handed on (Dei Verbum 10; CCC 86). St Irenaeus, remembered today, did exactly what this reading asks. Against teachers who claimed a secret knowledge, he pointed to the faith publicly handed down from the apostles through the bishops of the churches, above all the church of Rome, with which, he wrote, every church must agree (Against Heresies 3.3; CCC 834).

John 15:16–17 · You did not choose me but I chose you

Setting.

These verses belong to the Last Supper discourse. Jesus has spoken of himself as the true vine and of the disciples as branches (15:1–8), and has told them: “No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends” (15:13). He no longer calls them servants but friends (15:15). Now he tells them where their calling comes from and what it is for.

Structure.

  • v. 16a: the initiative is his: I chose you.
  • v. 16b: you are appointed to go and bear lasting fruit; the Father will give what you ask in my name.
  • v. 17: the purpose of the commands: love one another.

Key words.

  • exelexamēn, I chose: In the ancient world a disciple chose his teacher. Here the Teacher chooses. Every vocation begins in the unearned love of Christ, not in our own merit or plan.
  • ethēka, I appointed: Literally “I placed” or “I set”. The same verb is used when the good shepherd “lays down” his life (10:11). The one who lays down his life for his friends also sets them in their mission.
  • karpos menē, fruit that will last: The verb menein, to abide or remain, runs through the whole chapter. Fruit that lasts grows only on branches that abide in the vine (15:5).

Verse by verse.

“You did not choose me but I chose you.” (15:16)

A word of humility and of comfort. Humility, because no one can boast of his calling; comfort, because the one who chose us knew our weakness and still chose us.

“And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last,” (15:16)

The disciples are not chosen to stay in the upper room but to go. The fruit is both the holiness of their lives and the people brought to Christ through them.

“I am giving you these commands so that you may love one another.” (15:17)

All the commands come down to one. The fruit that lasts is love.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these words the source of every vocation, especially the call of the apostles and of their successors the bishops. The Catechism teaches that Christ chose the Twelve and made them sharers in his mission (CCC 858), and that the new commandment of love sums up the whole law of the Gospel (CCC 1970). St Augustine, preaching on this passage, says that the Lord did not choose the disciples because they were good, but made them good by choosing them (Tractates on John 86, in substance).

Questions people ask

Paul says to avoid wrangling over words. Does that mean that exact words in matters of faith are unimportant?

No. The Church has always cared about the right words: the Creed, the definitions of the councils, the words of the sacraments. What Paul condemns is quarrelling for its own sake, clever debate that serves pride and leaves the hearers worse off. Careful words that guard the truth are a service of love; empty arguments are not. St Irenaeus fought hard for the true faith, but always so that ordinary believers might keep it clearly and simply.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers Mar Irenaeus today, the day on which the wider Church keeps the memory of St Irenaeus of Lyons. Born in Asia Minor around AD 130, he heard as a young man the preaching of St Polycarp of Smyrna, who had known the apostle John. He became bishop of Lyons in Gaul and wrote Against Heresies, the great defence of the apostolic faith against those who claimed secret knowledge. His name means “peaceful”, and he lived up to it, pleading with Pope Victor for peace among the churches in the dispute over the date of Easter. He taught that “the glory of God is man fully alive, and the life of man is the vision of God” (Against Heresies 4.20.7; CCC 294). In 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church with the title “Doctor of Unity”. A Church that holds the faith of the apostles and longs for the unity of all Christians can pray today with him.

For study and teaching

  • Paul tells Timothy to be a worker who need not be ashamed before God. In conversations about faith at home or online, do I seek to win, or to help the other person come closer to the truth?
  • Jesus says, “You did not choose me but I chose you.” How does knowing that Christ chose me change the way I look at my own vocation and my weaknesses?
