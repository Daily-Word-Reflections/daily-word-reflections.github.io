Word for the day

“This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them, and we know that his testimony is true.” John 21:24

Reflection

Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Yohannan Shleeha, St John the Apostle, son of Zebedee and brother of Mar Yaqob, whom the Panchangam remembered on 30 April. He was a fisherman of Galilee, called with his brother from their father's boat; with Peter and James he saw the raising of Jairus' daughter, the Transfiguration and the agony in Gethsemane. The Church's tradition, from St Irenaeus of Lyons in the second century, recognises him as “the disciple whom Jesus loved,” who leaned on the Lord's breast at the Supper and later gave the Church the fourth Gospel while living at Ephesus (Against Heresies III.1.1, in substance). Many Eastern Churches keep his memory on this day in May. The Gospel shows him in the last scene by the lake after the Resurrection. Jesus has just asked Peter three times, “Do you love me?” and told him to feed his sheep. Then “Peter turned and saw the disciple whom Jesus loved following them.” Peter asks, “Lord, what about him?” and Jesus answers, “If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? Follow me!” Each disciple has his own road. Peter will glorify God by a martyr's death; John will remain long, bearing witness. We are not to measure our call against another's. The only question that matters is the one Jesus puts to each of us: will you follow me?

The Gospel ends with John's own seal: “This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them, and we know that his testimony is true.” The beloved disciple is above all a witness. He stood at the Cross when others had fled, he ran to the empty tomb and “saw and believed,” and he recognised the risen Lord on the shore before anyone else: “It is the Lord!” Love made him see. St Paul's words in the Epistle could stand over John's whole life: “Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?” And the answer: “No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” Nothing in all creation, “neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come,” can separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. St Jerome tells that when John was very old at Ephesus and could no longer walk, he was carried into the assembly and would say only, “Little children, love one another.” When asked why he always repeated it, he answered that it was the Lord's command, and if this alone were done, it would be enough (Commentary on Galatians 6:10, in substance). The apostle of love ended as he began, resting on the heart of Christ.

From the Cross Jesus entrusted his Mother to this disciple: “Here is your mother,” and “from that hour the disciple took her into his own home” (John 19:27). It is fitting, then, that today the Panchangam also remembers the founding of the Congregation of the Daughters of Mary, the Mary Makkal, on 8 May 1938 at Marthandam, in the time of the Servant of God Archbishop Mar Ivanios. Like John, the sisters took the Mother of the Lord into their home and their name. For nearly ninety years they have gone to the villages and to the poor in Kerala, Tamil Nadu and far beyond, in mission, teaching, nursing and the care of the forgotten, bearing quiet witness that nothing can separate us from the love of Christ. Thank God today for the sisters you know, pray for vocations to religious life in our Church, and ask Mar Yohannan to teach you his two lessons: to rest on the heart of Christ in prayer, and to take Mary, the Mother of God, into your home. As we wait in these days for the Holy Spirit, we wait as the apostles did, “constantly devoting themselves to prayer” with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14).

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Yohannan Shleeha, St John the Apostle, whom tradition knows as the disciple whom Jesus loved. He leaned on the Lord's breast at the Supper, he stood at the Cross, and he was the first to recognise the risen Lord on the shore.

In the Gospel Peter sees John and asks: Lord, what about him? Jesus answers: if it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? Follow me! (John 21:21–22). We are not to compare our road with another's. Each of us has one task: to follow Jesus.

St Paul asks: who will separate us from the love of Christ? Hardship, distress, persecution, famine, sword? No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us (Romans 8:35, 37). John lived this. In his old age, St Jerome tells us, he would say only: little children, love one another.

From the Cross Jesus gave his Mother to John, and John took her into his home. Today we also thank God for the Daughters of Mary, founded on this day in 1938, who serve the poor in the name of Mary. Let us take Mary into our homes, and with her wait in prayer for the Holy Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Today we remember St John. He was one of Jesus' best friends! At the Last Supper he sat right next to Jesus. On Good Friday, when many friends ran away, John stayed near the Cross with Mother Mary. Jesus said to him, “Here is your mother,” and John took care of her. When John was very old, he kept saying, “Little children, love one another.” Let us say it too, and do it!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you loved Mar Yohannan Shleeha and let him rest on your heart; from the Cross you gave him your Mother, and after your Resurrection you called him to follow you. By his prayers make us witnesses whose testimony is true, convinced that nothing can separate us from your love. Bless the Daughters of Mary and all consecrated women, and give our Church many generous vocations. Teach us to take your Mother into our homes, and to wait with her in prayer for the Holy Spirit. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Yohannan Shleeha, St John the Apostle, both readings speak of love. Paul asks, “Who will separate us from the love of Christ?”, and answers that nothing in all creation can. The Gospel shows the disciple whom Jesus loved, following the risen Lord, and tells us that his testimony is true. John's whole life was a following and a witnessing, held up by a love that did not let him go. The key words are love, follow and witness.

Romans 8:35–39 · Nothing can separate us from the love of God

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome, a church he had not founded, probably around AD 57 or 58, from Corinth, before taking the collection to Jerusalem (15:25–26). He hoped to visit Rome and go on to Spain. The letter sets out his gospel more fully than any other.

Chapter 8 describes life in the Spirit: no condemnation for those in Christ (8:1), the Spirit crying “Abba! Father!” in us (8:15), creation groaning in hope (8:22). From v. 31 Paul sings a hymn of confidence: “If God is for us, who is against us?” Today's verses are its climax.

Structure.

  • v. 35: the question, and a list of hardships.
  • v. 36: a psalm of suffering (Psalm 44:22).
  • v. 37: in all these, more than conquerors.
  • vv. 38–39: no power in all creation can separate us from God's love in Christ.

Key words.

  • hypernikōmen, we are more than conquerors: A word Paul seems to have coined: to win beyond winning. The victory is not that the hardships stop, but that they cannot break the bond with Christ. It comes “through him who loved us”.
  • agapē tou Christou, the love of Christ: Paul means Christ's love for us, not first our love for him. That is why nothing can separate us from it. Our love can grow weak; his does not.
  • pepeismai, I am convinced: A perfect tense: I have been persuaded and I remain so. Paul does not speak as one who hopes it may be true, but as one who has tested it in prisons, shipwrecks and beatings (2 Corinthians 11:23–27).

Verse by verse.

“Who will separate us from the love of Christ?” (8:35)

Paul's list is not imagined. He had known hardship, persecution, hunger, nakedness, danger and the threat of the sword. He names the worst and says they are not stronger than love.

“As it is written, “For your sake we are being killed all day long; we are accounted as sheep to be slaughtered.”” (8:36)

Psalm 44 is the prayer of faithful people who suffer although they have not forsaken God. Paul does not hide that Christians may suffer in the same way. Suffering is not a sign that God has stopped loving.

“No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” (8:37)

“In” all these things, not “after” them. The victory is given in the middle of trouble.

“nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.” (8:39)

The love of Christ in v. 35 is now called the love of God in Christ. For Paul they are one love. The only thing the list leaves out is our own deliberate refusal, and even that God's grace patiently seeks to overcome.

How the Church reads it.

The Church offers this passage among the readings for funerals, because it speaks the hope of Christians at the grave: not even death can separate us from God's love. The Catechism quotes the verse that comes just before, “all things work together for good for those who love God” (8:28), when it teaches that God can bring good out of evil (CCC 313). The martyrs of every age, the Apostle John among the witnesses who suffered for Christ, have shown that this is not a fine phrase but a lived truth.

John 21:20–24 · The disciple whom Jesus loved

Setting.

Chapter 21 is the epilogue of John's Gospel. By the Sea of Tiberias the risen Lord has given the disciples a great catch of fish and shared a meal of bread and fish with them. Then he has asked Peter three times, “Do you love me?”, entrusted his sheep to him, and foretold the death by which Peter would glorify God (21:15–19). He ends with the command, “Follow me.”

Now Peter turns and sees another disciple. The Gospel never gives his name. It calls him “the disciple whom Jesus loved”, the one who reclined next to Jesus at the supper (13:23), stood by the Cross with the Mother of Jesus (19:26–27) and ran with Peter to the tomb (20:2–8). From the second century the Church has identified him with John the son of Zebedee.

Structure.

  • v. 20: Peter sees the beloved disciple following.
  • v. 21: “Lord, what about him?”
  • v. 22: Jesus' answer: what is that to you? Follow me!
  • v. 23: a rumour corrected.
  • v. 24: this disciple is the witness who wrote these things.

Key words.

  • akolouthein, to follow: The word of discipleship from the first chapter, when two disciples followed Jesus and asked where he was staying (1:37–38). The beloved disciple is already following before Peter is told to.
  • menein, to remain: One of John's favourite words, often translated “abide”: “Abide in me as I abide in you” (15:4). Here it means to remain alive until the Lord comes, but the Johannine reader hears more: this disciple's life was one long abiding.
  • martyria, testimony: John's Gospel is full of witnesses: John the Baptist, the Samaritan woman, the works of Jesus, the Father, the Spirit. It ends by naming its own author as a witness whose testimony is true.

Verse by verse.

“Peter turned and saw the disciple whom Jesus loved following them;” (21:20)

He does not need to be called. Love has already made him a follower.

“Lord, what about him?” (21:21)

Peter has just heard that he will die for Christ. It is natural to ask about another's path. Jesus will not compare.

“what is that to you? Follow me!” (21:22)

Each disciple has his own road. Peter will shepherd and die on a cross; John will live long and bear witness. Both follow the same Lord.

“This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them, and we know that his testimony is true.” (21:24)

The “we” is the community around the disciple, who vouch for his word. The Gospel rests on an eyewitness who loved and was loved.

How the Church reads it.

St Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, says that John, the disciple of the Lord who leaned on his breast, published his Gospel while living at Ephesus (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the four Gospels are of apostolic origin and faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught (Dei Verbum 18–19). St Jerome tells that the aged John, carried into the assembly at Ephesus, would only say, “Little children, love one another”, and when asked why, answered that this is the Lord's command, and if it alone is kept, it is enough (in substance).

Questions people ask

Is the beloved disciple certainly John the son of Zebedee?

The Gospel itself does not name him, and scholars have discussed the question at length. From St Irenaeus onward the tradition of the Church has identified him with the Apostle John, and the liturgy honours John as the Evangelist. What is certain from the text is that the Gospel rests on the witness of a disciple who was close to Jesus at the supper, at the Cross and at the empty tomb.

Did the rumour mean that John would never die?

No, and the Gospel corrects it at once: Jesus did not say he would not die. According to early tradition John lived to a great age at Ephesus and died there. He was the witness who remained, while Peter was the shepherd who gave his life.

Was there rivalry between Peter and John?

The Gospel shows them together, not as rivals: at the supper, at the tomb, at the lake. Peter receives from Christ the charge to feed the sheep; John is the witness who first recognises the Lord (21:7). The Church needs both the shepherd's authority, which comes from Christ, and the beloved disciple's contemplation.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church honours Mar Yohannan Shleeha today, as the Byzantine Churches also do on this day; the Latin Church keeps his main feast on 27 December. The beloved disciple took the Mother of Jesus into his own home (John 19:27), and every Christian family is invited to do the same. He leaned on the Lord's breast at the supper, and in the Holy Qurbono we come to the same table and receive the same Lord. As we receive the Holy Mysteries this Saturday, we ask Mar Yohannan to teach us to follow without comparing, and to love as we have been loved.

For study and teaching

  • Peter asked, “What about him?” When do I compare my path with someone else's instead of following?
  • Paul's list of hardships ends in confidence. Which hardship in my life most tempts me to doubt God's love?
  • The beloved disciple took Mary into his home. How does our family make room for the Mother of the Lord?
  • If Jesus' one command were “love one another”, as John repeated in old age, what would change in our home this week?
