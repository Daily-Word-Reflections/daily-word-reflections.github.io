Word for the day

“For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing;” 2 Corinthians 2:15

Reflection

Paul writes today from a worried heart. He has come to Troas to proclaim the good news, and the Lord has opened a door for him, yet he cannot rest because his brother Titus is not there with news from Corinth. So he leaves the open door and goes on to Macedonia. Then, almost in the same breath, the worry turns into thanksgiving: “But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads in every place the fragrance that comes from knowing him.” The picture is of a victory procession in which incense is carried, so that the scent reaches the crowds long before they see the one who is passing. Paul does not claim to be the victor. He is part of the procession, and the fragrance is Christ's. Like every scent, it cannot choose whom it reaches: “For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing; to the one a fragrance from death to death, to the other a fragrance from life to life.” The same gospel is life to one who welcomes it and a sharp reproach to one who closes the door. Paul himself trembles at this: “Who is sufficient for these things?”

He answers by describing the kind of messenger God can use: “For we are not peddlers of God's word like so many; but in Christ we speak as persons of sincerity, as persons sent from God and standing in his presence.” A peddler waters down his goods to please the buyer. A person sent from God speaks as one who stands before God. In the Kerala monsoon, when rain has soaked the house and the cupboards smell of damp, an old household remedy is to burn a little frankincense or sambrani on a few embers. The smell fills the rooms, clings to clothes and stays in the hair. We carry with us the fragrance of whoever we have spent time with. The disciple who has stood in the presence of Christ in prayer and in the Holy Qurbono carries something of him into the market, the office and the kitchen without having to say a word.

The Gospel shows us the Lord himself in the synagogue of Nazareth, “as was his custom,” on the sabbath. The scroll of Isaiah is given to him, and he finds the place: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim release to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to let the oppressed go free, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor.” The very word Christ means the Anointed One. The Spirit rests on him not for his own comfort but for the sake of the poor, the captive, the blind and the oppressed. Luke tells us that he then says: “Today this scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.” The good news is not an idea about the future; it begins to happen when he opens his mouth. Paul's fragrance and the Lord's anointing are one thing seen from two sides: Christ is anointed, and the fragrance of that anointing is spread through those who belong to him.

Today the Panchangam also remembers St Alexius of Rome. The old Life of Alexius, whose historical details cannot be confirmed and which is best read as a story told by the Church to praise a way of life, tells of the only son of a wealthy Roman family who left his home and bride, lived for years as a beggar in a far-off city, and finally returned unrecognised to his father's house, where he lived out his days among the poor at the door. Whatever the details, the Church has loved this story because it makes Christ's own path visible: he who was rich became poor for our sake. Alexius's name is also borne today by Most Rev. Yoohanon Mar Alexios, whose name day the Panchangam keeps, and the Church remembers with thanks the anniversary of the episcopal ordination of Most Rev. Thomas Mar Koorilos in 1997. A bishop is anointed so that he may be a person “sent from God and standing in his presence.” We pray that these pastors, and all who serve us, may spread the fragrance of Christ.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul says something beautiful: thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumph, and through us spreads in every place the fragrance that comes from knowing him (2 Corinthians 2:14). The picture is of a procession in which incense is carried, so that its scent reaches people before they even see the one who is passing. Paul is not the victor; Christ is. But Paul and all of us who belong to Christ carry his fragrance.

In these monsoon days, many of our homes use a little incense to drive away the damp smell. The smell stays in our clothes and hair for hours. Paul says it is like this with the gospel: those who have been with Christ carry something of him wherever they go. He adds: we are not peddlers of God's word like so many, but in Christ we speak as persons of sincerity, as persons sent from God and standing in his presence (2 Corinthians 2:17). Let us not water down the gospel to please people.

In the Gospel, Jesus stands up in the synagogue of Nazareth and reads from Isaiah: The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). The Anointed One is anointed for the poor, the captive, the blind and the oppressed. That is the fragrance we are to spread. Today the Church remembers St Alexius of Rome, who, according to the old story, left his riches to live among the poor, and she prays for Thomas Mar Koorilos on the anniversary of his episcopal ordination and for Yoohanon Mar Alexios on his name day.

Let us ask the Lord to make us people who carry the fragrance of Christ: in our kindness to the poor, in our honesty, in the way we pray and the way we speak. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever smelled incense in the church, or a sweet flower in the garden? You can smell it even if you cannot see it! St Paul says that people who stay close to Jesus carry something sweet with them, the love of Jesus. When you are kind to your friends, when you share with someone poor, when you say sorry, people can tell that you belong to Jesus. Today, try to carry the sweet smell of Jesus to everyone you meet.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, anointed by the Father with the Holy Spirit to bring good news to the poor, we thank you that in you we are always led in triumph. Let the fragrance of your knowledge spread through us in every place, in our homes, our work and our parish. Keep us sincere, as persons sent from God and standing in his presence, and make us gentle with the poor, the captive and the oppressed. Through the prayers of St Alexius, who for your sake became poor, and of all your saints, bless our bishops and priests, and bless Thomas Mar Koorilos and Yoohanon Mar Alexios whom we remember today. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers St Alexius of Rome; according to an old story, which first circulated in Syriac, he gave up wealth and a promising marriage to live as a poor pilgrim. The readings it gives are those of the weekday. In the Epistle Paul says that those who preach Christ are “the aroma of Christ to God”, a fragrance that reaches everyone and is received in different ways. In the Gospel Jesus stands up in the synagogue at Nazareth and reads the prophecy that tells what his mission is: good news to the poor, release to captives, sight to the blind. Tomorrow is the Ninth Sunday after Pentecost. The key words are fragrance and anointing.

2 Corinthians 2:12–17 · The fragrance of Christ

Setting.

Paul’s relations with the Corinthians had gone through a painful time. After a hard visit he wrote them a severe letter (2:1–4) and sent it by Titus. He then left Ephesus for Troas, hoping to meet Titus there with news of how the letter was received. Titus was not at Troas, and Paul, though the Lord had opened “a door” for preaching, could not rest. He went on to Macedonia, where Titus met him with good news (7:5–7).

At that point Paul breaks off the story (2:13) and turns in thanksgiving to God: his anxiety has not stopped his mission. The verses that follow are some of the most vivid in the letter: a triumphal procession, the smell of incense, and a question that every minister of the word must ask.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: Paul at Troas: a door opened for the gospel, but no peace of mind without Titus.
  • v. 14: thanks to God, who leads us in triumph in Christ and spreads the fragrance of knowing him everywhere.
  • vv. 15–16a: we are the aroma of Christ, for those being saved and those perishing: life to life, death to death.
  • v. 16b: “Who is sufficient for these things?”
  • v. 17: not peddlers of God’s word, but persons of sincerity sent from God.

Key words.

  • thriambeuō, to lead in triumph: The word comes from the Roman triumph, in which the victorious general rode through the city with his army and captives, while incense burned along the road. Scholars are divided whether Paul sees himself as a captive led in God’s train or as a soldier sharing in the victory. In either case the victory is God’s.
  • osmē / euōdia, smell, fragrance: The smell of the incense of the triumph meant honour to the victors and death to the captives. Paul uses the image of the Gospel: the same fragrance is received as life by some, and refused by others. In the Old Testament the smell of a sacrifice rising to God is a sign that it is accepted (Genesis 8:21; Exodus 29:18).
  • kapēleuontes, peddlers: The word was used of retail traders, especially wine-sellers, who, as many explain, watered down their wine to make a bigger profit. Paul’s opponents, he implies, thin out the word of God for gain. He prefers to give it undiluted.

Verse by verse.

“When I came to Troas to proclaim the good news of Christ, a door was opened for me in the Lord;” (2:12)

A “door” is an opportunity. Paul recognises that the opening comes from the Lord, and in the next verse, that even an open door does not give him rest while the Corinthians are on his heart.

“But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads in every place the fragrance that comes from knowing him.” (2:14)

Paul’s thanksgiving appears suddenly, after his anxiety. He says that the mission does not depend on his state of mind. “Through us” God spreads a fragrance: the apostle is the carrier and not the source.

“For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing;” (2:15)

Paul says that the aroma is offered “to God”: whatever the response, the preaching is a pleasing offering. The one aroma has two effects, depending on how it is received. He says “being saved” and “perishing”, in the present: the outcome is still open.

“Who is sufficient for these things?” (2:16)

This is the honest question of every priest, catechist and parent. Paul answers in 3:5: “our competence is from God”. The minister does not have to be enough; God is.

“For we are not peddlers of God’s word like so many; but in Christ we speak as persons of sincerity, as persons sent from God and standing in his presence.” (2:17)

Sincerity means speaking and living “in his presence”. The gospel is God’s word, and no one may water it down to please a crowd or to gain from it.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has found in this passage a picture of her own ministry. Those who preach the gospel are sent by God and speak “in Christ”, and the fruit does not depend on them. The use of incense in worship, so dear to the West Syriac tradition, has long been understood as a sign of the prayer of the Church rising to God and of the fragrance of Christ spreading from the altar into the world. In this way the Holy Qurbono teaches what Paul teaches: those who receive Christ become, in their turn, a fragrance for others. The Church also remembers what Paul says in verse 16, “death to death” or “life to life”: the word of God is serious, and the response to it matters.

Luke 4:16–19 · The Spirit of the Lord is upon me

Setting.

This is the first public teaching of Jesus that Luke describes in detail, after his baptism, his temptation and his return to Galilee “filled with the power of the Spirit” (4:14). Luke places it at Nazareth, where Jesus grew up, and presents it as the programme of his whole ministry. He goes into the synagogue “as was his custom”, which tells us that Jesus prayed with his own people, and he stands up to read.

The passage he reads is from Isaiah 61:1–2, with a line from Isaiah 58:6 about the oppression that must end. It is a prophecy of the one who is anointed by the Spirit to announce God’s liberating favour. Jesus stops reading before the words “and the day of vengeance of our God”; and after he sits down he says, “Today this scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing” (4:21).

Structure.

  • v. 16: Jesus at Nazareth on the sabbath, as was his custom, stands to read.
  • v. 17: the scroll of Isaiah is given to him, and he finds the place.
  • v. 18: the Spirit is upon him; he is anointed to bring good news to the poor, to proclaim release to captives and sight to the blind, and to set the oppressed at liberty.
  • v. 19: to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favour.

Key words.

  • echrisen, he anointed: The verb behind “Christ”, “the Anointed One”. In Israel kings, priests and sometimes prophets were anointed with oil to show that God had set them apart. Jesus is anointed by the Spirit at his baptism, and at Nazareth he says that this anointing is for the poor.
  • euangelizasthai, to bring good news: The verb of the Gospel itself (euangelion). The first to hear the good news are the poor, as in Mary’s hymn, “he has filled the hungry with good things” (1:53).
  • aphesis, release, forgiveness: The same word is used twice in verse 18, and elsewhere for the forgiveness of sins (Luke 24:47). The release that Jesus proclaims includes freedom from every chain: of debt, of illness, of sin.
  • eniautos kyriou dektos, the year of the Lord’s favour: An echo of the Jubilee year (Leviticus 25), when slaves were released, debts cancelled and land returned. Jesus announces that the great Jubilee has begun in his own person.

Verse by verse.

“When he came to Nazareth, where he had been brought up, he went to the synagogue on the sabbath day, as was his custom.” (4:16)

Jesus prays in the synagogue every sabbath, as his own people do. The Church, which learned from Israel to gather for prayer and the reading of Scripture, keeps the same pattern in the Liturgy of the Word.

““The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor.” (4:18)

The whole Trinity is in this sentence: the Spirit, the Lord, and the one who is sent. The “poor” are those who have nothing to rely on but God, and not only those without money. The Church has always read this as the programme of the mission of Christ and therefore of his Church.

“He has sent me to proclaim release to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to let the oppressed go free,” (4:18)

Luke’s Gospel will show these things in action: the healing of a man with a withered hand, a woman bent over for eighteen years, a blind beggar near Jericho. The release is real, in body and in soul.

“to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor.”” (4:19)

The “year of favour” is the time of God’s mercy, not a calendar year. Jesus says that this time has come, and that it is “today”. Every time the Gospel is read in the Holy Qurbono, the same “today” is heard again.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that “Christ” means “anointed”, and that Jesus is the Christ because he was anointed by the Spirit for the mission that this word signifies (CCC 436). The Church sees in today’s Gospel the programme of her own mission, which she carries out in Christ’s name and with a love for the poor that belongs to her constant tradition (CCC 2443–2446). The Church’s reading does not turn against Israel. The Prophet Isaiah is Israel’s prophet, and Jesus is reading Israel’s Scripture in Israel’s synagogue. The Catechism teaches that the Spirit who anointed the Lord at the Jordan is the same Spirit who anoints every Christian in Baptism and Chrismation (CCC 695).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus stop reading in the middle of the sentence in Isaiah?

Isaiah 61:2 goes on, “and the day of vengeance of our God”. Jesus reads only “the year of the Lord’s favor” and then closes the scroll. Many interpreters see this as a sign of the character of his mission, which at this first coming is the proclamation of mercy. The day of judgment is not denied; it belongs to the end, when he comes again. The Church reads the passage as one of the clearest places where we see that the kingdom Jesus announces is first of all grace.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks, “Who is sufficient for these things?” and answers that competence comes from God. Where in my service do I feel inadequate, and how can I place it in God’s hands?
  • Jesus says that the scripture is fulfilled “today”. What word from the Gospel do I need to hear as spoken to me today, and for whose sake?
