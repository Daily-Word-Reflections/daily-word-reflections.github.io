Word for the day

“This is my Son, the Beloved, with whom I am well pleased.” Matthew 3:17

Reflection

Denho means “rising” or “manifestation”. At the Jordan the hidden Child of Bethlehem is revealed to the world. When Jesus comes up from the water, “the heavens were opened to him and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and alighting on him. And a voice from heaven said, ‘This is my Son, the Beloved, with whom I am well pleased.’” For the first time the whole Holy Trinity is manifested: the Father speaks, the Son stands in the water, the Spirit descends.

John protests: “I need to be baptized by you.” Jesus answers: “Let it be so now; for it is proper for us in this way to fulfill all righteousness.” The sinless One goes down into the waters of repentance with sinners. He does not need to be cleansed; he cleanses the waters. The Fathers say that by his Baptism Christ sanctified the waters for our Baptism.

The Old Testament readings are full of water: Rebekah drawing water for a stranger and his camels; water filling the dry valley; Ezekiel's river flowing from the Temple, where “everything will live where the river goes”; Isaiah's promise: “With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation.” All these streams flow into the Jordan, and from the Jordan into our baptismal font.

St Paul tells Titus: “the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all”, and God saved us “through the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit”. Denho is our feast too. In Baptism the Father said to each of us, “You are my beloved child”, and the Spirit came to dwell in us. Today we renew that grace.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today is Denho, the feast of the manifestation of the Lord. The Child of Bethlehem, hidden for thirty years in Nazareth, comes to the Jordan and is revealed to the world. When he comes up out of the water, the heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father's voice says: “This is my Son, the Beloved, with whom I am well pleased.”

John did not want to baptise him: “I need to be baptised by you!” But Jesus went down into the water with sinners. Why? He had no sin to wash away. The Fathers answer: he went down to make the waters holy for us. The Jordan became the source of every baptismal font in the world.

Our Old Testament readings are full of water: Rebekah at the well, a river flowing from the Temple bringing life wherever it goes, Isaiah's joyful promise: “With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation.” In our Baptism we drank from those wells. And the same voice spoke over each of us: “You are my beloved child.”

Today, as the waters are blessed, let us renew our baptismal promises with faith. Take the blessed water home; bless your children with it each night. Find out the date of your Baptism and thank God for it every year. Live as beloved children of the Father, filled with the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Dear friends, long ago Isaiah cried out: O that you would tear open the heavens and come down (Isaiah 64:1). Israel longed for God to come close. Dear brothers and sisters, most of us were baptised as infants. We do not remember the water, the oil or the candle. Today, as we celebrate the Lord's own baptism, the Church invites us to remember what God did to us.

Paul asks: do you not know that all of us who have been baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death? We were buried with him by baptism, so that we too might walk in newness of life (Romans 6:3–4). The font is both a tomb and a womb.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Denho, a Syriac word for rising or dawning, is the day the Churches of the East have long kept as the manifestation of the Lord at the Jordan. Everything today is water. The Evening Gospel tells of the baptism in Mark's plain words, with the heavens “torn apart” (Mark 1:10). The Old Testament lessons fill the day with wells and rivers: Rebekah at the well, drawing for a stranger and his camels (Genesis 24:10–20); water that fills the valley and looks to Moab as red as blood (2 Kings 3:21–27); Isaiah's joy in drawing “water from the wells of salvation” (Isaiah 12:3). In Acts, disciples who knew only John's baptism are baptised in the name of the Lord Jesus and receive the Holy Spirit (Acts 19:1–7).

The three readings studied here run as one stream. Ezekiel sees a river rising from God's house and bringing dead waters to life. Titus says what that river is for us: “the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit”. Matthew shows the source, when the sinless Son goes down into the Jordan and the Father and the Spirit reveal him. The key words are river, appearing and righteousness.

Ezekiel 47:1–12 · The river flowing from the Temple

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest, taken to Babylon with the first group of exiles in 597 BC (1:1–3). He saw in vision the glory of the Lord leave the Temple (chapters 10–11), and the city fell in 587/586 BC. Chapters 40–48, dated about 573 BC (40:1), are his great vision of a restored Temple and land, given to people who have lost both. The glory comes back from the east (43:1–5).

A real Jerusalem has no large river; its water is a modest spring. So the geography here is that of hope. From the threshold of God's house water flows east, down the dry Judean hills and into the Dead Sea, where nothing lives. Eden's river (Genesis 2:10) stands behind the vision, as does the promise of other prophets (Joel 3:18; Zechariah 14:8).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the source: below the threshold, flowing east.
  • vv. 3–6: four measurements of a thousand cubits each: ankle, knee, waist, then a river that cannot be crossed.
  • vv. 7–10: trees on the banks; the sea made fresh; fish and fishermen.
  • v. 11: the marshes stay salt.
  • v. 12: trees with monthly fruit and healing leaves.

Key words.

  • naḥal, river, wadi: The usual word for a valley stream, which runs in the rains and dries in summer. This one has no tributaries and yet grows deeper at every stage. Its source is God alone.
  • nirpə’û, will become fresh: Literally “will be healed”. The verb is the common Hebrew word for healing. The NRSV's “fresh” is accurate about salt water, but the Hebrew tells us this is medicine for a dead sea.
  • miqdāš, sanctuary: “Because the water for them flows from the sanctuary” (v. 12) is the reason for everything. The life of the land is the overflow of God's dwelling.

Verse by verse.

“Then he brought me back to the entrance of the temple; there, water was flowing from below the threshold of the temple” (47:1)

It starts as a trickle, and it comes from below, on the south side of the altar. The place of sacrifice is near the source.

“Again he measured one thousand, and it was a river that I could not cross, for the water had risen;” (47:5)

A thousand cubits is roughly half a kilometre. No one has added water, yet it rises. God's gift is measured out by steps, and at the end it is out of the prophet's depth.

“Wherever the river goes, every living creature that swarms will live,” (47:9)

“Live” comes again and again in this verse. The lowest and deadest spot on earth becomes a place for spreading nets (v. 10). Christian readers easily think here of the disciples called to fish for people (Matthew 4:19).

“But its swamps and marshes will not become fresh; they are to be left for salt.” (47:11)

A small realistic note. Salt was needed for sacrifice and for daily life (Leviticus 2:13). Even in the renewal, God leaves room for what has its own use.

“Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for healing.”” (47:12)

Revelation takes up this line for the river that flows “from the throne of God and of the Lamb” (22:1–2): “the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations”.

How the Church reads it.

On the last day of Tabernacles, Jesus stands up and cries, “Let anyone who is thirsty come to me” (John 7:37), and says that “rivers of living water” will flow (7:38), which John explains as the Spirit. John has already told us that the temple Jesus spoke of was his body (2:21). From his pierced side “blood and water came out” (19:34), and many Fathers saw in this the Church's sacraments. So the Church reads Ezekiel's river as the gift of the Spirit flowing from the risen Christ. The Catechism explains the symbol of water in this way: it signifies the Holy Spirit's action in Baptism (CCC 694), and it lists water as the source of life among the Old Testament signs of Baptism (CCC 1218).

Titus 2:11–3:9 · The water of rebirth and renewal

Setting.

Titus was a Greek Christian and a co-worker of Paul (Galatians 2:1–3), left in Crete to organise the communities (Titus 1:5). The letter is one of the Pastorals, which carry Paul's name. Many modern scholars think they were written by a follower of Paul after his death; others hold that Paul stands behind them. The Church receives them as inspired Scripture.

Most of the letter is about order and conduct. But twice, in the middle of ordinary advice for families and citizens, the author lifts his eyes and gives a short creed (2:11–14; 3:4–7). Both begin with the same verb: God's grace and kindness “appeared”. That is why this reading belongs to Denho.

Structure.

  • 2:11–14: the grace of God has appeared: what it teaches, and what we wait for.
  • 2:15: Titus is to teach this with authority.
  • 3:1–2: conduct toward rulers and neighbours.
  • 3:3: what we once were.
  • 3:4–7: the second appearing: God saved us through the water of rebirth.
  • 3:8–9: the saying is sure, so do good and avoid useless quarrels.

Key words.

  • epephanē / epiphaneia, appeared / manifestation: This is the Greek root of “Epiphany”. It was used for the visit of a god or a ruler. Grace “appeared” in Christ's first coming (2:11; 3:4), and we wait for his “manifestation” at the end (2:13). The Syriac denḥo, the rising of a light, says the same thing.
  • palingenesia, rebirth: Literally “being born again”. In the New Testament it occurs elsewhere only at Matthew 19:28, for the renewal of all things. Baptism is that coming renewal, begun in one person.
  • loutron, washing, bath: The NRSV says “water”; the Greek word means a bath or a washing. It is also used in Ephesians 5:26.
  • philanthrōpia, loving kindness: Literally “love of humankind”, a word used in Greek praise of good rulers. Here it describes God.

Verse by verse.

“For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all,” (2:11)

“To all”: on the day when the Lord is shown to the world, the reach of the grace is stated at the start.

“while we wait for the blessed hope and the manifestation of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ.” (2:13)

A note on the translation. One Greek article governs “God and Savior”, and the NRSV takes the phrase of one Person, Jesus Christ. The POC can be read as naming two. The NRSV reading is what most scholars today prefer, and it is a clear statement of Christ's divinity.

“But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us, not because of any works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy, through the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit.” (3:4–5)

The heart of the reading, set directly against v. 3. Nothing we did began this. Within three verses all three divine Persons appear: God our Savior, the Holy Spirit, and Jesus Christ (v. 6).

“This Spirit he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Savior,” (3:6)

“Richly”: the language of the river again. The Spirit is not rationed.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes this verse as a name for Baptism: “the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit” (CCC 1215). It teaches that our justification comes from God's grace, an undeserved help (CCC 1996), and it also speaks of merit, which is itself God's gift (CCC 2006–2011). St Gregory of Nazianzus, in his oration on Baptism, calls this gift the most beautiful and magnificent of God's gifts, and speaks of it under many names, among them enlightenment and grace (Oration 40; CCC 1216; in substance).

Matthew 3:13–17 · This is my Son, the Beloved

Setting.

John has been preaching in the wilderness and baptising people who confess their sins (3:1–6). He has warned of one more powerful who is coming, and who will baptise “with the Holy Spirit and fire” (3:11). Now the coming One stands in the line. All four Gospels treat this meeting as the start of the public ministry of Jesus. Matthew alone gives the short exchange in vv. 14–15.

The Jordan had its own memory. Israel crossed it to enter the land under Joshua, whose name is the same as that of Jesus (Joshua 3). Elijah crossed it before he was taken up (2 Kings 2:8–11). A new entry into God's promises begins at the same river.

Structure.

  • v. 13: Jesus comes from Galilee.
  • v. 14: John tries to stop him.
  • v. 15: Jesus' answer, and John's consent.
  • v. 16: he comes up; the heavens open; the Spirit descends.
  • v. 17: the voice of the Father.

Key words.

  • dikaiosynē, righteousness: A central word in Matthew (5:6; 6:33). It means doing what God wills, here in his plan to save. By standing with sinners, Jesus does what the Father wants.
  • plērōsai, to fulfill: Matthew's great verb, used for Scripture and God's plan (5:17). These are the first words Jesus speaks in this Gospel, and they say what his whole life will be.
  • agapētos, beloved: The word used in the Greek of Genesis 22:2 for Isaac. The voice joins three texts: the royal son of Psalm 2:7, the delight of the Servant of Isaiah 42:1, and perhaps the beloved son of Abraham. At the very start the title holds the King, the Servant and the sacrifice.

Verse by verse.

“John would have prevented him, saying, “I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?”” (3:14)

The Greek verb suggests he kept trying to hold him back. John knows who this is, and also knows that his rite is for sinners. The early Church also felt the difficulty, and Matthew answers it here.

“But Jesus answered him, “Let it be so now; for it is proper for us in this way to fulfill all righteousness.” Then he consented.” (3:15)

“Now”: for this moment the order is reversed. “Us”: John and Jesus together serve the plan. John's obedience is to do what seemed to him out of order.

“And when Jesus had been baptized, just as he came up from the water, suddenly the heavens were opened to him and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and alighting on him.” (3:16)

Isaiah had begged: “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down” (64:1). They are open now. The dove recalls the Spirit over the first waters (Genesis 1:2) and the dove after the flood (Genesis 8:11).

“And a voice from heaven said, “This is my Son, the Beloved, with whom I am well pleased.”” (3:17)

In Mark the voice speaks to Jesus: “You are my Son”. In Matthew it speaks to all who stand there: “This is”. It is a public statement, for the day of manifestation. For the first time the Father, the Son and the Spirit are shown together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in this baptism Jesus' acceptance of his mission as the suffering Servant, and it calls it a manifestation (CCC 535–537). The Fathers teach in many ways that he did not need purifying, and that he went down to bless the waters. St Ignatius of Antioch, at the start of the second century, already writes that he was born and baptised so that by his suffering he might purify the water (Letter to the Ephesians 18, in substance). St Gregory of Nazianzus preaches on this day that Christ goes down into the water and we go down with him, that we may rise with him (Oration 39, in substance). The Catechism adds that the Spirit who hovered over the first waters descends now on Christ as a prelude of the new creation (CCC 1224).

Questions people ask

If Jesus had no sin, why was he baptised?

Not for his own need. He stood with sinners and took their place, as the Servant would (Isaiah 53:12), and he opened the way for us (CCC 536, 1224). The Fathers add that his touch made the waters holy, and not the other way round.

Was John's baptism the same as ours?

No. John's was a sign of repentance that pointed ahead. In Acts 19:1–7, read today, Paul finds disciples who knew only John's baptism; they are then baptised in the name of the Lord Jesus and receive the Holy Spirit. Christian Baptism gives what John's only promised: new birth and the Spirit (CCC 1265).

Does Titus 3:5 mean that good works do not matter?

No. It says that works did not earn our salvation; three verses later it says that believers should “devote themselves to good works” (3:8). Grace comes first, and it bears fruit. Catholics and Lutherans stated their common understanding of this in the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Denho is a day of obligation in the Malankara Church, with the Holy Qurbono for the people, and in many parishes the waters are solemnly blessed. The Syriac tradition loves to set the Jordan beside Mary's womb: St Ephrem and Mar Jacob of Serugh both speak of the water of Baptism as a womb from which the children of God are born (in substance). When the parish takes home the blessed water, it carries an echo of the river Ezekiel saw. Keep this day as a family feast of Baptism: bring out the baptismal certificate or candle, tell the children who stood for them, and renew the promises together.

For study and teaching

  • Ezekiel's river grows from ankle-deep to too deep to cross. How deep have I let the grace of Baptism go into my life?
  • Titus 3:3 describes what we once were, and 3:4 begins “But”. What is the “but” in my own story?
  • John resisted and then consented. Where is God now asking me to do something I think is out of order?
  • If the Father's words are also said over each baptised person, what would change if I really believed them today?
