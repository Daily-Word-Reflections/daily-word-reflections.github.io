Word for the day

“Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested.” Hebrews 2:18

Reflection

Two short readings today tell one story from two sides: how far God was willing to come to find us. The Letter to the Hebrews begins with a plain fact about us: “the children share flesh and blood.” We are bodies that get tired, fall ill, grow old and die. Then it tells us what the Son of God did about it: “he himself likewise shared the same things, so that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil.” He did not rescue us from a distance, like a ship that throws a rope and sails on. He came on board. He took a body from the Virgin Mary, the same flesh and blood as ours, so that death, the one thing we all share, could become the place where he met the enemy and defeated him. “For it is clear that he did not come to help angels, but the descendants of Abraham.” God's mercy is not for some other, cleaner kind of creature. It is for us, the children of Abraham's faith, with our weak bodies and our anxious hearts.

From this Hebrews draws a conclusion that is full of comfort: “Therefore he had to become like his brothers and sisters in every respect, so that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God, to make a sacrifice of atonement for the sins of the people. Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested.” Our High Priest is not a stranger to temptation, hunger, betrayal or grief. When we bring him our struggle, we are not explaining something he cannot imagine; we are speaking to someone who has been there. In the Gospel the same mercy wears an everyday face. “Or what woman having ten silver coins, if she loses one of them, does not light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it?” A silver coin of that kind was roughly a day's wage, and for a poor household one coin mattered. The house is dark, the floor is earth, and the coin is somewhere in the dust. She does not shrug and say she still has nine. She lights a lamp, she sweeps, she searches until she finds it.

St Gregory the Great read this parable as a picture of the Incarnation: the woman is the Wisdom of God, and the lamp she lights is light in a clay vessel, the divinity of the Son shining in our flesh; with it she sweeps the house of our conscience until the lost image of God is found again (Homilies on the Gospels 34, in substance). The coin, after all, bears an image, and we were made in God's image. That image can be buried in dust, but it is never erased. The parable ends in a party: “When she has found it, she calls together her friends and neighbors, saying, ‘Rejoice with me, for I have found the coin that I had lost.’ Just so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents.” In this season of Pentecost, the Spirit is the lamp and the broom in the hands of the Church. Is there someone you have written off as lost, a relative, a former friend, a young person who has stopped coming to church? Do not say there are still nine. Light a lamp: pray. Sweep: make the first move. Search carefully: keep looking until the coin is found. And if you are the lost coin today, know that you are being searched for, and come to the sacrament of Confession, where heaven's joy begins.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews tells us how far God came to find us. Since the children share flesh and blood, he himself likewise shared the same things, so that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death (Hebrews 2:14). The Son did not save us from a distance. He took our body, so that he could meet death from the inside and defeat it.

Therefore he had to become like his brothers and sisters in every respect, so that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest (Hebrews 2:17). Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested (Hebrews 2:18). When we pray in temptation, we speak to one who has been there.

In the Gospel Jesus gives us a homely picture of this mercy. A woman loses one of her ten silver coins. She lights a lamp, sweeps the house, and searches carefully until she finds it (Luke 15:8). Then she calls her friends and neighbours to rejoice. Just so, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents (Luke 15:10).

St Gregory the Great saw in the lamp the divinity of Christ shining in our flesh. The Spirit now puts that lamp in our hands. Let us search for those who are lost: pray for them, make the first move, keep looking. And if we ourselves are the lost coin, let us come to Confession and let heaven rejoice. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever lost something precious, a toy or a pencil box, and looked everywhere for it? Jesus told a story about a woman who lost one coin. She lit a lamp, swept the whole house, and looked under everything until she found it. Then she was so happy that she called all her friends! Jesus says God looks for us like that when we go away from him, and heaven is full of joy when we come back.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you shared our flesh and blood and through your death you destroyed the power of the one who held us in fear. You became like us in every respect, a merciful and faithful High Priest, tested as we are tested. Help us in our hour of trial. Like the woman who lit a lamp and swept her house, you search for every one of us who is lost. By your Holy Spirit make us lamps in your hands, to seek out those who have strayed and to rejoice with heaven over every sinner who returns. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings show a God who comes down to seek us. Hebrews says that the Son shared our flesh and blood so that he might become “a merciful and faithful high priest” and help those who are tested. In Luke a woman lights a lamp, sweeps the house and searches for one lost coin, and there is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. The key words are shared and found.

Hebrews 2:14–18 · He shared our flesh and blood

Setting.

The first chapters of Hebrews show that the Son is greater than the angels (chapter 1). Then the author faces a question: if the Son is so great, why did he become man and suffer? He answers from Psalm 8: the Son was made “for a little while lower than the angels” so that “by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone” (2:9). Today's verses explain why this was fitting. This is the first time Hebrews calls Jesus “high priest”, the great theme of the letter.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: he shared flesh and blood to destroy the power of death and the devil.
  • v. 16: he came to help the descendants of Abraham, not angels.
  • vv. 17–18: like us in every respect, a merciful and faithful high priest who helps those who are tested.

Key words.

  • kekoinōnēken, share: The children “share” flesh and blood; the Son “likewise shared” the same things. The word for fellowship, koinōnia, is in the root. God enters into fellowship with our human condition.
  • archiereus eleēmōn kai pistos, a merciful and faithful high priest: Merciful toward us, faithful toward God. A high priest stands between God and the people. Jesus can do this perfectly because he is both.
  • hilaskesthai, to make a sacrifice of atonement: The verb of the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16), when the high priest entered the Holy of Holies with blood. Hebrews will show that Christ fulfils this once for all with his own blood (9:11–14).

Verse by verse.

“Since, therefore, the children share flesh and blood, he himself likewise shared the same things,” (2:14)

The Incarnation is not a disguise. The Son took a real human nature, body and soul, and lived a real human life.

“and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by the fear of death.” (2:15)

Fear of death can rule a whole life. Christ went through death and broke its power, so that his own need not live as slaves of that fear.

“Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested.” (2:18)

Jesus knows our trials from within. Hebrews later adds that he was tested in every way as we are, “yet without sin” (4:15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as a key to the Incarnation and the priesthood of Christ. St Gregory of Nazianzus wrote that what was not assumed was not healed (Letter 101, in substance): the Son took all that is human in order to save all that is human. The Catechism teaches that Christ is the one high priest, in whom everything the priesthood of the Old Covenant prefigured is fulfilled (CCC 1544), and that he conquered the tempter for us, as one able to sympathize with our weaknesses (CCC 540).

Luke 15:8–10 · The woman who searches for the lost coin

Setting.

Luke 15 holds three parables of the lost: the sheep, the coin and the son. They answer the grumbling of some Pharisees and scribes who said, “This fellow welcomes sinners and eats with them” (15:2). The parable of the coin is found only in Luke. It stands between the shepherd and the father, and here the one who searches is a woman.

Structure.

  • v. 8: one coin lost; lamp, broom and careful search.
  • v. 9: found; friends and neighbours called to rejoice.
  • v. 10: so there is joy before the angels over one sinner who repents.

Key words.

  • drachmē, silver coin: A Greek silver coin, roughly a day's wage for a labourer. For a poor woman ten coins could be all her savings.
  • metanoeō, repent: Literally “change one's mind”: a turning of the whole person back to God. In the parable the coin does nothing; the woman does all the searching. Repentance is first of all being found.

Verse by verse.

“does not light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it?” (15:8)

Houses of poor families often had few windows and earthen floors. Finding a small coin meant patient, careful work. God searches for us with the same patience.

“Rejoice with me, for I have found the coin that I had lost.” (15:9)

Joy is meant to be shared. Heaven's joy over a sinner who returns is a feast to which the whole community is invited.

“Just so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents.” (15:10)

“In the presence of the angels” is a reverent way of speaking of God himself. It is God who rejoices.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in this parable the heart of God who seeks sinners. The Catechism teaches that Jesus invites sinners to the table of the Kingdom and that he speaks of the boundless mercy of the Father for them and of the joy in heaven over one sinner who repents (CCC 545). Some Fathers, among them St Gregory the Great (Homilies on the Gospels 34, in substance), saw in the woman the Wisdom of God, and in the lamp the light of God shining in Christ's humanity. The sacrament of Penance is the place where the lost coin is found again.

Questions people ask

If Jesus was tested like us, could he have sinned?

The Church teaches that Jesus was truly tempted but was without sin (Hebrews 4:15). His temptations were real, not play-acting, but his will was perfectly united with the Father's. Because he never gave in, he knows the full weight of temptation better than we do, and he can help us in ours.

For study and teaching

  • What fear has the strongest grip on my life, and how does the victory of Christ over death speak to it?
  • Is there someone in my family or parish who is “lost” and whom I could help God to search for?
