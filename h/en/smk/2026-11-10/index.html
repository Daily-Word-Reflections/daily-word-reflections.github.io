Word for the day

“So the last will be first, and the first will be last.” Matthew 20:16

Reflection

Paul's words to Christian slaves sound strange to us, but their core is clear: whatever our station, we serve so that “the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed”, and we serve a believing master “all the more” because he is a brother. Work done well and honestly is a witness to Christ in every office, field and kitchen.

In the parable the owner goes out at dawn, at nine, at noon, at three and at five to call workers into his vineyard, and pays the last the same as the first. Those who worked all day grumble. “Are you envious because I am generous?” God's call comes at every hour, and his reward is not wages but himself. Samuel heard the call as a boy; Leo the Great served as Pope in hard times. Each answered when called.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul's words to slaves may sound strange to us, but their core is clear: whatever our station, we serve so that the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed (1 Timothy 6:1). Our work is our witness.

In the parable of the labourers in the vineyard, the landowner calls workers early in the morning, at nine, at noon, at three, and even at five o'clock, and in the end gives all the same wage. Are you envious because I am generous, he asks (Matthew 20:15). God calls at every hour, and his generosity is greater than our calculations.

Today the Church remembers the prophet Samuel and Pope St Leo the Great. The boy Samuel said: speak, Lord, for your servant is listening (1 Samuel 3:10). At every age and at every hour, let this be our prayer. Amen.

For children

Children, the boy Samuel said to God: speak, I am listening. Say the same prayer tonight before you sleep.

Prayer

Lord of the vineyard, you call us at every hour and pay beyond our deserving. Free us from envy, make our work an offering, and give us Samuel's listening heart. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two readings about work and reward. Paul tells Christian slaves to serve well, so that the name of God is not blasphemed. Jesus tells of an owner who pays the last as he pays the first. In both, the measure is not our own claim but God's goodness and the honour of his name.

1 Timothy 6:1–2 · So that the name of God may not be blasphemed

Setting.

The Pastoral Letters (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus) give Timothy, Paul's coworker, instructions for ordering the church at Ephesus. Here they speak to Christians who were slaves, of whom there were many in the early communities. Slavery was a legal institution of the Roman world; the letter does not treat it as a law to reform, but speaks to believers living inside it.

The New Testament had already planted the seed that would undo slavery: in Christ “there is no longer slave or free” (Galatians 3:28), and Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother” (Philemon 16). The Church today teaches without hesitation that slavery is an offence against human dignity (Gaudium et Spes 27; CCC 2414).

Structure.

  • v. 1: slaves of unbelieving masters: honour them, so that God's name and the teaching are not blasphemed.
  • v. 2a: slaves of believing masters: do not despise them because they are brothers; serve them all the more.
  • v. 2b: “Teach and urge these duties.”

Key words.

  • blasphēmeō, to blaspheme, speak evil of: The concern is mission: outsiders judge the Gospel by the conduct of Christians.
  • adelphoi, brothers: Master and slave are brothers in the Church. This word, more than any law, began to change the relationship from within.

Verse by verse.

“Let all who are under the yoke of slavery regard their masters as worthy of all honor, so that the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed.” (6:1)
“rather they must serve them all the more, since those who benefit by their service are believers and beloved.” (6:2)

Belonging to the same Church does not cancel daily duties; it deepens them. Faith shows itself first at work.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses as a lesson for all work: to serve honestly and well, as for the Lord, is a witness to the Gospel. Human work can be a means of sanctification (CCC 2427), while every form of enslaving human beings is firmly condemned (CCC 2414).

Matthew 20:1–16 · The laborers in the vineyard

Setting.

The parable is found only in Matthew. It follows Peter's question, “Look, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?” (19:27), and Jesus' saying that many who are first will be last (19:30). The parable ends with the same saying turned around (20:16).

A denarius was the usual daily wage of a laborer. Day laborers waited in the marketplace to be hired, and the Law required that they be paid each day before sunset (Deuteronomy 24:15). The day was counted from about six in the morning, so the third, sixth, ninth and eleventh hours become, in the NRSV, nine o'clock, noon, three and five.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–7: the owner goes out five times to hire laborers.
  • vv. 8–10: payment begins with the last; all receive a denarius.
  • vv. 11–12: the first grumble.
  • vv. 13–15: the owner's answer.
  • v. 16: the last will be first.

Key words.

  • hetaire, friend: A polite but distant form of address. Matthew uses it three times: here, to the guest without a wedding robe (22:12), and to Judas in the garden (26:50).
  • ophthalmos ponēros, an evil eye: “Are you envious because I am generous?” is literally “Is your eye evil because I am good?” The evil eye is the jealous look that cannot bear another's good fortune.

Verse by verse.

“When those hired about five o’clock came, each of them received the usual daily wage.” (20:9)

The owner begins with the last, so that the first will see. The parable wants us to feel the shock.

“Friend, I am doing you no wrong; did you not agree with me for the usual daily wage?” (20:13)

Justice is kept, and generosity goes beyond it. No one is wronged by grace given to another.

“Or are you envious because I am generous?” (20:15)

How the Church reads it.

Many Fathers read the hours of the day as the ages of salvation history from Adam to Christ, and also as the stages of a human life, so that no one is called too late; so Origen and St Gregory the Great (in substance). The Catechism teaches that before God we have no strict right to merit, and that our merits are themselves gifts of his grace (CCC 2007, 2009).

Questions people ask

Is the owner unfair to the first workers?

No. They receive what was agreed. The parable is not a rule for wages but a picture of grace: eternal life is not earned by hours, but given by the Father's goodness, to the first and to the last.

For study and teaching

  • Do I serve better when people are watching? What would it mean to work “so that the name of God may not be blasphemed”?
  • When does God's generosity to others make me grumble?
