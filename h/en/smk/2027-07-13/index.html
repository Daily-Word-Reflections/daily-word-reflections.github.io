Word for the day

“If we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord; so then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's.” Romans 14:8

Reflection

The Epistle gives us one of the shortest and fullest summaries of the Christian life: “We do not live to ourselves, and we do not die to ourselves. If we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord; so then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's.” Paul is writing to a church in which believers were judging one another over food and holy days. He answers not with a rule but with a belonging: we are the Lord's, and so is the brother or sister whom we are tempted to despise. “For to this end Christ died and lived again, so that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living. Why do you pass judgment on your brother or sister? Or you, why do you despise your brother or sister? For we will all stand before the judgment seat of God.” Then he quotes the prophet, “As I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall give praise to God,” and concludes, “So then, each of us will be accountable to God.” The Church teaches that each person comes before the Lord at the moment of death and that our life is the time given for accepting his grace (Catechism 1021–1022, in substance). So today's reading is not a threat but a freedom: if we belong to the Lord, we need not be anxious about the judgments of others, and we have no right to pass them on others.

The Gospel shows how the Lord himself looks at people. “Then Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and proclaiming the good news of the kingdom, and curing every disease and every sickness. When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” St John Chrysostom, speaking of this verse, notes that the Lord is moved not by the crowd's strength but by its need, and that his compassion is the mark of the true shepherd (Homilies on Matthew, in substance). Then comes the saying that has sent so many to their vocation: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” St Gregory the Great, preaching on the same words in Luke's Gospel, said that we cannot speak of them without sorrow, for there are many who would listen to good things, but few to speak them (Homilies on the Gospels 17, in substance). The remedy Jesus gives is first of all prayer: ask the Lord of the harvest. And then, in the next chapter, the disciples who prayed are the ones sent out.

The Panchangam today remembers Mar Sheelas, St Silas, one of those laborers. We meet him in the Acts of the Apostles when the Church of Jerusalem resolved, after the first great council, to send two men to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas: “Judas called Barsabbas, and Silas, leaders among the brothers.” They carried the letter that welcomed the Gentiles without burdening them, and they “were themselves prophets, said much to encourage and strengthen the believers.” After the quarrel between Paul and Barnabas, “Paul chose Silas and set out, the believers commending him to the grace of the Lord.” At Philippi the two were beaten and thrown into prison, though both were Roman citizens, and at midnight, with their feet fastened in the stocks, “Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them.” That night the jailer asked, “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?”, and they answered, “Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household.” He is usually identified with Silvanus, the form of the name found in the letters: Paul writes with “Silvanus and Timothy” to Thessalonica, and Peter says, “Through Silvanus, whom I consider a faithful brother, I have written this short letter to encourage you.” He served both Paul and Peter and carried the Church's peace from Jerusalem to the nations. Little else is known for certain, and later traditions about his end are not firm. Today we also remember Most Rev. Antony Mar Silvanos on his name day, and we pray that he may be a faithful brother and a servant of the Church's unity. Lord of the harvest, send out laborers.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul gives us in a few words the whole of the Christian life: We do not live to ourselves, and we do not die to ourselves. If we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord; so then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's (Romans 14:7–8). Everything that follows grows from this belonging. If we are the Lord's, then so is our brother, and so is our sister. That is why he asks: Why do you pass judgment on your brother or sister? (Romans 14:10).

The Gospel shows how the Lord looks at people. When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36). And then he says to his disciples: The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest (Matthew 9:37–38). Notice: he does not first say, go; he says, pray. And those who pray are then the ones he sends.

Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Sheelas, St Silas. He was one of those laborers. The Church at Jerusalem sent him to Antioch with a letter of peace, and he strengthened the believers there. Then he went with Paul, was beaten and put in prison at Philippi, and at midnight he was singing hymns to God (Acts 16:25). Later he served Peter too. He carried the Church's peace and he sang in prison. We pray today also for Most Rev. Antony Mar Silvanos on his name day.

So let us live this day as the Lord's. Let us not judge. Let us pray for laborers for the harvest, and let us ask what work he is asking of us. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember St Silas, who was a helper of St Paul. Once Silas and Paul were put in prison for talking about Jesus. At midnight, in the dark, they did not cry; they sang hymns to God! And the other prisoners listened. Then the doors of the prison opened and the jailer and his family became Christians. Jesus says that many people are waiting to hear about him, and he needs helpers. We can be his helpers too, by being kind, by praying, and by singing and speaking about Jesus. This week, sing a song of praise at home, and ask Jesus to send more helpers to his work.

Prayer

Lord of the harvest, we are yours in life and in death, and so are all our brothers and sisters. Keep us from judging and despising one another, and give us the compassion with which you looked at the crowds. Through the prayers of Mar Sheelas, who carried the Church's peace and sang your praise in prison, send out laborers into your harvest, and make us ready to be among them. Bless Most Rev. Antony Mar Silvanos on his name day and all our bishops, that they may be faithful brothers and servants of unity. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today our calendar remembers Mar Sheelas, the Silas of the Acts of the Apostles, a trusted companion of Paul. The two readings fit his memory well. In the Epistle Paul says that no Christian lives or dies for himself or herself alone: “whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord’s.” In the Gospel Jesus looks at the crowds with compassion, “like sheep without a shepherd”, and asks his disciples to pray for labourers. Silas was one of those labourers, and he was answered in his own life. The key words are belonging and harvest.

Romans 14:7–12 · We are the Lord’s

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome from Corinth, around AD 57. In chapters 14 and 15 he speaks to a quarrel in the Roman community. Some believers (Paul calls them “the strong”) felt at liberty to eat anything and did not keep special days; others (“the weak”) ate only vegetables and kept certain days as holy (14:1–6). Each side was tempted to look down on the other. Paul does not say who is right on every point. He asks both sides to remember who they belong to.

Our verses give the reason. Whatever we do, we do for the Lord, because he is Lord of the living and the dead.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: no one lives or dies for himself or herself; living or dying, we are the Lord’s.
  • v. 9: this is why Christ died and lived again: to be Lord of the dead and the living.
  • v. 10: so why judge or despise a brother or sister? All will stand before God.
  • vv. 11–12: a word of Scripture: every knee will bow and every tongue give praise to God; each of us will be accountable to God.
  • ¶ Paul moves from belonging, to the Lord’s death and resurrection, to the call for gentleness among believers.

Key words.

  • kyrios, Lord: Paul uses this word again and again in these verses. In verse 11 he quotes Isaiah 45:23, where the speaker is the Lord, the God of Israel, and he applies the promise to Christ, as he does in Philippians 2:10–11.
  • krinō, to judge, to pass judgment: The main verb of chapter 14 (see vv. 3, 4, 5, 10, 13). Paul does not forbid every kind of judging; he forbids despising a brother or sister over matters on which believers may differ in good conscience.
  • bēma, judgment seat: A raised platform from which a magistrate gave his verdict. Paul himself was brought before the bēma of the governor Gallio at Corinth (Acts 18:12–17), the city from which he writes.

Verse by verse.

“We do not live to ourselves, and we do not die to ourselves.” (14:7)

The Christian is not a private owner of his or her life. The sentence is not a burden but a comfort: our life and our death both have a meaning outside ourselves.

“whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord’s.” (14:8)

This is one of the shortest summaries of Christian life in the New Testament. Nothing, not even death, takes us out of his hands.

“For to this end Christ died and lived again, so that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living.” (14:9)

The cross and the resurrection are the ground of the Lordship of Christ. Because he has passed through death, no one who has died is beyond his care.

“For we will all stand before the judgment seat of God.” (14:10)

Some later manuscripts read “of Christ” here, borrowing the words of 2 Corinthians 5:10. The NRSV and the POC follow the oldest reading, “of God”. The meaning is the same in the end, since the Father judges through the Son (John 5:22).

“every knee shall bow to me” (14:11)

Paul takes the words from Isaiah 45:23, which the Greek Old Testament renders with “every tongue shall confess”. The prophet pictures the nations turning to the one God; Paul reminds us that this same God is the judge of all, so no believer is the judge of another.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses verse 9 when it speaks of the Lordship of Christ: Christ died and rose “that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living” (Romans 14:9), and so his Lordship reaches the whole of history and the world of the dead (CCC 668). The Church also teaches that full right to pass final judgment on the works and hearts of men belongs to Christ as Redeemer of the world (CCC 679). That is why Paul’s lesson is humble: the judge is already chosen, and it is not we. In the Roman funeral lectionary these verses are among the readings offered, because they tell the mourning that the dead are still “the Lord’s”. We pray in the Holy Qurbono for the living and the dead for the same reason: they belong to him, and we belong to one another in him.

Matthew 9:35–38 · The harvest is plentiful

Setting.

These verses close the first great section of Matthew’s Gospel. In chapters 5–7 Jesus teaches (the Sermon on the Mount); in chapters 8–9 he heals and forgives. Matthew now sums up both in a single sentence, almost word for word like the summary in 4:23, so that the two chapters are enclosed between matching verses: “teaching … proclaiming … curing”.

Then he lets us see Jesus’ heart. The crowds are not a problem to be managed; they are scattered people to be gathered. The next verse, 10:1, begins the sending of the Twelve, so the prayer of 9:38 is answered at once by the people who are asked to pray.

Structure.

  • v. 35: Jesus in all the towns and villages: teaching, proclaiming, healing.
  • v. 36: his compassion for the crowds, harassed and helpless like sheep without a shepherd.
  • vv. 37–38: the harvest is great, the labourers few: pray to the Lord of the harvest.

Key words.

  • splanchnizomai, to have compassion: From splanchna, the inner organs, the “gut” where deep feeling is felt. The word is used in the Gospels almost only of Jesus, or of figures in his parables who show God’s mercy. It is a love that moves a person to act.
  • poimēn, shepherd: In the Old Testament the Lord is the shepherd of Israel (Psalm 23; Ezekiel 34), and the leaders who fail the people are blamed as bad shepherds. Jesus sees the people as the flock that God himself promised to gather.
  • ekbalē, send out: The verb literally means “to cast out, to thrust out”, and it is the same one used for driving out demons. The strong verb stresses how urgent the harvest is.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and proclaiming the good news of the kingdom, and curing every disease and every sickness.” (9:35)

Jesus goes to the people where they are. His teaching and his healing belong together: word and deed show the same kingdom.

“When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” (9:36)

The picture comes from Numbers 27:17, where Moses asks that the community not be “like sheep without a shepherd”, and God gives him Joshua, whose name in Hebrew is the same as Jesus. Jesus is the shepherd for whom Moses prayed.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few;” (9:37)

Jesus does not say that the field is poor. The need is large, and God’s work is ready; what is lacking is the hands.

“therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” (9:38)

The harvest belongs to God, and so does the sending. Prayer is the first work of everyone who cares for the Church’s mission. And those who pray find that they are sometimes the answer.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always taken verse 38 as the command to pray for vocations: priests, deacons, monks and nuns, catechists and missionaries, and the faithful who serve in the world. The Catechism teaches that all the baptized share in Christ’s mission as priest, prophet and king (CCC 783–786), so “laborers” include every disciple, each in his or her own way. Mar Sheelas, whom we remember today, shows what this means. He was one of the leaders chosen by the apostles and elders at Jerusalem to carry their letter to Antioch (Acts 15:22), and he went with Paul on his second journey. At Philippi, in prison with Paul at midnight, he was heard “praying and singing hymns to God” (Acts 16:25). He prayed for the harvest, and then he went into it.

Questions people ask

Paul says “do not judge”. Does that mean Christians may never say that something is wrong?

No. In the same letter Paul speaks plainly of sin, and in 1 Corinthians 5–6 he asks the community to correct grave wrongdoing. What he rules out here is contempt: looking down on a brother or sister over matters on which believers may differ in good conscience, such as foods or customs. The final judgment of a person’s heart belongs to God alone (CCC 679). To tell the truth about good and evil, with love, is not the same as despising a person.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that we are the Lord’s in life and in death. How would this change one worry that I carry today?
  • Jesus sees the crowd and feels compassion before he gives any instruction. How can I look at the people around me, at home and in the parish, in the same way? Whom shall I pray for by name today, as a labourer for the harvest?
