Word for the day

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”; and she told them that he had said these things to her.” John 20:18

Reflection

Who was Mary Magdalene? The Gospels tell us less than we would like, and what they tell us is enough. She came from Magdala, a town on the Sea of Galilee. Luke names her among the women who followed Jesus and “provided for them out of their resources”: “Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out.” She was a woman whom the Lord had delivered, and she did not go away afterwards. She was standing “near the cross of Jesus” with his mother and the other women; she saw where he was buried; and on the first day of the week she is the first to come to the tomb. We should say plainly what the Gospels do not say: they never say that she is the sinful woman of Luke chapter 7 or Mary of Bethany. An older tradition of the Western Church joined these women together, but the Church today keeps them distinct, and we read her as the Gospel gives her to us: a woman healed by the Lord, a faithful friend at the cross, and the first witness of the resurrection. Today's Epistle is the best commentary on her life: “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!”

Now follow her through the Gospel. “Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb and saw that the stone had been removed.” In her first fear she runs to Peter and the beloved disciple, and they run to the tomb, see the linen wrappings, and return to their homes. But “Mary stood weeping outside the tomb.” The disciples have gone home, and she stays. She bends and looks in, and sees two angels in white who ask: “Woman, why are you weeping?” She answers: “They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him.” Then she turns and sees Jesus standing there, “but she did not know that it was Jesus.” He asks: “Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you looking for?” Supposing him to be the gardener, she says: “Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.” She speaks as a woman who has only one wish: to give the body of the Lord an honourable place.

Then comes the shortest and greatest sentence of the Gospel: “Jesus said to her, ‘Mary!’ She turned and said to him in Hebrew, ‘Rabbouni!’ (which means Teacher).” The risen Lord is not first known by sight or by argument. He is known when he calls her by name, as the Good Shepherd calls his own sheep by name. And then he gives her a mission: “Do not hold on to me, because I have not yet ascended to the Father. But go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.’” She must not cling to the Lord as he was; a new relationship is beginning, in which he will be with his Church by the Spirit. And look at the words: “my brothers.” They were the men who fled, and he calls them brothers. “Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, ‘I have seen the Lord’; and she told them that he had said these things to her.” This is why the Church has called her the apostle of the apostles, the one who was sent to the Apostles.

Paul's words in the Epistle describe what she became: “All this is from God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ, and has given us the ministry of reconciliation; that is, in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting the message of reconciliation to us. So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us.” Mary Magdalene is the first ambassador of the risen Christ. She is sent to men who had abandoned the Lord, with a message of forgiveness: my brothers, my Father and your Father. In our tradition the morning prayer, Sapro, is the prayer of daybreak that remembers the resurrection, and in the dark of the monsoon mornings many Christians rise early to pray, as she came to the tomb while it was still dark. Let us ask her intercession: for the grace to stay when others go home, to seek the Lord with love even through tears, to listen for him to call us by name, and to go and tell others, with joy, “I have seen the Lord.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today we remember St Mary Magdalene. The Gospels tell us that she was a woman from Magdala whom Jesus had delivered, that she stood near his cross, and that she came to the tomb early on the first day of the week while it was still dark (John 20:1). She is not the sinful woman of Luke chapter 7; the Gospel does not say so, and we read her as the Gospel gives her. She was a faithful friend.

Peter and the other disciple saw the empty tomb and went home. But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb (John 20:11). She stayed and kept looking. Two angels asked her why she was weeping, and then Jesus himself asked her, and she thought he was the gardener. Then Jesus said her name: Mary! She turned and said, Rabbouni! (John 20:16). The Lord knows his own sheep by name.

Jesus said: Do not hold on to me, but go to my brothers and say to them, I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God. And Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, I have seen the Lord (John 20:17–18). She was the first to announce the resurrection, and so the Church calls her the apostle of the apostles. St Paul says: God has given us the ministry of reconciliation, and we are ambassadors for Christ (2 Corinthians 5:18, 20).

Let us ask St Mary Magdalene to teach us to stay and seek the Lord with love, to listen as he calls us by name, and to go and tell others, with joy, that we have seen the Lord. Amen.

For children

Children, imagine you are very sad because you cannot find someone you love. Mary Magdalene was like that on the first Easter morning. She went to the tomb while it was still dark, and she cried. Then someone said, Mary! And she knew at once it was Jesus, because he said her name in his special way. He was alive! Jesus knows your name, too. And like Mary, we can run to tell our friends: Jesus is alive, I have seen the Lord!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you called Mary Magdalene by name in the garden and sent her to your brothers with the first word of the resurrection. We thank you for her faithfulness at the cross, her tears at the tomb and her joy in announcing that she had seen the Lord. Make us new creations in you. When we cannot find you, give us the love that stays and seeks. Call us by name, and when we hear your voice, send us as ambassadors of your reconciliation. Through the prayers of St Mary Magdalene, apostle of the apostles, strengthen the faith of your Church and lead us to see you, face to face, in the light of your kingdom. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers St Mary Magdalene, and both readings fit her well. Paul says that anyone in Christ is a new creation and that God has given us the ministry of reconciliation, to be ambassadors for Christ. In the Gospel, Mary stands weeping at the empty tomb, is called by name by the risen Lord, and is sent to tell the disciples: “I have seen the Lord.” The key words are new creation, reconciliation and sent.

2 Corinthians 5:17–21 · A new creation and a ministry of reconciliation

Setting.

Second Corinthians is Paul's most personal letter. He has been defending his ministry against those in Corinth who doubted him, and in chapter 5 he explains what drives him: “the love of Christ urges us on” (5:14). Christ died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves but for him who died and was raised (5:15). From that moment, Paul says, he no longer looks at people “from a human point of view” (5:16).

Our verses are the heart of that explanation. Paul speaks of what God has done, which he calls reconciliation, and then of what God asks of those who have received it.

Structure.

  • v. 17: anyone in Christ is a new creation.
  • v. 18: all this is from God, who reconciled us through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation.
  • v. 19: in Christ God was reconciling the world, not counting trespasses, and entrusted the message to us.
  • v. 20: we are ambassadors for Christ; God appeals through us: be reconciled.
  • v. 21: God made the sinless one to be sin for us, so that we might become the righteousness of God.

Key words.

  • kainē ktisis, new creation: The words echo Isaiah 43:18–19 and 65:17, where God promises to do a new thing and to create new heavens and a new earth. In Christ that creation has already begun in each baptized person (Galatians 6:15).
  • katallagē, reconciliation: Restoring a relationship that was broken. Paul uses it only of what God does: God reconciles us. We do not first reconcile God; God's love comes first.
  • presbeuomen, we are ambassadors: The word used for an envoy who speaks in the name of a king. An ambassador does not speak for himself. When the apostles speak, God himself is making his appeal.

Verse by verse.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (5:17)

Being in Christ, which Baptism makes real, is not a small improvement. The old life with its slavery to sin has ended, and a new life begins that will be complete only at the resurrection.

“in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting the message of reconciliation to us.” (5:19)

God does not wait until we repent before he begins to love us. In Christ he comes to us first, and then he asks us to carry the message. The message is a gift entrusted to people, not an idea they own.

“So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us;” (5:20)

An ambassador lives in a foreign land for the sake of a home country. Christians are sent into the world with the authority of the one who sends them.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (5:21)

Christ was without sin and was treated as sin for us. Many interpreters hear a reference to the sin-offering of the Law, in which the Greek word for “sin” can also mean the offering for sin. The exchange is the Gospel in a sentence: he takes our place, and we are made righteous in him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 21 when it teaches that God made Christ “to be sin” for us, to reconcile us to himself, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God (CCC 602). It also teaches that Christ entrusted the ministry of reconciliation to the apostles, and that in the Church this ministry is exercised in the sacrament of forgiveness (CCC 1442). Paul's word “ambassadors” is a reminder that priests who forgive sins in the name of Christ are not acting by their own right, and that all the baptized share in this task when they forgive and seek peace with their neighbours.

John 20:1–2, 11–18 · I have seen the Lord

Setting.

John tells the discovery of the empty tomb in 20:1–10: Mary comes early on the first day of the week, finds the stone rolled away and runs to Peter and the Beloved Disciple. They run to the tomb, see the grave clothes and believe, but leave. Our reading joins 20:1–2 to 20:11–18, which tells what happened to Mary after they had gone. The Gospel puts her alone with the Risen Lord, and the scene is set in a garden (19:41), recalling the first garden of Genesis.

Mary Magdalene, that is, Mary from Magdala on the Sea of Galilee, is named in all four Gospels. Luke says that seven demons had gone out of her (Luke 8:2) and that she followed Jesus and helped to provide for him and his disciples out of her own resources. She stood by the Cross (John 19:25) and watched the burial (Mark 15:47). She is not the sinful woman of Luke 7:36–50, and she is not Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus. The Gospels never say so. Both identifications were made in the West by later preachers, and the Church does not teach them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Mary comes while it is dark, finds the stone removed and tells Peter and the other disciple.
  • vv. 11–13: Mary weeps at the tomb; two angels ask why she weeps.
  • vv. 14–15: Jesus stands there; she thinks he is the gardener.
  • v. 16: he calls her name: “Mary!” and she answers “Rabbouni!”
  • vv. 17–18: he sends her to his brothers; she announces: “I have seen the Lord.”

Key words.

  • Mariam, Mary: Jesus says just one word and she knows him. He had said, “the sheep hear his voice. He calls his own sheep by name” (10:3). The Risen Lord calls his own by name.
  • rhabbouni, my teacher: An Aramaic word, a little more personal than rabbi. John translates it as “Teacher”. Mary answers with the old relationship, and Jesus must teach her that things are now different.
  • mē mou haptou, do not hold on to me: Literally “stop clinging to me”. Mary is told not to hold the old way of being with him, because he is going to the Father; a new and deeper presence will be given through the Spirit.

Verse by verse.

“Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb and saw that the stone had been removed from the tomb.” (20:1)

“The first day of the week” is the day of the new creation; “still dark” is Mary's grief and also the darkness that the Light is about to end. Her love brought her before anyone else.

“Jesus said to her, “Mary!” She turned and said to him in Hebrew, “Rabbouni!” (which means Teacher).” (20:16)

The whole Gospel turns on this moment. She had looked at him and not known him; she knows him when he speaks her name. Faith is a personal meeting.

“Do not hold on to me, because I have not yet ascended to the Father.” (20:17)

Jesus is not refusing love. He is saying that the time of holding him in the old way is over, and the next stage begins: his return to the Father and the gift of the Spirit.

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”;” (20:18)

The first witness of the Resurrection is a woman, and her message is the whole Easter faith in five words: “I have seen the Lord.” The Lord's “brothers” are the disciples, now called brothers because the Father of Jesus is their Father too (20:17).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Mary Magdalene and the holy women were the first to meet the Risen Lord, and thus the first messengers of the Resurrection to the apostles themselves (CCC 641). Hippolytus of Rome called the women at the tomb apostles to the apostles (in substance), and St Thomas Aquinas, commenting on this passage, calls Mary Magdalene “apostle of the apostles” (in substance). In 2016 the Roman Church raised her memorial to the rank of a feast, in recognition of that title. The Panchangam keeps her memory today with this Gospel of her meeting with the Risen Lord.

Questions people ask

Was Mary Magdalene the sinful woman who washed Jesus' feet?

The Gospels do not say so. Luke 7:36–50 tells of an unnamed sinful woman; Mary Magdalene is introduced only in Luke 8:2, and no Gospel connects the two. A homily by Pope Gregory the Great in the sixth century joined them, with Mary of Bethany, and the Western tradition followed him for centuries. Most scholars today keep them apart, and so does the Church's present liturgy. What the Gospels tell us about Mary Magdalene is enough: she was healed by Jesus, followed him, stood at the Cross and was the first to see the Risen Lord.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Every Sunday the Church of the Syriac tradition proclaims the Resurrection of the Lord, and the first person who did so was a woman, Mary Magdalene. She reminds the preacher and the faithful that what the Church has to say is not an idea but an encounter: “I have seen the Lord.” In the Holy Qurbono the faithful receive the peace of the Risen One, who said to his disciples, “Peace be with you” (John 20:19), and who calls each of us by name.

For study and teaching

  • The Lord called Mary by her name, and she knew him. When have I heard the Lord speaking to me personally, in Scripture, in prayer or in someone's words?
  • Paul says we are ambassadors for Christ. Whom am I sent to, in my family or at work, with a message of reconciliation?
