Word for the day

“Why does this generation ask for a sign? Truly I tell you, no sign will be given to this generation.” Mark 8:12

Reflection

Today's Epistle is among the hardest passages in all of Paul, and it helps to remember why he wrote it. Some in Thessalonica had been shaken by a rumour that the day of the Lord had already come, and Paul writes to steady them. He speaks of “the mystery of lawlessness”: “For the mystery of lawlessness is already at work, but only until the one who now restrains it is removed. And then the lawless one will be revealed, whom the Lord Jesus will destroy with the breath of his mouth, annihilating him by the manifestation of his coming.” Christians have read this passage in many ways over the centuries, and the Church has never turned it into a timetable. What she takes from it is plain. Evil is real and works through deception, with “signs” and “wonders” that are false. But the Lord Jesus needs no great army to overcome it: the breath of his mouth is enough. And the reason why some are lost is not that God hides the truth but that they “refused to love the truth and so be saved.” The danger Paul names is a heart that has stopped loving what is true.

That is exactly the heart we meet in the Gospel. The Pharisees came and began to argue with Jesus, “asking him for a sign from heaven, to test him.” Only a few verses earlier, Mark has told us that Jesus fed four thousand people with seven loaves, with baskets left over. The signs were there. What they wanted was not a sign but a proof on their own terms, so that they could stay in charge of the question. Mark tells us something we do not often hear about the Lord: “And he sighed deeply in his spirit and said, ‘Why does this generation ask for a sign? Truly I tell you, no sign will be given to this generation.’” It is not anger; it is the deep sigh of one who loves and is grieved by a closed door. Then he does a quiet and painful thing: “And he left them, and getting into the boat again, he went across to the other side.” The Lord does not force himself on those who test him. In the other Gospels he adds that the one sign given will be the sign of Jonah, his own death and rising. In the Holy Qurbono that sign is given to us again every Sunday.

On this day the Panchangam remembers Morth Makrina, whom the Church calls Macrina the Younger. Her brother, St Gregory of Nyssa, wrote her Life soon after she died in the year 379, and he tells us how a young woman of a Christian family in Cappadocia became the teacher of her whole family. After the death of her fiancé she chose to remain unmarried, and with her mother, Emmelia, she turned the family estate on the river Iris into a community in which women and men lived by prayer, psalm-singing and work, sharing their goods with the poor. She helped her brother Basil, who had come home proud of his learning in the schools, to turn towards a life with Christ; she raised her youngest brother, Peter, who became bishop of Sebaste; and Gregory, bishop of Nyssa, calls her his teacher. When he visited her in her last illness, he found her lying on a plank on the ground, and he heard her speak so clearly about the soul and the resurrection that he later wrote their conversation as a book. She died after a prayer of thanksgiving, signing herself with the cross.

Macrina never asked for a sign, and she never performed one that we would call a show. Her life was the sign: a quiet, steady, hidden holiness in which the whole family came to see Christ. That is what the Lord means when he refuses a sign to the testing crowd and offers himself to those who love the truth. In our own time many look for a sign in every new claim and every dramatic report of a vision, and forget the ordinary signs God has already given: the Gospel, the Holy Qurbono, the forgiveness of sins, and the faithful lives of people like Macrina. Let us ask for what she had: a heart that loves the truth and does not need to test the Lord, and a faith that shows itself in sharing the household's bread with the poor. As the monsoon rain wakes the fields each year without any announcement, so God's grace works quietly in a hidden life.

Homily outline

Theme: “Why does this generation ask for a sign?”

On Monday of the ninth week after Pentecost: the mystery of lawlessness, the love of the truth, the sign refused, and Morth Makrina.

1. The breath of the Lord's mouth

  • The mystery of lawlessness is at work.
  • False signs and wonders deceive.
  • The Lord Jesus overcomes without an army.
“with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked.” Isaiah 11:4
“But the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one.” 2 Thessalonians 3:3

Apply: When you feel afraid of the evil around you, say the name of Jesus and trust his faithfulness.

2. Love of the truth

  • The danger is a heart that does not love what is true.
  • God hides nothing from those who seek.
  • Truth is a person, Jesus.
“Lead me in your truth, and teach me, for you are the God of my salvation; for you I wait all day long.” Psalm 25:5
“Jesus said to him, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” John 14:6

Apply: Take up one question of faith you have put off and read the Gospel on it.

3. The sign refused

  • The Pharisees test him.
  • The deep sigh of the Lord.
  • The only sign: the sign of Jonah.
“do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness, when your ancestors tested me, and put me to the proof, though they had seen my work.” Psalm 95:8–9
“Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion.” Hebrews 3:15

Apply: Do what you already know is right without asking God for proof first.

4. Macrina, the quiet sign

  • Teacher of her brothers.
  • A home of prayer, psalms and charity.
  • Her life was the sign.
“She opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of kindness is on her tongue.” Proverbs 31:26
“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” Matthew 5:16

Apply: Do one hidden good deed at home this week, without comment.

The Lord gives himself to those who love the truth, and gives no show to those who only test.

A quiet life of prayer and charity, like Macrina's, is a sign that God is near.

Take-home resolution: Love the truth, stop testing the Lord, and shine quietly in good works.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel the Pharisees ask Jesus for a sign from heaven, to test him. And Mark tells us that Jesus sighed deeply in his spirit and said: Why does this generation ask for a sign? Truly I tell you, no sign will be given to this generation (Mark 8:12). Then he left them. He had fed four thousand people with seven loaves only a few verses before. The signs were there. The problem was not a lack of signs but a lack of love for the truth.

That is also the warning in the Epistle. Paul says that the mystery of lawlessness is already at work, with false signs and wonders and deceptions for those who are perishing, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved (2 Thessalonians 2:10). He does not tell us to fear. The Lord Jesus will destroy the lawless one with the breath of his mouth (2 Thessalonians 2:8). What the Lord wants from us is a heart that loves what is true.

Today the Church remembers Morth Makrina, the sister of St Basil and St Gregory of Nyssa. Gregory wrote her Life, and he calls her his teacher. She turned her family's home into a place of prayer, psalms and charity, and she never asked for a sign. Her whole life was a sign. Let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven (Matthew 5:16).

Let us ask the Lord for a heart like hers: honest, quiet and faithful. And let us not look for wonders when the Lord has already given us the Gospel and the Holy Qurbono. Amen.

For children

Children, imagine your friend says, I will only believe you love me if you do something amazing. That would make you sad, wouldn't it? That is how Jesus felt when some people asked him for a special sign. He had already done so many good things! Today we remember Morth Makrina. She showed Jesus to her brothers just by praying, teaching and sharing. You can also show Jesus to your family, just by being kind and honest every day.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sighed in your spirit over those who asked for a sign, and you give yourself to those who love the truth. Keep us from the deception of false wonders, and guard us from the evil one. Give us a heart that loves what is true and does not set conditions for you. Through the prayers of Morth Makrina, who taught her whole family by a hidden life of prayer, psalms and charity, make our homes places where your light is seen, and make us ready for the day of your coming. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the difference between faith and a demand for proof. In 2 Thessalonians, Paul says that evil is already at work, and that those who are lost are lost because they “refused to love the truth”. In Mark, the Pharisees ask Jesus for a sign from heaven, and he sighs and refuses. Today the Panchangam also remembers Morth Makrina (St Macrina the Younger, who died in 379), sister of St Basil the Great and St Gregory of Nyssa. The key words are truth, sign and deception.

2 Thessalonians 2:7–12 · Refusing to love the truth

Setting.

Some in Thessalonica had been shaken by a report, perhaps a letter claimed to come from Paul, that “the day of the Lord is already here” (2:2). The letter answers that certain things must happen first: the rebellion, and the revealing of “the lawless one” (2:3). Many scholars think 2 Thessalonians was written by a disciple in Paul's name, because it is so close to 1 Thessalonians and yet differs in its picture of the end; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture in either case.

Our verses are among the hardest in the New Testament. Paul had told the Thessalonians more in person (2:5), so we are hearing half of a conversation. “The one who now restrains” the lawless one is not named. Early Christian writers made different guesses, often the Roman order of law and government; modern commentators have proposed others. The Church has never defined it, and we should not pretend to know.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the mystery of lawlessness is already at work, held back for now.
  • v. 8: the lawless one will be revealed, and the Lord Jesus will destroy him by the breath of his mouth.
  • vv. 9–10: his coming is by Satan's working, with lying signs and every wicked deception, for those who are perishing.
  • vv. 11–12: the reason: they refused to love the truth, and so a powerful delusion leads them on to condemnation.

Key words.

  • anomia, lawlessness: Literally “being without law”. In the Bible it is not only breaking a rule; it is a rebellion against God's order. Jesus uses the same word in Matthew 24:12, where lawlessness makes love grow cold.
  • apatē, deception: A trick, an enticing lie. The evil Paul describes does not usually look like evil. It comes with signs and wonders, and it is believed by people who have stopped wanting the truth.
  • agapē tēs alētheias, love of the truth: Not only agreeing with the truth but loving it, being drawn to it. The danger Paul names is not that people lack information. It is that they refuse to love what is true.

Verse by verse.

“For the mystery of lawlessness is already at work,” (2:7)

“Mystery” here means something hidden that is being worked out in history, as the mystery of God's plan is. Evil is not only a future event. It is already present, and Christians must not be surprised by it.

“whom the Lord Jesus will destroy with the breath of his mouth, annihilating him by the manifestation of his coming.” (2:8)

The words recall Isaiah 11:4: “with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked”. The Lord does not need an army. His coming is enough, as light is enough to end darkness.

“because they refused to love the truth and so be saved.” (2:10)

Salvation here is tied to loving the truth. The Church teaches that faith is the willing response of the whole person, and that no one can be forced to believe (CCC 160).

“For this reason God sends them a powerful delusion, leading them to believe what is false,” (2:11)

Hebrew thought often says that God does what he allows to happen. The Church teaches that God is in no way the cause of moral evil, but permits it out of respect for freedom, and brings good out of it (CCC 311). The delusion follows a choice already made: first they refused the truth, and only then were they handed over to what they had chosen.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this passage when it speaks of the final trial that will shake the faith of many believers. It teaches that the Church will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this last Passover, and that the supreme religious deception is that of the Antichrist, a pseudo-messianism in which man glorifies himself in place of God (CCC 675–677). The Church has never named a person, a date or a nation as the lawless one, and she warns against every attempt to do so. The Christian answer is not fear but watchfulness: to love the truth, to hold to what we have been taught (2 Thessalonians 2:15), and to trust that the final word belongs to the Lord Jesus.

Mark 8:11–13 · No sign will be given

Setting.

Mark has just told the feeding of the four thousand (8:1–9), and Jesus has landed at Dalmanutha (8:10). At once the Pharisees come to argue and ask for a sign from heaven. They had seen healings and the multiplication of bread; they wanted something beyond all that, a proof from the sky that would settle the question. Not every Pharisee was hostile to Jesus (compare Luke 13:31); Mark speaks here of those who came to argue with him.

After our verses, Jesus gets into the boat and speaks to his own disciples about “the yeast of the Pharisees” (8:14–21). Right after this scene Mark places the healing of the blind man at Bethsaida (8:22–26), then Peter's confession (8:27–30): sight and blindness run through the whole section.

Structure.

  • v. 11: the Pharisees ask him for a sign from heaven, to test him.
  • v. 12: Jesus sighs deeply and refuses: no sign will be given to this generation.
  • v. 13: he leaves them and crosses to the other side.

Key words.

  • peirazontes, testing: The same word is used of Satan's temptations in the wilderness (1:13). A demand for a sign meant to test God is a way of putting God on trial.
  • anastenaxas, sighed deeply: A strong word for a groan that rises from the depth of the heart. Mark often shows the feelings of Jesus: here sorrow, not anger.
  • genea, generation: In the Old Testament a faithless “generation” is the people who tested God in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 32:5; Psalm 95:10). Jesus is not condemning a nation but pointing to a way of resisting him that any generation can share.

Verse by verse.

“The Pharisees came and began to argue with him, asking him for a sign from heaven, to test him.” (8:11)

They already had signs in plenty. What they asked for was a sign made to their own measure. Faith that sets its own conditions is not yet faith.

“And he sighed deeply in his spirit and said, “Why does this generation ask for a sign?” (8:12)

The question is a real one, asked with sadness. Jesus grieves over hearts that will not open, as he wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41).

“Truly I tell you, no sign will be given to this generation.” (8:12)

This is the form of an oath, a flat refusal. Matthew adds “except the sign of Jonah” (Matthew 16:4), pointing to the Resurrection. Mark leaves the refusal bare, so that the reader will think of the whole Gospel as the sign.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the miracles of Jesus are signs of the kingdom that invite belief. To those who come to him in faith he grants what they ask; but he refuses signs that satisfy curiosity or feed unbelief (CCC 547–548). The greatest sign is the Cross and Resurrection, and the Church keeps that sign in front of the world in every Holy Qurbono, where the Lord's death and rising are proclaimed until he comes. Faith asks no further proof than the one already given.

Questions people ask

Does this mean it is wrong to ask God for a sign or a proof?

Asking God for light is not wrong. Gideon, Hezekiah and others asked for signs, and God sometimes gave them. What Jesus refuses is a demand made to test him, by people who have already seen enough and decided not to believe. The Church invites us to ask humbly, to read the signs that God has already given in Scripture and in the life of the Church, and to say, with the father of the sick boy, “I believe; help my unbelief!” (Mark 9:24).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today Morth Makrina, the elder sister of Basil and Gregory. Morth is the Syriac title for a holy woman, as Mor is for a holy man. Macrina taught her younger brothers, and turned the family estate in Pontus into a community of women and servants who lived in prayer and work. Her brother Gregory of Nyssa wrote her life soon after her death, and also a dialogue in which she is the teacher: On the Soul and the Resurrection. She loved the truth and helped others to love it, which is the opposite of what Paul describes in the epistle. We ask her prayers when we are tempted to demand proofs instead of trusting.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says some people “refused to love the truth”. Is there a truth about my own life, or about my faith, that I have been avoiding?
  • The Pharisees asked for one more sign. Where do I ask God for one more proof before I will do what I already know he asks?
