Word for the day

“Without any doubt, the mystery of our religion is great: He was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels, proclaimed among Gentiles, believed in throughout the world, taken up in glory.” 1 Timothy 3:16

Reflection

June begins, and in Kerala it begins with two familiar sounds: the first heavy rain of the monsoon on the roof, and children in new uniforms walking back to school with umbrellas bigger than themselves. It is a month of beginnings, and the Church gives us on its first day a saint who spent half his life searching and the other half teaching: St Justin, philosopher and martyr. In the Epistle St Paul writes to his young co-worker Timothy, who has been left in charge of the Church at Ephesus: “I am writing these instructions to you so that, if I am delayed, you may know how one ought to behave in the household of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and bulwark of the truth.” Paul does not call the Church a club of the like-minded or an office that hands out certificates. She is a household, with a Father, brothers and sisters, and a table. And she is a pillar and a bulwark: a pillar holds something up so that it can be seen; a bulwark protects it so that it cannot be knocked down. What the Church holds up and protects is not her own opinion but the truth that God has revealed.

Then Paul breaks into what may be one of the oldest hymns of the Church, sung perhaps in Ephesus before he wrote it down: “Without any doubt, the mystery of our religion is great: He was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels, proclaimed among Gentiles, believed in throughout the world, taken up in glory.” Six short lines tell the whole story of Christ: the Word made flesh, his life and death, his Resurrection shown to be true by the Spirit, the angels at the empty tomb, the apostles going out to the nations, the faith of the peoples, and the Ascension. We have just walked through all of this from Christmas to Pentecost. The “mystery” here is not a riddle that cannot be solved; it is a secret that God has now opened. St Justin was a man who had looked for that secret in every school of his time. Born in Samaria about the year 100, he went from teacher to teacher: a Stoic who did not think it necessary to know God, a Peripatetic who asked first about his fee, a Pythagorean who wanted music and geometry before anything else, a Platonist whose ideas pleased him for a while. Then, as he tells us himself, an old man he met walking by the sea told him to read the prophets, who had spoken of Christ long before he came. “A fire was kindled in my soul,” Justin writes, and he found in Christ the only philosophy that is sure and profitable (Dialogue with Trypho 8, in substance).

In the Gospel the question that Justin had been asking all his life is put to Jesus directly: “Who are you?” Jesus does not answer with a definition. He points to the Cross: “When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will realize that I am he, and that I do nothing on my own, but I speak these things as the Father instructed me.” “I am he” echoes the name God gave to Moses at the burning bush. It will be on the Cross, lifted up between earth and heaven, that Jesus shows most clearly who he is: the Son who does always what pleases the Father, and who loves to the end. Justin understood this with his life. He opened a school in Rome, wore the philosopher's cloak to the end, wrote two Apologies to the emperors defending the Christians, and the Dialogue with Trypho, a long and courteous conversation with a Jewish teacher about the Scriptures they both revered. About the year 165 he was arrested with six companions and brought before the prefect Rusticus. Asked to sacrifice to the gods, they answered that they wished for nothing more than to suffer for our Lord Jesus Christ and so be saved (Acts of the Martyrdom of St Justin, in substance). They were beheaded. The man who had searched for the truth in books gave his life for the Truth who is a person. On this first day of June, when students go back to their desks, ask St Justin to teach us to love learning and to seek in it the One who is the Truth.

Full homily

Dear friends, today June begins. The rain has come, and our children are back at school. On this day of beginnings the Church gives us St Justin, a man who loved to study, who searched for the truth for many years, and who finally found it in Jesus Christ and gave his life for him.

St Paul tells Timothy that the Church is the household of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and bulwark of the truth (1 Timothy 3:15). The Church is our home, and she is also a pillar that holds the truth high so that everyone can see it. And what is that truth? Paul sings it: Without any doubt, the mystery of our religion is great: He was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels, proclaimed among Gentiles, believed in throughout the world, taken up in glory (1 Timothy 3:16).

In the Gospel the people ask Jesus, “Who are you?” (John 8:25). Jesus answers: When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will realize that I am he (John 8:28). We learn who Jesus is when we look at the Cross. Justin learned it from the Scriptures, and when the judge in Rome told him to sacrifice to the gods, he and his companions said they wanted nothing more than to suffer for Jesus Christ. He died a martyr about the year 165.

St Justin also left us the oldest full description of the Sunday Eucharist: the readings, the homily, the prayers, the bread and wine, the great thanksgiving and the Amen of the people. It is what we do in every Holy Qurbono. So today let us ask two graces: a love of learning that leads us to the truth, and the courage to confess Jesus, the Truth in person, wherever we are. Amen.

For children

Children, are you happy to go back to school? New books, new bags, maybe a new umbrella for the rain! Today we remember St Justin. He was a student who never stopped asking questions. He wanted to know: who is God? Many teachers could not answer him. Then he read the Bible and found Jesus. Jesus is the answer to our biggest questions. So when you open your books this year, say a small prayer: Jesus, help me to learn, and help me to know you better.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, revealed in flesh and taken up in glory, you are the truth that St Justin sought in every school and found at last in the Scriptures and in your Cross. Keep your Church faithful as the pillar and bulwark of the truth, and keep us faithful as members of your household. Bless the children and young people who return to school in these days, and their teachers. Give us a mind that loves to learn and a heart that is ready, like St Justin, to confess you before all. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today ask the same question: who is Jesus? Paul answers with an early hymn of the Church: “He was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels.” In John the question is put to Jesus directly, “Who are you?”, and he answers that it will become plain when he is lifted up on the cross. The Church, Paul says, is the household that holds up this truth for the world to see. The key words are mystery and lifted up.

1 Timothy 3:14–16 · The pillar and bulwark of the truth

Setting.

The First Letter to Timothy is written to Paul's young co-worker, left in charge of the Church at Ephesus (1:3). It gives instructions on prayer, on the qualities of bishops and deacons (3:1–13), and on the life of the community. Many scholars think that the Pastoral Letters (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus) were written in Paul's name by a disciple after his death; others hold that they come from Paul himself, late in his life. Either way, the Church receives them as inspired Scripture.

Today's three verses are the hinge of the letter. Paul explains why he writes, and then breaks into what most scholars take to be a hymn or confession already in use in the Church's worship.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: why he writes: so that Timothy may know how to behave in the household of God.
  • v. 16: the hymn of the mystery, in six short lines.
  • ¶ The lines come in pairs that join earth and heaven: flesh and spirit, angels and nations, the world and glory.

Key words.

  • oikos theou, household of God: Not a building but a family. The same letter asks that a bishop manage his own household well, “for if someone does not know how to manage his own household, how can he take care of God's church?” (3:5). The Church is God's family, with its order, its table and its care for the weak.
  • stylos kai hedraiōma, pillar and bulwark: A pillar holds something up so that it can be seen; a bulwark (foundation, support) keeps it firm. The temple of Artemis at Ephesus was famous for its many pillars. Paul says the true pillar is the Church of the living God, and what she holds up is the truth.
  • to tēs eusebeias mystērion, the mystery of our religion: Literally “the mystery of godliness” or “of devotion”. In Paul a mystery is not a riddle but God's plan, once hidden and now revealed in Christ (Colossians 1:26).

Verse by verse.

“you may know how one ought to behave in the household of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and bulwark of the truth.” (3:15)

Conduct comes first. Before he speaks of doctrine, Paul speaks of how people behave in God's house. Truth and life belong together.

“Without any doubt, the mystery of our religion is great:” (3:16)

The Greek word translated “without any doubt” means “by common confession”: what follows is what all believers confess together.

“He was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels,” (3:16)

The best and oldest manuscripts begin “who was revealed”; some later manuscripts read “God was revealed in flesh”. Both say the same faith: the one revealed in the flesh is the eternal Son. “Vindicated in spirit” points to the Resurrection, in which the Spirit showed him to be righteous.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it speaks of the Church as the guardian of revealed truth: the Church, “the pillar and bulwark of the truth”, faithfully guards the faith handed on once for all to the saints (CCC 171). It also quotes the hymn of verse 16, “the mystery of our religion”, and sees in it the heart of faith in the true Incarnation of the Son of God (CCC 463). The Church does not stand above the truth; she serves it, holds it up and keeps it safe.

John 8:25–30 · When you have lifted up the Son of Man

Setting.

Jesus is teaching in the temple in Jerusalem during the feast of Tabernacles (7:2, 7:14; 8:20). The conversation in chapter 8 is tense. In John's Gospel “the Jews” in such scenes usually means the religious authorities who opposed Jesus; here the questioners are the Pharisees of 8:13. John himself, like Jesus and the first disciples, was a Jew, and today's passage ends with many of the hearers coming to faith (8:30).

Just before, Jesus has said: “you will die in your sins unless you believe that I am he” (8:24). The question “Who are you?” follows from that.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–27: “Who are you?”; Jesus speaks of the one who sent him; they do not understand.
  • vv. 28–29: when the Son of Man is lifted up, they will know that “I am he”.
  • v. 30: many believe in him.

Key words.

  • egō eimi, I am he: Literally “I am”. In the Greek Old Testament God speaks of himself with these words: “that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he” (Isaiah 43:10). Jesus uses the same words of himself (8:24, 28, 58).
  • hypsoō, lift up: The word means both to raise up and to exalt. In John it always points to the cross, where Jesus is raised up and glorified at the same time (3:14; 12:32–33).
  • ho pempsas me, the one who sent me: One of John's favourite ways of speaking of the Father. Everything Jesus says and does comes from the Father who sent him.

Verse by verse.

“They said to him, “Who are you?”” (8:25)

Jesus's answer in the Greek is one of the hardest phrases in John. The NRSV renders it as a question, “Why do I speak to you at all?”; other translations, like the POC, read it as “What I have told you from the beginning”. Either way, Jesus will not give a new title. He has already told them.

“When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will realize that I am he,” (8:28)

A startling promise: the moment of his deepest humiliation will be the moment his identity shines out. At the cross the centurion in Mark says, “Truly this man was God's Son!” (Mark 15:39).

“he has not left me alone, for I always do what is pleasing to him.” (8:29)

Even on the cross Jesus is not abandoned by the Father. His whole life is obedience, and in that obedience he is never alone.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 28 when it teaches about the divine name. God revealed himself to Moses as “I AM”; Jesus reveals that he himself bears that name, and that this will be seen when he is lifted up (CCC 211). The Church therefore reads this passage as a revelation of the divinity of Christ, which is shown most clearly not in power but in the cross.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Justin, philosopher and martyr. Born about the year 100 at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria, he searched for truth among the philosophers and found it in Christ. He opened a school in Rome and was beheaded there with several companions around 165. His First Apology gives the oldest full description of the Sunday Eucharist, which the Catechism quotes at length (CCC 1345). Justin is a fitting saint for these readings: he went looking for the answer to the question “Who are you?” and found it in the crucified Lord.

For study and teaching

  • Paul calls the Church “the household of God”. How do I behave in God's house, and in my own home, so that the truth can be seen?
  • Jesus says that people will know who he is when he is lifted up. What does the cross teach me about who Jesus is?
