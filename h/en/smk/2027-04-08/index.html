Word for the day

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Hebrews 13:7–8

Reflection

Christ is risen! Eleven days after Qyomtho the Church opens the last chapter of the Letter to the Hebrews and hears a command that fits a memorial day: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” Today the Panchangam remembers Most Rev. Lawrence Mar Ephrem, a bishop of our Church who died in 1997. To remember a bishop is not to keep a photograph on the wall. The letter asks for three things: remember those who spoke God's word to you, look at how their lives ended, and copy their faith. Then it gives the reason that makes such remembering possible: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” The shepherds pass; the Chief Shepherd does not. The bishop who laid his hands on a priest, the priest who baptized a child, the catechist who taught her the Creed: all of them handed on one Lord, risen and alive, who is today what he was on the morning of the Resurrection. This is why the Church prays for her departed bishops and gives thanks for them in the Holy Qurbono: their work was his work, and he has not changed.

The Gospel puts a question to every leader and to every disciple: “Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” A steward does not own the house. He is given charge of it, and his task is to feed the household on time. “Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” The wicked servant says to himself, “My master is delayed,” and turns the trust into a chance to beat others and to feast. St Gregory the Great, writing on the shepherd's care, said that the one placed over others is first a servant of their needs (in substance). The alternative Gospel shows the same thing from the shepherd's side: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” The food the household needs most is the word of God and the Bread of life. A bishop, a priest, a parent and a teacher are all stewards in their measure, and the master who will come is the risen Lord himself.

Hebrews then speaks of the altar: “We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” Jesus “suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood,” and from that sacrifice comes the food of the Church. The Fathers read this altar as the table of the Lord's Body and Blood, where the one sacrifice of the Cross is made present and we truly receive him (Catechism 1362–1367). From the altar the letter sends us out: “Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name. Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” Here is how to keep this Easter week: come to the Holy Qurbono, praise God with your lips, and share what you have. Say a prayer today for the bishops who have served our Church and gone to their rest, and for the bishops who serve us now. Ask the risen Lord to make you a faithful steward of whatever he has placed in your hands.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today the Letter to the Hebrews says: remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith (Hebrews 13:7). The Panchangam remembers today Most Rev. Lawrence Mar Ephrem, a bishop of our Church who died in 1997.

Shepherds come and go, but Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever (Hebrews 13:8). Every shepherd who served us faithfully handed on the one risen Lord, who does not change. That is why we pray for our departed bishops and give thanks for them.

The Gospel asks: who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time? (Matthew 24:45). Every bishop, priest, parent and teacher is such a steward. The food the household needs most is the word of God and the Bread of life.

We have an altar, says Hebrews: the sacrifice of Christ, made present in the Holy Qurbono. From that altar let us go out to offer God a sacrifice of praise, to do good and to share what we have. May the risen Lord find each of us at work when he comes. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Do you remember who taught you to make the sign of the Cross, or to pray the Our Father? Maybe a grandmother, a teacher or a priest. Today the Bible says: remember the people who told you about God, and try to be like them. Tonight, say thank you to God for one of those people, and say a prayer for them.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the same yesterday and today and forever, the Good Shepherd who laid down his life for the sheep and took it up again. We thank you for the shepherds who spoke your word to us, and we remember today your servant Lawrence Mar Ephrem; grant him rest in the light of your face. Make our bishops and priests faithful and wise stewards, who feed your household at the proper time. Feed us at your altar, and teach us to offer you a sacrifice of praise and to share what we have. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers Most Rev. Lawrence Mar Ephrem, a bishop of our Church and the first Bishop of Marthandom, who died in 1997, and the readings fit a day of remembering shepherds. Hebrews tells us to remember our leaders and imitate their faith, because “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever”, and it speaks of an altar and a sacrifice of praise. In Matthew, Jesus asks who is the faithful and wise servant whom the master will find at work when he comes. The Panchangam also allows John 10:11–18, the Good Shepherd who lays down his life and takes it up again, the risen Lord in whom both readings meet.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · Remember your leaders

Setting.

Hebrews is a long sermon on the priesthood of Christ, sent as a letter to Christians who were weary and tempted to fall back (10:32–36; 12:12). It does not name its author. From early times many in the East linked it with Paul, and the Church long counted it among his letters, while most scholars today think that it was written by another teacher of the first generation. Chapter 13 gathers last counsels: love of the brothers and sisters, hospitality, faithfulness in marriage, and trust in God rather than in money (13:1–6).

Verse 7 speaks of leaders who “spoke the word of God”, and asks the readers to consider “the outcome of their way of life”. Many commentators think that these were the founders of the community who had already died, perhaps as witnesses for Christ. Later, in 13:17, the letter speaks of the leaders now living.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: remember your leaders and imitate their faith; Jesus Christ is the same for ever.
  • v. 9: do not be carried away by strange teachings; the heart is strengthened by grace.
  • vv. 10–12: we have an altar; as the sin offering was burned outside the camp, Jesus suffered outside the gate.
  • vv. 13–14: let us go to him outside the camp, for we seek the city that is to come.
  • vv. 15–16: offer a sacrifice of praise, and do not neglect to do good and to share.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: Literally “those who lead”. The letter uses the word three times in this chapter (13:7, 17, 24). Their authority is bound to the word of God that they spoke.
  • thysiastērion, altar: The ordinary word for an altar of sacrifice. Many Catholic commentators hear in “we have an altar” the table of the Lord's Body and Blood; others think first of the Cross itself. The two belong together, for the altar makes present the one sacrifice of the Cross.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: An Old Testament phrase for a thanksgiving offering. The letter explains it as “the fruit of lips that confess his name”, an echo of Hosea 14:2 in its Greek form.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

Three verbs: remember, consider, imitate. To remember a bishop rightly is to look at how his life ended and to copy his faith.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

Leaders die; the Lord they preached does not change. That is why their faith can be imitated: it rested on the same Christ who is with us today.

“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood.” (13:12)

On the Day of Atonement the bodies of the sin offerings were burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27). Jesus died outside the walls of Jerusalem (John 19:20), and the letter sees in this the fulfilment of that rite.

“Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name.” (13:15)

The praise of this verse and the sharing of verse 16 do not replace the one sacrifice of Christ: they flow from it. We receive at the altar, and our life becomes an offering.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is the sacrifice of praise by which the Church sings the glory of God in the name of all creation (CCC 1361), and that in it the lives of the faithful, their praise, sufferings, prayer and work, are united with the offering of Christ (CCC 1368). So it is fitting to remember a departed bishop at the Holy Qurbono: we pray for him at the same altar from which he fed us.

Matthew 24:45–51 · The servant found at work

Setting.

Jesus is speaking on the Mount of Olives about the end of the age and his coming (24:3). He has just said, “Keep awake therefore, for you do not know on what day your Lord is coming” (24:42). Now he gives the first of several parables about waiting: the servant left in charge, the ten bridesmaids and the talents (25:1–30).

Luke has the same parable (Luke 12:42–46), where it answers Peter's question whether it is meant for the disciples or for everyone (12:41). In both Gospels it speaks most directly to those whom the master has put “in charge of his household”.

Structure.

  • v. 45: who is the faithful and wise servant set over the household?
  • vv. 46–47: blessed is the one found at work; he will be set over all.
  • vv. 48–49: the wicked servant thinks, “My master is delayed”, and abuses the others.
  • vv. 50–51: the master comes at an unexpected hour, and the end is weeping.

Key words.

  • doulos, slave, servant: The NRSV says “slave”. In the households of that time a trusted slave could manage the whole estate. Christian leaders are servants of the household, not its owners.
  • phronimos, wise: Practical good sense, the wisdom of one who sees what the hour requires. The wise bridesmaids in 25:2 are given the same word.
  • chronizei, is delayed: The master takes his time. The danger is not the delay itself but what the servant does with it.

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” (24:45)

The task is simple and daily: food at the right time. The Church has always heard in it the duty of pastors to give the word and the sacraments faithfully.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)

The blessing is not for one who guesses the date but for one who is found doing the work.

“But if that wicked slave says to himself, ‘My master is delayed,’” (24:48)

Evil begins with a sentence spoken inside: there is time. The beating of fellow servants and the drinking follow from forgetting that the master will come.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the time between the Lord's Ascension and his coming in glory is a time of waiting and watching (CCC 672). St Augustine, speaking of his own office, told his people that for them he was a bishop and with them he was a Christian, and that the first name was a danger while the second was salvation (Sermon 340, in substance). This parable keeps that danger and that hope before every shepherd.

Questions people ask

What does “he will cut him in pieces” mean?

The words are strong on purpose, in the style of a prophetic warning. Many scholars think that the phrase means to cut off, to separate the servant from the household, which fits the next words, “put him with the hypocrites”. The point is that a servant who abuses the trust given to him cannot remain in the master's house. Jesus warns in order to save, and the Church prays that none of her shepherds may come to such an end.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Most Rev. Lawrence Mar Ephrem, whom the Panchangam remembers today, was a bishop of the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, the first Bishop of Marthandom, who died in 1997. Our Antiochene tradition remembers its departed fathers and shepherds in the intercessions of every Holy Qurbono. To remember him with Hebrews 13 is to thank God for the word he spoke, to look at the faith in which he died, and to pray that the Lord give him rest.

For study and teaching

  • Who first spoke the word of God to me? How can I thank God for that person today, and imitate that faith?
  • If the Master came today, what work would he find me doing for his household?
