Word for the day

“May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that you may abound in hope by the power of the Holy Spirit.” Romans 15:13

Reflection

After Herod's death an angel tells Joseph in a dream to return with the child and his mother to the land of Israel. Fearing Herod's son Archelaus, he settles in Galilee, in Nazareth, “so that what had been spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled, ‘He will be called a Nazorean.’” Like Israel, the child is called out of Egypt; like a hidden shoot, he grows up in an obscure village. Joseph's quiet obedience protects the life of the Saviour.

Paul tells Jews and Gentiles in Rome: “Welcome one another, therefore, just as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God.” Christ became a servant to Israel to confirm the promises, and the Gentiles glorify God for his mercy; Isaiah's “root of Jesse” rises to rule the nations, “in him the Gentiles shall hope.” At the end of the year, Paul's blessing is perfect: “May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing.”

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells the Romans: welcome one another, therefore, just as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God (Romans 15:7). Christ welcomed us when we were far from him. We are called to welcome others the same way.

Paul gathers several Scriptures to show that Christ came for the Gentiles as well as for Israel, and ends with Isaiah: the root of Jesse shall come, the one who rises to rule the Gentiles; in him the Gentiles shall hope. Then he prays: may the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that you may abound in hope by the power of the Holy Spirit (Romans 15:12–13).

In the Gospel, after Herod's death, an angel tells Joseph in a dream to return to the land of Israel. But fearing Herod's son, Joseph is warned again and goes to Galilee, settling in a town called Nazareth (Matthew 2:19–23).

The Holy Family's life was not settled. Exile in Egypt, return, fear, another change of plan. Joseph had to listen to God again and again. Yet in all this, the family stayed together and the Child grew.

Many of our families live like this: moving for work, uncertain futures, plans that change. The God of hope walks with us. He led Joseph step by step, and he leads us.

As the year ends, let us welcome one another as Christ welcomed us, especially those far from home. And let us ask the God of hope to fill us with joy and peace for the year to come. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul asks a divided community to welcome one another, because Christ came to confirm God's promises to Israel and to bring the nations to praise. He ends with “the root of Jesse” from Isaiah. The Gospel takes the Holy Family back from Egypt to an unknown village, where the promised one will be called a Nazorean. Both readings show God keeping his word in unexpected ways.

Romans 15:7–13 · The God of hope

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome in the later 50s, probably from Corinth. He did not found that Church. It seems to have had believers of both Jewish and Gentile background, who differed about food and holy days. Chapters 14 and 15 address this tension, speaking of the “weak” and the “strong”. Today's passage draws it together: both groups are one in Christ, and Scripture has always foreseen it.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the command: welcome one another, as Christ has welcomed you.
  • vv. 8–9a: Christ served the circumcised for God's truth, and the Gentiles for God's mercy.
  • vv. 9b–12: four quotations: Psalm 18:49, Deuteronomy 32:43, Psalm 117:1, Isaiah 11:10.
  • v. 13: a closing blessing: the God of hope.

Key words.

  • proslambanesthe, welcome: To take someone to oneself, to receive into one's home and circle. The word opened this section (14:1), and God is its subject there too (14:3).
  • diakonos peritomēs, servant of the circumcised: Jesus is a Jew who served his own people, to keep God's word to their ancestors. Paul says this to Gentile Christians, so that they will not forget it.
  • rhiza tou Iessai, root of Jesse: The shoot from David's family (Isaiah 11:1, 10). Today's Gospel may echo the same chapter.

Verse by verse.

“Welcome one another, therefore, just as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God.” (15:7)

The welcome is measured by Christ's welcome, not by how much we like one another.

“The root of Jesse shall come, the one who rises to rule the Gentiles; in him the Gentiles shall hope.” (15:12)

Paul's chain of texts draws on the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms: all of Scripture is singing the same song.

“May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that you may abound in hope by the power of the Holy Spirit.” (15:13)

Hope, in Paul, is not an optimistic temperament. It comes from God, and it grows in believing.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this blessing as the prayer of hope (CCC 2657) and describes hope as the virtue by which we rely not on our own strength but on the help of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1817). The welcome Paul asks for marks the Church, which is one people gathered from all nations (CCC 831).

Matthew 2:19–23 · He will be called a Nazorean

Setting.

Herod the Great died about 4 BC, and Augustus divided his kingdom among his sons. Archelaus received Judea. According to Josephus, he began his reign with a harsh crackdown at Passover in which many died, and he was finally deposed and banished in AD 6. Herod Antipas received Galilee. So Joseph's fear was reasonable. Nazareth was a small village, not mentioned in the Old Testament, in a region that Judeans did not much regard (compare John 1:46).

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: the dream in Egypt: go back to the land of Israel.
  • v. 21: Joseph obeys.
  • v. 22: fear of Archelaus, and a second warning.
  • v. 23: Nazareth, and the fulfilment.

Key words.

  • Nazōraios, Nazorean: Matthew hears the place name in this title. Two Old Testament words are often suggested: nēṣer, the “branch” from Jesse (Isaiah 11:1), and nāzîr, one consecrated to God, as Samson was (Judges 13:5). No one is sure which Matthew had in mind, and he may be thinking of both.
  • paralabe, take: Literally “take along with you”. Joseph is told to take “the child and his mother”, the same phrase that recurs through this chapter (2:13, 14, 20, 21).

Verse by verse.

“Get up, take the child and his mother, and go to the land of Israel, for those who were seeking the child’s life are dead.” (2:20)

This echoes God's word to Moses: “all those who were seeking your life are dead” (Exodus 4:19). Matthew shows Jesus reliving Israel's story and the story of Moses.

“Then Joseph got up, took the child and his mother, and went to the land of Israel.” (2:21)

Joseph says nothing in this Gospel. He is told and he does, in almost the same words.

“There he made his home in a town called Nazareth, so that what had been spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled, “He will be called a Nazorean.”” (2:23)

No Old Testament verse has these exact words. The Greek says “through the prophets”, in the plural (the POC has the singular), which suggests a general sense of prophecy and not one text. St Jerome draws this very conclusion: Matthew takes the sense of the Scriptures and not their words (in substance).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in the return from Egypt the Exodus story, with Jesus as the definitive liberator (CCC 530), and it treats the years at Nazareth as Jesus' sharing in the daily life of most people (CCC 531–533). Pope Francis, in Patris Corde 3, draws from Joseph's dreams the portrait of an obedient father, who at each step does as he is told.

For study and teaching

  • Who is the person in my parish or family that I find it hardest to “welcome”? What would Christ's welcome look like toward them?
  • Joseph acted at once on a word he could only half understand. What small step of obedience is before me now?
