Word for the day

“Then God said to him in the dream, “Yes, I know that you did this in the integrity of your heart; furthermore it was I who kept you from sinning against me. Therefore I did not let you touch her.” Genesis 20:6

Reflection

Abraham, the friend of God who had pleaded so boldly for Sodom, now behaves like a frightened man. Living as a foreigner in Gerar, he says of Sarah, “She is my sister,” and King Abimelech takes her. It is a half-truth told out of fear, and it puts at risk the very promise God has just renewed: that Sarah herself will bear Isaac. God steps in, not to Abraham, but to the pagan king in a dream: “You are about to die because of the woman whom you have taken; for she is a married woman.” Abimelech protests his innocence, and God agrees: “Yes, I know that you did this in the integrity of your heart; furthermore it was I who kept you from sinning against me.” Two things stand out. The foreign king has a more honest conscience in this story than the father of believers. And God does not discard his flawed servant: “return the man’s wife; for he is a prophet, and he will pray for you and you shall live.” God guards his promise even when his friend fails.

Paul says that Abimelech's kind of honesty is no accident: “When Gentiles, who do not possess the law, do instinctively what the law requires… They show that what the law requires is written on their hearts, to which their own conscience also bears witness.” And “it is not the hearers of the law who are righteous in God’s sight, but the doers.” Abraham knew God's word, yet in fear he hid the truth. Jesus asks for the opposite courage: “everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God.” Fear makes us disown what we belong to, as Abraham disowned his wife. But Jesus promises help: “the Holy Spirit will teach you at that very hour what you ought to say.” He also warns that “whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven.” St Augustine taught that this sin is the hardness of heart that refuses to the end the forgiveness the Spirit offers (in substance). No one who fears having committed it has committed it; the door of mercy is open to all who knock.

Full homily

Dear friends, Abraham, the friend of God, became afraid in Gerar and said of Sarah: she is my sister (Genesis 20:2). A half-truth told in fear put God's promise at risk.

But God guarded his promise. He spoke to King Abimelech in a dream: yes, I know that you did this in the integrity of your heart; furthermore it was I who kept you from sinning against me (Genesis 20:6). And he still called Abraham a prophet who would pray for the king. God does not discard his weak servants.

Paul says the law is written on the hearts of Gentiles too, and their conscience bears witness. It is not the hearers of the law who are righteous in God's sight, but the doers (Romans 2:13, 15).

Jesus asks for courage: everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God. And he promises: the Holy Spirit will teach you at that very hour what you ought to say (Luke 12:8, 12). As the third week of the Fast ends, let us ask the Spirit for truthful lips and brave hearts. Amen.

For children

Children, even Abraham was sometimes afraid and did not tell the whole truth. But God still loved him and helped him. Jesus wants us to be brave and tell the truth, and not be ashamed of him. The Holy Spirit will help you. Try saying a prayer before lunch at school this week!

Prayer

Lord God, you guarded Sarah and your promise when Abraham failed through fear, and you kept Abimelech from sin. Thank you for the times you have kept us from sinning against you without our knowing. Write your law ever more deeply on our hearts, make us doers of your word, and give us courage to acknowledge your Son before others. Holy Spirit, teach us what to say. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A foreign king in Genesis listens to God in a dream, while Abraham, the friend of God, tells a half-truth out of fear. The Epistle (Romans 2:12–16) then speaks of Gentiles who show that “what the law requires is written on their hearts”. In Luke, Jesus calls for open witness and promises that the Holy Spirit will teach us what to say. The readings ask whether we will speak truthfully when we are afraid, and they assure us that God is not limited to the people we expect him to work through.

Genesis 20:1–7 · “She is my sister”

Setting.

Abraham moves south to the Negeb, and lives as an alien at Gerar. This is the second of three stories in Genesis in which a patriarch passes off his wife as his sister (12:10–20; 26:6–11). Many scholars think they are forms of an old tradition retold in different settings. As they stand, they show that faith does not remove fear overnight.

The timing makes the danger greater. Abraham has just been promised that Sarah will bear a son (18:10). If she is lost now, so is the promise. God himself steps in to protect what Abraham has put at risk.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Abraham's half-truth; Sarah is taken.
  • v. 3: God comes to Abimelech in a dream.
  • vv. 4–5: the king defends himself.
  • vv. 6–7: God answers: he knows, he has restrained him, and Abraham must pray.

Key words.

  • tom-lēbāb, integrity of heart: Wholeness, with nothing divided. A foreign king is the one who uses this phrase here, and God accepts it.
  • nābî’, prophet: This is the first time the word appears in the Bible, and it is used of Abraham. Here the mark of a prophet is not foretelling, but that he “will pray for you”.

Verse by verse.

“But God came to Abimelech in a dream by night,” (20:3)

God speaks to a king outside the covenant. He is not bound to the boundaries that we draw.

“Lord, will you destroy an innocent people?” (20:4)

In Hebrew the word for “innocent” is the same as Abraham's word for “righteous” in 18:23. The foreign king now asks God Abraham's own question.

“furthermore it was I who kept you from sinning against me.” (20:6)

A striking insight: God's care lies behind even the sins we have not committed. The king's innocence is in part God's gift.

“for he is a prophet, and he will pray for you and you shall live.” (20:7)

God gives Abraham, who has just failed, the task of interceding for the man he wronged. His weakness has not cancelled his calling.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that deep within the conscience a person discovers a law he has not laid on himself, which he must obey, and that conscience is man's most secret core and sanctuary, where he is alone with God (Gaudium et Spes 16; CCC 1776). The Church also teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel, but seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will, can attain salvation (Lumen Gentium 16). Abimelech is an early picture of this.

Luke 12:8–12 · The Spirit will teach you what to say

Setting.

Jesus is on his way to Jerusalem, and thousands are gathering (12:1). He warns the disciples against hypocrisy and against fear of those who can kill only the body (12:1–7). These verses continue: the one who fears God can speak openly before others. The “synagogues, the rulers, and the authorities” in verse 11 cover every sort of court, Jewish and Gentile alike, that the first disciples came to know.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–9: acknowledging and denying.
  • v. 10: the word against the Son of Man and the blasphemy against the Spirit.
  • vv. 11–12: do not worry; the Spirit will teach.

Key words.

  • homologeō, to acknowledge: To say the same thing, to confess publicly. It is the word for what the Church does in her creed.
  • blasphēmeō, to blaspheme: To speak against, to slander. See the question below about the sin against the Holy Spirit.
  • apologeomai, to defend oneself: The courtroom word, from which “apologetics” comes. The disciple does not have to prepare this alone.

Verse by verse.

“everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God;” (12:8)

A word said on earth has an echo in heaven. Jesus describes himself as a witness who speaks for those who spoke for him.

“whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven.” (12:10)

A word against the Son of Man can be forgiven, and Peter, who denied him and was prayed for, is the proof (Luke 22:32). This is something else: see the question below.

“for the Holy Spirit will teach you at that very hour what you ought to say.” (12:12)

Spoken to frightened people, not to clever ones. Many martyrs' accounts show simple believers answering judges with surprising clarity.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the disciple of Christ must not only keep the faith and live on it, but also profess it, confidently bear witness to it, and spread it, and it quotes the parallel saying from Matthew 10:32–33 (CCC 1816). Those who belong to Christ through faith and Baptism must confess their baptismal faith before others (CCC 14).

Questions people ask

What is the sin against the Holy Spirit? Have I committed it?

The Catechism explains it this way: there are no limits to the mercy of God, but anyone who deliberately refuses to accept his mercy by repenting rejects the forgiveness of his sins and the salvation offered by the Holy Spirit (CCC 1864). So it is not a particular word said in anger, or a terrible thought that comes to a troubled mind. It is a closed door kept shut from the inside. Anyone who fears having committed it, and is sorry, is showing that the door is not shut.

For study and teaching

  • When has fear led me to say something less than the truth? What did it put at risk?
  • Who has shown me integrity of heart from outside my own community? What can I learn from it?
