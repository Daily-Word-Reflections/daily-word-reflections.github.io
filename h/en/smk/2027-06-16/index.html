Word for the day

“Moreover, it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.” 1 Corinthians 4:2

Reflection

Today the Apostles' Fast begins. On Sunday the priests reminded us of it, as the Panchangam asked them to do, and from today until the feast of Sts Peter and Paul the Church keeps it. Why fast now, only a few weeks after the joy of Pentecost? Jesus himself gave the answer when he was asked why his disciples did not fast: “The wedding guests cannot mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them, can they? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast.” The Bridegroom has ascended to the Father; the Spirit has come; and now the Church, like the apostles, sets out on the long road of mission. The Acts of the Apostles shows how the first Christians did it. At Antioch, “while they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.’” Then, after more fasting and prayer, they laid hands on them and sent them off. The fast of the apostles is a fast for mission: we deny ourselves a little so that the Church may be sent, and so that we ourselves may go where the Spirit sends us. St Leo the Great, preaching in Rome on the fast that followed Pentecost, told his people that after the days of holy rejoicing the Church fasts so that the gifts of the Spirit may be guarded by self-discipline, and he called it a fast handed down from the apostles (Sermons on the Fast of Pentecost, in substance). Our Orthodox brothers and sisters of the Malankara tradition keep this fast too, and it is good to know that in these days many Christians of our land are praying and fasting together.

The Gospel gives the fast its faces. “Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” Then Matthew does something he rarely does: he writes out a list of names. “First, Simon, also known as Peter, and his brother Andrew; James son of Zebedee, and his brother John; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew the tax collector; James son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus; Simon the Cananaean, and Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” These are not titles but persons: two pairs of brothers from the fishing boats of Galilee, a tax collector who had worked for the hated ruling power, and a Simon whose surname probably marked him as a man of zeal, two men who might once have quarrelled bitterly, now seated at one table. Our own Mar Thoma is there, the apostle whom our fathers' faith remembers as the one who brought the Gospel to India. Bartholomew, whom the Panchangam remembered last week, and Thaddaeus, whom it will remember on Saturday, are there too. And the list ends with a name that should make every disciple humble: Judas, chosen like the others, who betrayed. Jesus chose these twelve and gave them his own authority; through them the Church is apostolic. Our bishops are their successors, and our priests share their work. In this fast we pray for them by name.

St Paul tells the Corinthians how to think of the apostles and of all who serve the Church: “Think of us in this way, as servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries. Moreover, it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.” A steward manages what belongs to someone else. The mysteries of God, the word, the sacraments, the Holy Qurbono which our tradition loves to call the holy mysteries, do not belong to the priest; he holds them in trust. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, says that a steward does not treat his master's goods as his own, but distributes them faithfully, and so must every servant of the Church (Homilies on 1 Corinthians 10, in substance). And in a smaller way every Christian is a steward: of time, of money, of the body, of the faith received in Baptism. That is what the fast trains us for. When we fast, pray more and give alms, we learn that our food, our hours and our money are not simply ours. Paul adds a warning for the fast: “Therefore do not pronounce judgment before the time, before the Lord comes, who will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart.” A fast that makes us harsh judges of others, especially of our priests, is no fast at all. Let these days make us trustworthy stewards, slow to judge and quick to pray.

Homily outline

Theme: “It is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.”

At the beginning of the Apostles' Fast: a fast for mission, the Twelve called by name, and the trustworthy stewards of God's mysteries.

1. Stewards of God's mysteries

  • Held in trust, not owned.
  • The priest at the altar, the parent at home.
  • Time, money, body and faith.
“Not so with my servant Moses; he is entrusted with all my house.” Numbers 12:7
“Like good stewards of the manifold grace of God, serve one another with whatever gift each of you has received.” 1 Peter 4:10

Apply: Make an honest list of what God has entrusted to you and choose one thing to use more faithfully during the fast.

2. The Twelve called by name

  • Fishermen, a tax collector, a zealot.
  • Mar Thoma among them.
  • Bishops, successors of the apostles.
“Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.” Isaiah 43:1
“And when day came, he called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles” Luke 6:13

Apply: Pray each day of the fast for the Pope, your bishop and your parish priest by name.

3. Fasting with the apostles

  • When the Bridegroom is taken away.
  • Antioch: worship, fasting, sending.
  • A fast without judging.

“While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.”” Acts 13:2

Apply: Decide today on one fast, one daily prayer and one act of almsgiving for the whole Apostles' Fast.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Apostles' Fast begins, and it will end with the feast of Sts Peter and Paul. Jesus said that when the bridegroom is taken away, his friends will fast (Matthew 9:15). The Lord has gone up to the Father and the Spirit has come. Now, like the apostles, we fast and pray so that the Church may go out with the Good News.

In the Gospel Jesus calls the Twelve and gives them authority over unclean spirits and every sickness (Matthew 10:1). Then we hear their names: Peter and Andrew, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew, James and Thaddaeus, Simon, and Judas who betrayed him (Matthew 10:2–4). Our own Mar Thoma is among them. Jesus does not choose an idea; he chooses persons. Our bishops are their successors today, and in this fast we pray for them.

St Paul says: think of us in this way, as servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries (1 Corinthians 4:1). A steward takes care of what belongs to his master. The holy mysteries do not belong to the priest; he holds them in trust. And we too are stewards of our time, our money and our faith. Fasting teaches us this. It is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy (1 Corinthians 4:2).

Paul also warns: do not pronounce judgment before the time (1 Corinthians 4:5). A fast that makes us harsh with others is not the fast of the apostles. So let us fast humbly, pray for our shepherds, give what we save to the poor, and leave judgment to the Lord. Amen.

For children

Children, today a special time of prayer begins: the Apostles' Fast. The apostles were Jesus' twelve special friends. He called each one by his name, Peter, John, Thomas and the others, and sent them to tell the whole world about God. Before they went, they prayed and fasted. During these days you can give up one sweet or one cartoon, and pray for our bishop and our priest. And the money you save for a chocolate can go to a child who needs it. That is how we help the apostles' work today.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you called the Twelve by name, gave them your authority and sent them to proclaim your kingdom. As we begin the Apostles' Fast, strengthen us by your Holy Spirit to deny ourselves for the sake of your Gospel. Make us trustworthy stewards of your mysteries and of all you have entrusted to us. Keep us from judging before the time, and fill your Church with apostles who will carry your word to every nation. Bless our Holy Father the Pope, our bishops and our priests, the successors and helpers of your apostles. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Apostles' Fast begins today, and the readings set the apostles before us. In the Gospel Jesus gives the Twelve authority and Matthew records their names, from “first, Simon, also known as Peter” to Judas Iscariot. Paul tells the Corinthians how to think of those who serve the Church: “as servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries”, of whom one thing is required, that they be found trustworthy. The key words are authority and faithfulness.

1 Corinthians 4:1–5 · Stewards of God's mysteries

Setting.

The church at Corinth was divided into parties: “I belong to Paul,” “I belong to Apollos,” “I belong to Cephas” (1:12). In chapter 3 Paul answers that he and Apollos are only servants: one planted, the other watered, but God gave the growth (3:6). Chapter 4 opens with the conclusion: this is how you should regard us. Some at Corinth were also judging Paul and comparing him unfavourably with others; he answers that only the Lord can judge.

Structure.

  • v. 1: servants of Christ, stewards of God's mysteries.
  • v. 2: what is required of stewards: trustworthiness.
  • vv. 3–4: Paul is not troubled by human judgment, nor does he judge himself; the Lord judges.
  • v. 5: wait for the Lord, who will bring hidden things to light.

Key words.

  • hypēretēs, servant, assistant: A helper who carries out another's orders. Luke uses the same word for John Mark, who assisted Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:5). The minister is never the owner of the work.
  • oikonomos, steward: The manager of a household, often a trusted slave, who looked after the master's goods and gave each member of the household what was needed. He owned nothing; he answered for everything.
  • mystēria, mysteries: In Paul, God's plan, once hidden and now revealed in Christ (2:7). The Eastern Churches came to call the sacraments the Holy Mysteries, in Syriac rāzē, because in them that plan is given to us.

Verse by verse.

“Think of us in this way, as servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries.” (4:1)

Two images: servants who obey, stewards who distribute. The ministers of the Church do not invent the faith or the sacraments; they hand on what they have received (11:23).

“Moreover, it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.” (4:2)

Not brilliance, not popularity, but faithfulness. The same is asked of every Christian who has been given something to care for: a family, a gift, a task in the parish.

“It is the Lord who judges me.” (4:4)

Paul's conscience is clear, but he knows that a clear conscience is not the final verdict. Only God sees the whole heart.

“Therefore do not pronounce judgment before the time, before the Lord comes,” (4:5)

This is not a ban on all judgment of right and wrong; Paul himself gives firm judgments in chapter 5. It forbids judging the hidden motives of others, which only the Lord can bring to light.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that the Greek word mystērion was rendered in Latin by two words, mysterium and sacramentum, so that the sacraments are the signs and instruments by which the one mystery of Christ is given to us (CCC 774). The Church has always applied this verse to bishops and priests, who are stewards of the Holy Mysteries, and it reminds them that faithfulness is what the Lord asks. It reminds the faithful, too, that they should pray for their pastors more than they judge them.

Matthew 10:1–4 · The names of the twelve apostles

Setting.

Just before this passage Jesus sees the crowds “harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd” and tells his disciples to ask the Lord of the harvest to send out labourers (9:36–38). Now he himself sends labourers. Matthew 10 is the second of the five great discourses in his Gospel, the missionary discourse, and it begins by naming those who are sent.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Jesus gives the Twelve authority over unclean spirits and every disease.
  • vv. 2–4: the names of the twelve apostles, in pairs.

Key words.

  • exousia, authority: Not their own power but a share in the authority of Jesus himself, who has shown it over sickness, sin and the demons (chapters 8–9).
  • apostolos, apostle, one who is sent: This is the only place where Matthew uses the word. An apostle is a messenger who carries the authority of the one who sends him.
  • prōtos, first: Peter stands first in every list of the apostles in the New Testament, and Judas Iscariot is last in every Gospel list. Matthew adds the word “first”, which is not needed for counting; it marks Peter's place.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” (10:1)

The words “every disease and every sickness” repeat what Matthew has said of Jesus himself (4:23; 9:35). The apostles are sent to do what Jesus does.

“These are the names of the twelve apostles: first, Simon, also known as Peter,” (10:2)

Twelve recalls the twelve tribes of Israel. Jesus is gathering the people of God anew, and the Twelve are its foundation (Revelation 21:14).

“Thomas and Matthew the tax collector;” (10:3)

Only Matthew's list adds “the tax collector” to his own name, remembering where the Lord found him (9:9). Thomas, named beside him, is the apostle whom the Christians of India have honoured as their father in faith.

“Simon the Cananaean, and Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” (10:4)

“Cananaean” is not about Canaan; it comes from an Aramaic word meaning “zealous”, and Luke calls him “the Zealot” (Luke 6:15). The list ends soberly with Judas: to be chosen is a gift that must be kept by faithfulness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Lord Jesus gave his community a structure that will remain until the kingdom is fully achieved: he chose the Twelve with Peter as their head, and they are the foundation stones of the new Jerusalem (CCC 765). It also teaches that Simon Peter holds the first place in the college of the Twelve (CCC 552). The bishops are the successors of the apostles, and through them the authority given in today's Gospel continues in the Church.

Questions people ask

Why does the Church fast so soon after Pentecost?

Jesus said that his disciples would fast when the bridegroom was taken away from them (Matthew 9:15). After the Ascension and Pentecost the Church sets out on her mission, and in Acts the first Christians fast before sending out missionaries: “while they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul’” (Acts 13:2–3). St Leo the Great preached in Rome on a fast after Pentecost and spoke of it as handed down from the apostles (Sermons on the Pentecost fast, in substance). The Apostles' Fast is a fast for mission, kept in the company of the apostles.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today the Apostles' Fast, the Shleeha Nombu, begins in our Church, and it is kept until the feast of Sts Peter and Paul on 29 June. The fast is not only a discipline of food. It is a time to pray for the Church's mission and for those who carry it: the Holy Father, our bishops and priests, missionaries and catechists. Today's Gospel gives the fast its faces, the twelve names. It is good to pray for one apostle each day, and to remember especially St Thomas, through whose preaching, as our tradition holds, the faith first came to our land.
