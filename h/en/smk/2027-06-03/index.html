Readings

  • Gospel Luke 11:24–26 · The house swept and put in order, and the seven spirits

Word for the day

“in order that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.” Galatians 3:14

Reflection

Before the monsoon, every Kerala household does a thorough cleaning: the attic is cleared, the drains are opened, the walls are whitewashed, the old things are thrown out. A swept house is a good thing. But today Jesus tells a strange little story about a swept house that ended badly: “When the unclean spirit has gone out of a person, it wanders through waterless regions looking for a resting place, but not finding any, it says, ‘I will return to my house from which I came.’ When it comes, it finds it swept and put in order. Then it goes and brings seven other spirits more evil than itself, and they enter and live there; and the last state of that person is worse than the first.” The house was clean, but it was empty. Nobody had come to live in it. The point is sharp: it is not enough to get rid of evil; the place it leaves must be filled with good. A person who gives up drinking but finds nothing to fill the evenings, a family that stops quarrelling but never learns to pray together, a young man who leaves a bad company but finds no good one: all these are swept houses, and they are in danger.

St Paul's Epistle tells us who must come to live in the swept house. He writes to the Galatians, who had received the Gospel with joy and were now being told that they must also take on the whole Law of Moses to be true children of Abraham. Paul answers by going back to Abraham himself: “so, you see, those who believe are the descendants of Abraham. And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, declared the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, ‘All the Gentiles shall be blessed in you.’” Abraham was counted righteous because he believed God, long before the Law was given. Paul honours the Law as God's gift; what he insists on is that no one is made right with God by keeping it by his own strength: “Now it is evident that no one is justified before God by the law; for ‘The one who is righteous will live by faith.’” And then he says what Christ has done: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us.” The one who hung on the tree took on himself everything that stood against us. St Augustine, commenting on this verse, said that Christ took our punishment without taking our guilt, so that by bearing what was ours he might give us what was his (Commentary on Galatians, in substance).

Why did he do it? Paul answers in the last verse, and it is the verse for this season: “in order that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.” The house is swept by the Cross and filled by the Holy Spirit. This is what happened to us at Baptism: the unclean spirit was driven out by the prayers of exorcism and renunciation, and the Holy Spirit came to dwell in us, sealed in the holy Myron. It is what happens every time we make a good confession: the house is swept. And it is why the Church does not leave us there but calls us to the Holy Qurbono, to prayer, to the word of God, to works of mercy, so that the house is not left empty but is lived in. In these weeks after Pentecost, let us ask the Spirit not only to clean us but to move in and stay. A house where the Spirit lives has no room left for seven other spirits.

Full homily

Dear friends, before the rains every family cleans the house. Today Jesus tells a story about a house that was swept clean but left empty. The unclean spirit came back, found it swept and put in order, and brought seven other spirits more evil than itself (Luke 11:25–26). A clean house is good. An empty house is dangerous.

St Paul tells us who must live in our house. Those who believe are the descendants of Abraham (Galatians 3:7). Abraham believed God, and God blessed him and promised that all the nations would be blessed in him. Then Paul says what Jesus has done: Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). On the Cross he took our place.

And why? In order that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith (Galatians 3:14). The Cross sweeps the house clean, and the Holy Spirit comes to live in it. That happened at our Baptism. It happens again in every good confession.

So do not stop at sweeping. If you have given up something wrong, fill that place with something good: prayer, the word of God, the Holy Qurbono, service to others. A heart where the Holy Spirit lives has no room for the seven spirits. Amen.

For children

Children, does your family clean the house before the rainy season? Jesus told a story about a clean house where nobody lived. Because it was empty, the bad things came back! Your heart is like a house. When you say sorry to God, he cleans it. But then you must invite the Holy Spirit to live there, and fill your heart with good things: prayers, kind words, helping others. Then there is no room for the bad things to come back.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you became a curse for us on the tree so that the blessing of Abraham might come to all the nations and we might receive the promise of the Spirit. Sweep our hearts clean of every evil, and do not leave them empty: fill them with your Holy Spirit, with prayer, with love and with good works. Keep us faithful children of Abraham through faith, and guard us from falling back into what you have set us apart from. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In this week after Pentecost both readings speak of the Spirit. Paul tells the Galatians that Christ took our curse upon himself “so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.” Jesus warns that a soul emptied of evil but left empty is in danger: the house must be filled. The key words are blessing and the filled house.

Galatians 3:7–14 · Blessed with Abraham who believed

Setting.

Paul writes to the Churches of Galatia, in what is now central Turkey, probably in the mid-50s AD. After he left, other teachers had come and told the Gentile converts that to belong fully to God's people they must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. Paul answers that they received the Spirit not by works of the law but by believing the Gospel (3:2–5).

Today's verses form a chain of Old Testament texts: Genesis 12:3 and 18:18 (the blessing of the nations), Deuteronomy 27:26 (the curse), Habakkuk 2:4 (the righteous live by faith), Leviticus 18:5 (life by doing the law) and Deuteronomy 21:23 (one hanged on a tree). Paul argues as a trained Jewish teacher, from Scripture to Scripture.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–9: the children of Abraham are those who believe; the nations are blessed in him.
  • vv. 10–12: the law, taken alone, cannot give life and leaves us under a curse.
  • vv. 13–14: Christ became a curse for us, so that the blessing and the Spirit come to all.

Key words.

  • pistis, faith: Trust in God and his promise, as Abraham trusted (3:6; Genesis 15:6). For Paul faith is never idle: elsewhere in this letter he speaks of “faith working through love” (5:6).
  • erga nomou, works of the law: The observances of the Mosaic law, such as circumcision and the food laws, when they are made the condition for belonging to God's people apart from Christ. Paul calls the law itself holy (Romans 7:12).
  • exagorazō, redeem: To buy out, to ransom, as a slave is bought out of slavery. Christ paid the price for us with his own life.

Verse by verse.

“so, you see, those who believe are the descendants of Abraham.” (3:7)

Abraham's true family is not only a matter of birth. Abraham himself believed God before he was circumcised (Romans 4:10–11).

“Now it is evident that no one is justified before God by the law; for “The one who is righteous will live by faith.”” (3:11)

Paul quotes Habakkuk 2:4. In Hebrew the word means faithfulness, steady trust in God. Paul hears in it the way of faith opened in Christ.

“Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us” (3:13)

Deuteronomy 21:23 says that one hanged on a tree is under God's curse. Paul sees the cross in that light: Jesus took the place of the cursed, so that we might receive the blessing.

“so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.” (3:14)

The promise made to Abraham reaches its goal at Pentecost: the gift of the Holy Spirit poured out on Jews and Gentiles alike.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that justification has been merited for us by the Passion of Christ and is given through Baptism, the sacrament of faith (CCC 1992). Faith is the beginning of salvation, and the works that follow are themselves the fruit of grace, done in Christ and by his Spirit. Paul is not teaching that the law of Moses was evil or that God has rejected his people; the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). He teaches that in Christ the blessing promised to Abraham is opened to every nation.

Luke 11:24–26 · The house swept and put in order

Setting.

These verses close a controversy. Jesus has cast out a demon, and some say he does it by Beelzebul, the ruler of the demons (11:14–15). He answers that he casts out demons “by the finger of God” (11:20), and that “Whoever is not with me is against me” (11:23). Then comes this short warning. Right after it a woman calls out a blessing on his mother, and Jesus answers: “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” (11:28). Matthew has the same saying (Matthew 12:43–45).

Structure.

  • v. 24: the unclean spirit leaves, wanders, and decides to return.
  • v. 25: it finds the house swept and put in order.
  • v. 26: it brings seven worse spirits, and the last state is worse than the first.

Key words.

  • anapausis, resting place: Rest, a place to settle. The evil spirit finds no rest in the desert and looks for a human heart to live in.
  • sesarōmenon kai kekosmēmenon, swept and put in order: Clean and tidy, but in Matthew's version also “empty” (Matthew 12:44). Nothing new has come to live in the house.
  • hepta, seven: The number of fullness in the Bible. The danger is not a small one but complete.

Verse by verse.

“it wanders through waterless regions looking for a resting place,” (11:24)

In the thinking of the time the desert was a place of evil spirits. The image is of a restless power that does not give up easily.

“When it comes, it finds it swept and put in order.” (11:25)

Being cleaned is not enough. A life from which sin has been driven out must be filled with God, or it will be filled again by something worse.

“and the last state of that person is worse than the first.” (11:26)

A warning against half-hearted conversion: to turn away from evil without turning towards Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that conversion is not only a first step at Baptism but a continual task for the whole Church and for each Christian, a “second conversion” that lasts a lifetime (CCC 1428). The context shows how the house is filled: by hearing the word of God and keeping it (11:28), and, in this season, by the Holy Spirit, who comes to dwell in those who belong to Christ.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that Christ became a curse for us. How does that change the way I look at my own sins and at the cross?
  • After I have confessed a sin, how do I fill the “swept house” with prayer, the word of God and good works?
