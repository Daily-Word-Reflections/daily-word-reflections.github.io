Readings

Word for the day

“If the part of the dough offered as first fruits is holy, then the whole batch is holy; and if the root is holy, then the branches also are holy.” Romans 11:16

Reflection

St Paul asks a question that he has clearly asked himself with tears: “So I ask, have they stumbled so as to fall? By no means!” He is speaking of his own people, the Jews, most of whom had not yet recognised Jesus as the Messiah. Paul had once said that he could wish himself cut off from Christ for the sake of his kindred (Romans 9:3), so this is no cold discussion. He answers that their stumbling is not the end of the story. “But through their stumbling salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make Israel jealous.” God can bring good even out of a stumble, and the good that came to the nations is meant to draw Israel back with a holy longing. “Now if their stumbling means riches for the world, and if their defeat means riches for Gentiles, how much more will their full inclusion mean!” Paul is looking ahead to a day of fullness. The Catechism says that the glorious coming of the Messiah awaits the recognition of him by all Israel, and that the Church looks to that day with the hope of the whole world (Catechism 674, in substance). Meanwhile the Church must never speak of the Jewish people as rejected: the Jewish covenant has never been revoked (Catechism 839–840, in substance).

Paul then turns to us, the Gentiles: “Now I am speaking to you Gentiles. Inasmuch then as I am an apostle to the Gentiles, I glorify my ministry in order to make my own people jealous, and thus save some of them.” An apostle to the nations whose heart is still with his own people: this is the Paul we love. He adds, “For if their rejection is the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance be but life from the dead!” When God's whole plan is complete it will feel like a resurrection. Then he gives two pictures: “If the part of the dough offered as first fruits is holy, then the whole batch is holy; and if the root is holy, then the branches also are holy.” In the Law a cake from the first of the dough was set aside for God (Numbers 15:17–21), and the whole baking was blessed by it. The patriarchs and the faith of Israel are that first portion and that root. In the verses that follow Paul will say that we Gentiles are wild branches grafted onto the tree, and that the root bears us and not we the root (Romans 11:17–24). Every Qurbono shows this. We sing the psalms of David, we cry Alleluia, a Hebrew word, we read Moses and the prophets before the Gospel, and our whole prayer breathes the air of the Scriptures of Israel. The Church of St Thomas in India has always lived on that root.

The Gospel brings us down from the mountain. “On the next day, when they had come down from the mountain, a great crowd met him.” The day before, on the mountain, Peter, James and John had seen the glory of Jesus; now, in the valley, there is a father in anguish. “Teacher, I beg you to look at my son; he is my only child. Suddenly a spirit seizes him, and all at once he shrieks.” Luke notes, as he does for the widow's son at Nain and for Jairus's daughter, that it is an only child. “I begged your disciples to cast it out, but they could not.” Jesus answers with a sigh that belongs to every teacher who has seen a lesson not understood: “You faithless and perverse generation, how much longer must I be with you and bear with you? Bring your son here.” It is not a verdict on any one people; it is the sorrow of a master whose own disciples have not yet learned. Then, “While he was coming, the demon dashed him to the ground in convulsions. But Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit, healed the boy, and gave him back to his father.” The boy has been thrown down, but he is raised and given back; the Lord, who had come down the mountain, takes hold of him. St Mark's longer account records the father's cry, “I believe; help my unbelief!” and says that the boy lay like a corpse until Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him up (Mark 9:24, 26–27). Stumbling is not falling. As the psalm says, “though we stumble, we shall not fall headlong, for the Lord holds us by the hand.” And Luke ends: “And all were astounded at the greatness of God.” In the valley, where the sick are, there the greatness of God is seen.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul asks today: have they stumbled so as to fall? By no means! (Romans 11:11). He is speaking of his own people, whom he never stops loving. And he gives us a picture to hold on to: if the root is holy, then the branches also are holy (Romans 11:16). We are branches that have been grafted on, and the root is the faith of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the Scriptures of Israel, the psalms we sing at every Qurbono. So there is no room for pride, and no room for contempt, but only gratitude.

The Gospel brings us down from the mountain into the valley. A father cries out of the crowd: Teacher, I beg you to look at my son; he is my only child (Luke 9:38). His son is thrown to the ground again and again. The disciples tried and could not. And Jesus says, with a sigh: Bring your son here (Luke 9:41). Then he rebukes the unclean spirit, heals the boy, and gives him back to his father (Luke 9:42).

Do you see how much love there is in those words, gave him back to his father? Jesus does not heal the child and keep him; he returns him to his father's arms. There is not a single sorrow of a parent that he does not see. And when we ourselves have stumbled, when we feel that we have fallen for good, it is the same Lord who takes our hand: though we stumble, we shall not fall headlong, for the Lord holds us by the hand (Psalm 37:24).

So today let us bring someone to Jesus: a sick relative, a troubled friend, or ourselves. Let us say with the father in Mark's Gospel: I believe; help my unbelief (Mark 9:24). And let us thank God for the root that bears us. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever fallen down while running and thought you could never get up? Then someone gave you a hand and you were up again. Today's Gospel is about a father whose little boy was very sick. The father brought his boy to Jesus, and Jesus made him well and gave him back to his father, safe and sound. Jesus always takes our hand when we fall. Also, St Paul tells us we are like branches of a big tree, and the roots of the tree are the people of the Bible, Abraham and Moses and David. We say thank you to them in our hearts. This week, when you fall or make a mistake, say: Jesus, hold my hand. And ask Jesus to help a child who is sick.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you came down from the mountain to the valley of the suffering and gave the child back to his father. We thank you for the root from which we have been fed, the faith of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob and the Scriptures of your people, and we ask you to hasten the day when all Israel will be saved and all the nations will be one in your love. When we stumble, hold us by the hand; when we cannot cast out our own evil, say to us, “Bring him here.” Heal the sick, comfort their parents, and make us glad to praise the greatness of God. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today turn on a single question: has everything been lost? In the Epistle Paul asks whether Israel's stumbling means that Israel has fallen for good, and answers, “By no means!” The root is holy, and so are the branches. In the Gospel, coming down from the mountain of the Transfiguration, Jesus meets a father whose only son is held by an evil spirit that the disciples could not drive out, and he gives the boy back whole. The key words are holy root and given back.

Romans 11:11–16 · The root is holy

Setting.

These verses belong to the same argument of Romans 9–11 as yesterday's, but they come earlier in it. Paul has just said that God has not rejected his people (11:1–10): a remnant of Israel believes, as Paul himself does. Now he asks a second question: those who have not accepted Christ, have they fallen beyond recovery?

Paul writes to a church in Rome that includes many Gentile Christians, and he speaks to them directly (v. 13). He is afraid they may look down on the Jews who have not believed. He wants them to see that they have been brought into a story that began with Israel, and that they must not boast.

Structure.

  • v. 11: Israel's stumbling is not a fall; through it salvation has come to the Gentiles.
  • v. 12: if their stumbling brings riches to the world, how much more their full inclusion.
  • vv. 13–14: Paul, apostle to the Gentiles, honours his ministry in the hope of saving some of his own people.
  • v. 15: their rejection has meant reconciliation for the world; their acceptance will be life from the dead.
  • v. 16: two images: the first fruits and the dough; the root and the branches.

Key words.

  • ptaiō / piptō, to stumble / to fall: Paul distinguishes between the two. A man who stumbles may rise again; one who falls is down. Israel has stumbled, but the Lord who calls them has not let them go.
  • parazēlōsai, to make jealous: Paul takes a word from Deuteronomy 32:21, where God says he will make Israel jealous with “a foolish nation”. Here he hopes that the Gentiles' joy in Christ may awaken a holy longing in his own people to share it.
  • plērōma, full inclusion, fulness: The word means filling up to the full measure. Paul looks to the day when Israel is included in its fulness, a day whose time is in God's hands.
  • aparchē, first fruits: The first of the dough or the harvest offered to God makes all the rest holy (Numbers 15:17–21). Paul sees in the patriarchs and in the first believers a first fruit that sanctifies the whole people.

Verse by verse.

“So I ask, have they stumbled so as to fall? By no means!” (11:11)

“By no means” is Paul's strong refusal of a thought that the Roman Christians may have held. God's call to Israel has not failed.

“For if their rejection is the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance be but life from the dead!” (11:15)

“Their rejection” does not mean that God has cast them out, for in 11:1 Paul has said the opposite. It means the setting aside of those who have not yet accepted the Gospel, through which the Gospel has gone to the world. “Life from the dead” may refer to the general resurrection at the end, or to a great spiritual renewal; Catholic commentators have read it in both ways.

“if the root is holy, then the branches also are holy.” (11:16)

The holiness of the patriarchs passes to the whole people. The next verses (11:17–24) will take up the image of the olive tree and tell the Gentiles that they do not carry the root, but the root carries them.

How the Church reads it.

The Church gathers from these verses a teaching that she has repeated since the Second Vatican Council: God has not rejected his people, the Jews remain beloved because of the patriarchs, and the Church “cannot forget that she received the revelation of the Old Testament through the people with whom God, in his inexpressible mercy, concluded the Ancient Covenant” (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839–840). The Church does not replace Israel; she is grafted into the root that supports her. The Catechism teaches that the Church's own pilgrimage looks to the day when Israel will recognise the Messiah, and that this day is in the hands of God (CCC 674). Meanwhile the Church works and prays with respect for the Jewish people, and rejects every hatred and every persecution of them (Nostra Aetate 4).

Luke 9:37–43 · He gave him back to his father

Setting.

The scene follows the Transfiguration. Peter, James and John had been with Jesus on the mountain, where they saw his glory and heard the Father's voice, “This is my Son, my Chosen; listen to him!” (9:35). “On the next day” they come down, and a crowd is waiting. The same incident is told in Matthew 17:14–21 and Mark 9:14–29; Luke tells it more briefly and puts the weight on the father and his only son.

Luke notes three times in his Gospel that someone's only child is in danger: Jairus's daughter (8:42), the widow's son at Nain (7:12) and here. In each case Jesus gives the child back to the parent. Luke is also fond of showing Jesus' compassion for the parent's sorrow.

Structure.

  • v. 37: after the mountain, a great crowd meets Jesus.
  • vv. 38–40: the father begs for his only son; the disciples could not cast out the spirit.
  • v. 41: Jesus' lament and his command, “Bring your son here.”
  • v. 42: the spirit throws the boy down; Jesus rebukes it and gives the boy back to his father.
  • v. 43: all are astounded at the greatness of God.

Key words.

  • monogenēs, only child: Luke uses the word for the daughter of Jairus, the widow's son and this boy. It is the word John later uses of Jesus himself, the Father's “only Son”. The father's grief points to the Father who will give his only Son.
  • diestrammenē, perverse: Jesus' words recall Deuteronomy 32:5, where Moses speaks of a “crooked and perverse generation”. Moses had also come down from a mountain to find the people in failure (Exodus 32). Luke has placed Moses on the mountain of the Transfiguration (9:30), and the likeness is deliberate.
  • megaleiotēs, greatness, majesty: A rare word in the New Testament. Peter uses it of the glory of Christ seen on the holy mountain (2 Peter 1:16). The people see in the healing the same majesty that the three disciples saw on the mountain.

Verse by verse.

“I beg you to look at my son; he is my only child.” (9:38)

The father does not give orders; he asks Jesus to “look upon” his son, with the word used elsewhere of God's gracious regard for a person in need. It is the prayer of every parent.

“You faithless and perverse generation, how much longer must I be with you and bear with you?” (9:41)

This is a sigh and not an outburst. Jesus speaks to everyone present, the crowd and the disciples who could not heal, not as one outside Israel, but from within the long history of God's patience with his people; the words come from Moses' own song. The words are not a judgment on the Jewish people.

“healed the boy, and gave him back to his father.” (9:42)

The same phrase is said of the widow's son at Nain, who was given back to his mother (7:15). Jesus does not only cure; he restores the child to his family. Salvation in Luke is always a gift that gives people back to one another.

“And all were astounded at the greatness of God.” (9:43)

The people see more than a cure: they see God at work. Luke moves at once to the Passion prediction (9:44): the glory of God and the cross belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees here a sign of Jesus' victory over the evil one. The Catechism teaches that Jesus' exorcisms show that his kingdom is stronger than Satan's (CCC 550), and the Church carries on this ministry in Christ's name, with prudence, in the rite of exorcism (CCC 1673). The same paragraph says that the Church takes care, before an exorcism, to be sure that it is the presence of the Evil One and not an illness. The Church prays for the sick and for their families with equal care. The scene is also a lesson on discipleship: the disciples could not heal because they trusted their own strength; Mark records Jesus' answer, “This kind can come out only through prayer” (Mark 9:29). The Church returns to the Lord in prayer, above all in the Holy Qurbono, for those whom she cannot help herself.

Questions people ask

Was the boy suffering from epilepsy, or from an evil spirit?

The Gospels describe the sufferings of the boy carefully; Matthew uses a word that is often translated “epileptic” (17:15). The Gospel writers do not separate medicine from the spiritual struggle as we sometimes do. The Church today asks that sickness always be treated with care and with every medical help, and that exorcism be used only after the possibility of illness has been properly excluded (CCC 1673). What the passage teaches is that Jesus has authority over every power that harms people, whatever name we give it.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “the root is holy”. How does my gratitude to the people through whom God first spoke show itself in the way I speak about them?
  • A father brings his only son to Jesus and begs. Whom do I bring to Jesus in prayer today, and do I trust that he “gives back” what he heals?
