Word for the day

“For the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.” 1 Corinthians 1:18

Reflection

In the first week of June the farmers of Kerala watch the sky. When the monsoon breaks, they plant: paddy seedlings in the flooded fields, pepper cuttings at the foot of the trees, tapioca stems in the wet red earth. Nothing looks less impressive than a stick pushed into mud. Yet a few months later there is a harvest. Today's readings speak of God's way of working, which is very like the farmer's in June: small, hidden, apparently foolish, and in the end astonishingly fruitful. St Paul begins with the hardest example of all: “For the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.” To the educated people of Corinth, a city proud of its orators and its schools, a crucified Jew from Galilee was not a message of salvation but a scandal. The cross was the punishment for slaves and rebels, a thing one did not mention in polite company. And Paul builds everything on it.

He knows the objections and names them: “For Jews demand signs and Greeks desire wisdom, but we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles, but to those who are the called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.” Paul is himself a Jew, and he is not mocking anyone; he is describing two honest human hungers, the hunger for a mighty deed and the hunger for a convincing argument, and saying that God has answered both in a way no one expected. The sign is the Son of God dying out of love; the wisdom is the plan of the Father to save the world through weakness. “For God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God's weakness is stronger than human strength.” St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, marvelled that twelve unlettered fishermen, armed only with the message of a crucified man, had persuaded the cities and the philosophers of the world; he said that this itself was the greatest proof that the Cross is the power of God (Homilies on 1 Corinthians 4, in substance). We who make the sign of the Cross so easily, on our foreheads, over our food, over our children at night, should remember what a bold thing it is: we are signing ourselves with God's foolishness and declaring that it is wiser than anything the world can offer.

In the Gospel Jesus gives two small pictures of the same truth. “What is the kingdom of God like? And to what should I compare it? It is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in the garden; it grew and became a tree, and the birds of the air made nests in its branches.” And again: “It is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” The mustard seed was proverbial among the Jews for smallness. Three measures of flour was a very large amount, enough to feed a whole household and its guests, and a little yeast, hidden inside it, changes all of it. Neither the seed nor the yeast makes any noise. Both work from within and both take time. This is how the Holy Spirit, poured out at Pentecost, works in the Church and in each of us: through a word of Scripture that stays in the heart, through a Holy Qurbono attended faithfully on an ordinary weekday, through a small act of kindness that nobody sees. We grow impatient and want quick results, big crowds, visible success. The Lord asks us to plant and to knead, and to trust him for the growth. As the rains come down on the fields this week, let us ask him to let the seed of his word fall into our hearts and to let the yeast of the Gospel work quietly through our homes and our parish.

Homily outline

Theme: “For the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.”

On Wednesday of the second week after Pentecost, in the season of monsoon sowing: the foolishness of the Cross, the mustard seed and the yeast, and God's small and hidden ways.

1. The foolishness of the Cross

  • Signs and wisdom: two honest hungers.
  • God answers both in Christ crucified.
  • Twelve fishermen and the cities of the world.
“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, nor are your ways my ways, says the Lord.” Isaiah 55:8
“May I never boast of anything except the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world.” Galatians 6:14

Apply: Put a small cross where you will see it at your desk or in your shop, and look at it when you are tempted to trust only in cleverness or money.

2. The mustard seed

  • The smallest of beginnings.
  • Growth is God's work.
  • Room in its branches for the birds.
“On the mountain height of Israel I will plant it, in order that it may produce boughs and bear fruit, and become a noble cedar. Under it every kind of bird will live; in the shade of its branches will nest winged creatures of every kind.” Ezekiel 17:23
“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.” 1 Corinthians 3:6

Apply: Begin one small, regular good practice this week (a daily decade of the Rosary, a weekly visit to a lonely person) and keep it up.

3. The yeast in the flour

  • Hidden but active.
  • It changes the whole batch.
  • A Christian in an office, a classroom, a family.
“For whoever has despised the day of small things shall rejoice” Zechariah 4:10
“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” Matthew 5:16

Apply: Choose one place where you spend much time (work, school, a WhatsApp group) and be quietly kind and honest there all this week.

God works like a farmer in June: he plants what looks small and foolish, and waits for the harvest.

The Cross, the seed and the yeast all say the same thing: trust his hidden power.

Take-home resolution: Do not despise small things: plant, knead, and leave the growth to God.

Full homily

Dear friends, in these days the monsoon has come and our farmers are planting. A seedling in the mud does not look like much. But they plant in hope, because they know what will come. Today God's word tells us that he works the same way.

St Paul says: For the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God (1 Corinthians 1:18). Some people wanted miracles, others wanted clever arguments. God gave the world something nobody expected: his Son dying on a cross out of love. For God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God's weakness is stronger than human strength (1 Corinthians 1:25).

Jesus gives two pictures. The kingdom of God is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in the garden; it grew and became a tree (Luke 13:19). And it is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened (Luke 13:21). The seed is tiny; the yeast is hidden. But they change everything, slowly, from inside.

So let us not despise small things. A prayer said every night, a Holy Qurbono on a weekday, a kind word, a small gift to the poor: these are seeds and yeast. We plant; God gives the growth. And let us make the sign of the Cross with faith, because the Cross, which looks like weakness, is the power of God. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever planted a seed? You put a tiny seed into the soil, you water it, and you wait. One day a little green shoot comes up! Jesus said God's kingdom is like a tiny mustard seed that grows into a big plant where birds make their nests. Every time you say a prayer, share your snack, or help your mother, you are planting a little seed for God. Do not worry if it is small. God will make it grow.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, crucified and risen, you are the power of God and the wisdom of God. When the world laughs at the message of your Cross, keep us faithful to it; when we look for great signs and clever words, turn our eyes back to your love poured out for us. Make your kingdom grow in us like the mustard seed and work in us like yeast hidden in the flour. Bless the rains that fall on our fields in these days and the seed that our farmers sow. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are about how God works: not through what the world counts as strong and impressive, but through what looks small and weak. Paul preaches “Christ crucified”, which seems foolish, yet is “the power of God and the wisdom of God”. Jesus compares the kingdom to a tiny mustard seed and to a little yeast hidden in a great mass of flour. The key words are the cross and small beginnings.

1 Corinthians 1:18–25 · The message about the cross

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, probably around AD 54–55, to a young Church he had founded a few years earlier. Corinth was a rich, proud port city, and the community was splitting into groups that boasted of their favourite teachers: “I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos” (1:12). Some were attracted by clever speech and philosophy.

Paul answers by going to the root: the Gospel is not a human philosophy to be admired but the message of a crucified Messiah. He quotes Isaiah 29:14 from the Greek Old Testament.

Structure.

  • v. 18: the message of the cross: foolishness to some, the power of God to others.
  • vv. 19–21: God overturns the wisdom of the world (Isaiah 29:14).
  • vv. 22–24: signs, wisdom, and Christ crucified.
  • v. 25: the foolishness and weakness of God.

Key words.

  • ho logos tou staurou, the message about the cross: Literally “the word of the cross”. Crucifixion was the most shameful death in the Roman world, kept for slaves and rebels. To preach a crucified man as Lord sounded absurd.
  • mōria, foolishness: The word is related to our “moron”. Paul uses the very insult that was thrown at Christians and turns it around.
  • skandalon, stumbling block: A trap or an obstacle that makes one trip. A crucified Messiah seemed to contradict what many expected of God's anointed one.

Verse by verse.

“For the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.” (1:18)

Paul does not say “the message about wisdom” or “the message about miracles”. The power of God is found in the cross. The verbs are in the present: salvation is a road on which we are travelling.

“but we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles,” (1:23)

Paul, himself a Jew, describes the two great reactions he met in his preaching. He is not condemning a people; in the next verse he says that the called, “both Jews and Greeks”, find in Christ the power and wisdom of God.

“For God’s foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God’s weakness is stronger than human strength.” (1:25)

A bold paradox. God has no foolishness or weakness in himself; Paul means what looks foolish and weak to us, above all the cross.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it speaks of God's almighty power: it is shown most mysteriously in the voluntary humiliation and the Resurrection of his Son, so that Christ crucified is “the power of God and the wisdom of God” (CCC 272). The Church does not hide the cross; she places it above the altar and signs every Christian with it.

Luke 13:18–21 · The mustard seed and the yeast

Setting.

Luke places these two short parables right after Jesus heals a woman who had been bent over for eighteen years, on a sabbath in a synagogue (13:10–17). The leader of the synagogue was indignant, but “the entire crowd was rejoicing” (13:17). Luke then writes, “He said therefore”: the kingdom is already at work in such small signs. Matthew gives the same two parables together (Matthew 13:31–33).

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19: the mustard seed that grows into a tree where birds nest.
  • vv. 20–21: the yeast that leavens three measures of flour.

Key words.

  • sinapi, mustard: The black mustard of Palestine has a tiny seed, proverbial for smallness, yet it can grow into a large shrub, as tall as a man or more.
  • zymē, yeast: Usually an image of corruption in the Bible (Luke 12:1; 1 Corinthians 5:6–8). Here Jesus surprises his hearers by using it for the kingdom.
  • sata tria, three measures: A very large quantity of flour, enough to make bread for a great crowd. Sarah used the same amount when she baked for the three visitors at Mamre (Genesis 18:6).

Verse by verse.

“What is the kingdom of God like? And to what should I compare it?” (13:18)

Jesus looks for images from everyday life. The kingdom cannot be defined in one sentence; it is shown in pictures that set us thinking.

“it grew and became a tree, and the birds of the air made nests in its branches.” (13:19)

The words echo Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4, where a great tree shelters every kind of bird, a picture of a kingdom that gives shelter to the nations. The small seed becomes a home for many.

“It is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” (13:21)

The Greek verb translated “mixed in” means literally “hid”. The yeast works unseen, from within, until the whole is changed.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church is on earth “the initial budding forth” of the kingdom, and that while she slowly grows she longs for the completed kingdom (Lumen Gentium 5). St Ambrose, in his commentary on Luke, sees Christ himself in the mustard seed, small and despised, buried in the earth, and rising to give shelter to all (in substance). The parables give courage to small parishes, to families and to all who think their faithfulness makes no difference.

Questions people ask

If the Church is the beginning of the kingdom, why does she look so weak and small in many places?

Because God's way is the way of the mustard seed and the yeast. The kingdom grows hidden, from within, often through what seems insignificant: a family praying, a teacher's patience, a sick person offering suffering. Its growth is God's work, not a matter of numbers. Paul says the same in today's Epistle: God's weakness is stronger than human strength.

For study and teaching

  • Where in my life have I been tempted to trust in what looks impressive rather than in the cross?
  • What small seed of faith or kindness can I sow today, trusting God to make it grow?
