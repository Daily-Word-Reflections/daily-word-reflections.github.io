Word for the day

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth” John 16:13

Reflection

St Paul's letters are full of names we pass over quickly, and today the Church stops at one of them. Epaphroditus was a member of the little church at Philippi, sent by them to Paul in prison with a gift and with orders to stay and help him. In prison he fell ill, so ill that he nearly died, and when he recovered he was homesick and worried that his people were anxious about him. So Paul sends him home with a letter, and with a testimonial that any of us would treasure: “Still, I think it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother and co-worker and fellow soldier, your messenger and minister to my need.” Brother, co-worker, fellow soldier: three titles of friendship, labour and struggle. And the word translated “messenger” is the Greek word apostolos: the Philippians had sent him, and he was their apostle to Paul. The Church in her season of Pentecost remembers that the apostolic mission does not belong only to the Twelve; it is carried on by thousands of people whose names never reach a history book.

Paul asks the Philippians for something important: “Welcome him then in the Lord with all joy, and honor such people, because he came close to death for the work of Christ, risking his life to make up for those services that you could not give me.” Honour such people. Every parish is held up by an Epaphroditus or two: the catechism teacher who has given thirty Sunday mornings a year for twenty years, the sacristan who opens the church before dawn, the woman who sits with the sick when no one else will go, the young people who carry chairs and clean the hall. In these first weeks of June, when the schools of Kerala reopen in the monsoon rain, we might think also of teachers, bus drivers and cooks in school kitchens who will spend themselves for our children all year. And we may think of the many nurses from our parishes who serve in hospitals far from home, like Epaphroditus, sometimes at real risk to their health. Paul also says something tender: “God had mercy on him, and not only on him but on me also, so that I would not have one sorrow after another.” The great apostle does not pretend to be beyond grief; he thanks God for sparing his friend. St John Chrysostom remarks that Paul is not ashamed to show that he was anxious and would have mourned, because love for the brethren is no weakness in a saint (Homilies on Philippians 9, in substance).

The Gospel takes us back to the Last Supper: “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” Jesus is a patient teacher. He does not pour everything into the disciples at once; he knows what they can carry. The Spirit continues his teaching through the whole life of the Church, leading her deeper into the one truth that Jesus is, not adding a new Gospel but opening up the one Gospel: “he will take what is mine and declare it to you.” This is what happens in the councils of the Church, in the prayer of the saints, in the slow growth of a believer's understanding over a lifetime. It happens to each of us too. There are things in our faith we could not bear at fifteen and understand at fifty; there are crosses whose meaning we will see only later. Like a good teacher at the start of a school year, the Spirit begins where we are. Our part is to stay in the classroom: to keep coming to the Holy Qurbono, where the word is read and the Bread of Life is given, and to let him guide us, one step at a time, into all the truth.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul introduces us to a man most of us hardly know: Epaphroditus. Paul calls him my brother and co-worker and fellow soldier, your messenger and minister to my need (Philippians 2:25). The Philippians had sent him to help Paul in prison, and he became so ill that he nearly died.

Paul says: Welcome him then in the Lord with all joy, and honor such people (Philippians 2:29). Every parish is held up by people like Epaphroditus, catechists, sacristans, those who care for the sick, those who clean and arrange. This month the schools reopen, and many teachers and staff will work hard for our children. Let us honour such people, and thank them.

In the Gospel Jesus says: I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth (John 16:12–13). The Holy Spirit is a patient teacher. He teaches the Church through the centuries, and he teaches each of us through our lives, step by step.

So let us stay in his school. Let us keep coming to the Holy Qurbono, keep reading the word, keep praying, even when we do not understand everything. And let us be co-workers like Epaphroditus, giving ourselves for the work of Christ without asking for honour. Amen.

For children

Children, schools are opening again! Your teachers will help you learn new things, a little at a time. Jesus said that the Holy Spirit is like a teacher too: he helps us understand God a little more every day. Today we also heard about Epaphroditus, a helper of St Paul who worked so hard that he became sick. St Paul said: honour people like him. Who helps you every day? Your parents, your teachers, your bus driver? Say thank you to them this week!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sent your apostles and their co-workers to carry the Gospel, and you send your Spirit of truth to guide your Church into all the truth. We thank you for every Epaphroditus among us, for those who serve without being noticed, for teachers and catechists, for nurses and carers, for those who risk their health for others. Have mercy on them in sickness, as you had mercy on your servant, and spare them one sorrow after another. Teach us patiently, as we are able to bear it, and lead us one step at a time into the fullness of your truth. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today a little-known name meets one of the greatest promises of the Gospel. Paul sends home Epaphroditus, who nearly died in his service, and tells the Philippians to “honor such people”. In the Gospel Jesus promises that the Spirit of truth will guide the disciples into all the truth. The Spirit's work is seen not only in great teaching but also in the quiet courage of people like Epaphroditus. The key words are service and truth.

Philippians 2:25–30 · Honor such people

Setting.

Paul writes to the church at Philippi from prison (1:7, 13); scholars discuss whether he was held in Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea. The Philippians had sent Epaphroditus to him with a gift (4:18) and to stay and help him. In Paul's service he fell gravely ill. Now recovered, he is being sent home, probably carrying this letter. Just before, Paul has spoken of sending Timothy (2:19–24). Both men are living examples of the mind of Christ that Paul has just described in the great hymn of 2:5–11.

Structure.

  • v. 25: who Epaphroditus is: brother, co-worker, fellow soldier, messenger and minister.
  • vv. 26–27: his longing for home, his illness and God's mercy.
  • v. 28: why Paul sends him back.
  • vv. 29–30: welcome him with joy and honour such people.

Key words.

  • apostolos, messenger: The word from which “apostle” comes. Here it has its plain sense: one sent, the delegate of the church at Philippi. Paul gives this ordinary helper a title of honour.
  • leitourgos, minister: A word for public service, used in the Greek Old Testament for priestly service in the Temple; from it comes “liturgy”. Paul uses the same family of words for the Philippians' help in 2:30 (“services”) and calls their gift “a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God” (4:18).
  • paraboleusamenos, risking: A rare word for staking everything, as a gambler stakes his money. Epaphroditus put his life on the table for the work of Christ.

Verse by verse.

“He was indeed so ill that he nearly died. But God had mercy on him, and not only on him but on me also, so that I would not have one sorrow after another.” (2:27)

Paul, through whom God worked healings (Acts 19:11–12), did not heal his friend at will. He calls the recovery mercy, for both of them. An apostle grieves and is relieved like any friend.

“Welcome him then in the Lord with all joy, and honor such people,” (2:29)

Paul is perhaps forestalling any thought that Epaphroditus had come home early or failed in his task. He asks the church to receive him as a hero, not as one who fell short.

“because he came close to death for the work of Christ,” (2:30)

The “work of Christ” here is very concrete: carrying a gift and caring for a prisoner. To serve a brother in need is to serve Christ: “I was in prison and you visited me” (Matthew 25:36).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Epaphroditus the many who serve without becoming famous: those who carry help to prisoners, who nurse the sick, who keep a parish going. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Christian vocation is by its very nature a vocation to the apostolate, so that every member of the Church shares in her mission (Apostolicam Actuositatem 2). The passage also shows how the Church prays for the sick: with honest anxiety, with hope, and with thanksgiving when God shows mercy.

John 16:12–15 · He will guide you into all the truth

Setting.

Still in the farewell discourse. Jesus has said that it is to the disciples' advantage that he goes away, for otherwise the Advocate will not come to them (16:7), and that the Spirit will prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment (16:8–11). Now comes the last of the five sayings about the Advocate in these chapters (14:16–17; 14:26; 15:26; 16:7–11; 16:12–15).

Structure.

  • v. 12: many things still to say, but they cannot bear them now.
  • v. 13: the Spirit of truth will guide them into all the truth, speaking only what he hears.
  • v. 14: he will glorify Jesus, taking what is his and declaring it.
  • v. 15: all that the Father has belongs to the Son.

Key words.

  • bastazein, to bear: To carry a load. Before the cross and the Resurrection the disciples are not yet able to carry what Jesus has to tell them. Understanding grows as God gives it.
  • hodēgēsei, will guide: To lead along the way, as a guide leads travellers. The Greek Bible uses the same verb in Psalm 25:5: “Lead me in your truth.” The Spirit does not lead to new truths beside Christ, but further into the truth that Christ is (14:6).
  • anangelei, will declare: To announce, to report. The Spirit passes on what he receives; he does not speak on his own. The word occurs three times in three verses (vv. 13, 14, 15).

Verse by verse.

“I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now.” (16:12)

God teaches as a good teacher does, at the pace of the learner.

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth;” (16:13)

“All the truth”, not “all truths”: the whole truth of Christ, unfolded over the ages.

“All that the Father has is mine.” (16:15)

A plain statement of the unity of the Father and the Son. The Fathers used verses like this against those who denied that the Son is equal to the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees here how the one Revelation given in Christ is understood ever more deeply under the Spirit's guidance. There will be no new public revelation before the Lord's return (CCC 66), yet “there is a growth in the understanding of the realities and the words which have been handed down” (Dei Verbum 8; CCC 94). St Gregory of Nazianzus, explaining why the divinity of the Spirit was made plain only step by step, quoted this verse: the Old Testament proclaimed the Father openly and the Son more obscurely, the New manifested the Son and suggested the Spirit, and now the Spirit dwells among us and gives a clearer showing of himself (Oration 31, in substance).

Questions people ask

Why did Paul not simply heal Epaphroditus?

Healing is God's gift, not something an apostle commands at will. Paul also left Trophimus ill at Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20), and he himself carried a “thorn” that God did not remove (2 Corinthians 12:7–9). Illness is not a sign of weak faith. Paul prays, hopes and gives thanks, and he sees God's mercy in the recovery when it comes.

For study and teaching

  • Who are the people like Epaphroditus in my parish, serving quietly? How can I “honor such people” this week?
  • Jesus says, “you cannot bear them now.” Which truth of faith have I come to understand only slowly, and how did the Spirit lead me to it?
