Word for the day

“His name is John.” Luke 1:63

Reflection

The Church keeps the earthly birthday of very few saints. For most, she celebrates the day of their death, their birth into heaven. But she keeps the birth of Christ, the birth of his Mother, and the birth of John the Baptist, whom our tradition calls Yohannan Mamdana, John the Baptizer. St Augustine already remarked that the Church honours only two births with a solemn feast in his day, that of the Lord and that of John, because John's birth was itself part of the good news (Sermon 287, in substance). Before he was born, the angel told Zechariah: “even before his birth he will be filled with the Holy Spirit.” And when the Mother of God, carrying the Lord in her womb, came to greet Elizabeth, “the child leaped in her womb.” The forerunner was sanctified by the presence of Christ before he saw the light of day. The feast falls six months before Christmas, as Luke's Gospel suggests, and Augustine noticed that after John's birth the days of the northern year begin to shorten, while after Christ's birth they begin to lengthen: the creation itself seems to repeat John's words, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30; Augustine, Sermons on the Nativity of John, in substance).

St Luke tells the birth simply: “Now the time came for Elizabeth to give birth, and she bore a son. Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” An old couple who had waited all their lives now hold a child, and the whole village shares their joy. Then comes the naming. “On the eighth day they came to circumcise the child, and they were going to name him Zechariah after his father.” In our own families it is the custom to name the first son after his grandfather, and everyone knows the quarrels that can follow when parents choose otherwise. Elizabeth breaks the custom: “No; he is to be called John.” The relatives object: “None of your relatives has this name.” They turn to Zechariah, who has been silent for nine months since he doubted the angel in the temple. “He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, ‘His name is John.’ And all of them were amazed.” The name was not the family's choice but God's, and it means that the Lord has shown favour. The moment Zechariah obeys, his silence ends: “Immediately his mouth was opened and his tongue freed, and he began to speak, praising God.” The tongue that was tied by doubt is loosened by obedience, and its first words are praise.

The neighbours were filled with awe, and the news ran through the hill country of Judea. “All who heard them pondered them and said, ‘What then will this child become?’ For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.” That question is asked over every cradle and at every baptismal font. This month, as the schools of Kerala reopen in the rain and children set out with new books and new uniforms, parents ask it again: what will this child become? John became a prophet of the desert, whose food was locusts and wild honey, a man of prayer and fasting who prepared a people for the Lord. It is fitting that his feast falls within the Apostles' Fast, for he prepared the Apostles too: St John's Gospel tells us that Andrew, the brother of Simon Peter, was first a disciple of John, and that it was John who pointed him to Jesus with the words “Look, here is the Lamb of God!” (John 1:36). The greatest of the prophets was the one who sent his own disciples away from himself to Christ.

The Epistle of the feast gives the shape of such a life: “Pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.” Holiness is not a decoration for a few; it is the road by which anyone at all comes to see God. The letter warns against “a root of bitterness” (Hebrews 12:15) that springs up and defiles many, and against becoming “like Esau, an immoral and godless person, who sold his birthright for a single meal.” John never sold his birthright. He would not claim to be the Messiah when the crowds wanted him to, and he would not keep silent before Herod when silence would have saved his life. The fast trains us in the same freedom of heart: to refuse the single meal, the comfort, the flattery, for which we might trade our place as children of God. Today the Panchangam also remembers Cheppattu Philipose Ramban, and gives for his memory the words, “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” We know little of him beyond his name and his title, but the Church asks us to remember those monks, priests and teachers who handed on the faith in our land, and to be, like them and like John, faithful servants whom the Master finds at work when he comes.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate a birthday. The Church keeps the birthday of very few saints, but she keeps the birth of John the Baptist, because his birth was part of the good news. Before he was born he was filled with the Holy Spirit, and when the Mother of God came to visit Elizabeth, the child leaped in her womb (Luke 1:41).

St Luke tells us that when Elizabeth bore a son, her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her (Luke 1:58). Then they wanted to name the child after his father. But Elizabeth said: No; he is to be called John (Luke 1:60). And Zechariah, who had been silent for months, asked for a writing tablet and wrote, His name is John (Luke 1:63). At once his mouth was opened and he began to speak, praising God (Luke 1:64). Obedience opened his lips, and his first words were praise.

The people asked: What then will this child become? For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him (Luke 1:66). John became the voice in the desert, the man of fasting and prayer who prepared the way of the Lord and pointed his own disciples to Jesus. Some of the Apostles were first his disciples. In this Apostles' Fast we thank God for him.

The Epistle tells us how to follow him: Pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord (Hebrews 12:14). Do not be like Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal (Hebrews 12:16). Today we also remember Cheppattu Philipose Ramban and all who spoke the word of God to us. Let us imitate their faith, let us pray for our children, and let us say with John: He must increase, but I must decrease. Amen.

For children

Children, today is the birthday of John the Baptist! His parents were very old, and God gave them a baby as a surprise. Everyone wanted to call him by his father's name, but God had chosen the name John. His father wrote it on a little board: His name is John! When John grew up, he told everyone: Look, there is Jesus, the Lamb of God! You have just begun a new school year. Like John, you can help your friends to know Jesus, by being kind and by praying for them.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Lamb of God, your forerunner John leaped for joy in his mother's womb at the greeting of your holy Mother, and his birth filled his parents and neighbours with gladness. As you loosened the tongue of Zechariah when he obeyed your word, open our lips to praise you. Give us the holiness without which no one will see you, keep us from every root of bitterness, and let us never sell our birthright for a single meal. Bless the children of our families as they begin a new school year, and let your hand be with them as it was with John. Through the prayers of John the Baptist, and in memory of your servant Philipose Ramban and of all who spoke your word to us, make us faithful servants, ready for your coming. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church keeps the birth of Yohannan Mamdana, John the Baptist. The Gospel tells of his birth and naming: “His name is John”, and with those words his father's tongue is set loose to praise God. The epistle from Hebrews may seem at first a surprising choice, but it describes the life John would live: “Pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.” John lived that holiness in the desert, and he was the one who saw the Lord and pointed him out to others. The Panchangam also keeps today the memory of Cheppattu Philipose Ramban, a monk-priest, with its own readings: Hebrews 13:7–16, “Remember your leaders… and imitate their faith”, and Matthew 24:45–51, the faithful and wise servant whom the master finds at work.

We study the two readings of the feast. The key words are holiness, grace and name.

Hebrews 12:14–17 · The holiness without which no one will see the Lord

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews does not name its author; the Church has long read it with the letters of Paul. Chapter 12 opens with the image of a race: “let us run with perseverance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus” (12:1–2). The author then speaks of hardship as the discipline of a loving Father (12:5–11) and urges the tired to “lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees” (12:12).

Our verses turn from encouragement to warning. Right after them comes the great contrast between Mount Sinai, with its fire and trembling, and Mount Zion, “the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:18–24). The warning is given to people who are on their way to that city, so that none of them should fall out on the road.

Structure.

  • v. 14: the double command: peace with all, and holiness.
  • v. 15: watch over one another: that no one misses grace, that no bitter root springs up.
  • vv. 16–17: the example of Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal.

Key words.

  • diōkete, pursue: A strong verb: to run after, to chase. Peace and holiness do not happen by themselves; they must be pursued with effort, like the runner in 12:1.
  • hagiasmos, holiness: The process of being made holy, a life set apart for God. It is God's gift (10:10), and it grows as we cooperate with him.
  • episkopountes, see to it: Literally “watching over”, the root of the word episkopos, bishop or overseer. Here the whole community is told to keep watch over one another.

Verse by verse.

“Pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.” (12:14)

To “see the Lord” is the final goal of the Christian life. Jesus says the same in the Beatitudes: “Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God” (Matthew 5:8).

“that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble, and through it many become defiled.” (12:15)

The image comes from Deuteronomy 29:18, where it describes a person who turns away from God and poisons others. Bitterness hidden in one heart can spread through a whole community.

“See to it that no one becomes like Esau, an immoral and godless person, who sold his birthright for a single meal.” (12:16)

The story is in Genesis 25:29–34. Esau traded what was holy and lasting for what was immediate. “Godless” translates bebēlos, one who treats holy things as common.

“he found no chance to repent, even though he sought the blessing with tears.” (12:17)

The blessing had already been given to Jacob, and Isaac could not take it back (Genesis 27:30–40). Commentators differ on whose change of mind is meant, Esau's or his father's. The point is that some losses, once chosen, cannot be undone in this life.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that all the faithful, of whatever state or rank, are called to the fullness of Christian life and the perfection of charity (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013). John the Baptist shows what that call can look like: a life stripped of everything that does not serve God, and a heart without envy, so that when people told him that everyone was going to Jesus, he answered, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30). The warning about Esau does not close the door of mercy. The Church teaches that there is no offence, however serious, that the Church cannot forgive, if the sinner truly repents (CCC 982).

Luke 1:57–66 · His name is John

Setting.

Luke begins his Gospel with two births told side by side. The angel Gabriel announces the birth of John to Zechariah in the Temple (1:5–25), and then the birth of Jesus to Mary in Nazareth (1:26–38). The two mothers meet at the Visitation, and the unborn John leaps in Elizabeth's womb (1:39–45). Now the first promise is fulfilled. The birth of Jesus will be told in the same pattern: birth, rejoicing, circumcision on the eighth day, naming (2:1–21). John is always shown as great, but always as the one who goes before a greater.

When Zechariah doubted the angel's word, he was told he would be unable to speak until these things took place (1:20). Circumcision on the eighth day was commanded in the Law (Genesis 17:12; Leviticus 12:3), and by this time it was the custom to give the child his name at the circumcision.

Structure.

  • vv. 57–58: the birth, and the joy of neighbours and relatives.
  • vv. 59–61: the eighth day: they want to name him Zechariah; Elizabeth says “John”.
  • vv. 62–64: Zechariah writes “His name is John”, and his mouth is opened.
  • vv. 65–66: fear and wonder through the hill country: “What then will this child become?”

Key words.

  • Yôḥānān, John: The Hebrew name means “The Lord has been gracious” or “the Lord has shown favour”. The name chosen by God sums up what is happening: God's mercy has come to an old couple and, through their child, to his people.
  • eleos, mercy: In verse 58 the neighbours hear that the Lord “had shown his great mercy” to Elizabeth. Mercy is the great word of Luke's first chapter: Mary sings of it (1:50, 54) and Zechariah will sing of it (1:72, 78).
  • pinakidion, writing tablet: A small wooden tablet, often coated with wax, on which one wrote with a pointed stylus. Zechariah's first words after months of silence are written: God's chosen name.

Verse by verse.

“Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” (1:58)

The angel had promised: “many will rejoice at his birth” (1:14). The promise begins to come true among the neighbours. Joy shared is a sign of God at work.

“But his mother said, “No; he is to be called John.”” (1:60)

Luke does not say how Elizabeth knew the name. Many think Zechariah had written it for her; others see her speaking by the Holy Spirit, with whom she was filled at the Visitation (1:41). Either way, the family custom gives way to God's word.

“He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, “His name is John.” And all of them were amazed.” (1:63)

Zechariah does not write “His name shall be John” but “His name is John”: the name has already been given by God. His obedience now undoes his earlier doubt.

“Immediately his mouth was opened and his tongue freed, and he began to speak, praising God.” (1:64)

His first words are praise. The words of his song, the Benedictus, follow in 1:68–79: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favorably on his people.”

““What then will this child become?” For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.” (1:66)

“The hand of the Lord” is an Old Testament way of speaking of God's power with his chosen ones, such as the prophets (Ezekiel 1:3). The question will be answered when John appears in the wilderness (3:1–6).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that John is the Lord's immediate forerunner, sent to prepare his way; “prophet of the Most High”, he surpasses all the prophets and is the last of them (CCC 523). In John the Holy Spirit completes the work of preparing a people for the Lord (CCC 717–720). The Church keeps the birthday of very few saints, because she usually honours the day of their death. She keeps the birth of Christ, of his Mother, and of John. St Augustine noted in his sermons for this feast that in his day the Church kept the birthday of only two, the Lord and John (in substance). The feast falls six months before the Lord's birth, as Luke indicates (1:26, 36).

Questions people ask

Why does the Church keep John's birthday, when for other saints she keeps the day of their death?

Because John's birth was itself part of the good news. He was filled with the Holy Spirit even before his birth (Luke 1:15) and leapt for joy in his mother's womb at the presence of the Lord (1:41, 44). The Church therefore holds that he was already sanctified when he was born. That is why, besides the Lord's own birth, she keeps the births of only the Mother of God and of John the Baptist. His martyrdom is remembered too, on its own day.

Hebrews says that Esau “found no chance to repent”. Can a person ever be beyond forgiveness?

No one who truly repents is beyond God's mercy. The Church teaches that there is no sin so serious that it cannot be forgiven when the sinner turns back to God (CCC 982). Hebrews speaks of the blessing that Esau could not get back, not of God refusing a contrite heart. The warning is that we should not treat holy gifts lightly, as if we could always recover them later. The time to turn to God is now.

Was Zechariah deaf as well as mute?

Luke says that the people “began motioning to his father” (1:62), which suggests that he could not hear either. The word used for him in 1:22, kōphos, can mean both unable to speak and unable to hear. Luke wants us to see a man shut into silence until God's word is fulfilled, and then opened to praise.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our tradition names John Yohannan Mamdana, John the Baptizer. In the West Syriac calendar he already appears in the Sundays of Suboro before Yaldo, where the announcement to Zechariah and the birth of John are read on their own Sundays as the Church prepares for the Lord's birth. Today the Panchangam keeps the birth again in summer, in the Apostles' Fast. The memory of Cheppattu Philipose Ramban, kept on the same day, joins John's example with the call of Hebrews 13 to remember those who spoke the word of God to us and to imitate their faith. Like John, every faithful monk and priest is a voice that points beyond himself to Christ, the Lamb of God whom we receive in the Holy Qurbono.

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews says, “Pursue peace with everyone.” With whom do I need to pursue peace actively, instead of waiting for it to happen?
  • Is there a “root of bitterness” in my heart or my family? What would it take to pull it out?
  • Elizabeth and Zechariah let God's name for their son replace the family custom. Where is God asking me to let his word come before what “everyone has always done”?
  • John's whole life was summed up in “He must increase, but I must decrease.” What would that mean in my work, my family and my parish this week?
