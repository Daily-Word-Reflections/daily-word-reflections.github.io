Word for the day

“And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it.” Matthew 16:18

Reflection

At Caesarea Philippi Jesus asks: “But who do you say that I am?” Simon Peter answers: “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” Jesus replies: “Blessed are you, Simon son of Jonah! For flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven. And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven.” The Church rests on the faith Peter confessed and on the office Christ gave him, continued in the Bishop of Rome.

After the Resurrection, by the lake, Jesus asks Peter three times: “Simon son of John, do you love me?” Three times Peter had denied him; three times he now confesses his love; three times Jesus entrusts him: “Feed my lambs… Tend my sheep… Feed my sheep.” Peter's authority is born from mercy and love, and it ends in martyrdom: “someone else will fasten a belt around you and take you where you do not wish to go.”

Paul writes from prison near the end of his life: “I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” The persecutor became the apostle of the nations; the fisherman became the rock. Both gave their lives in Rome under Nero, and the Church of Rome is built on their blood.

In Acts, Peter sleeps in prison between two soldiers, and “the church prayed fervently to God for him.” An angel comes, the chains fall from his hands. Today we pray for Peter's successor, Pope Leo XIV, and for our Major Archbishop, in communion with him. The Syro-Malankara Church entered this communion in 1930 precisely to be united with the see of Peter.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today we honour two very different men. Peter, a fisherman from Galilee, impulsive and warm-hearted, who denied Jesus three times. Paul, an educated Pharisee, who persecuted the Church. Christ chose both, changed both, and both gave their lives in Rome.

At Caesarea Philippi Jesus asked: “Who do you say that I am?” Peter answered: “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” And Jesus said: “You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom.” This is why we love and obey the Pope, Peter's successor. Our Malankara Church came into Catholic communion in 1930 precisely to be one with Peter.

But see how Peter's authority was born. After his denial, Jesus asked him three times: “Do you love me?” Peter answered: “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” And Jesus said: “Feed my sheep.” Authority in the Church is born from mercy and is exercised in love. A pope, a bishop, a priest, a parent: first love Jesus, then feed his sheep.

Paul, near his death, wrote: “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” May each of us say that at the end of our life. Today, let us pray for Pope Leo, our Major Archbishop and our bishop, as the Church prayed fervently for Peter in prison. Sts Peter and Paul, pray for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of the holy apostles Peter and Paul the readings trace the path of two men whom Christ called by name, sent out, and brought at last to martyrdom in Rome. The evening Gospel (Matthew 16:13–20) gives Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God”, and the Lord's reply, “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church.” In Genesis 12:1–8 the Lord calls Abram to leave his country for a land he will be shown, and Abram goes. Proverbs 13:1–9 praises the one who listens to correction and guards his mouth. In Daniel 1:1–9 the young Daniel, taken to Babylon, resolves not to defile himself with the king's food, faithful to God in a foreign capital. In Acts 12:6–19, while “the church prayed fervently to God for him” (12:5, just before the reading), an angel leads Peter out of Herod's prison and the chains fall from his wrists.

We study three readings more closely. In Isaiah the Lord says to his people in exile, “Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.” In 2 Timothy, Paul, facing death, looks back: “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” In John, the risen Lord asks Peter three times, “Do you love me?”, entrusts his flock to him, and foretells the death by which he would glorify God. The key words are called by name, poured out and love.

Isaiah 43:1–7 · I have called you by name, you are mine

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC. Many scholars think these chapters come from a prophet of the exile who continued the tradition of Isaiah of Jerusalem. They open with the cry “Comfort, O comfort my people” (40:1) and are full of tender promises.

Just before our passage the prophet has told the people that their suffering came because they did not walk in God's ways (42:24–25). Then, with the words “But now”, the tone changes completely. God does not cast off his sinful people. He reminds them that he created them, redeemed them and called them by name, and he promises to gather their children from every direction of the earth.

Structure.

  • v. 1: do not fear: I created you, I redeemed you, I called you by name.
  • v. 2: through water and fire, I will be with you.
  • vv. 3–4: you are precious in my sight; I give nations as your ransom.
  • vv. 5–7: do not fear: I will gather your sons and daughters from east and west, north and south.

Key words.

  • gā’altîkā, I have redeemed you: From gā’al, the duty of the next of kin, the gō’ēl, who buys back a relative sold into slavery or a family field that was lost (Leviticus 25:25). God acts as Israel's nearest kinsman.
  • qārā’tî bᵉšimkā, I have called you by name: To call by name is to know personally, to choose and to claim. God names his people as a father names a child.
  • kōper, ransom: The price paid to release someone. The language is poetic: the Lord says no price is too high for his people. Christians hear it fulfilled when Jesus gives “his life a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45).

Verse by verse.

“Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.” (43:1)

“Do not fear” comes twice in the passage (vv. 1, 5). The ground of courage is not our strength but God's claim on us.

“When you pass through the waters, I will be with you;” (43:2)

Israel remembered the Red Sea and the Jordan. Christian readers have heard here a promise for Baptism: God brings his people through the waters, not around them.

“Because you are precious in my sight, and honored, and I love you,” (43:4)

One of the most tender verses of the Old Testament. God's love does not depend on Israel's worth; it gives Israel its worth.

“everyone who is called by my name, whom I created for my glory, whom I formed and made.” (43:7)

The goal of the gathering is God's glory. A people called by God's name exists to show his goodness to the world.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God calls each one by name and that everyone's name is sacred (CCC 2158), and that in Baptism the Christian receives his name in the Church (CCC 2156). On the feast of Peter and Paul these verses take on a special colour. Jesus gave Simon a new name, Peter (Matthew 16:18), and called Saul on the Damascus road by his own name (Acts 9:4). Neither was chosen for his perfection: Peter would deny the Lord, and Paul had persecuted the Church. Both were redeemed, called by name, and made precious instruments of God's glory.

2 Timothy 4:6–10 · I have fought the good fight

Setting.

These verses read as Paul's farewell. In the letter he is a prisoner in Rome, abandoned by many, awaiting the end. Many scholars think that the Pastoral Letters were written after Paul's death by a disciple in his name, drawing on his memory and perhaps on his notes; others hold that Paul wrote them himself. Either way, the Church has always heard in this passage the authentic voice of the apostle's last witness.

Ancient tradition, going back to St Clement of Rome at the end of the first century (1 Clement 5), tells that Peter and Paul both bore witness by their death in Rome under the emperor Nero, in the 60s AD. Paul, a Roman citizen, was beheaded, tradition says, on the road to Ostia, where the Basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls now stands.

Structure.

  • v. 6: my life is being poured out; the time of departure has come.
  • v. 7: three verbs in the perfect tense: fought, finished, kept.
  • v. 8: the crown of righteousness, for me and for all who long for his appearing.
  • vv. 9–10: come quickly: Demas has deserted me; others are away.

Key words.

  • spendomai, I am being poured out as a libation: A drink offering of wine poured out at the altar (Numbers 28:7). Paul sees his coming death as an act of worship. He used the same image years earlier in Philippians 2:17.
  • analysis, departure: The word for loosing a ship from its moorings or striking a tent. Death is not an end for Paul but a setting out.
  • stephanos, crown: Not a king's diadem but the wreath given to the winner of a race. Paul has run the course; the Lord, the righteous judge, will give the prize.

Verse by verse.

“As for me, I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come.” (4:6)

Paul's death is joined to the sacrifice of Christ. The martyr does not merely suffer; he offers.

“I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” (4:7)

The first two images come from the games, the third from a steward's duty. Faith is a treasure entrusted to him, and he hands it on intact.

“From now on there is reserved for me the crown of righteousness,” (4:8)

The crown is “not only to me but also to all who have longed for his appearing”. Paul's hope is not private. Every faithful Christian shares in it.

“for Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me” (4:10)

Demas had been Paul's co-worker (Colossians 4:14; Philemon 24). The apostle's last days were lonely. Even great saints know the pain of being abandoned.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that our merit before God comes from the fact that God has freely chosen to associate us with the work of his grace (CCC 2008). The crown is truly given for the race that was run, yet the strength to run was God's gift. St Augustine, reflecting on this passage, says that when God crowns our merits he crowns his own gifts (in substance). Paul's words have comforted dying Christians for centuries, and they are fitting for priests and religious who have served long and faithfully: the fight was hard, the race long, but the faith was kept.

John 21:15–19 · Feed my sheep

Setting.

John 21 is an epilogue to the Gospel. The risen Jesus appears by the Sea of Tiberias to seven disciples who have gone back to fishing. After a night with no catch, at his word they haul in a net full of fish, and on the shore he has prepared a charcoal fire with fish and bread (21:9). The Greek word for that charcoal fire, anthrakia, appears only one other time in the New Testament: at the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter warmed himself and denied Jesus (18:18).

So by a charcoal fire the denial is healed. Three times Peter had denied being his disciple; three times now the Lord asks, “Do you love me?”

Structure.

  • v. 15: first question: “more than these?”; feed my lambs.
  • v. 16: second question; tend my sheep.
  • v. 17: third question; Peter is hurt; “Lord, you know everything”; feed my sheep.
  • vv. 18–19: Peter will stretch out his hands and be led where he does not wish to go; “Follow me.”

Key words.

  • agapas me… philō se, do you love me… I love you: John alternates two Greek verbs for love. Many scholars think the change is a matter of style. Others, including Pope Benedict XVI in a general audience of 2006, saw in it Jesus coming down to the measure of Peter's humbled love, until the third time he asks in Peter's own word.
  • boske… poimaine, feed… tend: To feed is to give food; to tend is the whole work of a shepherd: to guide, protect and govern. The Greek also alternates between lambs and sheep. The whole flock, small and grown, is entrusted to Peter.
  • ektenei tas cheiras sou, you will stretch out your hands: Early Christian writers, among them Tertullian, understood this as a reference to crucifixion. Verse 19 confirms that Jesus was speaking of the kind of death by which Peter would glorify God.

Verse by verse.

“Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?” (21:15)

Jesus calls him by his old name, Simon, as at their first meeting (1:42). Before Peter can feed the flock, his own heart must be made new.

“Peter felt hurt because he said to him the third time, “Do you love me?”” (21:17)

The third question touches the deepest wound. Peter no longer boasts, as he did at the Supper (13:37). He simply appeals to the Lord's knowledge of his heart.

“Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” (21:17)

Peter's love now rests not on his own strength but on Christ's knowledge. This is the humility from which true authority in the Church is born.

“After this he said to him, “Follow me.”” (21:19)

The first call by the lake (Mark 1:17) is renewed. This time Peter knows where following will lead: to the cross.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus entrusted a specific authority to Peter, the power of the keys, and that after his resurrection the Good Shepherd confirmed this mandate: “Feed my sheep” (CCC 553). The office of Peter continues in the Bishop of Rome, the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity both of the bishops and of the whole company of the faithful (Lumen Gentium 23; CCC 882). The Catechism also sees in Peter's conversion after his threefold denial a witness to the mercy of Jesus' look (CCC 1429). The one who holds the keys is a forgiven sinner, and the authority he receives is the authority of love.

Questions people ask

Is the “rock” Peter himself or Peter's faith?

The Fathers explored both, and the two do not compete. The Catechism says that Christ built his Church on the rock of the faith confessed by St Peter (CCC 424), and also that he made Simon, whom he named Peter, the rock of his Church and gave him the keys (CCC 552, 881). Jesus speaks to Peter in person: “you are Peter”. The man and his confession belong together. Peter is the rock because the Father revealed to him who Jesus is, and he remains the rock by holding fast to that faith and strengthening his brothers in it (Luke 22:32).

Peter and Paul once disagreed sharply at Antioch. Why are they honoured on the same day?

Paul tells of that disagreement himself (Galatians 2:11–14), and he also tells how James, Cephas and John gave him “the right hand of fellowship” (Galatians 2:9). They had different missions, Peter to the circumcised and Paul to the Gentiles, but one faith and one Lord. St Augustine preached that the two apostles share one feast because they were one, and though they suffered on different days, they were as one (Sermon 295, in substance). Their feast teaches that unity in the Church is not uniformity of temperament but communion in the truth and in love.

Peter denied Jesus three times. How could he still be made shepherd of the whole Church?

Because Christ's choice rests on grace, not on our record. Peter's fall taught him humility, and his restoration taught him mercy. Jesus had already prayed that Peter's faith would not fail, and told him, “when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). A shepherd who has himself been forgiven knows how to seek the lost. The Church's authority comes from Christ, not from the personal perfection of the one who holds it.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today the Apostles' Fast comes to its end with the feast of the two great apostles. The West Syriac tradition, to which our Church belongs, has always looked to Peter with special love, for ancient tradition holds that he presided over the church of Antioch before going to Rome, and Antioch was where the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). Paul too was sent out from Antioch on his missionary journeys (Acts 13:1–3). In 1930 the Malankara Catholic Church entered into full communion with the successor of Peter in Rome, and today, in praying for the Pope, we give thanks for that gift. We pray also with our brothers and sisters of the Orthodox Churches, who honour Peter and Paul with the same devotion, that the Lord may bring all his flock into one fold under the one Shepherd (John 10:16).

For study and teaching

  • God says, “I have called you by name, you are mine.” How does my own baptismal name remind me of God's personal love and call?
  • Paul could say at the end, “I have kept the faith.” What helps me guard the faith I received, and what am I doing to hand it on whole to my children?
  • Jesus healed Peter's threefold denial with a threefold question of love. Is there a failure in my life that the Lord wants to heal not by forgetting it but by asking me again, “Do you love me?”
  • How can our family and parish pray more faithfully for the Pope, for our bishops, and for the unity of all Christians?
