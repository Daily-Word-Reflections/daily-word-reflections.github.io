Readings

  • Epistle 2 Corinthians 7:10–16 · Godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret
  • Gospel Luke 13:31–35 · “Jerusalem, Jerusalem”: Jesus longs to gather her children as a hen gathers her brood under her wings

Word for the day

“For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” 2 Corinthians 7:10

Reflection

Today's Epistle is a letter of relief after a hard season. Paul had written to the Corinthians a sharp letter that caused them pain, and for a while he wondered whether he had done right. Then Titus came back with good news, and Paul could say, “I rejoice, because I have complete confidence in you.” In the middle of that joy he explains why the pain was worth it: “For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” There are two kinds of sorrow over sin. Worldly grief looks inward: it sulks, it makes excuses, it blames others, it sinks into shame and self-pity, and in the end it leaves a person more alone. Godly grief looks upward: it says, “I have sinned,” and gets up and goes to the one who forgives. Peter wept bitterly and came back to the Lord; the same night Judas was sorry too, but he looked at his sin and not at his Master, and despair finished what sin began. Paul lists what godly grief produced in Corinth: “what earnestness … what eagerness to clear yourselves … what longing, what zeal.” A good confession leaves us lighter, more alive and more eager to love.

In the Gospel some Pharisees come to Jesus with a warning: “Get away from here, for Herod wants to kill you.” They may be sincere friends. Jesus is not frightened. He calls Herod “that fox” and says that he will go on “casting out demons and performing cures today and tomorrow, and on the third day I finish my work.” He is not on Herod's timetable but on his Father's, and the road runs to Jerusalem, “because it is impossible for a prophet to be killed outside of Jerusalem.” Luke has already told us that “he set his face to go to Jerusalem” (Luke 9:51). Then the voice of the Lord changes from calm to tender: “Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” This is the lament of a son of Israel who loves the holy city. The Church teaches that what was done in his Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews of that time or against the Jewish people today (Nostra Aetate 4); the Gospel shows how deeply the Lord loved the city to the end.

In Kerala we have all seen a hen when the monsoon rain comes down: she spreads her wings and the chicks run in, until only small beaks and feet show from beneath her. That is how God longs to gather us, and the sentence that grieves him is the last one: “you were not willing.” God is never unwilling; the only closed door is ours. Godly grief is simply the moment when we stop being unwilling and run under the wings. The passage ends with a promise: you will see him “when you say, ‘Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.’” At every Holy Qurbono we say it, and he comes.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells the Corinthians that there are two kinds of sorrow. Worldly grief produces death; godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret (2 Corinthians 7:10). When I have done wrong, worldly grief makes me hide, blame and sink into shame. Godly grief makes me stand up and go to the Lord, who is waiting.

Think of Peter and Judas. Peter wept bitterly, and the Lord took him back; Judas looked at his sin and not at his Master, and despair finished the work of sin. The Sacrament of Reconciliation is the road of Peter: we confess, and we are lifted up and made lighter.

In the Gospel Jesus is warned that Herod wants to kill him, and he is not afraid. He goes on towards Jerusalem on his Father's timetable. And then he weeps over the city: How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing (Luke 13:34). The Church teaches that the guilt of his Passion belongs to no people but to all of us as sinners.

So today let us stop being unwilling. Let us run under his wings, as chicks run to the hen in the monsoon rain. Let us go to him with our sorrow and receive his mercy. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever seen a mother hen when the rain comes? She calls her chicks, and they run quickly under her wings, and soon you cannot see them at all! Jesus says that is how much he loves us. When we do something wrong, he wants us to run to him, not away from him. Say, “Sorry, Lord,” and he will keep you warm and safe. And remember to say sorry to the person you hurt too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you longed to gather the children of Jerusalem as a hen gathers her brood, and you long to gather us. Turn our sorrow for sin into the sorrow that leads to life, and make us willing to come to you. Give us the courage to follow you on the road you walked, unafraid of the fox, and let us live under the shelter of your wings. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about a change of heart that God asks for and longs to receive. In Corinth, Paul rejoices that the grief his letter caused led the Corinthians to repentance and not to despair. On the road to Jerusalem, Jesus laments over a city that he wanted to gather like a hen with her chicks, and he does not turn back from the road that leads to his own death. The key words are grief that leads to repentance and gathering.

2 Corinthians 7:10–16 · Godly grief and joy

Setting.

Second Corinthians is the most personal of Paul’s letters. After a painful visit and a severe letter written “out of much distress and anguish of heart and with many tears” (2:4), Paul waited anxiously for news. Titus met him in Macedonia with good news: the Corinthians had been grieved by the letter, had taken it to heart and had set matters right (7:5–9). Scholars discuss whether the “severe letter” is lost or is part of what we have in the canon; either way, our verses are the heart of Paul’s relief.

Paul does not rejoice that they were hurt, but that the hurt led somewhere (7:9). He draws a lesson from what happened: not every sorrow is good, and the difference is in the direction it takes.

Structure.

  • v. 10: two kinds of grief: godly grief leads to repentance and salvation; worldly grief leads to death.
  • v. 11: the fruits of godly grief in Corinth: earnestness, eagerness, longing, zeal.
  • vv. 12–13: Paul’s purpose in writing: that their zeal for him be made known before God; the comfort of Titus.
  • vv. 14–16: his boasting about them proved true; Titus’s affection; Paul’s full confidence in them.

Key words.

  • metanoia … ametamelēton, repentance … no regret: Repentance is a change of mind and heart. The second word means “not to be regretted”, and Paul uses it for a sorrow that one never has to take back.
  • lypē kata theon, grief according to God: Sorrow in line with God’s own will. It looks at the wrong done and also at the God who forgives, and so it turns toward him. Worldly grief looks only at the damage to oneself and may end in bitterness.
  • spoudē, earnestness: Eagerness, haste to do what is right. Real repentance shows itself in action, in the list of fruits that Paul gives in verse 11.

Verse by verse.

“godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret” (7:10)

The sorrow that God gives is a grace. It is not the end of the road but a door: it opens to the mercy that heals.

“see what earnestness this godly grief has produced in you” (7:11)

Paul’s long list shows that repentance is a change in whole life, with action, zeal and a longing to put things right.

“we rejoiced still more at the joy of Titus” (7:13)

Paul’s joy is shared joy. The comfort of one pastor, Titus, adds to the comfort of another, Paul, and the whole community is knit together by it.

How the Church reads it.

The Church uses this chapter to teach about contrition. The Catechism describes interior penance as a radical reorientation of our whole life, a return to God, with sorrow for sin and the intention to sin no more (CCC 1431), and it teaches that contrition is a sorrow of the soul and a detestation of the sin committed, together with the resolution not to sin again (CCC 1451). The line between godly and worldly sorrow is the line between Peter, who wept and was restored, and a despair that closes the heart. In the Syro-Malankara and other Eastern traditions, the prayers of repentance are often full of hope, because the sinner is always speaking to a merciful Lord.

Luke 13:31–35 · Jerusalem, Jerusalem

Setting.

Jesus is on the journey to Jerusalem that fills the middle of Luke’s Gospel (9:51–19:27). Just before our passage he has taught about the narrow door (13:22–30). Some Pharisees now come with a warning: Herod wants to kill him. We should note that Luke shows Pharisees helping Jesus here, and not only opposing him; they care about his life.

“That fox” is Herod Antipas, the ruler of Galilee, who had John the Baptist beheaded (9:9; 3:19–20). In Jesus’ day the fox was a symbol of cunning, but also of someone with small power. Jesus speaks of his work as a timetable set by God: “today and tomorrow, and on the third day”. He must reach Jerusalem, where the prophets are sent, and where he will give his life.

Structure.

  • v. 31: Pharisees warn Jesus about Herod.
  • vv. 32–33: Jesus’ answer: his work continues, and his road leads to Jerusalem.
  • v. 34: the lament: the hen who wants to gather her chicks.
  • v. 35: the house is left to them until the day they say, “Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord”.

Key words.

  • episynagagein, to gather: The verb is used for gathering a scattered people (the prayer in 2 Maccabees 1:27 asks God to gather those who are dispersed). Jesus is the one who gathers the children of Jerusalem.
  • ēthelēsa, I desired / I willed: Jesus’ own will, spoken with tenderness. The final words, “you were not willing”, show that God’s love invites and never forces.
  • eulogēmenos ho erchomenos, blessed is the one who comes: A line from Psalm 118:26, sung by pilgrims at the temple. Luke’s crowds will sing it on Palm Sunday (19:38).

Verse by verse.

“I am casting out demons and performing cures today and tomorrow, and on the third day I finish my work.” (13:32)

Jesus is not frightened. His work is directed by the Father, and it will be completed, in the Gospel’s own pattern, on “the third day”.

“it is impossible for a prophet to be killed outside of Jerusalem.” (13:33)

A sad irony: the holy city, God’s city, is the place where prophets have often met their end. Jesus knows that his own road will end there too.

“How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” (13:34)

One of the most tender images in the Gospels. The “wings” recall the psalms that speak of shelter under God’s wings (Psalms 17:8; 36:7; 91:4). Jesus loves Jerusalem as one of its own children.

“Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.” (13:35)

The lament ends with a promise: the day will come when this blessing is said, and it is Christ who comes.

How the Church reads it.

Christian tradition has long read the lament as the love of Christ, who weeps over the city, as in Luke 19:41. The words do not condemn the Jewish people. Jesus himself is a Son of Israel, speaking out of love for his own city and people, and the Church teaches that what happened in his Passion cannot be blamed on all the Jews then living, nor on the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). The Church also reads in the hen’s wings the motherly tenderness of God, and in verse 35 the hope of a final welcome of the Lord who comes.

For study and teaching

  • Paul distinguishes godly grief from worldly grief. When I am sorry for something I have done, does my sorrow turn me toward God’s mercy or in on myself?
  • Jesus says, “How often have I desired to gather your children together.” Where in my parish or family is God trying to gather people, and what in me says, “I am not willing”?
