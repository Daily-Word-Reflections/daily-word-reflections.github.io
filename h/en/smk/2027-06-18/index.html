Readings

Word for the day

“I do not call you servants any longer, because the servant does not know what the master is doing; but I have called you friends, because I have made known to you everything that I have heard from my Father.” John 15:15

Reflection

Today our Church keeps the memory of Mar Aprem Malpan, St Ephrem the Syrian, the greatest poet of our Syriac tradition. He was born at Nisibis in Mesopotamia at the beginning of the fourth century, served the Church there as a deacon and as a teacher of the faith, and when Nisibis was handed over to Persia in 363 he went with many Christians to Edessa, where he taught and wrote until his death in 373. He never became a priest or a bishop; he remained a deacon, a servant, all his life. Yet his hymns, the madrashe, taught the faith to whole peoples, and they are still sung in the prayers of the Syriac Churches, ours among them. He wrote them for choirs of women and girls, so that the doctrine of the Church might be carried home on melodies that people loved. The Syriac tradition calls him the Harp of the Spirit. In 1920 Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the universal Church. It is fitting that his memory falls in the Apostles' Fast: Ephrem was a man of fasting and prayer, and tradition remembers that in his last years, when famine struck Edessa, he left his cell to organise help for the hungry and the sick.

The Epistle of the day is a passage Ephrem would have loved. “Since the law has only a shadow of the good things to come and not the true form of these realities, it can never, by the same sacrifices that are continually offered year after year, make perfect those who approach.” The sacrifices of the old covenant were true worship given by God to Israel, but they were shadows cast ahead by the coming light. “For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.” Only the one offering of Christ does that. Ephrem saw the whole Bible and the whole of creation as full of such shadows and symbols, which our tradition calls raze, mysteries. The lamb, the ark, the manna, the rock in the desert, the dew, the pearl, the olive and its oil: everything, for him, was a window through which Christ could be seen. He wrote that the Word of God is like a living spring: whoever drinks from it takes only a little and leaves far more behind, and should give thanks for what he has received instead of grieving over what he could not take in (Commentary on the Diatessaron 1, in substance). That is how he read Scripture, with wonder and thanksgiving, never pretending to have exhausted it. And Ephrem sang tenderly of the holy Mother of God, through whom the reality came into the world. Addressing Christ, he wrote that only he and his Mother are beautiful in every way, for in him there is no blemish and in his Mother no stain (Nisibene Hymns 27, in substance).

The Gospel shows what the shadows were pointing to: not only a perfect sacrifice but a friendship. “This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you. No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends. You are my friends if you do what I command you. I do not call you servants any longer, because the servant does not know what the master is doing; but I have called you friends.” The old sacrifices could not make the worshippers perfect; the sacrifice of Christ makes servants into friends. And Jesus adds: “You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last.” Ephrem's fruit has lasted for sixteen centuries; every time the Syriac Churches sing his hymns, the friend of Christ is still bearing fruit. Ours may be smaller and hidden: a prayer taught to a child, a hymn sung with love, a meal given to a hungry neighbour. On this Friday of the fast, let us give thanks for the friendship Christ offers us, and ask Mar Aprem to teach us to sing it.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we remember Mar Aprem Malpan, St Ephrem the Syrian. He was a deacon in Nisibis and Edessa in the fourth century, and he wrote hundreds of hymns that taught the faith. Our Syriac tradition calls him the Harp of the Spirit, and the whole Church honours him as a Doctor.

The Epistle says that the law has only a shadow of the good things to come (Hebrews 10:1). The old sacrifices pointed forward, but it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins (Hebrews 10:4). Only Jesus, by his one offering, takes away sin. Mar Aprem loved to find these shadows and symbols in Scripture and in nature, and to show how all of them point to Christ.

And what do they point to? To friendship. Jesus says: I do not call you servants any longer, but I have called you friends (John 15:15). No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends (John 15:13). On the Cross, Jesus made us his friends. And friends of Jesus must love one another as he loved us.

Mar Aprem was such a friend. He prayed, fasted, sang and, when famine came, cared for the hungry. In this Apostles' Fast, let us ask him to teach us to see Christ in the Scriptures, to praise him with joy, and to love one another in deeds. Amen.

For children

Children, do you like to sing? Today we remember a saint who loved to sing about Jesus: Mar Aprem. He lived long, long ago and wrote many, many songs to teach people about Jesus and Mother Mary. People called him the harp of the Holy Spirit, because his songs were like music played by God. Jesus says today: you are my friends. A friend of Jesus sings his praises and helps others. So today, sing a hymn for Jesus, and help someone who needs you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, the shadows of the law have passed and you have come, the true and only sacrifice that takes away sin. You no longer call us servants but friends. Through the prayers of Mar Aprem Malpan, the Harp of the Spirit, open our eyes to see you in the Scriptures and in all your creation, teach us to praise you with joy, and make us love one another as you have loved us. Let our lives bear fruit that will last, to the glory of your Father and the Holy Spirit, now and for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today we keep the memory of Mar Aprem Malpan, St Ephrem the Syrian, the poet of our Syriac tradition, who loved to find Christ foreshadowed throughout the Scriptures. The Epistle fits him well: the law has “only a shadow of the good things to come”. In the Gospel Jesus shows us the reality behind every shadow: love that lays down its life for friends. “I have called you friends.” The key words are shadow and friendship.

Hebrews 10:1–4 · A shadow of the good things to come

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews does not name its author. Ancient tradition linked it with Paul, but already in the third century Origen said that only God knows who wrote it. It is a long sermon to Christians tempted to grow tired in faith, and its centre is Christ the high priest. In chapters 8–10 the author compares the sacrifices of the first covenant, above all those of the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16), with the one sacrifice of Christ. Our verses explain why the old sacrifices were repeated year after year.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the law has a shadow, not the true form; its sacrifices cannot make perfect.
  • v. 2: if they could, they would have ceased.
  • v. 3: instead, they bring a yearly reminder of sin.
  • v. 4: the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins.

Key words.

  • skia / eikōn, shadow / true form: A shadow shows the outline of something real but is not the thing itself. The eikōn is the reality in its own form. The worship of Israel was given by God and was true worship, but it pointed beyond itself to Christ.
  • anamnēsis, reminder, remembrance: The same word Jesus uses at the Last Supper: “Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19). The old sacrifices recalled sin each year; the new remembrance makes present the sacrifice that takes sin away.

Verse by verse.

“Since the law has only a shadow of the good things to come and not the true form of these realities,” (10:1)

The “good things to come” have now come in Christ. The shadow is not despised; it is honoured as the sign that the light was coming.

“But in these sacrifices there is a reminder of sin year after year.” (10:3)

On the Day of Atonement the high priest entered the Holy of Holies once a year. The repetition itself showed that the final cleansing had not yet come.

“For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.” (10:4)

The psalms and the prophets had already said that God desires mercy and a contrite heart more than sacrifice (Psalm 51:16–17; Hosea 6:6). Hebrews goes on to quote Psalm 40: “a body you have prepared for me” (10:5). The true sacrifice is the self-offering of the Son.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the priesthood of the old covenant, though instituted by God, was powerless to bring about salvation, and so needed to repeat its sacrifices without end; only Christ's sacrifice, offered once for all, achieves it (CCC 1540). The Holy Qurbono does not add new sacrifices to his. It makes present the one sacrifice of the cross, so that the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice (CCC 1366–1367). The shadows of Israel's worship are fulfilled, not erased.

John 15:11–17 · I have called you friends

Setting.

These words belong to the farewell discourse at the Last Supper. Jesus has just spoken of himself as the true vine and his disciples as the branches (15:1–8), and has told them, “As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love” (15:9). Now he explains what that love asks and gives: joy, the commandment of love, friendship, and lasting fruit.

Structure.

  • v. 11: that my joy may be in you.
  • v. 12: the commandment: love one another as I have loved you.
  • v. 13: no greater love than to lay down one's life for friends.
  • vv. 14–15: you are my friends; I have made known everything.
  • vv. 16–17: I chose you to bear lasting fruit; love one another.

Key words.

  • philoi, friends: In the Old Testament, Abraham is called God's friend (Isaiah 41:8), and the Lord spoke to Moses “face to face, as one speaks to a friend” (Exodus 33:11). Jesus now gives this title to all his disciples.
  • agapē, love: The self-giving love that seeks the good of the other. Its measure is not our feeling but “as I have loved you”: the love shown on the cross.
  • menein, to remain, to last: The key word of chapter 15. The branch remains in the vine, the disciple in Jesus' love, and the fruit lasts.

Verse by verse.

“This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” (15:12)

“As I have loved you” makes the commandment new (13:34). It is not only “as you love yourself” but as Christ loves: to the end.

“No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” (15:13)

Jesus speaks these words a few hours before he lays down his own life. The commandment is preceded and made possible by his example.

“but I have called you friends, because I have made known to you everything that I have heard from my Father.” (15:15)

A servant receives orders; a friend receives confidences. Jesus shares with his disciples what is in the Father's heart.

“You did not choose me but I chose you.” (15:16)

Every vocation begins with Christ's choice. In rabbinic custom a disciple chose his master; here the master chooses the disciples, and sends them to bear fruit.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus makes charity the new commandment, and that by loving his own to the end he shows the Father's love which he receives; by loving one another, the disciples imitate the love of Jesus which they themselves receive (CCC 1823). The friendship of which Jesus speaks is the heart of Christian prayer and life. It is offered to every disciple, and the saints are those who accepted it without reserve.

Questions people ask

Does Hebrews say that the sacrifices of the Old Testament were worthless?

No. They were given by God, offered in obedience and faith, and they taught Israel about sin, holiness and God's mercy. Hebrews says they were a shadow, which means they were real signs of a reality still to come. The Church honours them as part of God's plan and sees in them a preparation for Christ (CCC 1540). The one who fulfils them is himself a son of Israel.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church keeps today the memory of Mar Aprem Malpan, St Ephrem the Syrian. He was born at Nisibis in Mesopotamia early in the fourth century, served there as a deacon and teacher, and when Nisibis was handed over to Persia in 363 he moved with many Christians to Edessa, where he died in 373. He remained a deacon all his life. His hymns, the madrāšē, taught the faith in song, and the Syriac Churches still pray with them; tradition calls him the Harp of the Spirit. Ephrem loved to see in Scripture and in nature symbols that point to Christ, and he would have recognized in today's Epistle his own way of reading: the shadows of the law are true, and they lead to the Son. He is a fitting patron for the Apostles' Fast, a man of prayer and fasting who, as tradition remembers, organised help for the hungry when famine struck Edessa.
