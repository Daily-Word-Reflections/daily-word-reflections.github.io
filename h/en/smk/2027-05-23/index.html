Word for the day

“By contrast, the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.” Galatians 5:22–23

Reflection

The season of Pentecost is the season of the Church's life in the Spirit. If Pentecost was the planting, these Sundays are about the fruit. St Paul names it: “the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control… If we live by the Spirit, let us also be guided by the Spirit.”

In the evening Gospel Jesus tells the parable of the sower. Seed falls on the path, on rock, among thorns and on good soil. “As for that in the good soil, these are the ones who, when they hear the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance.” The Spirit sows; we must become good soil: attentive, deep, free from the thorns of worry and riches.

In the Gospel the crowds follow Jesus after the multiplication of loaves, looking for more bread. He tells them: “Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life.” When they ask what work God wants, he answers: “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” “The bread of God is that which comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.” Israel's manna stopped when they reached the promised land; the true Bread will never fail.

Jeremiah gives a word of hope to people in exile: “I know the plans I have for you, says the LORD, plans for your welfare and not for harm, to give you a future with hope… When you search for me, you will find me; if you seek me with all your heart.” God has a plan, a vocation, for each of us; the Spirit leads us to discover it.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, last Sunday we celebrated Pentecost, the coming of the Holy Spirit. Today we ask: what difference does the Spirit make? St Paul answers: the Spirit bears fruit. “The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.” Read that list slowly; it is a portrait of Jesus, and of what he wants to grow in us.

In the evening Gospel Jesus tells of a sower. Some seed falls on the path, some on rock, some among thorns, and some on good soil. The seed is the same; the soil makes the difference. The good soil is “an honest and good heart” that hears the Word, holds it fast and bears fruit with patience. The Spirit keeps sowing; are we good soil?

In John's Gospel the crowds look for Jesus because he gave them bread. He tells them: “Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life.” And he says: “The bread of God is that which comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.” That bread is given to us at this altar.

Today is also a day to pray for vocations. Jeremiah says: “I know the plans I have for you, plans for your welfare, to give you a future with hope.” God has a plan for every young person here: priesthood, religious life, marriage, a mission. Let us ask the Spirit to show it, and let us choose this week one fruit of the Spirit to practise. Amen.

For children

Children, when a tree is healthy, it gives fruit. When the Holy Spirit lives in us, we give fruit too: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness!

Jesus told a story about a farmer sowing seed. The seed that fell on good soil grew a big harvest. Our hearts should be good soil for God's Word.

Which fruit will you grow this week? Kindness? Patience? Let's say: “Holy Spirit, help me grow good fruit!”

Prayer

Holy Spirit, sower of the Word, make our hearts good soil. Bear in us the fruit of love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control; feed us with the bread from heaven, and show each of us the plan you have for our lives. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the First Sunday after Pentecost the readings follow God's people on the road, fed and guided by him. The evening Gospel (Luke 8:4–15) is the parable of the sower: the word falls on every kind of ground, and in good soil it bears fruit “with patient endurance”. Exodus 12:31–42 tells of the night Israel went out of Egypt, “a night of vigil” for the Lord. Micah 3:8–10 shows the prophet “filled with power, with the spirit of the Lord”, who speaks the truth to rulers who build with injustice. In Jeremiah 29:10–16 God promises the exiles “a future with hope”. In Acts 17:10–15 the Jews of Beroea receive Paul's message eagerly and examine the scriptures every day to see whether these things are so.

We study three readings more closely. In Joshua the manna stops on the day Israel eats the produce of the promised land. In Galatians Paul sets the works of the flesh against the fruit of the Spirit. In John the crowd asks for bread like the manna, and Jesus speaks of the true bread from heaven that the Father gives. The key words are bread, Spirit and believe.

Joshua 5:9–12 · The manna ceased

Setting.

After forty years in the wilderness Israel has crossed the Jordan on dry ground (Joshua 3–4) and camped at Gilgal, on the plain near Jericho. Before any battle, two things are done. The men born in the wilderness are circumcised, so that the sign of the covenant is restored (5:2–8). Then Israel keeps the Passover, the first in the promised land.

The book of Joshua tells the entry into the land with the eyes of faith and of worship. Its story is shaped like a liturgy: the ark goes ahead, the people cross as they did at the Red Sea, stones are set up as a memorial (4:1–9), and the first act in the land is a feast of remembrance.

Structure.

  • v. 9: God rolls away the disgrace of Egypt; the name Gilgal.
  • v. 10: the Passover on the evening of the fourteenth day, on the plains of Jericho.
  • v. 11: the first food from the land: unleavened cakes and parched grain.
  • v. 12: the manna ceases; from now on they eat the crops of Canaan.

Key words.

  • gallôtî / Gilgāl, I have rolled away / Gilgal: A play on words: the name Gilgal sounds like the Hebrew verb “to roll”. The place itself would remind Israel of what God did there. “The disgrace of Egypt” is the shame of slavery, now taken away.
  • mān, manna: The name comes from the question the Israelites asked when they first saw it: “What is it?” (Exodus 16:15; in Hebrew mān hû’). It fed them for forty years (Exodus 16:35). It stops on the day that the land itself can feed them.
  • pesaḥ, Passover: The feast of the night of deliverance from Egypt. Israel's journey began with a Passover (Exodus 12), and the wilderness years end with one. Each new stage of life with God begins with remembering what he has done.

Verse by verse.

“Today I have rolled away from you the disgrace of Egypt.” (5:9)

“Today” is a favourite word of Deuteronomy and Joshua. God's saving act is not only a memory; it reaches the people now.

“While the Israelites were camped in Gilgal they kept the passover in the evening on the fourteenth day of the month in the plains of Jericho.” (5:10)

The date of the first Passover (Exodus 12:6). Forty years have passed, and God's people are where the promise said they would be. Their first act is worship.

“On the day after the passover, on that very day, they ate the produce of the land, unleavened cakes and parched grain.” (5:11)

Bread from the land they had not yet conquered, but had been given. The promised land is a gift before it is a task.

“The manna ceased on the day they ate the produce of the land,” (5:12)

God does not stop caring for his people. He changes the way he feeds them: no longer by a daily wonder, but through the harvest and their own work.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen the manna as a figure of the Eucharist. The Catechism names it among the Old Testament signs that the liturgy reads in the light of Christ: the manna in the desert prefigured the Eucharist, “the true bread from heaven” (CCC 1094). The manna fed Israel on the way; it ended when the people reached the land. The Eucharist is the food of the Church on her pilgrimage, the pledge of the glory to come (CCC 1402–1405), until the day when we sit at the banquet of the kingdom. Like Israel at Gilgal, the Church begins every stage of her life with a memorial of the Lord's Passover.

Galatians 5:16–26 · The fruit of the Spirit

Setting.

Paul wrote to the churches of Galatia, in the interior of what is now Turkey, when some teachers were urging his Gentile converts to be circumcised and to keep the Mosaic law in order to belong fully to God's people. Paul answers that we are made right with God through faith in Christ, and that the Spirit has been given to us (3:1–5).

But the liberty of God's children is not licence. Just before today's reading Paul warns: “do not use your freedom as an opportunity for self-indulgence, but through love become slaves to one another” (5:13). Now he shows where such love comes from: from the Spirit.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–18: live by the Spirit; Spirit and flesh are opposed.
  • vv. 19–21: the works of the flesh, and Paul's warning.
  • vv. 22–23: the fruit of the Spirit.
  • v. 24: those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh.
  • vv. 25–26: keep in step with the Spirit; no conceit or envy.

Key words.

  • sarx, flesh: Not the body as such, which God made good and which will rise. In Paul “flesh” means human nature turned in on itself and away from God. That is why the works of the flesh include not only sins of the body but also enmity, jealousy, factions and envy.
  • karpos, fruit: Paul speaks of the “works” (plural) of the flesh but of the “fruit” (singular) of the Spirit. Works are things we produce; fruit grows from a living root. The nine qualities are one harvest, and its first name is love.
  • stoichōmen, let us be guided: In v. 25 a verb for walking in line, like people keeping step in a procession. Having received life from the Spirit, we are to keep in step with him day by day.

Verse by verse.

“Live by the Spirit, I say, and do not gratify the desires of the flesh.” (5:16)

Paul's first answer to temptation is not a list of rules but a way of walking. The Greek says literally “walk by the Spirit”.

“By contrast, the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness,” (5:22)

Love comes first, and the others show what love looks like in daily life. Every quality in the list is first true of Christ himself.

“And those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.” (5:24)

Paul uses the past tense. In Baptism “our old self was crucified with him” (Romans 6:6). What happened in the sacrament must now be lived day by day.

“If we live by the Spirit, let us also be guided by the Spirit.” (5:25)

Gift and task in one sentence. The Spirit is already our life; now we must let him lead.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, following the Latin tradition, counts twelve fruits of the Spirit: charity, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, generosity, gentleness, faithfulness, modesty, self-control and chastity (CCC 1832). It quotes verses 19–21 to show the variety of sins (CCC 1852), and it quotes verse 25 to teach that the Spirit is the source of our new life and makes it possible for us to bear fruit (CCC 736). Paul's warning that those who do such things “will not inherit the kingdom of God” is serious, but it is a call to conversion, not a closed door. Grave sin can be forgiven, ordinarily through the sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 1856).

John 6:25–33 · The true bread from heaven

Setting.

John 6 begins with the feeding of the five thousand by the Sea of Galilee, when “the Passover, the festival of the Jews, was near” (6:4), and with Jesus walking on the sea (6:16–21). The next day the crowd looks for him and finds him on the other side of the lake. Today's reading opens the long discourse on the Bread of Life, which Jesus gives in the synagogue at Capernaum (6:59).

The conversation moves step by step, from the loaves they ate to the bread the Father gives. The crowd thinks of Moses and the manna; Jesus leads them to himself. The discourse goes on: “I am the bread of life” (6:35), and then, “the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh” (6:51).

Structure.

  • v. 25: “Rabbi, when did you come here?”
  • vv. 26–27: not the food that perishes, but the food that endures; the Father's seal.
  • vv. 28–29: the work of God is to believe.
  • vv. 30–31: “What sign are you going to give us?” The manna.
  • vv. 32–33: not Moses but the Father gives the true bread, which gives life to the world.

Key words.

  • ergazesthe / ergon, work for / work: Jesus plays on the word. They ask what works they must do; he answers that “the work of God” is to believe. Faith is God's work in us before it is ours, and from it all good works grow.
  • esphragisen, has set his seal: A seal marks what is owned and guarantees that it is genuine. The Father has marked the Son as the one who truly gives the food of eternal life.
  • ho artos ho alēthinos, the true bread: In John “true” means the real thing to which earlier signs pointed, as with “the true light” (1:9) and “the true vine” (15:1). The manna was a real gift of God; Jesus is the reality to which it pointed.

Verse by verse.

“you are looking for me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.” (6:26)

Jesus is not scornful of hunger; he has just fed them. But he wants them to see what the bread was pointing to.

“Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life,” (6:27)

Not a command to stop earning our daily bread. Jesus asks what we spend our deepest effort on.

“This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” (6:29)

The answer is short and searching. Believing is not idle; it is the first and greatest work, and the root of every other.

“it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but it is my Father who gives you the true bread from heaven.” (6:32)

The crowd quoted a Scripture about the manna (Psalm 78:24; see Exodus 16:4). Jesus explains it: the giver was God, not Moses, and the Father is giving now, in the present tense.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that St John, in place of an account of the institution, reports the words of Jesus in the synagogue at Capernaum that prepare for the Eucharist: Christ calls himself the bread of life, come down from heaven (CCC 1338). It also teaches that believing in Jesus Christ and in the One who sent him for our salvation is necessary for obtaining that salvation (CCC 161). St Augustine, preaching on verse 29, said: “Believe, and you have eaten” (Tractates on John 25.12, in substance). He did not mean that faith takes the place of the Sacrament. He meant that faith is how we first receive Christ, and that it opens the heart to receive him worthily at the altar.

Questions people ask

If faith is “the work of God”, do good works not matter?

They matter greatly, and they are done by grace. Jesus calls faith the work of God because it is God's gift first. From a living faith come works of love, and today's Epistle names their fruit: love, joy, peace and the rest. The Church teaches that, moved by the Holy Spirit and by charity, we can merit for ourselves and for others the graces we need, and eternal life itself, always as God's gift (CCC 2010–2011).

Was the manna only a picture, or was it real?

It was a real gift of God to Israel in the desert, and Jesus does not deny it. He says that the Father who gave the manna now gives the true bread. The manna fed bodies for a time; the bread that Christ gives brings eternal life. The figure was true, and it pointed to something greater.

Does Paul mean that those led by the Spirit can ignore the commandments?

No. The Spirit does not abolish God's law; he writes it within. “There is no law against such things” because love, joy and peace already do what the law asked. Paul says elsewhere that the just requirement of the law is fulfilled in those who walk according to the Spirit (Romans 8:4).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

With the First Sunday after Pentecost our Church begins the long series of Sundays after Pentecost, the time of the Church's life in the Spirit. Today's readings join the Spirit and the bread. In every Holy Qurbono the priest calls on the Holy Spirit to come upon the bread and wine, and the faithful receive the Body and Blood of Christ. Mar Aprem sang that in the Lord's bread the Spirit is hidden, who cannot be eaten, and in his wine dwells a fire that cannot be drunk (Hymns on Faith 10, in substance). The fruit of the Spirit in Galatians is the harvest this bread is meant to grow in us. The evening Gospel of the sower reminds us that the word sown each Sunday needs good soil.

For study and teaching

  • At Gilgal Israel's first act in the land was the Passover. How does our family begin new stages of life, a new home, a new job, a new school year, with God?
  • Which fruit of the Spirit is most missing in my life just now? Which work of the flesh most often takes its place?
  • Jesus says the crowd looked for him because they had eaten their fill. Why do I look for him?
  • How can I prepare to receive the Holy Qurbono with the faith that Jesus calls “the work of God”?
