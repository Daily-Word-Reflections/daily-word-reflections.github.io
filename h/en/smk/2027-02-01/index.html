Word for the day

“And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.” Matthew 6:12

Reflection

Paul tells the Colossians that he never stops praying for them: “we have not ceased praying for you and asking that you may be filled with the knowledge of God’s will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding, so that you may lead lives worthy of the Lord.” He prays that they may be “prepared to endure everything with patience, while joyfully giving thanks to the Father”, and then he names the great change that has already happened: “He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins.” The Christian life is lived in that new kingdom, and it is learned in prayer.

In the Gospel Jesus teaches the prayer of that kingdom: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.” Only one petition receives a commentary from the Lord himself, the one about forgiveness: “if you do not forgive others, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.” Our Church prays the Lord’s Prayer at every hour of the Shehimo and before Holy Communion in the Qurbono. In a week the Great Fast will open with Shubkono, the service of mutual forgiveness. This week is the time to say the Our Father slowly and to ask: whom have I still not forgiven? St Cyprian of Carthage wrote that we call God “Father” together, not “my Father”, because we pray as one people and for one another (in substance).

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells the Colossians: we have not ceased praying for you, that you may be filled with the knowledge of God’s will (Colossians 1:9). Before he teaches them anything, he prays for them.

Then he reminds them what God has done: he has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins (Colossians 1:13–14). We belong to a new kingdom.

In the Gospel Jesus gives us the prayer of that kingdom, the Our Father. Of all its lines he explains only one: if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you (Matthew 6:14).

Next Monday the Great Fast opens with Shubkono, when we ask one another for forgiveness. Let us use this week to say the Our Father slowly and to make peace with anyone we have not yet forgiven. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus gave us his own prayer, the Our Father. When we say it, we ask God to forgive us, and we promise to forgive others too. Is there someone you need to forgive today?

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The week after Aneede Sunday, the last week before the Great Fast, opens with prayer. In Colossians, Paul tells a young Church what he asks God to give it. In Matthew, Jesus gives his disciples the words to ask with. The two readings share more than a subject: both speak of the kingdom, both speak of forgiveness, and both use the same Greek verb for God's rescue of us.

Colossians 1:9–14 · Rescued and carried across

Setting.

Colossae was a small town in the Lycus valley, in what is now south-western Turkey. Paul did not found the Church there. It learned the gospel from Epaphras (1:7), and it seems that many there had never seen Paul's face (2:1). The letter names Paul and Timothy as its senders (1:1). Many scholars think a disciple wrote it in Paul's name, while others hold it to be his own; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

These verses follow the thanksgiving of 1:3–8 and run straight into the great hymn to Christ (1:15–20). The closing words about the “beloved Son” are the bridge to that hymn.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: what Paul asks for: a knowledge of God's will that turns into a way of life.
  • v. 11: strength, and the patience to endure.
  • v. 12: thanks to the Father for a share in the inheritance.
  • vv. 13–14: the reason for the thanks: rescue, transfer, redemption.

Key words.

  • epignōsis, knowledge: A fuller word than plain knowing. It is not knowledge for its own sake: in v. 10 it is both the root and the fruit of a good life.
  • methistēmi, to transfer: The verb means to move someone from one place to another, and writers of the time also used it for moving whole populations. Paul pictures the baptized as a people resettled from one realm to another.
  • apolytrōsis, redemption: Literally a release on payment, the word used of setting captives at liberty. Here Paul explains it himself: “the forgiveness of sins”.

Verse by verse.

“so that you may lead lives worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing to him, as you bear fruit in every good work and as you grow in the knowledge of God.” (1:10)

Knowing God and doing good feed each other. The verse does not let us choose between a Church that studies and a Church that serves.

“He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son,” (1:13)

Both verbs are in the past tense. Paul does not say that God will one day do this, but that he has done it. The verb for “rescued” (rhyomai) is the one used in the last petition of the Lord's Prayer in today's Gospel.

“in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins.” (1:14)

The kingdom of the Son is not first a territory. It is a place where sins are forgiven, and we are in it “in him”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites these verses when it says that Christ's whole life is a mystery of redemption (CCC 517). It also uses them when it teaches why even children need Baptism: every person born into our fallen state needs to be delivered from the power of darkness and brought into the realm of the children of God (CCC 1250). Each baptism in the parish is this verse happening again.

Matthew 6:9–15 · Pray then in this way

Setting.

The Lord's Prayer stands near the middle of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), in a section on almsgiving, prayer and fasting done for the Father “who sees in secret” (6:1–18). Luke gives a shorter form (Luke 11:2–4); the Catechism notes that Luke has five petitions and Matthew seven, and that the Church's liturgy follows Matthew's (CCC 2759).

Jesus prays here as a son of Israel. Its first petitions are close in spirit to Jewish prayers such as the Kaddish, though we know that prayer only from later texts. Very early the Christians made it their daily prayer: the Didache, a Church manual probably from around the end of the first century, tells them to pray it three times a day (Didache 8).

Structure.

  • v. 9a: the address: “Our Father in heaven”.
  • vv. 9b–10: three petitions about God: his name, his kingdom, his will.
  • vv. 11–13: four petitions about us: bread, forgiveness, testing, rescue.
  • vv. 14–15: a comment on one petition only, forgiveness.

Key words.

  • epiousios, daily: A puzzle. Origen already remarked that the word was not known to him from other Greek writers. It may mean “for today”, “for tomorrow” or “needed for existence”. The Catechism holds the senses together: bread for this day, and, taken literally, “super-essential” bread, the Bread of Life (CCC 2837).
  • opheilēmata, debts: Matthew writes “debts”; Luke 11:4 writes “sins”. In the Aramaic that Jesus spoke, and in the Syriac Bible of our own tradition (ḥawbā), the same word means both a debt and a sin. Sin leaves us owing something we cannot repay.
  • peirasmos, time of trial: A test or a trial, and also a temptation. The word can look to the daily struggle and to the great final trial as well.

Verse by verse.

“Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name.” (6:9)

“Our”, not “my”. Nobody can say this prayer alone, even in an empty room. To hallow the name is to know it as holy and to treat it so. As a petition, it draws both God and us into the work (CCC 2807).

“And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.” (6:12)

This is the only petition with a condition attached, and the only one that Jesus comments on afterwards (vv. 14–15). The Catechism is realistic about it: we cannot make ourselves stop feeling a hurt or forget it, but a heart given to the Holy Spirit can turn the injury into compassion (CCC 2843).

“And do not bring us to the time of trial, but rescue us from the evil one.” (6:13)

A note on the text. The oldest Greek manuscripts end here. The closing “for the kingdom and the power and the glory” is found in later manuscripts, and a form of it is already in the Didache. It grew from the Church's praying of this prayer, and the Eastern liturgies use it.

How the Church reads it.

Tertullian called this prayer “truly the summary of the whole gospel”, and St Augustine said that every prayer of Scripture is found in it (in substance; CCC 2761–2762). The Catechism closes its whole teaching on prayer with a commentary on it (CCC 2759–2865). In the Holy Qurbono we say it just before Communion: we ask for daily bread and forgiveness at the moment we are about to receive the Bread of Life with our neighbour beside us.

Questions people ask

Does God lead us into temptation?

No. “God cannot be tempted by evil and he himself tempts no one” (James 1:13). The Catechism explains that the Greek idea behind the petition includes both “do not let us enter into temptation” and “do not let us give way to it” (CCC 2846). We are asking the Father to hold on to us in the hour of testing.

For study and teaching

  • Paul prays that the Colossians may grow in knowledge and in good works together. Which of the two do I find harder to keep alive?
  • Is there one person whose name makes “as we also have forgiven” hard to say? What would it mean to bring that difficulty honestly to the Father before receiving Communion?
