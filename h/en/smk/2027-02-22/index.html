Word for the day

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Hebrews 13:8

Reflection

Today our Church keeps the feast of the See of St Peter at Antioch. Ancient tradition holds that Peter led the Church at Antioch before he went to Rome, and it was in Antioch “that the disciples were first called ‘Christians.’” Our Syro-Malankara Church, which prays in the Antiochene tradition, honours this throne of the Apostle with special love. It is not a feast of furniture but of a ministry: the Lord said to Peter, “I have prayed for you that your own faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers.” St Leo the Great taught that the firmness of Peter's faith, which Christ praised, lives on in the Church through his successors (in substance). So the Letter to the Hebrews says: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Leaders change; the Lord they serve does not.

Genesis shows what a true leader does for his people: he stands before God for them. “Abraham remained standing before the Lord. Then Abraham came near and said, ‘Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?’” Six times he asks, from fifty righteous down to ten, humbly, “I who am but dust and ashes,” yet boldly: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?” And six times God agrees. Intercession is the heart of every shepherd's work. In the Gospel Jesus asks: “Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” Peter and his successors, our bishops and priests, are servants who feed the household with the word and the sacraments. Today we also remember Thomas Mar Dioscoros, who died in 1943, and we give thanks for the consecration of St Mary's Cathedral in Thiruvananthapuram in 1965.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we honour the See of St Peter at Antioch, where the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). Jesus prayed for Peter that his faith might not fail, and told him to strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:32).

Hebrews says: remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever (Hebrews 13:7–8). Shepherds come and go; Christ remains.

A true shepherd stands before God for his people. Abraham remained standing before the Lord and asked: will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked? (Genesis 18:22–23). From fifty to ten he pleaded, and God listened.

Jesus asks: who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household? (Matthew 24:45). Let us pray for the Pope and our bishops, and let each of us be faithful in the place God has given us. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus chose St Peter to be the leader of his apostles and said: strengthen your brothers. Today we pray for the Pope and our bishops, who take care of the Church like shepherds. Abraham prayed for a whole city. You can pray for your town tonight!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the same yesterday and today and forever. You prayed for Peter that his faith might not fail and made him the rock of your Church, whose throne at Antioch we honour today. Strengthen the Pope, our Major Archbishop-Catholicos and all our bishops in the faith of Peter. Make us intercessors like Abraham and faithful servants in your household. Grant eternal rest to Thomas Mar Dioscoros, and bless all who pray in our cathedral church. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Our Church keeps today the feast of the See of St Peter at Antioch, in the third week of the Great Fast. The three readings together draw a portrait of the one who is given charge of God's people. In Genesis, Abraham stands before the Lord and pleads for a city that is not his own. In Hebrews, the believers are told to remember those who led them, and to hold on to the one Lord who does not change when leaders pass away. In Matthew, a slave is put in charge of a household to feed the others at the proper time, and must answer for how he did it. The key words are standing before God, remembering and feeding: this is the feast of a ministry, of which the chair is the sign.

Genesis 18:22–33 · Abraham pleads for the city

Setting.

The scene follows straight on from Mamre, where Abraham welcomed three visitors and heard the promise of a son (18:1–15). As the visitors leave, the Lord decides not to hide from Abraham what he is about to do. He has heard a great outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah, and he will go down to see (18:20–21). The word “outcry” is the cry of people who suffer wrong.

Abraham's nephew Lot lives in Sodom (13:12), but Abraham never mentions him. He pleads for the whole city, and his question is about God's own justice. May the righteous be swept away with the wicked? Then the question grows: may the few righteous save the many who are not?

Structure.

  • v. 22: the men go on toward Sodom; Abraham stays before the Lord.
  • vv. 23–25: the bold question about justice.
  • vv. 26–32: six requests, from fifty down to forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty and ten; each is granted.
  • v. 33: the Lord goes his way; Abraham returns to his place.
  • ¶ Abraham is the one who stops counting. The text never says that God refused him.

Key words.

  • tsaddîq, righteous: A person who is in right relationship with God and neighbour. Later in Genesis the same word is used by the king of Gerar, in 20:4.
  • shōphēṭ kol-hā’ārets, Judge of all the earth: A title that reaches past Israel to the whole world. Abraham appeals to God against the fear that God might act unjustly, and in so doing he confesses who God is.
  • ‘āphār wā’ēpher, dust and ashes: A rhyming pair in Hebrew, found also in Job 30:19 and 42:6. It names the creature's lowliness and mortality. Abraham's boldness never forgets it.

Verse by verse.

“while Abraham remained standing before the Lord.” (18:22)

Ancient Jewish scribal lists count this verse among places where the wording was changed out of reverence, so that it once said that the Lord remained standing before Abraham. Scholars discuss how far these lists are reliable as history, but the tradition itself shows how astonishing the scene seemed: God waits for the man to speak.

“Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?” (18:25)

This is prayer, not rebellion. Abraham holds God to what God has shown himself to be. Only someone who trusts God's justice can ask this.

“Let me take it upon myself to speak to the Lord, I who am but dust and ashes.” (18:27)

Humility and courage appear in one sentence. The prayer of the Bible does not choose between them.

“For the sake of ten I will not destroy it.” (18:32)

God's answer never falls short of the request. Abraham's prayer begins to show something that Jeremiah 5:1 and Isaiah 53 will carry further: a few, or even one, may stand for many.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Abraham's heart is attuned to his Lord's compassion for men and that he dares to intercede for them with bold confidence (CCC 2571). Christian intercession is a sharing in the prayer of Jesus, “the one intercessor with the Father on behalf of all men” (CCC 2634). The Church reads the shrinking numbers in the light of the one righteous man, of whom Paul writes: “by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19). On a feast of Peter this reading says what a shepherd does first of all: he stands before God for his people.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · Remember your leaders; the Lord does not change

Setting.

The letter does not name its author, and calls itself a “word of exhortation” (13:22). It seems to be addressed to Christians who had grown tired under pressure. Chapter 13 gathers its closing practical advice.

The leaders in verse 7 seem to be people who have already died, since their “outcome” can now be looked at. The first generation is passing, and new teachings are going about (13:9). The writer's answer is the sentence in the middle: Jesus Christ does not pass.

Structure.

  • v. 7: remember those who spoke the word of God, and imitate their faith.
  • vv. 8–9: Christ the same; do not be carried away by strange teachings.
  • vv. 10–14: an altar, the sacrifice outside the gate, and the city to come.
  • vv. 15–16: the sacrifices that remain: praise, doing good, and sharing.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: “Those who lead”, a plain word and not a title of rank, used three times in this chapter (13:7, 17, 24). Here their leading consists in speaking God's word and in a life worth copying.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: The Greek Old Testament uses this phrase for an offering of thanksgiving, as in Psalm 50. Here it is offered “through him”, through Christ.
  • koinōnia, sharing: Translated “to share what you have” in verse 16. It is the word for fellowship and for what the first Christians held in common (Acts 2:42). Hebrews names it, too, as a sacrifice.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

The Church's memory of her leaders is not nostalgia. The point of remembering them is to do what they did: to believe.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

Leaders die and teachings change, but the one they preached does not. Every ministry in the Church, Peter's included, lives only by serving him.

“We have an altar” (13:10)

Scholars discuss whether the writer is thinking first of the Cross or of the Eucharist. The Letter's own argument centres on the sacrifice of Christ, and the Church, feeding on that sacrifice, has not been wrong to hear in this line the altar of her own worship.

“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood.” (13:12)

The writer sees a sign in the bodies of the sin offerings burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27). His aim is to draw tired Christians toward Christ, not to speak against Israel, whose Scriptures he is using to think with.

“Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name.” (13:15)

Together with verse 16, this ties worship and generosity into one offering. In the Great Fast, prayer and giving to others belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses verse 15 when it lists the names of the Eucharist, among them “the sacrifice of praise” (CCC 1330). For the Church's understanding of leadership, the feast points to Antioch itself. It was from that Church that St Ignatius of Antioch was led away to martyrdom at Rome. In his letters he urged the Christians to stay united with their bishop, and he is the first we know of to use the phrase “the Catholic Church” (To the Smyrnaeans 8).

Matthew 24:45–51 · The slave put in charge of the household

Setting.

These verses come near the end of the long discourse on the Mount of Olives (chapters 24–25). Jesus has just said that no one knows the day or the hour (24:36). Now he tells a series of parables about the time of waiting.

In Luke's version of the same saying, Peter is the one who asks, “Lord, are you telling this parable for us or for everyone?” (Luke 12:41). The answer there, as here, concerns someone given charge over others. This explains why the parable suits a feast of Peter, though it speaks to everyone with any responsibility for others: parents, teachers, employers and clergy.

Structure.

  • v. 45: the question: who is the faithful and wise slave?
  • vv. 46–47: the blessing on the one found at work.
  • vv. 48–49: the other way of using the time.
  • vv. 50–51: the unexpected return and the judgement.

Key words.

  • pistos kai phronimos, faithful and wise: Phronimos is practical good sense, the wisdom of the bridesmaids with oil in the next chapter (25:2). Faithfulness and common sense are asked for together.
  • en kairō, at the proper time: The food comes when it is needed. A good steward does not feed people when it suits him.
  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: In Greek, originally a stage actor. The wicked slave plays at being a steward while he uses the position for himself.

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” (24:45)

He remains a slave among slaves. He is not the owner of the house. Authority here is defined by the food it gives.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)

Readiness for the Lord's return is not anxious watching of the sky. It means being found doing the work given to you.

“and he begins to beat his fellow slaves, and eats and drinks with drunkards,” (24:49)

Two sins of those in charge are named: harshness to those under them, and self-indulgence at their expense.

“He will cut him in pieces and put him with the hypocrites,” (24:51)

A fierce image in the style of parable. It is not a description to be taken literally, but its weight is real: responsibility for others will be answered for.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the office given to the pastors of God's people is in the strict sense of the term a service (CCC 1551). St Augustine told his people: for you I am a bishop, with you I am a Christian; the first is a danger and the second a grace (Sermon 340, in substance). St Gregory the Great called himself “servant of the servants of God”, and the popes have kept the title. Peter himself was given the same charge three times: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17).

Questions people ask

What is the See of St Peter at Antioch, and was Peter really there?

The New Testament places Peter at Antioch (Galatians 2:11), the city where the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). Early writers such as Origen and Eusebius connect him with the beginnings of the Church there. The New Testament does not say what title he held or how long he stayed, and we should not claim to know more than that. A “see” or “throne” is the bishop's chair, the sign of his office of teaching. The feast honours Peter's ministry at Antioch; it is not in competition with his see at Rome. The Catechism teaches that the Pope, as Peter's successor, is the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity of the bishops and of the whole company of the faithful (CCC 882).

Did Abraham change God's mind?

Genesis tells the story in human terms, as a bargaining, and it wants us to feel the boldness of it. But the Lord himself begins the conversation, by not hiding his plan from Abraham (18:17), and he never refuses. The story shows a God who wants to be asked, and who draws a human being into his own mercy. Prayer does not teach God to be merciful. It teaches us to want what he wants.

Why does it stop at ten?

The text does not explain it. Abraham simply stops asking, perhaps out of reverence. The next chapter shows that fewer than ten were found, yet “God remembered Abraham” and brought Lot out (19:29). Abraham's prayer did more than he knew.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Syro-Malankara Church prays in the Antiochene, West Syriac tradition, and in full communion with the Bishop of Rome. This feast joins these two sides of our identity: the Apostle who taught at Antioch is the one whose ministry continues at Rome. Our sister Churches of the Syriac tradition also honour the Antiochene see, and today is a good day to pray for the unity of all who look to Peter's confession. The Panchangam also remembers Thomas Mar Dioscoros (1943) and the anniversary of the consecration of St Mary's Cathedral, Thiruvananthapuram (1965), so that the universal and the local are held together in one day's prayer.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham prays for a city he does not belong to. For whom, outside my own circle, do I pray in this Fast?
  • Who spoke the word of God to me? What in their faith can I imitate this week?
  • Hebrews 13:15–16 joins praise and sharing as sacrifices. How do these two come together in the way I take part in the Holy Qurbono?
  • In what place am I “put in charge” of others? What is the food they need from me at the proper time?
