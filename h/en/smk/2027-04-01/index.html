Word for the day

“He is God not of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong.” Mark 12:27

Reflection

Christ is risen! In the white week of Easter the Church sets before us a question that was once meant as a trap. “Some Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, came to him.” They tell of a woman married in turn to seven brothers, each of whom died, and ask: “In the resurrection whose wife will she be?” The story is built to make the hope of rising look foolish. Jesus does not mock them; he shows them where they went astray: “Is not this the reason you are wrong, that you know neither the scriptures nor the power of God?” Many of the Jewish people of his day, with the Pharisees, already believed that God would raise the dead, as the prophets and the Maccabean martyrs had hoped (Daniel 12:2; 2 Maccabees 7:9). Jesus stands with that faith of Israel and deepens it. The risen life is not this life repeated; “when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven.” This does not make marriage small. Marriage belongs to this age as a sacrament of Christ's love for his Church, and it points beyond itself to the wedding feast of the Lamb, where every love that was faithful is fulfilled, not cancelled (Catechism 1619–1620).

Then the Lord goes to the book of Moses itself, to the burning bush. God said, “I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.” He did not say “I was.” Long after the patriarchs had been buried, God still named himself by them, because they were alive to him. “He is God not of the dead, but of the living.” The Letter to the Hebrews shows how this hope reaches us. It quotes Psalm 8: God made human beings “for a little while lower than the angels” and crowned them “with glory and honor.” Then it admits honestly: “As it is, we do not yet see everything in subjection to them, but we do see Jesus, who for a little while was made lower than the angels, now crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone.” What Psalm 8 promised to humanity is already true in one Man, the risen Jesus. St Irenaeus wrote that the glory of God is a human being fully alive, and that true life is to see God (in substance). On Easter morning that glory shone first in the body of Christ, and in him it is promised to us.

How do we live this Thursday of Hevoro week? First, speak of our departed with Easter words. When we remember parents, grandparents and friends who have died, let us say what Jesus says: God is their God still, and they are alive to him. Pray for them at the Holy Qurbono, and light the lamp at home for them this evening with an Easter hymn. Second, honor marriage as a road to heaven. Husbands and wives can pray together tonight for the grace to love each other in a way that will not be ashamed on the last day. Third, when someone laughs at the faith with a clever question, answer as the Lord did: patiently, from the Scriptures, and with trust in the power of God. Hevoro week continues until New Sunday, when the Church will hear how the risen Lord showed his wounds to Thomas.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! In today's Gospel some Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, bring Jesus a story about seven brothers and one wife. They want to make the hope of rising look foolish.

Jesus answers gently but clearly: you are wrong, because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God (Mark 12:24). The life to come is not this life repeated. It is the life of God, where every faithful love is fulfilled.

Then he reminds them of the burning bush: I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. God is not the God of the dead, but of the living. The patriarchs were alive to him. The Letter to the Hebrews adds: we do see Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because he tasted death for everyone (Hebrews 2:9).

So in this Hevoro week, let us speak of our departed with Easter hope, pray for them at the Holy Qurbono, and live as people who believe that God is the God of the living. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Some people asked Jesus a tricky question because they did not believe that people who die will live again. Jesus said: God is the God of the living! Abraham, who lived long, long ago, is alive to God. Jesus rose at Easter, and one day he will raise us too. Tonight, can you pray for someone in your family who has gone to God?

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In this white week the Church keeps returning to one truth from different sides: death has been answered. Hebrews takes up Psalm 8, which marvels that God crowned human beings with glory, and admits that we do not yet see everything under their feet, “but we do see Jesus”. In the Gospel some Sadducees bring Jesus a puzzle meant to make the resurrection look absurd, and he answers that God “is God not of the dead, but of the living”. One reading shows the risen Lord crowned; the other shows why that crown is meant for us too.

Hebrews 2:5–9 · We do see Jesus, crowned with glory

Setting.

Hebrews is a sermon in written form, which its author calls a “word of exhortation” (13:22). The author does not give his name. The early Church already discussed whether it was Paul's, and Origen said that only God knows who wrote it; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture. Chapter 1 has shown the Son far above the angels. Chapter 2 now asks why, then, he was made lower than them for a time.

To answer, the author quotes Psalm 8 from the Greek Bible. The psalm speaks of humanity in general: the small human being whom God has crowned and set over creation. The author notices that this is not what we see now. Then he finds one human being in whom the psalm has already come true.

Structure.

  • v. 5: the world to come is not placed under angels.
  • vv. 6–8a: Psalm 8: what are human beings, that you are mindful of them?
  • v. 8b: we do not yet see everything subject to them,
  • v. 9: but we see Jesus, lowered for a little while, crowned because of the suffering of death, tasting death for everyone.

Key words.

  • oikoumenē mellousa, the coming world: Literally “the inhabited world to come”. It is the new creation that began when Christ rose and entered glory. The author adds “about which we are speaking”, because the whole sermon is about it.
  • brachy ti, a little, for a little while: The Hebrew of Psalm 8 says that God made human beings a little lower than ’elōhîm, “God” or “heavenly beings”, and the Greek translators wrote “angels”. The Greek phrase can mean “a little” in rank or “for a little while” in time. Hebrews uses the second sense: the Son was lower than the angels only for the short time of his earthly life and death.
  • geusētai thanatou, taste death: A Semitic way of saying “experience death”. It does not mean a small sip. He went all the way into death, and he did it “for everyone”.

Verse by verse.

“What are human beings that you are mindful of them, or mortals, that you care for them?” (2:6)

The Greek has “man” and “son of man”, which NRSV renders as “human beings” and “mortals”. Christian readers have long heard in “son of man” the title Jesus used of himself. The author lets both senses stand: Jesus is the true man in whom the calling of all humanity is fulfilled.

“As it is, we do not yet see everything in subjection to them,” (2:8)

An honest sentence. The author does not pretend that the world already looks redeemed. Death, sickness and injustice are still here.

“but we do see Jesus, who for a little while was made lower than the angels, now crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death,” (2:9)

This is the first time the sermon uses the name Jesus, and it comes at the high point. The crown is not given in spite of the suffering but “because of” it.

“so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone.” (2:9)

A few ancient witnesses read “apart from God” instead of “by the grace of God”, and Origen already knew both readings. The two phrases look alike in Greek (chariti theou, chōris theou). The best manuscripts and almost all translations have “by the grace of God”, and that is the reading the Church proclaims.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites this verse when it teaches that Jesus Christ tasted death for the benefit of every human being, and that it was truly the Son of God made man who died and was buried (CCC 629). The Second Vatican Council teaches that Christ, the new Adam, fully reveals man to himself and makes his high calling clear (Gaudium et Spes 22). So the psalm's question, “What are human beings?”, finds its full answer in the risen Lord. His glory is not a prize for a stranger; it is the first fruit of our own.

Mark 12:18–27 · God of the living

Setting.

Jesus is in the Temple at Jerusalem in the last days before his Passion. One group after another comes to question him: the chief priests, scribes and elders about his authority (11:27–33), then Pharisees and Herodians about the tax (12:13–17), and now Sadducees. This is the only time they appear in Mark's Gospel.

The Sadducees were a priestly and wealthy party, closely linked with the Temple. They held to the written law of Moses and did not accept the traditions that the Pharisees taught, and they denied the resurrection of the dead (see Acts 23:8). Their question rests on the law of levirate marriage in Deuteronomy 25:5–10: a brother was to marry the widow of a man who died without a son, so that the dead man's name would not be blotted out in Israel.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–23: the Sadducees' case: seven brothers and one wife.
  • v. 24: Jesus' answer: you know neither the scriptures nor the power of God.
  • v. 25: the power of God: the risen do not marry.
  • vv. 26–27: the scriptures: the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob is the God of the living.

Key words.

  • anastasis, resurrection: Literally “standing up again”. In verse 23 the Sadducees use it as a trap, but it is the word of Easter.
  • planasthe, you are wrong: From planaō, to wander or go astray; our word “planet”, a wandering star, comes from the same root. Jesus uses it at the beginning and at the end of his answer (vv. 24, 27), like a frame.
  • dynamis tou theou, the power of God: The Sadducees pictured the life to come as this life carried on, and so it looked absurd. Jesus says that God's power makes something new.

Verse by verse.

“Is not this the reason you are wrong, that you know neither the scriptures nor the power of God?” (12:24)

Two failures, and Jesus answers each in turn: the power of God in verse 25, the scriptures in verses 26 and 27.

“For when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven.” (12:25)

The risen do not become angels. They share the angels' undying life, and so marriage, which carries life forward through children, has finished its earthly task. Love itself is not lost.

“He is God not of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong.” (12:27)

God spoke to Moses at the bush long after the patriarchs had died (Exodus 3:6), and he still called himself their God. A bond with God is not ended by death. Those whom God has made his own are kept alive in him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this answer when it teaches that Jesus firmly taught the resurrection of the dead (CCC 993), and then adds that he binds faith in the resurrection to his own person: “I am the resurrection and the life” (John 11:25; CCC 994). What Jesus argued in the Temple, he showed at the tomb. The patriarchs, the martyrs and our own beloved dead live to God, and every Holy Qurbono is offered in communion with them.

Questions people ask

Will husband and wife know each other in heaven?

Jesus does not say that the bonds of this life are forgotten. He says that marriage, as an institution of this age with its task of bringing children into the world, belongs to this age. The Catechism describes heaven as the perfect life with the Holy Trinity, a communion of life and love with the Trinity, the Virgin Mary, the angels and all the blessed (CCC 1024). Every true love that was lived in God is purified and fulfilled there, not erased.

For study and teaching

  • The Sadducees' puzzle tried to make faith look foolish. How do I answer, gently and clearly, when someone mocks the hope of the resurrection?
  • “We do not yet see everything in subjection.” Where do I find it hard to believe that Christ has already won? What would help me to “see Jesus” there?
