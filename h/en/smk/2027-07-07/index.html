Word for the day

“Let us also go, that we may die with him.” John 11:16

Reflection

St Paul writes to the Corinthians with a feeling that he himself calls “a divine jealousy”: “I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” The picture is a betrothal. In the Bible the love of God for his people is often told as a marriage (Hosea 2:19–20), and Paul sees himself as the friend of the bridegroom whose task is to bring the bride to him and to guard her faithfulness (John 3:29). His fear is not that the Corinthians will openly deny Christ but that they will be led astray little by little: “I am afraid that as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning, your thoughts will be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ.” The serpent did not at first ask Eve to reject God; he asked a clever question. In the same way, Paul says, a stranger may come and proclaim “another Jesus than the one we proclaimed,” “a different spirit,” “a different gospel”, and the people “submit to it readily enough.” In our days voices about Jesus reach us from every side, from pulpits, screens and forwarded messages, and the question is the same: is this the Jesus whom the Apostles proclaimed, the Jesus of the Gospels and the Creed, who is the Son of God, died and rose? We ask it humbly, without contempt for anyone who sincerely seeks him; we ask it chiefly of ourselves, so that our own devotion remains simple and pure.

The Gospel begins when Jesus, having waited two days after hearing that Lazarus was ill, says to his disciples, “Let us go to Judea again.” The disciples are afraid: “Rabbi, the Jews were just now trying to stone you, and are you going there again?” (When John writes “the Jews” here he means some of the people and leaders of Judea who were hostile on that occasion; Jesus and his disciples were Jews themselves, and the Church does not blame the Jewish people as a whole for what happened to him: Nostra Aetate 4.) Jesus answers with a riddle about the day: “Are there not twelve hours of daylight? Those who walk during the day do not stumble, because they see the light of this world. But those who walk at night stumble, because the light is not in them.” The hours of Jesus are measured by the Father's will. Whoever walks with him walks in daylight, even on a dangerous road, and cannot stumble; whoever walks without him walks in the dark, even on a smooth one. Then he says: “Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going there to awaken him.” The disciples take this for ordinary sleep and are relieved. “Then Jesus told them plainly, ‘Lazarus is dead.’”

Christians speak of the dead as those who have fallen asleep. The Greek word for a burial ground, koimeterion, from which we get “cemetery”, means a sleeping-place. St Augustine puts it in a phrase: to the Lord Lazarus was asleep, though to men he was dead, for the Lord could wake him as easily as one wakes a sleeper (Tractate on John 49, in substance). We do not grieve as others do who have no hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13). And then the most unexpected voice speaks: “Thomas, who was called the Twin, said to his fellow disciples, ‘Let us also go, that we may die with him.’” Only four days ago, on Saturday, we kept the Dukrono of Mar Thoma Shleeha, the Apostle whom our tradition says carried the Gospel to India. Most people remember him for one doubt after the Resurrection, but here he speaks first and bravely. He does not understand what the Lord is about to do; he is gloomy, and he is ready to die. He goes. St Paul wished the Corinthians a sincere and pure devotion; Thomas shows what it looks like, a love that follows the Lord to Judea, to the tomb and to the end, even before it understands. The Lord says, “For your sake I am glad I was not there, so that you may believe. But let us go to him.” Let us also go.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Epistle St Paul speaks of a divine jealousy. He has betrothed the Corinthians to one husband, to present them as a chaste virgin to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2). He is afraid that, as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning, their thoughts will be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:3). Another Jesus, a different spirit, a different gospel: people accept such things readily enough (2 Corinthians 11:4). In our own days many voices speak about Jesus; we must test them against the Gospel and the Creed, and keep our own devotion simple.

In the Gospel Jesus says, Let us go to Judea again (John 11:7). The disciples are afraid, since people there had lately tried to stone Jesus. Jesus says that there are twelve hours of daylight, and those who walk by day do not stumble because they see the light of this world (John 11:9). Whoever walks with Jesus walks by day. He adds: Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going there to awaken him (John 11:11). For Christians death is a sleep from which the Lord awakens us.

And then Thomas speaks: Let us also go, that we may die with him (John 11:16). It is the bravest sentence the disciples say in this chapter. On Saturday we kept the Dukrono of Mar Thoma Shleeha, and today we see him follow his Lord, without understanding everything, to Judea and to the tomb.

Let us ask the Lord for a pure devotion and for the courage of Thomas, so that whenever he goes before us we may say, Let us also go. Amen.

For children

Children, do you remember the Apostle Thomas, whose feast we kept last Saturday? Today we hear about a brave thing he did. Jesus said that he was going to a place where some people wanted to hurt him. The other friends were afraid, but Thomas said: “Let us also go!” He did not want to leave Jesus alone. We can be like Thomas. When a friend is sad, when someone is sick, or when it is hard to do the right thing, we can say, “Let us also go; Jesus is with us.” Let us ask Jesus to make us brave friends.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, bridegroom of your Church, you wish to present her to the Father without spot or wrinkle. Keep us in a sincere and pure devotion to you, and guard us from every other Jesus, every different spirit and every different gospel. Let us walk with you in the light of day, never stumbling, and never afraid to follow you even to Judea. Give us the courage of your Apostle Thomas, who said, “Let us also go, that we may die with him,” and who carried your name to distant lands. Be with those who grieve, as you were with Martha and Mary, and awaken all who have fallen asleep in you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about faithfulness to Christ when danger and false voices are near. In the Epistle Paul, who feels a “divine jealousy” for the Corinthians, fears that they will be led away from a sincere devotion to Christ and will accept “another Jesus”. In the Gospel Jesus decides to go back to Judea, where he was nearly stoned, because his friend Lazarus has died, and Thomas answers, “Let us also go, that we may die with him.” The key words are devotion and going with Jesus.

2 Corinthians 11:1–6 · One husband, one Christ

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 Paul defends his ministry against newcomers in Corinth who despise him and boast of their own gifts. Many scholars think these chapters come from a sharper letter that Paul wrote separately; others read 2 Corinthians as one letter. Either way they are among the most personal pages he wrote.

Here Paul begins what is called his “fool's speech” (11:1 to 12:13). He dislikes boasting, but he agrees to speak as a “fool” for a while, because the Corinthians are being impressed by people who boast. He calls these people, with some irony, “super-apostles” (11:5). They preached something different from what Paul had preached, and Paul fears for the faith of a church he had founded.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Paul asks the Corinthians to bear with a little foolishness.
  • vv. 2–3: his jealousy is divine; he has betrothed them to one husband, and fears the serpent's deceit.
  • v. 4: they accept readily another Jesus, another spirit, another gospel.
  • vv. 5–6: Paul is not inferior to the “super-apostles”: untrained in speech, but not in knowledge.

Key words.

  • theou zēlos, a jealousy of God, a divine jealousy: In the Old Testament God himself is “jealous” for his people: he wants their whole love, as a husband wants the love of his wife (Exodus 20:5; Hosea 2). Paul shares that jealousy: it is not envy but a burning care for the faith of those he loves.
  • hērmosamēn, I betrothed (you): Paul speaks like the friend of the bridegroom, who brings the bride to her husband. He presents the community to Christ as a bride. The image runs through Scripture, from the prophets (Isaiah 54:5) to Ephesians 5:25–27 and Revelation 19:7.
  • allon Iēsoun, another Jesus: Paul does not mean that there are two Jesuses. He means that a different message, with a Jesus who is not the Crucified and Risen Lord whom Paul preached, is no gospel at all.

Verse by verse.

“I feel a divine jealousy for you,” (11:2)

The apostle is like a father who has given his daughter in marriage and cannot bear that she be wooed away. His love for the Corinthians is the love of a pastor.

“But I am afraid that as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning,” (11:3)

The serpent in Genesis 3 did not begin by denying God outright; he twisted God's word a little. Paul fears the same subtle way for the Corinthians: not open denial, but a gradual drifting away from simple devotion to Christ.

“I may be untrained in speech, but not in knowledge;” (11:6)

Corinth admired fine oratory. Paul admits that he is no orator (compare 1 Corinthians 2:1–5), but the knowledge of Christ that he gives is true and deep. The truth of the message does not depend on the polish of the messenger.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in these verses the image of herself as the bride of Christ, a bride whom the Lord “loved and handed himself over for” to make her holy (CCC 796). The same image shows why unity in the faith matters: the bride must remain faithful to the one Lord and the one Gospel handed down. This is why the Church guards the Apostles' teaching in her creeds and in her liturgy. Paul's warning about “another Jesus” is a reminder that the Jesus we worship is the Jesus of the Scriptures and the apostolic Tradition, and not a figure shaped to our own taste.

John 11:7–16 · Let us also go, that we may die with him

Setting.

Lazarus of Bethany, brother of Martha and Mary, is ill, and his sisters have sent word to Jesus (11:3). Jesus had left Judea for the other side of the Jordan after the authorities there tried to stone him (10:31, 39–40). He stays two more days where he is (11:6), and then says to his disciples, “Let us go to Judea again.”

John's word “the Jews” here means the authorities and those in Judea who were hostile to Jesus (compare 10:24, 31), not the Jewish people as a whole; Jesus, his disciples, Mary, Martha and Lazarus are all Jews. The disciples' fear is real: Bethany lies close to Jerusalem (11:18), and the danger there is the danger that leads at last to the Cross.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: Jesus proposes to return to Judea; the disciples remind him of the stoning.
  • vv. 9–10: the parable of day and night: walking in the light.
  • vv. 11–13: Lazarus has fallen asleep; the disciples misunderstand.
  • vv. 14–15: Jesus speaks plainly: Lazarus is dead; he is glad, for their sake, that he was not there.
  • v. 16: Thomas: “Let us also go, that we may die with him.”

Key words.

  • hōrai, hours (of the day): The Jewish day was counted from sunrise to sunset in twelve hours. Jesus uses an everyday truth: there is a time set for work, and no one can stop the day. His “hour” is in the Father's hands, and he walks in the light of the Father's will.
  • kekoimētai, has fallen asleep: Sleep was a common word for death in the Old Testament and the New (Daniel 12:2; Matthew 27:52; 1 Thessalonians 4:13). For believers death is a sleep from which the Lord wakes us. Jesus uses the word in this deeper way; the disciples take it only of ordinary sleep.
  • Didymos, Twin: This is the Greek translation of the Aramaic Tomā, “twin”. John gives both names (11:16; 20:24; 21:2). We do not know who the twin was.

Verse by verse.

“Are there not twelve hours of daylight?” (11:9)

Jesus is not afraid of the danger, because the Father has fixed the time. Whoever walks in God's will walks in daylight and does not stumble. The one who walks without that light, “in the night”, has no light in himself.

“Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going there to awaken him.” (11:11)

“Our friend”: Jesus speaks of Lazarus with love and calls him a friend of the disciples as well. His going to Bethany is the going of a friend, and his waking of Lazarus is the sign of what he will do for all.

“Let us also go, that we may die with him.” (11:16)

The sentence is ambiguous: “with him” may mean Lazarus or Jesus. The context, and the disciples' fear of the stoning, make most readers take it to mean Jesus. Thomas is loyal and ready to die, though he does not yet understand. At the Supper he will ask, “How can we know the way?” (14:5), and after the Resurrection he will confess, “My Lord and my God!” (20:28).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the raising of Lazarus as a sign of the resurrection that Christ promises to all who believe in him (CCC 994). Jesus is the Lord of life: he goes into the place of danger to give life to his friend, and in this he foretells his own death and rising. The Church has long heard in Thomas's words a model of loyalty: to follow Christ may mean to go where the danger is. According to tradition, Thomas later carried the Gospel as far as India and gave his life there for Christ. Because of Christ, Christian death is not an end but a passage; as Paul says, “For to me, living is Christ and dying is gain” (Philippians 1:21; CCC 1010).

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus say he is glad that he was not at Bethany?

He does not rejoice in Lazarus's death, and in 11:35 he weeps. He is glad “for your sake”, because the disciples will now see something greater than a healing: the raising of a man already dead, and so a sign of his power over death itself. The delay is not neglect. It is the love of one who has a greater gift in hand (11:4).

For study and teaching

  • Paul fears that the Corinthians may be led away from a “sincere and pure devotion to Christ”. What most easily draws my own devotion away: noise, fashion, or an easier gospel?
  • Thomas says, “Let us also go.” Where is the Lord asking me to go with him, even though it is difficult?
