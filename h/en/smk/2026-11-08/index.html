Word for the day

“Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” John 2:19

Reflection

Last Sunday we celebrated the Church as God's holy dwelling. Today, Hudos Eetho, we celebrate her renewal. The two belong together. The Church is holy because Christ sanctifies her, yet she is made of sinners and is always in need of purification. The Second Vatican Council says it plainly: the Church, “holding sinners in her bosom, is at the same time holy and always in need of being purified” (Lumen Gentium 8).

The readings show both the danger and the remedy. At the foot of Sinai the people make a golden calf, worshipping a god they can control. Solomon, dedicating the Temple, admits that “heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, much less this house that I have built” (1 Kings 8:27). God's house can never be our possession. The risen Christ says to the Church of Laodicea: “Because you are lukewarm… I am about to spit you out of my mouth” (Revelation 3:16). The danger of a Church is not only sin but comfort.

Jesus enters the Temple with a whip of cords and drives out the traders. His anger is not against worship but against worship turned into business. Then he points to himself: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” He was speaking of the temple of his body. The true renewal of the Church is always a return to Christ, crucified and risen, whose blood, says Hebrews, purifies “our conscience from dead works to worship the living God” (Hebrews 9:14).

Renewal does not come from outside the Church but from her own sources: the Word, the Mysteries, prayer and repentance. That is why the Lord, after rebuking Laodicea, says: “Listen! I am standing at the door, knocking; if you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in to you and eat with you.”

Homily outline

Theme: “Renew the house of God, beginning with yourself.”

Last Sunday we celebrated the Church as God's holy dwelling. Today, Hudos Eetho, the Church asks to be renewed. Jesus enters the Temple, drives out the traders and speaks of a new temple, his own body. Renewal is not a new building or a new programme; it is a return to God.

1. Zeal for the Father's house

  • Jesus makes a whip and overturns the tables: he will not allow God's house to become a market.
  • At Sinai the people exchanged the living God for a golden calf; idols always creep into the house of God.
  • Our idols today: money, status, rivalry and noise in God's house, and the busy “trade” in our own hearts.
“And they said, “These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!”” Exodus 32:4
“He told those who were selling the doves, “Take these things out of here! Stop making my Father's house a marketplace!”” John 2:16

Apply: Name one idol that crowds God out of your heart, and remove it this week.

2. Christ is the true temple

  • Solomon knew that no building could contain God.
  • Jesus says: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up”: he speaks of his body.
  • Through his body, crucified and risen, we meet God; in Baptism we become members of that body, the Church.
“But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Even heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, much less this house that I have built!” 1 Kings 8:27
“Jesus answered them, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” … But he was speaking of the temple of his body.” John 2:19, 21

Apply: Before Holy Qurbono, remember: you are entering the body of Christ, and you will receive him into your own.

3. Renewal begins with repentance

  • Moses interceded and God relented; there is always a way back.
  • Isaiah invites: “Seek the LORD while he may be found.”
  • The risen Christ knocks at the door of a lukewarm Church; renewal is opening the door to him.
“Seek the LORD while he may be found, call upon him while he is near; let the wicked forsake their way, and the unrighteous their thoughts; let them return to the LORD, that he may have mercy on them.” Isaiah 55:6–7
“Listen! I am standing at the door, knocking; if you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in to you and eat with you, and you with me.” Revelation 3:20

Apply: Make a good confession before the Annunciation season advances.

The Church is renewed when each of us is renewed. Jesus still cleanses the temple, not with a whip of cords now, but with his word and his sacraments.

Let us open the door to him, cast out our idols, and become again a house of prayer for all peoples.

Take-home resolution: Spend ten minutes in silence in church this week, asking: “Lord, what must you cleanse in me?”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, last Sunday we celebrated the Church as God's holy house. Today, Hudos Eetho, we celebrate the renewal of the Church. Both are true at once: the Church is holy because Christ makes her holy, and she is always in need of renewal because she is made of us, sinners.

Our readings show two dangers. At Sinai the people made a golden calf, a god they could see and control. And the risen Lord says to the Church of Laodicea: “Because you are lukewarm, I will spit you out of my mouth.” The danger of the Church is not only sin, but comfort.

Then Jesus enters the Temple and overturns the tables of the traders. He was not against worship; he was against worship turned into business. And he said: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” He was speaking of his body. Every true renewal of the Church is a return to Christ, crucified and risen.

So where does renewal begin? Not with someone else. With me. With a good Confession, with silence and reverence in church, with a heart that opens when the Lord knocks. “If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in and eat with him.” In a few moments he will do exactly that at this altar. Amen.

For children

Children, what happens if nobody cleans the house for a long time? Dust everywhere! Jesus once went into the Temple and cleaned it, because people had made it a market.

Our hearts are God's house too. We clean them by saying sorry to Jesus and by praying. Jesus says: “I am standing at your door, knocking.” Shall we open the door to him? Let's say: “Come in, Jesus!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you cleansed the Temple and raised up the temple of your body. Purify your Church, free us from idols and lukewarmness, and come in to eat with us at your holy table. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hudos Eetho, the Sunday of the Renewal of the Church, follows Qudos Eetho, the Sunday of her Sanctification, at the opening of the West Syriac liturgical year. Last Sunday the Church was consecrated; today she is renewed. The readings are many, and all of them circle one place: the house of God. In the evening Gospel (Mark 11:15–17) Jesus calls the Temple “a house of prayer for all the nations”. Exodus 32 shows the people making a god they can control, and Moses standing between them and God's anger. Jeremiah 31 and Isaiah 55 promise a scattered people that God will gather them and feed them again. In Revelation 3 the risen Lord knocks at the door of a comfortable, lukewarm Church.

This study takes three of them: Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the Temple, the Letter to the Hebrews on the true sanctuary, and the Gospel of John, where Jesus cleanses the Temple and speaks of the temple of his body. The line runs from a house of stone, to a tent not made with hands, to the body of Christ, destroyed and raised. Its key words are dwelling, cleansing and renewal.

1 Kings 8:22–30 · Can God dwell on the earth?

Setting.

Solomon has finished the Temple in Jerusalem, in the tenth century BC, and the ark of the covenant has been carried into the inner sanctuary. The cloud of the Lord's glory fills the house, so that the priests cannot stand to minister (8:10–11). Then the king turns to pray. Chapter 8 is the great dedication chapter of the Old Testament: the procession of the ark (vv. 1–13), Solomon's address to the people (vv. 14–21), his long prayer (vv. 22–53), the blessing (vv. 54–61), and the sacrifices and the feast (vv. 62–66).

The books of Kings received their final form during or after the Babylonian exile, when the Temple lay in ruins. For those first readers the prayer had a sharp edge. God had never been shut inside the building, and he still hears prayer offered toward this place from a far country (8:46–50).

Structure.

  • v. 22: Solomon stands before the altar and spreads out his hands to heaven, the ancient posture of prayer.
  • vv. 23–26: praise of the God who keeps covenant, and a plea that the promise to David be confirmed.
  • v. 27: the great question: will God indeed dwell on the earth?
  • vv. 28–30: the request that God's eyes be open toward this house, and that he hear and forgive.
  • ¶ The prayer moves from praise, to promise, to humility, to petition. Its heart is v. 27, set between two requests: the house is real and holy, yet God is greater than it.

Key words.

  • ḥesed, steadfast love: Covenant faithfulness, love that keeps its word (v. 23). It is the love God has promised and will not take back.
  • šēm, name: “My name shall be there” (v. 29). God's name stands for God himself as he makes himself known and lets himself be called upon. His name dwells in the Temple, while he himself is in heaven. In this way the book holds together God's nearness and his greatness.
  • sālaḥ, forgive: The last word of the reading (v. 30). In the Old Testament this verb always has God as its subject: only God forgives in this full sense. The Temple is, above all, a house where sins are forgiven.

Verse by verse.

“Then Solomon stood before the altar of the Lord in the presence of all the assembly of Israel, and spread out his hands to heaven.” (8:22)

The king prays as head of the assembly, standing, with open hands. The first Christians prayed in the same way, as the praying figures painted in the catacombs show. Prayer begins with open hands: we come to receive.

“But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Even heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, much less this house that I have built!” (8:27)

Solomon has just built the finest building Israel would ever have, and his first words over it confess its smallness. “The highest heaven” is literally “the heaven of heavens”, a Hebrew way of saying the very highest. The house does not hold God; it is the place where he chooses to meet his people. The Gospel answers Solomon's question in a way he could not have imagined: “the Word became flesh and lived among us” (John 1:14).

“that your eyes may be open night and day toward this house, the place of which you said, ‘My name shall be there,’” (8:29)

Solomon does not ask that God live in the house, but that God look toward it. Daniel in exile prays three times a day at windows open toward Jerusalem (Daniel 6:10). The Church keeps this sense of direction: we pray toward the altar, and in the Syriac tradition toward the east, from where the Lord will come.

“O hear in heaven your dwelling place; heed and forgive.” (8:30)

The prayer ends in forgiveness. In the verses that follow (vv. 31–53) Solomon lists the troubles that will bring people to this house: injustice, defeat, drought, famine, the foreigner who comes from afar, exile. Each time the plea returns: hear and forgive.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to this prayer when it speaks of prayer in the time of the kings: the Temple was to be the place of Israel's prayer, and Solomon's prayer at its dedication rests on God's promise and covenant (CCC 2580, in substance). The Roman Rite also reads part of this chapter (8:22–23, 27–30) at the dedication of a church. Christians read v. 27 in the light of the Incarnation: the God whom the heavens cannot contain was carried in the womb of the Virgin Mary, as the hymns of the Churches of East and West love to sing.

Hebrews 9:1–14 · Until the time comes to set things right

Setting.

Hebrews is a sermon in writing, “a word of exhortation” (13:22), sent to Christians who were growing tired and were tempted to look back. Its author does not give his name; the Church received it with the letters of St Paul. Chapters 8–10 compare the worship of the first covenant with the one offering of Christ.

The background of chapter 9 is the Day of Atonement, Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16). Once a year the high priest went behind the curtain into the Holy of Holies with the blood of a bull and a goat, and sprinkled the mercy seat for his own sins and for the sins of the people. The author describes not the Temple of his own day but the tent of the desert, as the Book of Exodus describes it.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: the two rooms of the tent and their furnishings.
  • vv. 6–7: the priests enter the first room daily; the high priest alone enters the second, once a year.
  • vv. 8–10: what the Holy Spirit teaches by this arrangement: the way into the sanctuary was not yet open.
  • vv. 11–12: Christ enters the true sanctuary with his own blood.
  • vv. 13–14: if the blood of animals cleansed the flesh, how much more will the blood of Christ cleanse the conscience.

Key words.

  • diorthōsis, setting right: “Until the time comes to set things right” (v. 10). The word means to straighten what is crooked, to restore. The Malayalam POC renders it “the time of renewal” (നവീകരണകാലം), a fitting word for Hudos Eetho. For Hebrews, that time has come in Christ.
  • syneidēsis, conscience: The inner self that knows itself guilty or clean. The old offerings “cannot perfect the conscience of the worshiper” (v. 9); the blood of Christ can (v. 14). Renewal in Hebrews begins inside.
  • nekra erga, dead works: Works that come from sin and lead to death. Some also hear in the phrase the old rites, which could not give life. From these the conscience is cleansed, so that it may worship.

Verse by verse.

“By this the Holy Spirit indicates that the way into the sanctuary has not yet been disclosed as long as the first tent is still standing.” (9:8)

The arrangement of the tent was itself a lesson. A curtain, and a single entry once a year, said plainly that the way was still closed. When Jesus died, “the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom” (Mark 15:38), and the way was opened.

“he entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood, thus obtaining eternal redemption.” (9:12)

Once for all: the high priest had to return every year; Christ does not. In v. 11 the manuscripts differ between “the good things that have come” (NRSV) and “the good things to come” (POC). Both are true of Christ.

“how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to worship the living God!” (9:14)

The verse ends not with forgiveness alone but with worship. We are cleansed in order to serve the living God, and the verb is the word for liturgical service. The goal of redemption is a worshipping people.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ, the one high priest, entered once for all into the heavenly sanctuary (CCC 662), and that the Eucharist is the memorial which makes present his one sacrifice (CCC 1364–1366). The Second Vatican Council says that the Church, holding sinners in her bosom, is at once holy and always in need of being purified, and follows constantly the path of penance and renewal (Lumen Gentium 8). Hebrews shows where that renewal comes from: not first from new plans, but from the blood of Christ, which cleanses the conscience and makes us able to worship.

John 2:13–22 · The temple of his body

Setting.

John places this scene at the beginning of Jesus' public life, at the first of the three Passovers his Gospel mentions (2:13; 6:4; 11:55). Matthew, Mark and Luke tell it in the last week, after the entry into Jerusalem; this evening's Gospel from Mark is that account. Whatever the order of events, John has set it at the start as a sign that explains his whole Gospel: Jesus himself is the new Temple.

Pilgrims coming for Passover needed animals without blemish for sacrifice, and the Temple tax was paid in Tyrian silver coins, so money changers sat in the outer court. The trade was lawful and useful. Jesus' action is a prophetic sign, like the signs of Jeremiah, against worship turned into a market.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: Passover, Jerusalem, and the trade in the Temple courts.
  • vv. 15–16: the whip of cords, the overturned tables, and the word about his Father's house.
  • v. 17: the disciples remember Psalm 69.
  • vv. 18–20: the authorities ask for a sign; Jesus answers with the riddle of the temple destroyed and raised.
  • vv. 21–22: the evangelist explains: he spoke of his body, and the disciples understood after the Resurrection.

Key words.

  • hieron / naos, temple precinct / sanctuary: In vv. 14–15 John uses hieron, the whole Temple area with its courts, where the trading took place. In vv. 19–21 he uses naos, the sanctuary itself, the dwelling of God. The body of Jesus is the naos, the true dwelling of God among us.
  • zēlos, zeal: Burning, jealous love. Psalm 69 says that zeal for God's house “has consumed me”, in the past; John writes “will consume me”, in the future. The disciples see the Passion in it: Jesus' love for his Father's house will cost him his life.
  • oikos emporiou, marketplace, house of trade: Zechariah ends his prophecy with the day when “there shall no longer be traders in the house of the Lord of hosts” (Zechariah 14:21). Jesus acts as the one who brings that day.

Verse by verse.

“Making a whip of cords, he drove all of them out of the temple, both the sheep and the cattle.” (2:15)

Only John mentions the whip, and the sentence suggests that it was used to drive out the sheep and the cattle. The scene shows authority, not cruelty.

“Stop making my Father’s house a marketplace!” (2:16)

In his first scene in Jerusalem Jesus calls God “my Father”. The Temple is his Father's home, and he speaks as the Son of the house.

“Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” (2:19)

The command is ironic. Others will destroy the temple of his body, and he will raise it. At his trial the saying was twisted against him (Mark 14:58). The verb “raise up” is the verb used for the Resurrection.

“This temple has been under construction for forty-six years, and will you raise it up in three days?” (2:20)

Herod the Great began rebuilding the Temple around 20/19 BC, and the work went on long after. Forty-six years points to about AD 27/28, a useful date for the start of Jesus' ministry. The Temple was finished only a few years before the Romans destroyed it in AD 70.

“But he was speaking of the temple of his body.” (2:21)
“After he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this; and they believed the scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken.” (2:22)

Understanding comes later, in the light of Easter and through the Holy Spirit, who will “remind you of all that I have said to you” (John 14:26). The Church always reads the Scriptures from the empty tomb.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus went up to the Temple as the privileged place of meeting with God, and drove out the traders in jealous love for his Father (CCC 584). He did not despise the Temple. He presented himself as God's definitive dwelling among us, the true Temple, whose destruction and raising would be his death and Resurrection (CCC 586, in substance). When John writes “the Jews” here, he means the Temple authorities who questioned Jesus, not the Jewish people. The Church teaches that the Jewish people as such may not be blamed for Jesus' death (CCC 597; Nostra Aetate 4).

Because the risen body of Christ is the true Temple, the Church, his Body, is also God's temple, and each believer is a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16; 6:19; CCC 797). St Augustine, preaching at the dedication of a church, told his people that this building is the house of our prayers, but we ourselves are the house of God, built in this life and dedicated at the end of time (in substance). The church building is holy because it signifies and serves this living temple (CCC 1179–1180).

Questions people ask

Was Jesus angry? Is anger a sin?

Anger is a passion; it becomes sin when it seeks a wrong end or goes beyond measure. Jesus' anger is holy zeal: love for his Father's house, and for the nations who came to pray there (Mark 11:17). St Paul writes, “Be angry but do not sin” (Ephesians 4:26).

If God cannot be contained in a building, why do we consecrate churches?

Solomon asked the same question. God does not need a house, but we do. A church is the visible sign of the Church, the place where the community gathers, hears the Word and celebrates the sacraments (CCC 1180–1181, in substance). It is holy not because it holds God, but because he has chosen to meet his people there.

Did Jesus cleanse the Temple once or twice?

John places it at the beginning, the other three Gospels at the end. Some interpreters think there were two cleansings; many think John set the one event at the beginning for its meaning, as a key to his whole Gospel. The Church has not decided the question, and the teaching is the same either way: Jesus is the Lord of the Temple and its fulfilment.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac calendar the liturgical year opens with the Sundays of the Church: Qudos Eetho, when the Church is sanctified, and Hudos Eetho, when she is renewed. Before the Sundays of the Annunciations begin next week, the Church looks at herself. A church and its altar are consecrated with holy Myron, and the altar becomes the place of the Lord's presence among his people. Today's Gospel asks every parish a plain question: is our church a house of prayer, or a marketplace? The renewal that Hudos Eetho prays for begins in the heart, is fed at the altar in the Holy Qurbono, and shows itself in the life of the parish.

For study and teaching

  • Solomon asks, “Will God indeed dwell on the earth?” How does the Incarnation answer him, and how does the Holy Qurbono?
  • Hebrews 9:10 speaks of “the time to set things right”. What in my life, my family or my parish most needs to be set right this year?
  • What “trading” has crept into the way I pray or serve in the Church?
  • Jesus' disciples understood his word only after the Resurrection. Which word of the Gospel have I understood only later, through experience?
