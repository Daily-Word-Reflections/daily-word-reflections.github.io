Word for the day

“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,” Matthew 5:44

Reflection

The Letter to the Hebrews lets us overhear what the Son said as he came into the world. Taking the words of a psalm, he says to the Father: “Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body you have prepared for me; in burnt offerings and sin offerings you have taken no pleasure. Then I said, ‘See, God, I have come to do your will, O God.’” This is the prayer of the Incarnation. The body that the Father prepared was formed in the womb of the Virgin Mary, and from the first moment it was a body offered: a life lived entirely to do the Father's will, from the manger to the Cross. “And it is by God's will that we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.” The animal sacrifices could not reach the heart; what pleases God is a will joined to his. St Augustine wrote that Christ is at once the priest who offers and the offering itself, and that he wished the Church's daily sacrifice to be the sign of this, so that the Church, being his body, learns to offer herself through him (City of God 10, in substance). That is why the Holy Qurbono is not a show we watch but a sacrifice we enter: we bring our bodies, our work and our fasting, and place them with his. On this Saturday of the Apostles' Fast, let your prayer be the same as his: “See, I have come to do your will.”

The Gospel shows what doing the Father's will looks like when someone strikes us. “You have heard that it was said, ‘An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.’” That old law was not cruel; it was a limit, given to stop revenge from growing larger than the injury. Jesus goes further: “But I say to you, Do not resist an evildoer. But if anyone strikes you on the right cheek, turn the other also; and if anyone wants to sue you and take your coat, give your cloak as well; and if anyone forces you to go one mile, go also the second mile.” In his time a soldier could force a civilian to carry his pack for a mile. Jesus says: carry it two. He is not asking us to approve of injustice; when he himself was struck before the high priest, he asked, “why do you strike me?” He is asking us to break the chain of evil by refusing to pay it back in the same coin. And then the summit: “Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you.” This is exactly what the body prepared for him did on the Cross, where he prayed for those who crucified him. A fast from food is good; a fast from revenge, from the sharp reply, from the grudge we feed every night, is better still.

Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Yuda Thaddai Shleeha, St Jude, one of the Twelve. Matthew and Mark call him Thaddaeus; Luke calls him Judas son of James. The Gospel of John remembers one question he asked at the Last Supper: “Lord, how is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world?” Jesus answered: “Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” Jude wanted a great revelation; Jesus promised a home. Christian tradition remembers him as a preacher of the Gospel in the East and a martyr, and links him with the short Letter of Jude, which urges the faithful to “contend for the faith that was once for all entrusted to the saints” and to “keep yourselves in the love of God.” Because his name was so like that of the traitor, he was for centuries less invoked; yet the faithful came to love him as the helper in desperate causes, and in many parishes of Kerala people come to novenas in his honour with burdens that seem hopeless. He teaches us that no question is too bold to bring to Jesus, and no situation too dark for the God who comes to make his home with those who love him.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews tells us what Jesus said when he came into the world: a body you have prepared for me; then I said, ‘See, God, I have come to do your will, O God’ (Hebrews 10:5, 7). Jesus took a body from the Virgin Mary to offer it for us. And it is by God's will that we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all (Hebrews 10:10).

In the Gospel Jesus shows what doing the Father's will means. You have heard, an eye for an eye; but I say to you, do not resist an evildoer; turn the other cheek; go also the second mile (Matthew 5:38–41). Jesus is not saying that evil does not matter. He is teaching us to stop the chain of revenge. And he goes even further: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (Matthew 5:44). That is what he did on the Cross.

Today we remember Mar Yuda Thaddai Shleeha, St Jude, one of the Twelve. At the Last Supper he asked: Lord, how is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world? Jesus answered: those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them (John 14:22–23). Many people today come to St Jude with problems that seem hopeless. He points them to the God who makes his home in us.

In this Apostles' Fast, let us fast also from revenge. Let us pray for someone who has hurt us, go the second mile for someone who does not deserve it, and bring our hardest intentions to the Lord with St Jude. Amen.

For children

Children, what do you do when someone pushes you or says something bad to you? It is easy to push back. But Jesus says: do not hit back; be kind instead, and pray for that person. That is very hard! But Jesus did it on the Cross, when he prayed for the people who hurt him. Today we also remember St Jude, a friend of Jesus. Many people ask St Jude to pray for them when they have big problems. Tonight, say a prayer for someone who was not kind to you. Jesus will be very happy.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, when you came into the world you said to the Father: see, I have come to do your will; and by the offering of your body once for all you have made us holy. Teach us to join our will to yours, to refuse the way of revenge, to go the second mile and to love even our enemies. Through the prayers of your apostle Mar Yuda Thaddai, who asked how you would reveal yourself, come with the Father and make your home in us. Help all who feel that their cause is hopeless, and keep us in your love. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about a will joined to God's will. Hebrews lets us hear the Son say to the Father as he comes into the world: “See, God, I have come to do your will.” In Matthew, Jesus shows what that will looks like when someone wrongs us: no revenge, the second mile, and love even for the enemy. On this Saturday of the Apostles' Fast we also remember St Jude Thaddaeus, one of the Twelve. The key words are body, will and enemy.

Hebrews 10:5–10 · See, I have come to do your will

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews does not name its author. The early Church in the East received it among the letters of Paul; already in the third century Origen said that only God knows who wrote it. It is a long sermon to Christians who were tempted to grow tired and fall back. Chapters 8 to 10 compare the sacrifices of the old covenant, repeated year after year, with the one sacrifice of Christ.

Just before our passage the author says that “it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (10:4). He then puts the words of Psalm 40 on the lips of Christ. He quotes the psalm in its Greek form, the Septuagint, which reads “a body you have prepared for me” where the Hebrew text has “you have given me an open ear”.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: the Son's words as he enters the world: not sacrifices, but a body and an obedient will.
  • vv. 8–9: the author explains: the first order of sacrifice is taken away to establish the second.
  • v. 10: the result: we are made holy by the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.

Key words.

  • sōma, body: The key to the passage. The Hebrew psalm speaks of an ear opened to obey; the Greek speaks of a body. Both point to the same thing: a whole human life given over to God. For Hebrews this body is the Incarnation itself, offered on the Cross.
  • thelēma, will: God's will is not a cold decree. It is his saving plan. Christ's human will said yes to it at every moment, and in that yes we are made holy.
  • ephapax, once for all: A favourite word of Hebrews (7:27; 9:12; 10:10). The sacrifice of Christ does not need to be repeated, because nothing is lacking to it.

Verse by verse.

“Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body you have prepared for me;” (10:5)

The prophets had already said that God wanted obedience more than sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6). The old sacrifices were not bad, but they could only point beyond themselves.

“See, God, I have come to do your will, O God” (10:7)

The whole life of Jesus is in this sentence. He says it at his coming into the world, in Nazareth, in Gethsemane (“not my will but yours be done”, Luke 22:42) and on the Cross.

“He abolishes the first in order to establish the second.” (10:9)

The “first” is the system of animal sacrifices; the “second” is the doing of God's will by Christ. This is not contempt for Israel's worship, which God himself had given, but its fulfilment.

“we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.” (10:10)

“Have been sanctified” is a perfect tense in Greek: something done once whose effect remains. Holiness is first a gift received, before it is a task.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage when it teaches the Incarnation: the Son took a body in order to offer it (CCC 462), and Christ's whole life is an offering to the Father, from his coming into the world to the Cross (CCC 606). The Church teaches that the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Holy Qurbono are one single sacrifice: the victim is the same, only the manner of offering is different (CCC 1367). The Qurbono does not add a new sacrifice to the Cross. It makes the one offering present, so that we can join our own bodies and wills to it (Romans 12:1).

Matthew 5:38–44 · Love your enemies

Setting.

These verses belong to the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5 to 7), and to a series of six sayings in which Jesus says: “You have heard that it was said… But I say to you” (5:21–48). He has just said that he came not to abolish the law and the prophets but to fulfil them (5:17). These sayings do not cancel the Law. They go to its root, the heart.

“An eye for an eye” is found in the Law of Moses (Exodus 21:24; Leviticus 24:20; Deuteronomy 19:21). It was a rule for judges, and it set a limit: the penalty must not be greater than the injury. In later Jewish practice it was generally applied as payment of damages. Jesus does not call the old rule cruel. He asks his disciples to go beyond justice to a generosity that breaks the chain of revenge.

Structure.

  • v. 38: the old rule: an eye for an eye.
  • vv. 39–42: four examples: the cheek, the coat, the mile, the one who asks.
  • vv. 43–44: the old saying about neighbour and enemy, and the new command: love and pray.

Key words.

  • antistēnai, resist: Here it means to strike back, to answer evil with evil, as in a lawsuit or a fight. Paul says the same: “Do not repay anyone evil for evil” (Romans 12:17).
  • angareuein, to force into service: A word from the Persian royal post. In Jesus' day a Roman soldier could make a civilian carry his load for a mile. The same verb is used of Simon of Cyrene, “compelled” to carry the cross (Matthew 27:32).
  • agapate, love: Not a feeling of liking, which cannot be commanded, but a will to do good to the other. Jesus joins it to prayer, the first good we can do for an enemy.

Verse by verse.

“But if anyone strikes you on the right cheek, turn the other also;” (5:39)

To strike the right cheek, a right-handed person would use the back of the hand. Many commentators think Jesus means an insult more than an assault. The disciple does not answer the insult with another.

“and if anyone wants to sue you and take your coat, give your cloak as well;” (5:40)

The coat was the inner tunic; the cloak was the outer garment that also served as a blanket at night. The Law protected the poor man's cloak: it had to be returned before sunset (Exodus 22:26–27). Jesus asks the disciple to give up even what the Law allowed him to keep.

“and if anyone forces you to go one mile, go also the second mile.” (5:41)

The first mile is forced; the second is a gift. The disciple turns an act of oppression into an act of service.

“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,” (5:44)

The Law of Moses never commands hatred of the enemy. It commands help even for an enemy's ox or donkey (Exodus 23:4–5), and Proverbs says, “If your enemies are hungry, give them bread to eat” (Proverbs 25:21). The saying “hate your enemy” in verse 43 was probably a popular narrowing of “neighbour”. Jesus opens the commandment to everyone.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in the Sermon on the Mount Jesus forbids anger, hatred and vengeance, and asks his disciples to turn the other cheek and love their enemies (CCC 2262). It also teaches that this does not take away the right and duty of legitimate defence, above all for those responsible for the lives of others (CCC 2263–2265). Jesus himself, struck before the high priest, asked calmly, “why do you strike me?” (John 18:23). What he forbids is revenge, not the defence of the innocent. Christian prayer reaches its height in forgiving enemies (CCC 2844).

Questions people ask

Does “do not resist an evildoer” mean that Christians must let evil go unchecked?

No. Jesus speaks of personal revenge, not of the protection of the weak or the work of just authority. Paul, in the same letter where he says “do not repay evil for evil”, says that rulers are God's servants for our good (Romans 13:4). The Catechism teaches that legitimate defence can be a grave duty for one responsible for others (CCC 2265). What the Gospel asks of each of us is to refuse hatred, to forgive, and to pray for those who wrong us.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today the Panchangam remembers Mar Yuda Thaddai Shleeha. Matthew and Mark call him Thaddaeus (Matthew 10:3; Mark 3:18); Luke calls him Judas son of James (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13). John remembers one question he asked at the Last Supper: “Lord, how is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world?” (John 14:22). Old traditions tell of his preaching in the East and his martyrdom, though the details are uncertain. The short Letter of Jude bears the name of “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James” (Jude 1); whether this is the apostle is discussed among scholars. Jude learned the lesson of today's Gospel from the Lord himself: the love that prays even for those who persecute.
