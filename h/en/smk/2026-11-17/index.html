Word for the day

“And just then some people were carrying a paralyzed man lying on a bed. When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.”” Matthew 9:2

Reflection

Paul compares God's people to an olive tree. Some natural branches were broken off; the Gentiles, a wild olive shoot, were grafted in to share “the rich root”. So he warns: “do not boast over the branches… it is not you that support the root, but the root that supports you.” We stand by faith and by grace alone, and God can graft back in those who were cut off. Humility, not pride, is the right response to being included.

Friends carry a paralysed man to Jesus. Seeing their faith, Jesus first says what no one expected: “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.” The scribes call it blasphemy. So Jesus heals the man's body too, “so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.” The crowds glorify God “who had given such authority to human beings”: the authority that Christ still exercises through his Church in Confession.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul compares God's people to an olive tree. Some natural branches were broken off; the Gentiles, a wild olive shoot, were grafted in to share the rich root. So he warns: do not boast over the branches; it is not you that support the root, but the root that supports you (Romans 11:17–18).

In the Gospel some people bring to Jesus a paralysed man lying on a bed. Seeing their faith, Jesus says: take heart, son; your sins are forgiven. And so that they may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, he heals him (Matthew 9:2–6). The faith of others carried him.

We too have been carried by the faith of others: our parents, our ancestors, the tradition of the St Thomas Christians. Today let us carry someone: a sick person, someone weary in faith, bringing them to Jesus in prayer. Amen.

For children

Children, friends carried a sick man to Jesus. Today pray for someone who is sick!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you saw the faith of friends and forgave the paralysed man. Forgive our sins, heal our weakness, and keep us humble branches on your living tree. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of belonging by grace. Paul warns Gentile believers, grafted into Israel's olive tree, not to boast: they stand only by faith. In the Gospel, a paralysed man carried by his friends receives first the forgiveness of his sins and then the use of his legs. In both, what we have is received, not earned.

Romans 11:17–24 · Grafted into the olive tree

Setting.

Romans 9–11 is Paul's anguished reflection on his own people, most of whom had not accepted Jesus as Messiah. He insists that God has not rejected his people (11:1), and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29). The olive tree is an old image for Israel (Jeremiah 11:16).

Structure.

  • vv. 17–18: you, the wild shoot, share the rich root: do not boast.
  • vv. 19–21: they were broken off through unbelief; you stand through faith: stand in awe.
  • v. 22: the kindness and the severity of God.
  • vv. 23–24: God can graft the natural branches back in.

Key words.

  • rhiza, root: The root is the patriarchs and God's promises to them (11:16, 28). The Church lives from Israel's root, not the other way round.
  • enkentrizō, to graft: Farmers grafted cultivated shoots onto wild stock. Paul knows he is describing the opposite, and says so: it is “contrary to nature” (v. 24). Grace does what nature cannot.

Verse by verse.

“do not boast over the branches. If you do boast, remember that it is not you that support the root, but the root that supports you.” (11:18)
“They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand only through faith. So do not become proud, but stand in awe.” (11:20)

The warning is for Christians. Pride over others is the surest way to lose what we have received.

“how much more will these natural branches be grafted back into their own olive tree.” (11:24)

Paul's hope for Israel is not weaker than his warning; it is stronger.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council takes up this image: the Church draws sustenance from the root of that good olive tree onto which the wild shoots, the Gentiles, have been grafted (Nostra Aetate 4). The Catechism teaches the Church's unique bond with the Jewish people, to whom God's call is irrevocable (CCC 839).

Matthew 9:1–8 · The paralytic forgiven and healed

Setting.

“His own town” is Capernaum, where Jesus made his home (4:13). Mark tells the same story with the roof opened above Jesus (Mark 2:1–12); Matthew tells it more briefly, so that the question of authority stands out.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the man is carried in; Jesus sees their faith and forgives.
  • v. 3: the scribes think “blasphemy”.
  • vv. 4–6: Jesus' question and the sign.
  • vv. 7–8: the man goes home; the crowds glorify God.

Key words.

  • tharsei, take heart: Jesus' first word is courage, then forgiveness. The same word is spoken to the woman with the haemorrhage (9:22).
  • exousia, authority: Not raw power but rightful authority. To forgive sins belongs to God alone; Jesus claims it as the Son of Man.

Verse by verse.

“When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.”” (9:2)

“Their faith”: the friends' faith counts. This does not mean the illness was caused by a personal sin (see John 9:3); Jesus goes first to the deepest need.

“But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins” (9:6)

Forgiveness cannot be seen; healing can. The visible sign confirms the invisible gift.

“When the crowds saw it, they were filled with awe, and they glorified God, who had given such authority to human beings.” (9:8)

“To human beings”, in the plural. Matthew's readers would think of the authority given to the Church to bind and loose (16:19; 18:18).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only God forgives sins, that Jesus, as Son of God, says of himself that he has this authority on earth, and that he gave this power to men to exercise in his name (CCC 1441–1442). This is what happens in the sacrament of Confession.

Questions people ask

Was the man paralysed because he had sinned?

The Gospel does not say so. Jesus elsewhere rejects that simple link (John 9:3). He forgives first because sin is the deeper paralysis of every human being.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “it is not you that support the root, but the root that supports you.” What do I owe to those who handed on the faith to me?
  • Whom could I “carry” to Jesus this week, by prayer or by bringing them to Confession?
