Word for the day

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Hebrews 13:8

Reflection

Today the Panchangam keeps in bold letters the memory of Pulikkottil Joseph Ramban. We know him here only by the name and the title that are printed. A ramban, in the Syriac tradition, is a monk-priest, a priest who has given himself to a life of prayer and service; we do not add dates or deeds that the Panchangam does not give, and we entrust to God what only he knows fully. The Church has always known how to remember. The Holy Qurbono names the faithful departed, and in Kerala families keep the ormma, the remembrance day, with a Qurbono and a prayer for the one who has gone before. The Epistle gives the reason and the rule: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” Remembering is not a sad habit; it is a school. We look at the end of a life and learn how to live. But note what the Letter says next, in the very next verse: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Leaders come and go; the faith we imitate is faith in the One who does not change.

From this foundation the Letter warns: “Do not be carried away by all kinds of strange teachings; for it is well for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by regulations about food, which have not benefited those who observe them.” Then comes a sentence that the Church has long heard as a word about the Holy Qurbono: “We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” The sacrifices of the old sanctuary were offered for sin and burned outside the camp, and those who served in the tent could not eat of that sin offering. But the sacrifice of Christ is different: it is given to us as food. At every Qurbono we are fed from the altar of the new covenant. St Cyril of Jerusalem teaches that we pray there for the holy fathers, bishops and all who have fallen asleep among us, believing that it is of the greatest benefit to them while the holy sacrifice lies before us (Mystagogical Catecheses 5.9, in substance). This is why we can keep the memory of a Ramban with the Holy Qurbono: we remember him at the altar.

The Letter then takes us out of the gate. “Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood. Let us then go to him outside the camp and bear the abuse he endured. For here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.” The Church has always seen in the monastic and priestly life one form of such going out, leaving the comfort of the camp for the sake of the Lord, but every Christian is called to walk that way. On this day the Panchangam also remembers a young witness to it, St Maria Goretti. She was a poor farm girl in Italy, one of a family who had lost their father and worked hard on the land. In July 1902, when she was eleven years old, a young man who lived in the same house tried to force her into sin. She refused, saying that it was a sin and that God did not want it. She was stabbed and died in hospital the next day, 6 July, after forgiving the man who attacked her. He later repented, and Maria's mother forgave him too. The Church honours her as a martyr who chose the Lord over her own safety, and as a patron of young people. Her last act was exactly what the Lord had done outside the gate: “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34).

The Epistle ends with what we can all do: “Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name. Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” Praise and sharing: these are the offerings of ordinary Christians, day after day. The Gospel then asks what the servant is doing while the master is away: “Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time? Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” The Church has always heard this as a word for pastors, who feed the household with the word and the sacraments, but it also speaks to every father, mother, teacher and neighbour who has a household in their care. The wicked slave says in his heart, “My master is delayed,” and that thought is the beginning of the fall. Maria had eleven years; she was found faithful. A Ramban's life, whatever its length, is measured by the same question. Let us ask it of ourselves: if the Lord came this evening, what would he find us doing?

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Panchangam keeps in bold the memory of Pulikkottil Joseph Ramban, a monk-priest, and it also remembers St Maria Goretti. The Epistle gives us a rule for such days: Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith (Hebrews 13:7). And it adds at once: Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever (Hebrews 13:8). People pass away, but he does not.

We have an altar, says the Letter (Hebrews 13:10). At the Holy Qurbono we are fed with the Body and Blood of the Lord, and we remember those who have gone before us, among them the priests and monks who once served at the altar. The Letter also says that Jesus suffered outside the city gate, and that we are to go to him outside the camp (Hebrews 13:12–13), for here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come (Hebrews 13:14).

St Maria Goretti did this at the age of eleven. She refused what was wrong, and when she was dying she forgave the one who had hurt her. She prayed, in her own way, what Jesus had prayed: Father, forgive them (Luke 23:34). The Gospel tells us to be like the faithful and wise servant, whom the master will find at work when he comes (Matthew 24:46).

So let us remember with thanks those who taught us the faith, let us offer God a sacrifice of praise, let us forgive anyone who has hurt us, and let us be ready. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know who taught you to pray? Perhaps your mother or grandmother or a priest. Today we say thank you to God for them. We also remember Maria Goretti, a girl who was only eleven years old. She was very brave. She said no to something that was wrong, and she forgave a person who had hurt her. Jesus is the same today as he was long ago, and he helps us to be brave and kind too. This evening, say a little prayer of thanks for someone who taught you about Jesus, and forgive anyone who has been unkind to you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the same yesterday and today and for ever. We thank you for those who spoke your word to us, and we commend to you your servant Pulikkottil Joseph Ramban, whose memory the Church keeps today; reward every priest and monk who served you faithfully. Let us consider the outcome of their lives and imitate their faith. Feed us at your altar, strengthen our hearts with your grace, and teach us to go out to you outside the camp, looking for the city that is to come. Through the prayers of St Maria Goretti, who refused what was wrong and forgave her enemy, make us brave in goodness and gentle in forgiveness. Find us at work, faithful and wise, when you come. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Panchangam gives for today the readings for the memory of a bishop or priest, and both speak of those who lead. Hebrews says, “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you,” and, in the same breath, “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” In Matthew, Jesus asks who is the faithful and wise slave whom the master finds at work when he arrives. The key words are remember and faithful.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · We have an altar

Setting.

These are the closing exhortations of Hebrews, a long sermon by an author the Church does not name; it was early received into the Pauline tradition, though most scholars see a different hand from Paul's. It was written for Christians of Jewish background who were tempted to fall back, and it has shown over twelve chapters that Christ fulfils everything in the worship of the old covenant. Now it turns to the practical life of the community.

The passage moves from the leaders who first preached to the community, to Christ who does not change, to the altar and the sacrifice outside the camp, and finally to the sacrifices of praise and sharing that the Christian offers.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–9: remember the leaders and imitate their faith; Jesus Christ is the same; do not be carried away by strange teachings.
  • vv. 10–14: we have an altar; as the bodies of the sin offerings were burned outside the camp, Jesus suffered outside the gate; let us go out to him; here we have no lasting city.
  • vv. 15–16: through him let us offer a sacrifice of praise, and do not neglect to do good and to share.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: Those who “lead”: the bishops and elders of the community. The writer speaks of leaders who have died (v. 7) and then, in v. 17, of living ones whom the community is to obey.
  • thysiastērion, altar: The place of sacrifice. Hebrews says that we have an altar, and the Church has long understood this of the Eucharist, where the one sacrifice of Christ is made present.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: The Greek word for “praise” is of the same family as thanksgiving. It recalls Psalm 50:14 and 116:17, and the Church's own word for the Eucharist, which means thanksgiving.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

The community is to look back at those who first gave them the faith, and to follow the same faith. A Church that forgets its founders has lost its roots.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

The leaders pass away, but Christ remains. Faith rests not on the leaders but on the one whom they preached.

“We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” (13:10)

The Church has understood this altar as the table of the Eucharist, from which the faithful eat, and which fulfils the sacrifices of the old covenant. Some scholars see here the cross itself; the two are not opposed, since the Eucharist makes present the sacrifice of the cross.

“For here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.” (13:14)

The Christian is a pilgrim. This is not a rejection of the world; it is a reminder that our true home is with God.

“Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name.” (13:15)

The praise of the Church goes to the Father through Christ, in his name. It is the sacrifice of the lips: the prayer and thanksgiving of the Church.

“Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” (13:16)

Praise and love of neighbour belong together. The offering of the lips is joined to the offering of the hands.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is a “sacrifice of praise”, naming Hebrews 13:15 among the many names of the Eucharist (CCC 1330), and that the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice (CCC 1367). The sacrifices of praise and of sharing that Hebrews describes flow from the Eucharist and are joined to it; they do not replace it. The Church also takes verse 7 as the call to honour the memory of her bishops and priests, which is why today's reading is used for the memory of a bishop or priest.

Matthew 24:45–51 · The faithful and wise slave

Setting.

This parable ends the first part of Jesus's last great teaching in Matthew, the discourse on the Mount of Olives (chapters 24–25). Jesus has spoken of the destruction of the temple and the end of the age, and has said that no one knows the day or the hour (24:36). The command to “keep awake” (24:42–44) leads into this parable.

Luke has a similar parable (12:41–48), where Peter asks whom it is for. In Matthew the picture is of a steward put in charge of his master's household, to feed his fellow servants at the right time.

Structure.

  • v. 45: the question: who is the faithful and wise slave?
  • vv. 46–47: the faithful one is found at work, and he is put in charge of everything.
  • vv. 48–51: the one who says “My master is delayed” and abuses his fellow servants is surprised and cast out.

Key words.

  • pistos kai phronimos, faithful and wise: Two qualities together: reliable and sensible. A faithful person keeps his trust, and a wise one knows what to do with it.
  • trophē en kairō, food at the proper time: The steward's task is to feed others. Authority in the household of God is for nourishing others, not for ruling over them.
  • chronizei, is delayed: The master seems to be late. The test is what the servant does when it seems that nothing is happening.

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” (24:45)

Jesus asks a question to which everyone must give an answer by his life. Those who have charge of others, in a family, a parish or the Church, are asked the same.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)

The blessing is for the one at work when the master comes: not waiting idly but doing what he was given to do.

“My master is delayed,” (24:48)

The danger is the thought that there is time. The wicked servant uses the delay as an excuse to do wrong. Jesus asks his followers to be as faithful in the long wait as in the moment.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the parable as a call to watchfulness and to responsible service. The Catechism teaches that since the Ascension the coming of Christ in glory is imminent, even though it is not for us to know the times and seasons (CCC 673), and the Church has often applied the parable especially to bishops and priests, who are put in charge of the household of God to give the faithful their food at the proper time, the word and the sacraments.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today the Panchangam keeps the memory of Pulikkottil Joseph Ramban and gives the readings for the memory of a bishop or priest. We remember him in prayer, as the Epistle asks: “Remember your leaders.” The Panchangam also remembers St Maria Goretti, a young girl of Italy who died in 1902, aged eleven, after defending her purity against an attack, and who forgave her attacker before she died. The Church honours her as a martyr and as a model of forgiveness.

For study and teaching

  • The Epistle says, “Remember your leaders”, and asks us to imitate their faith. Who first taught me the faith, and how can I thank them and pray for them?
  • The faithful slave is found at work. What has the Master put in my charge, and am I doing it as if he might arrive today?
