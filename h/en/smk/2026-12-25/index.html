Word for the day

“And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father's only son, full of grace and truth.” John 1:14

Reflection

Tonight and today the Church sings the mystery she has waited for since Qudos Eetho: “The Word became flesh and lived among us.” The eternal Son of the Father, through whom all things were made, becomes a child, born of the Virgin Mary in Bethlehem. God has not sent a message only; he has come himself.

The prophets had seen it from far away. Isaiah: “The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light… For a child has been born for us, a son given to us… and he is named Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.” Micah: from little Bethlehem shall come one “whose origin is from of old, from ancient days”. The Child is truly man, born in time, and truly God, born of the Father before all ages.

The Old Testament births read today, Isaac to old Sarah and a son to the Shunammite woman, were births of joy against all expectation. Sarah said: “God has brought laughter for me.” In Bethlehem the joy is greater, because this Child is not only a gift of God; he is God the Gift.

In the Malankara lectionary the Gospel of Yaldo is the coming of the Magi. Strangers from the East follow a star, kneel before the Child with Mary his mother, and offer gold, frankincense and myrrh: gold for a king, incense for God, myrrh for one who will die. From the first day, Christ is revealed to the nations. Our ancestors in India received the same faith from the East; today we kneel with the Magi.

Homily outline

Theme: “The light has come: receive him.”

Tonight the whole Church sings: a child is born for us. The eternal Word, through whom all things were made, lies in a manger. Three movements of the feast: light in darkness, God made small, and our response of welcome and worship.

1. Light shines in the darkness

  • Isaiah saw a people walking in darkness and promised a great light.
  • John proclaims Christ as the true light that enlightens everyone.
  • Whatever darkness we carry tonight (sin, sickness, grief, fear), the light has come for us.

“The true light, which enlightens everyone, was coming into the world.” John 1:9

Apply: Bring one dark area of your life to the crib and ask the Child to enlighten it.

2. God makes himself small

  • Not Jerusalem but little Bethlehem; not a palace but a manger.
  • There was no room in the inn: God comes humbly and can be refused.
  • His smallness is an invitation: we can approach him without fear.
“But you, O Bethlehem of Ephrathah, who are one of the little clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to rule in Israel, whose origin is from of old, from ancient days.” Micah 5:2
“He came to what was his own, and his own people did not accept him. But to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave power to become children of God.” John 1:11–12

Apply: Make room for Christ in someone who is poor or alone this Christmas.

3. Come, let us worship

  • Isaiah names the Child: Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Prince of Peace.
  • The Magi kneel, worship and offer gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Our gift is ourselves: our faith (gold), our prayer (incense), our sacrifices (myrrh).
“For a child has been born for us, a son given to us; authority rests upon his shoulders; and he is named Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.” Isaiah 9:6
“On entering the house, they saw the child with Mary his mother; and they knelt down and paid him homage. Then, opening their treasure chests, they offered him gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh.” Matthew 2:11

Apply: Kneel before the Child in the Holy Qurbono and receive him worthily.

The Word became flesh and dwelt among us. The God whom no one has ever seen has shown us his face in a child.

Let us receive him, adore him and carry his light into the new year.

Take-home resolution: Each day of the Christmas octave, kneel briefly before the crib and say: “Jesus, be born in my heart.”

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, a happy and blessed Yaldo to all of you! Since Qudos Eetho we have walked through the season of Suboro: the angel in the Temple, the angel in Nazareth, the Visitation, the birth of John, the dream of Joseph. Tonight the waiting is over. “The Word became flesh and lived among us.”

God did not send us only a letter or a messenger. He came himself. The One who made the stars lies in a manger. The One whom heaven cannot contain is held in Mary's arms. That is the wonder of this night: the eternal Son of God is truly one of us, true God and true man.

Isaiah said: “The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light.” Many of us bring some darkness to church tonight: a grave visited this year, a son or daughter far away, a debt, an illness, a broken relationship. The Light has come exactly for that darkness. And the darkness has not overcome it.

Our Gospel tells of the Magi, strangers from the East, who followed a star and knelt before the Child with Mary his mother. They offered gold, frankincense and myrrh. What will we offer? Perhaps a reconciliation, a new start at prayer, a gift to someone in need. Come, let us adore him. And in a few moments, at this altar, the same Lord comes to us as the Bread of Life. Glory to God in the highest! Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, a joyful and blessed Christmas to all of you! Tonight let us go in spirit to a small town in Judea. Its name was Bethlehem. In Hebrew, Bethlehem means house of bread. It was there, and nowhere else, that God chose to be born.

The prophet Micah had foretold it: from you, Bethlehem, small among the clans of Judah, shall come forth one who is to rule in Israel (Micah 5:2). God plans with care. The One who would say I am the bread of life (John 6:35) was born in the house of bread.

And where did Mary lay Him? In a manger (Luke 2:7). We have made the crib beautiful with lights and decorations, but a manger was a feeding trough, the place where animals came to eat. The prophet Isaiah had said that the ox knows its owner and the donkey its master's crib, but Israel does not understand (Isaiah 1:3). The Son of God was placed where food is placed. From His first night on earth He was saying: I have come to be your food.

That is why Christmas leads us to the altar. In a few moments, on this altar, the same Christ born in Bethlehem will become present under the appearance of bread. The shepherds knelt at the manger; we kneel before the Lord in the Holy Qurbono. They saw a child; we see bread. In both cases faith sees God hidden in humility. Receive Him tonight with the faith of the shepherds.

And the shepherds did not keep the good news to themselves. They told everyone what they had heard (Luke 2:17). Bread is meant to be shared. This Christmas, share your bread with someone who is hungry, lonely or forgotten: a meal, a visit, a phone call. Then Christmas will not end tonight. It will continue in every house where Christ, the Bread of Life, is received and shared.

Homily option 3 · Teaching homily: the Incarnation, true God and true man

Christmas is not only a sweet story. It is the heart of our faith: God became man.

1. The Word became flesh

  • The eternal Son took a human nature (John 1:14).
  • Not a phantom, not a half-god.
  • For us and for our salvation (Nicene Creed).
📖 Isaiah 7:14; 9:6
📖 John 1:1–14

Apply: Bow deeply at the words of the Creed: and became man.

2. True God and true man

  • One divine Person, two natures (CCC 464–469).
  • Councils of Nicaea, Ephesus, Chalcedon.
  • Mary is truly Mother of God, Theotokos (CCC 495).
📖 Isaiah 9:6–7
📖 Philippians 2:5–11

Apply: Call Mary Mother of God with faith in the Hail Mary.

3. Why God became man

  • To reconcile us with God (CCC 457).
  • To show us God's love (CCC 458).
  • To be our model of holiness and to make us sharers in the divine nature (CCC 459–460).
📖 Exodus 34:6
📖 2 Peter 1:4

Apply: Thank God that He came to make you His child.

The Incarnation is the greatest proof of God's love.

God became man so that we might share His divine life.

Take-home resolution: This Christmas I will bow at the words of the Creed and thank God for becoming man.

Dear brothers and sisters, a blessed Christmas to you all! We have heard the familiar story again: the journey, the manger, the shepherds. But behind the story is the most astonishing truth ever proclaimed: God became man. Tonight let us look at this mystery as the Church teaches it.

St John writes: in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; and the Word became flesh and lived among us (John 1:1, 14). The Child in the manger is not a good man whom God later adopted, nor a god who only seemed human. He is the eternal Son of God who took to Himself a full human nature, body and soul, in the womb of the Virgin Mary. That is why, in the Creed, at the words and became man, we bow; on Christmas day, we kneel.

The early Church had to defend this truth against many errors. The Councils of Nicaea, Ephesus and Chalcedon taught that Jesus Christ is one divine Person in two natures, divine and human, without confusion and without separation (CCC 464–469). Because the Child is God, the Council of Ephesus proclaimed Mary Theotokos, Mother of God (CCC 495). This is not an exaggeration about Mary; it is a protection of the truth about Jesus.

Why did God become man? The Catechism gives four reasons. To save us by reconciling us with God. So that we might know God's love. To be our model of holiness. And to make us partakers of the divine nature (CCC 457–460; 2 Peter 1:4). The Fathers of the Church summed it up: the Son of God became a son of man so that we might become children of God.

So Christmas is not only for children; it is the foundation of our whole faith. At this Holy Qurbono the same Word made flesh comes to us. Let us receive Him with wonder, and let His love, so humble and so close, change the way we live.

Homily option 4 · Peace on earth: the angels' song and our reconciliation

On the night of the Nativity the sky over Bethlehem was filled with an army of angels singing glory to God and peace on earth (Luke 2:13–14). Two thousand years later the world still longs for that peace: in the Holy Land where Christ was born, in every land torn by war or communal conflict, and in many of our own homes.

Tonight the Prince of Peace is born. He does not bring peace by force, but by coming close to us, small and defenceless.

1. The peace God gives

  • Peace is first a gift from God, not an agreement among people.
  • It comes through a Child: the Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6).
  • Christ is our peace; he breaks down the walls of hostility (Ephesians 2:14).
📖 Isaiah 9:6–7
📖 Luke 2:13–14

Apply: Ask the Child tonight for the gift of an inner peace that does not depend on circumstances.

2. Peace begins at home

  • Families divided over property, words or old hurts.
  • Brothers who have not spoken for years.
  • Couples who live under one roof but far apart.
📖 Genesis 33:4
📖 Matthew 5:23–24

Apply: Before the Christmas meal is over, take one step towards someone you are not at peace with.

3. Makers of peace in the world

  • Pray for peace in the Holy Land and wherever there is war.
  • Refuse hatred and division on social media.
  • Blessed are the peacemakers (Matthew 5:9).
📖 Micah 4:3
📖 Matthew 5:9

Apply: Pray each day of the Christmas season for one country at war.

The angels sang peace on earth among those whom God favours. God's favour rests on all who receive his Son. At this Holy Qurbono, let us exchange the sign of peace with sincerity, and carry it home.

Take-home resolution: This Christmas I will make peace with one person, and pray daily for one nation at war.

Dear friends, on the night of the Nativity the shepherds heard the angels sing: glory to God in the highest heaven, and on earth peace among those whom he favours (Luke 2:14). Glory to God and peace on earth go together. When God is given his glory, peace follows.

Isaiah had called the Child the Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6). Strange prince: born in a stable, laid in a manger, threatened by a king. Yet he brings what no army can bring. St Paul says: he is our peace; in his flesh he has broken down the dividing wall of hostility (Ephesians 2:14).

The world longs for peace. Tonight we pray for the land where Jesus was born, for all places torn by war, and for our own country wherever communities are divided. Christmas is not a holiday from the world's pain; it is God entering it.

But peace begins closer. In many families tonight there is an empty chair, not because of death, but because of a quarrel. Brothers who do not speak, sisters divided over property, a son estranged from his father. The Child in the manger asks: will you let me bring peace into your home?

Jacob, after twenty years of fear, ran to meet his brother Esau, and Esau embraced him (Genesis 33:4). Jesus taught: if your brother has something against you, go first and be reconciled (Matthew 5:24). Christmas is the best time to take that first step: a phone call, a visit, a slice of cake carried across the road.

At this Holy Qurbono, when we offer one another the sign of peace, let it be real. And let us go home as peacemakers, for blessed are the peacemakers; they will be called children of God (Matthew 5:9). A blessed and peaceful Christmas to all. Amen.

Take-home resolution: This Christmas I will share a meal or a gift with a migrant worker, a night worker or a poor family.

Dear friends, on that first Christmas night, while Bethlehem slept, shepherds were keeping watch over their flocks in the fields. To them, not to the powerful in Jerusalem or Rome, an angel appeared and said: do not be afraid; I am bringing you good news of great joy for all the people (Luke 2:10).

Shepherds in those days were poor and often looked down upon. Their work kept them away from the Temple and from respectable company. Yet God chose them. The prophet Ezekiel had promised that God himself would shepherd his people, seek the lost and bind up the injured (Ezekiel 34:11–16). The Good Shepherd announces himself first to shepherds.

The sign they were given was not a palace or a crown, but a child wrapped in bands of cloth and lying in a manger (Luke 2:12). The poor recognised him because he was poor like them. God did not come to the world from above, but from below.

Think of the shepherds of our time: the migrant worker from another state who builds our houses, the nurse on night duty in a hospital abroad, the fisherman at sea on Christmas night, the security guard at the gate, the family in a rented room. Christmas is first their feast.

The shepherds went with haste, found Mary and Joseph and the child, and then made known what had been told them. They returned glorifying and praising God (Luke 2:16–20). The poor became the first evangelists. They had nothing to give but their joy, and it was enough.

At this Holy Qurbono, Christ comes to us as he came to the shepherds: simply, humbly. Let us receive him, and then, like them, go out with haste: to share our Christmas food, to visit someone alone, to bring good news to the margins. A joyful Christmas to all. Amen.

Dear children and dear families, a very happy Christmas to you! Tonight I want to talk about three things you can see around you: the star, the crib and the gift.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Yaldo, the Syriac word for birth, gathers all the promises of Suboro into one night and day. The Evening Gospel gives the mystery in its deepest form: “And the Word became flesh and lived among us” (John 1:14). The Old Testament lessons then recall how God has worked through births before this one: Isaac, born to old Sarah, who says, “God has brought laughter for me” (Genesis 21:6); a son promised to the childless Shunammite woman (2 Kings 4:8–17); the ruler to come from little Bethlehem (Micah 5:1–4); and Isaiah's song, “For a child has been born for us, a son given to us” (Isaiah 9:6). In the Acts of the Apostles Peter declares that all the prophets “predicted these days” and that God sent his servant first “to bless you” (Acts 3:24, 26).

The three readings studied here form one line. Micah names the place: not the capital but a small village, from which a shepherd will come. Hebrews names the office: this child of the tribe of Judah is a priest for ever. Matthew shows who comes to him: wise men from the East, while those nearby are troubled. Place, priest and nations: a small town, an endless life, and a whole world on its knees.

Micah 5:1–4 · From you, little Bethlehem

Setting.

Micah came from Moresheth, a small town in the Judean lowlands, and prophesied in the late eighth century BC, in the time of Isaiah (Micah 1:1). He saw Assyria destroy the northern kingdom, and he saw Judah invaded and Jerusalem under pressure. He speaks sharply against the wealthy and powerful of the capital, who oppress the poor (2:1–2; 3:1–3).

This oracle begins in that crisis. The city is besieged, and its ruler is humiliated. Then the prophet looks away from the capital altogether, back to the village where David's family began. God's new beginning will come from where the old one started. A note on numbering: the Hebrew Bible counts this chapter one verse differently, so the Bethlehem verse is 5:1 in Hebrew and 5:2 in the NRSV and POC.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Jerusalem under siege; its ruler struck on the cheek.
  • v. 2: the promise to Bethlehem Ephrathah.
  • v. 3: God gives his people up until “she who is in labor” has given birth, and then the scattered return.
  • v. 4: the coming ruler shepherds the flock in the strength of the Lord, and his greatness reaches the ends of the earth.
  • ¶ The contrast is the point. Above stands a ruler with no power, struck in the face. Below is a small clan and a woman in labour, and from them comes a shepherd whose greatness has no limits.

Key words.

  • ’Eprātāh, Ephrathah: The clan name of David's family and the district around Bethlehem (Ruth 1:2; 1 Samuel 17:12). By using the old family name, Micah takes us back before the palace and before Jerusalem, to Jesse's sons and the sheep.
  • ṣā‘îr, little: The word means small or young, and it is the word used for the youngest. David himself was the youngest son, called in from the flock (1 Samuel 16:11–13). God has a habit of choosing what is small (Deuteronomy 7:7).
  • môṣā’ōtāyw, his origin: Literally “his goings out”. “From of old, from ancient days” may first of all have meant the ancient line of David. Reading this verse in the light of Christ, the Church has heard in it more than the prophet could have spelled out: the Son's eternal birth from the Father.
  • rā‘āh, to shepherd, feed: The word for what David did before he was king, and the word by which God himself is described (Psalm 23:1). True rule, for the Bible, is feeding the flock.

Verse by verse.

“But you, O Bethlehem of Ephrathah, who are one of the little clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to rule in Israel, whose origin is from of old, from ancient days.” (5:2)

“For me”: this ruler comes for God's purposes and not for his own. The name Bethlehem (bêt leḥem) means “house of bread”, and the Fathers loved to note that the One who is the Bread of Life was born there and laid in a feeding trough.

“Therefore he shall give them up until the time when she who is in labor has brought forth; then the rest of his kindred shall return to the people of Israel.” (5:3)

The woman is not named. The picture recalls Daughter Zion in labour (4:9–10) and the young woman of Isaiah 7:14, since Isaiah was Micah's contemporary. The Church reads her as Mary. The Second Vatican Council's teaching on Mary points to this very passage alongside Isaiah 7:14 (Lumen Gentium 55).

“And he shall stand and feed his flock in the strength of the Lord, in the majesty of the name of the Lord his God.” (5:4)

He stands, while the ruler of v. 1 is struck down. His strength is not his own: it is “in the strength of the Lord”. The following verse in the NRSV adds, “and he shall be the one of peace”.

How the Church reads it.

Matthew has the chief priests and scribes quote this verse to Herod (Matthew 2:6). The evangelist's version differs from Micah: he says “by no means least”, where Micah says “little”, and he joins it to God's word to David, “It is you who shall be shepherd of my people Israel” (2 Samuel 5:2). Christ has changed Bethlehem's rank.

St Justin Martyr in the second century cites Micah in his dialogue with Trypho and speaks of the birth in a cave near the village (Dialogue with Trypho 78). Origen also cites Micah and says that the cave and the manger were still shown at Bethlehem in his day (Against Celsus 1.51). The Catechism sees in this hidden birth the mystery of the kingdom: to enter it, one must become small (CCC 525–526).

Hebrews 7:14–17 · A priest for ever

Setting.

Hebrews calls itself a “word of exhortation” (13:22). It is a sermon to Christians who are growing tired, and in chapters 7–10 it presents Christ as high priest. Its author is not named; the Church has received it among the letters linked with St Paul.

The author has an obvious problem. In the Law of Moses, priests came only from the tribe of Levi, and Jesus was of the tribe of Judah. His answer draws on two passages. In Genesis 14:18–20 Melchizedek, king of Salem and “priest of God Most High”, appears without any family record, brings out bread and wine, and blesses Abraham. In Psalm 110:4 God swears an oath to the Messiah-king: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”. So Scripture itself had promised a priesthood not based on descent from Levi. It is fitting to hear this on the day we keep the birth of a child of Judah.

Structure.

  • v. 14: the fact: the Lord descended from Judah, and Moses said nothing about priests from that tribe.
  • v. 15: the priest who “arises” is like Melchizedek.
  • v. 16: the basis of his priesthood: not physical descent, but the power of an indestructible life.
  • v. 17: the proof: Psalm 110:4.

Key words.

  • anatetalken, was descended, has sprung: The verb is used of the rising of the sun or a star, and of a shoot springing up. The Greek Old Testament uses the related noun anatolē for the promised “Branch” (Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 3:8), and the same noun is what Matthew uses for the star “at its rising” (Matthew 2:2). The Malankara reader hears today's Gospel and epistle speaking the same language.
  • sarkinēs, physical, of flesh: Literally “a law of a fleshly commandment”. The old priesthood passed from father to son, from one mortal man to the next, because the priests died (7:23).
  • akatalytos, indestructible: A word found only here in the New Testament. It means what cannot be loosed or undone. The child born at Yaldo will die, but death will not undo his life, and his priesthood rests on that life.

Verse by verse.

“For it is evident that our Lord was descended from Judah, and in connection with that tribe Moses said nothing about priests.” (7:14)

This is one of the New Testament's clearest witnesses that Jesus was known to be of the tribe of Judah. See the genealogy in Matthew 1:2–3, and Micah's Bethlehem in today's first reading.

“one who has become a priest, not through a legal requirement concerning physical descent, but through the power of an indestructible life.” (7:16)

Christ's priesthood stands on his own risen life. That is why it does not end or pass on to a successor (7:24). Those whom the Church ordains do not replace him; they share in his one priesthood (CCC 1545).

“For it is attested of him, “You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.”” (7:17)

Jesus himself applies Psalm 110 to the Messiah (Mark 12:35–37), and it is the Old Testament text most quoted in the New Testament. “Forever” is the word that matters here: his priesthood is still at work today.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the Tradition of the Church considers Melchizedek a prefiguration of the priesthood of Christ, the unique high priest after the order of Melchizedek (CCC 1544), and sees in his offering of bread and wine a foreshadowing of the Church's own offering (CCC 1333). So the Church connects Bethlehem, the house of bread, with the altar: the Child who is born today is the priest and the offering.

From the moment he entered the world, Hebrews says, the Son came to offer himself: “a body you have prepared for me” (10:5). The Second Vatican Council teaches that Christ is present in the Eucharistic sacrifice, in the person of the priest and especially under the Eucharistic species (Sacrosanctum Concilium 7). What the Letter says about his priesthood “forever” is not just a thing of the past.

Matthew 2:1–12 · The Magi kneel before the Child

Setting.

Herod the Great ruled Judea under Rome from about 37 BC until his death in 4 BC. He was an able builder, who rebuilt the Temple on a grand scale, and a suspicious ruler, who had members of his own family killed. Because he died in 4 BC, Jesus was born a few years before the date our calendar gives, an old miscalculation.

Matthew has already given the genealogy and told of Joseph's dream (ch. 1). He does not describe the birth itself; he goes straight from “he named him Jesus” (1:25) to the arrival of foreigners. His first readers were probably Jewish Christians, and here at the start he shows what the end of his Gospel will command: “make disciples of all nations” (28:19). This is why the Malankara lectionary's Gospel for the feast fits so well: at Yaldo the Church sees not only who was born, but for whom.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the wise men arrive in Jerusalem with their question.
  • vv. 3–6: Herod is frightened; the chief priests and scribes find the place in Scripture.
  • vv. 7–8: Herod's secret meeting and his false request.
  • vv. 9–10: the star goes before them, and they are overwhelmed with joy.
  • v. 11: the house, the child with Mary, the homage, and the gifts.
  • v. 12: the dream and the other road.
  • ¶ The word for homage (proskynein) comes three times: the Magi's aim (v. 2), Herod's lie (v. 8) and the deed itself (v. 11). The whole passage turns on who truly bows down to this child.

Key words.

  • magoi, wise men: Originally a Persian priestly class, and later a general word for men learned in dreams and the stars. Matthew does not call them kings, and he does not say how many there were. Their number three was deduced from the three gifts, while some Syriac writings speak of twelve. “Kings” comes from reading Psalm 72:10 and Isaiah 60:3 alongside this story.
  • en tē anatolē, at its rising / in the East: The phrase can mean either. The NRSV reads “at its rising”, the POC “in the East” (കിഴക്ക്). Either way the star recalls Balaam's oracle: “a star shall come out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17).
  • proskynein, to pay homage, to worship: To bow to the ground. It can mean homage to a king or worship of God. The POC translates ആരാധിക്കുക, “worship”. Matthew's readers know that the word goes further at the end of his Gospel, when the disciples worship the Risen One (28:17).
  • basileus tōn Ioudaiōn, king of the Jews: The Magi say it first. In Matthew the title returns only in the Passion, in the mouths of Pilate and the soldiers and over the cross (27:11, 29, 37). Between the crib and the cross, the same title.

Verse by verse.

“asking, “Where is the child who has been born king of the Jews? For we observed his star at its rising, and have come to pay him homage.”” (2:2)

They read the sky, and it brings them as far as Jerusalem, but to find the place they need the Scriptures (vv. 4–6). The Catechism draws the lesson: the Magi seek in Israel the Messiah of the nations, and the nations can find him only by turning to the Jews and receiving from them the promises in the Old Testament (CCC 528).

“When King Herod heard this, he was frightened, and all Jerusalem with him;” (2:3)

The fear belongs to Herod and those around him; a tyrant's anger put a whole city on edge. This verse says nothing against the Jewish people. Joseph, Mary and Jesus himself are Jews, and it was the Scriptures of Israel that led the way (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). The warning is for anyone who knows the Scriptures well but stays at home.

“When they saw that the star had stopped, they were overwhelmed with joy.” (2:10)

The Greek piles up the words: they rejoiced with a very great joy. It is the first joy in Matthew's Gospel, and it is felt by foreigners.

“On entering the house, they saw the child with Mary his mother; and they knelt down and paid him homage. Then, opening their treasure chests, they offered him gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh.” (2:11)

“The child with Mary his mother”: Matthew uses this pairing five times in the chapter. The Magi find the Child with his mother, and they kneel to him. The gifts recall Isaiah 60:6 (“gold and frankincense”). St Irenaeus gives the classic reading: gold for a king, incense for God, myrrh for one who would die and be buried (in substance, Against Heresies 3.9.2). Myrrh returns in Nicodemus's burial spices (John 19:39).

“And having been warned in a dream not to return to Herod, they left for their own country by another road.” (2:12)

Matthew ends simply, with no further word of them. The old road ran through Herod's palace. Whoever has knelt before Christ does not go home by the same way.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees the Magi, representatives of the neighbouring pagan religions, as the first fruits of the nations, who welcome the good news of salvation through the Incarnation (CCC 528). St Leo the Great, preaching on the Epiphany, calls on all the nations to enter the family of the patriarchs and to adore the Maker of the universe, and sees in the Magi the beginning of what continues in the Church (in substance, Sermons on the Epiphany).

Syriac Christians have long felt close to this story, because the Magi come from the East, the direction of Mesopotamia and Persia, where Syriac Christianity spread. Syriac writings such as the Cave of Treasures tell legends about them. The Church does not read these legends as history, but they show how much the story was loved.

Questions people ask

Was Jesus really born on 25 December?

The Gospels do not give the date. The Church's feast is not a birth certificate. It is a day set apart to celebrate a real event. The observance on 25 December is attested at Rome in the fourth century, while many Eastern Churches first kept the Nativity on 6 January. St John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch around 386, says that the 25 December celebration had been known there for fewer than ten years. The Armenian Apostolic Church still keeps 6 January. What the Church asks us to believe is that the Son of God truly was born of Mary at Bethlehem, in the days of Herod.

Were the Magi three kings? What was the star?

Matthew says neither three nor kings: both are later, reverent readings (see the key word above). As for the star, people have proposed a planetary conjunction, a comet and a new star, and none can be proved. Matthew describes a star that goes before them and stops over a place, and his interest is less in astronomy than in what the star means: God can use even the sky to bring seekers to Christ, and the Scriptures complete the journey.

Why does Matthew's birth story have a house, while Luke has a manger?

Matthew and Luke each tell the story from a different angle, and neither tries to give every detail. Luke describes the night of the birth, with shepherds and a manger. Matthew describes a later visit: the Magi find a “house”, and Herod later orders the killing of children up to two years old (2:16). Yaldo brings the two scenes together, since both are true of the same Child.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the Malankara tradition the night service of Yaldo includes a fire, lit with frankincense and often with the palm leaves kept from Hosanna Sunday, while the Gloria of the angels is sung. Christ, the Light of the world, is proclaimed as born in the night, as Isaiah says: “The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light” (9:2). Today the Yaldo–Denho season begins. It is a joy of this Antiochene family that the Gospel of the feast is the Magi, for they come from the East with their gifts, and Indian Christians who trace their faith to St Thomas may hear this story with special gratitude. At the Holy Qurbono we kneel like the Magi, as if in the village whose name means house of bread, and receive the One who was born there.

For study and teaching

  • God chose small Bethlehem. Where in my family or parish is God at work in what seems small, and do I notice it?
  • The scholars in Jerusalem could name the place, and Matthew does not say that they went. What do I know well about faith but have not yet acted on?
  • “By another road.” After meeting Christ this Yaldo, what road in my life should I stop taking?
  • Gold, frankincense, myrrh. What is the most precious thing I could lay before the Child this year?
