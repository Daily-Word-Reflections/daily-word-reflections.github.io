Word for the day

“But when God, who had set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace, was pleased to reveal his Son to me, so that I might proclaim him among the Gentiles, I did not confer with any human being.” Galatians 1:15–16

Reflection

Christ is risen! The Resurrection did not stop with the Eleven in the upper room. Some years later the risen Lord met a man who was hunting his disciples, and today St Paul tells the Galatians what happened to him. He hides nothing: “You have heard, no doubt, of my earlier life in Judaism. I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it.” He was zealous, gifted, ahead of his companions. Then everything turned, and not by his own effort: “But when God, who had set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace, was pleased to reveal his Son to me, so that I might proclaim him among the Gentiles...” Notice the words. God set him apart, God called him, God was pleased to reveal his Son. Paul saw the risen Christ on the road to Damascus (Acts 9; 1 Corinthians 15:8), and he counts himself among the witnesses of the Resurrection. Paul does not reject his people or their faith; he speaks with respect of “the traditions of my ancestors” and later says that God has not rejected his people (Romans 11:1). His zeal was turned, not destroyed: the same fire that once persecuted would now carry the Gospel to the ends of the earth.

What did the newly called Paul do? Not what we might expect. “I did not confer with any human being, nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were already apostles before me, but I went away at once into Arabia, and afterwards I returned to Damascus.” Only “after three years” did he go up “to visit Cephas and stayed with him fifteen days.” A conversion needs hidden years. The Lord himself spent thirty years in Nazareth before three years of preaching. St John Chrysostom remarks that Paul went to Peter not to be taught as a beginner but to honour him as the first of the apostles (in substance). And the end of the passage is beautiful: the churches of Judea “only heard it said, ‘The one who formerly was persecuting us is now proclaiming the faith he once tried to destroy.’ And they glorified God because of me.” The best proof of the Resurrection is a changed life. When people who knew what we once were see what we have become, they do not praise us; they glorify God.

The Gospel brings us down from the great apostle to a small child. “An argument arose among them as to which one of them was the greatest.” Jesus does not argue back. He takes “a little child” and puts it by his side, and says, “Whoever welcomes this child in my name welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me; for the least among all of you is the greatest.” Paul learned this. The man who had been ahead of everyone called himself “the least of the apostles” (1 Corinthians 15:9). On this Friday of the Easter season, look honestly at your own past, as Paul did, without hiding it and without despair: the grace that turned Paul can turn anyone. Pray for someone you have given up on. Welcome a child, a newcomer or a person who seems unimportant as you would welcome Christ. And if an argument starts about who is greatest, at home, at work or in the parish committee, be the one who steps back.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today St Paul tells his own story: I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it (Galatians 1:13). Then God, who had set him apart before he was born, was pleased to reveal his Son to him.

Paul did not rush to the front. He went to Arabia, returned to Damascus, and only after three years went to visit Cephas. Conversion needs hidden time with God.

And the churches of Judea heard: the one who formerly was persecuting us is now proclaiming the faith he once tried to destroy. And they glorified God because of me (Galatians 1:23–24). A changed life is a witness to the Resurrection.

In the Gospel Jesus puts a little child by his side: the least among all of you is the greatest (Luke 9:48). Let us pray for someone we have given up on, and welcome the least in his name. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Once there was a man named Saul who hurt the friends of Jesus. Then Jesus, alive and full of light, met him on the road. Saul became St Paul, one of the greatest friends Jesus ever had. Nobody is too bad for Jesus to love and change. Can you pray today for someone who is unkind to you?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you met Saul on the road and turned the persecutor into your apostle. You know our past, and your grace is stronger than all of it. Reveal yourself to us more deeply, and to all who are far from you. Give us the humility to welcome the least in your name, and let our changed lives lead others to glorify the Father. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul tells his own story: the zealous persecutor whom God had set apart before he was born and called by grace, and to whom God was pleased “to reveal his Son”. The churches of Judea heard of it, “and they glorified God because of me.” The Gospel shows the disciples arguing about who is the greatest, and Jesus setting a little child beside him. Both readings show what the risen Lord does with human greatness: Paul's zeal is turned into a mission, and the disciples' ambition is turned toward the least.

Galatians 1:13–24 · God was pleased to reveal his Son to me

Setting.

Paul writes to the churches of Galatia, in the interior of what is now Turkey, because teachers had come after him saying that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the whole Law of Moses. They seem also to have said that Paul's gospel was second-hand, learned from others. So Paul tells how he received it: “I did not receive it from a human source” (1:12).

Paul never stopped being a Jew (Romans 11:1; Philippians 3:5). What changed was that God showed him Jesus as his Son. Many scholars therefore prefer to call what happened to him a call, like the call of the prophets, rather than a change of religion. His own words echo Jeremiah 1:5 and Isaiah 49:1.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: my earlier life: persecutor of the church, zealous for the traditions of my ancestors.
  • vv. 15–16a: but God set me apart, called me, and revealed his Son to me for the Gentiles.
  • vv. 16b–17: I consulted no one; I went to Arabia and returned to Damascus.
  • vv. 18–20: after three years, fifteen days with Cephas; of the other apostles I saw only James.
  • vv. 21–24: in Syria and Cilicia; the churches of Judea glorified God.

Key words.

  • Ioudaismos, Judaism: In the New Testament the word appears only here (1:13, 14). It means the Jewish way of life, lived with zeal. Paul is not mocking it; he is saying how far he had advanced in it.
  • aphorisas, set apart: God set Paul apart “before I was born”, literally from his mother's womb, as with Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:5). The call was older than the persecution.
  • historēsai, to visit: The word means to visit in order to get to know someone and to learn. Paul went up to Jerusalem to get to know Cephas, that is, Peter, and stayed with him fifteen days.

Verse by verse.

“I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it.” (1:13)

Paul does not soften his past. He calls the community he persecuted “the church of God”, the gathering that belongs to God.

“But when God, who had set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace, was pleased” (1:15)

Every word puts the first move with God: he set apart, he called, he was pleased. Grace comes before any merit of Paul's.

“Then after three years I did go up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and stayed with him fifteen days;” (1:18)

Paul insists that his gospel came from the Lord, yet he goes to Peter. The gospel he received by revelation is the same gospel that the apostles in Jerusalem keep.

“And they glorified God because of me.” (1:24)

The last word of the story is praise. A changed life gives glory to God, not to the one who was changed.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verses 15 and 16 when it explains the title Son of God: at the moment of his conversion on the road to Damascus, Paul came to know Jesus as the Son, as Peter had confessed him (CCC 442). Paul's life became the Church's great sign that no one is beyond the reach of grace, and that the persecutor can become the apostle.

Luke 9:46–48 · The least among you is the greatest

Setting.

This scene comes at the end of Jesus' ministry in Galilee. He has just told the disciples for the second time that he will be handed over, and Luke says that they did not understand and were afraid to ask (9:44–45). Into that silence comes an argument about rank. Soon afterwards Jesus “set his face to go to Jerusalem” (9:51).

In the world of that time a small child had no standing, no rights of its own and nothing to offer. To welcome a child was to welcome someone who could give nothing back.

Structure.

  • v. 46: the argument: who is the greatest?
  • v. 47: Jesus knows their thoughts and sets a child beside him.
  • v. 48: to welcome the child is to welcome Jesus and the Father; the least is the greatest.

Key words.

  • dialogismos, argument, inner thought: The same word names the argument among them in verse 46 and the “inner thoughts” that Jesus reads in verse 47. Rivalry in the heart becomes rivalry in speech.
  • paidion, little child: A small child. Jesus does not only speak about the child; he puts it “by his side”, the place of honour next to himself.
  • mikroteros, least: Literally “the smaller”. Jesus turns the scale around: in his Kingdom greatness is measured by smallness.

Verse by verse.

“An argument arose among them as to which one of them was the greatest.” (9:46)

Luke places this right after Jesus spoke of his Passion. The disciples were thinking of places of honour while he was thinking of the Cross.

“But Jesus, aware of their inner thoughts, took a little child and put it by his side,” (9:47)

Jesus answers a hidden thought with an action before a word. The child standing beside him is the answer.

“Whoever welcomes this child in my name welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me;” (9:48)

A chain of welcome: the child, Jesus, the Father. Whoever receives the smallest person receives God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that to become a child in relation to God is the condition for entering the Kingdom, and that for this we must humble ourselves and become little (CCC 526). Paul's own story fits this Gospel: the man who had advanced beyond many of his own age later called himself “the least of the apostles” (1 Corinthians 15:9).

Questions people ask

Paul calls James “the Lord's brother”. Did Jesus have brothers?

In the language of the Bible “brother” can mean a close relative. The Hebrew of Genesis calls Lot, Abraham's nephew, his “brother” (Genesis 14:14, 16). The Church has always professed that the Mother of the Lord remained ever virgin, and the Catechism explains that the “brothers” of Jesus are close relations of his (CCC 500). St Jerome argued this at length from Scripture (in substance). James, a leader of the church in Jerusalem, was such a kinsman of the Lord.

For study and teaching

  • Paul's zeal for God was real, but misdirected until God showed him his Son. Where might my zeal need to be turned by Christ?
  • Who is the “little child” in my world today, someone who can give me nothing back? How will I welcome that person in Christ's name?
