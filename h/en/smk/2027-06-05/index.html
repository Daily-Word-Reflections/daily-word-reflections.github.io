Readings

  • Epistle 2 Timothy 3:10–17 · From childhood you have known the sacred writings; all scripture is inspired by God
  • Gospel Mark 12:35–37 · “David himself calls him Lord; so how can he be his son?”

Word for the day

“All scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, so that everyone who belongs to God may be proficient, equipped for every good work.” 2 Timothy 3:16–17

Reflection

St Paul's second letter to Timothy is the letter of an old man in prison who knows that his end is near. He writes to his son in the faith not about himself but about what must be handed on. “Now you have observed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life, my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness, my persecutions, and my suffering the things that happened to me in Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra.” Timothy had seen it all at close range. Paul does not hide the cost: “Indeed, all who want to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted.” Then he points to the two things that will keep Timothy steady when he himself is gone: the people from whom he learned the faith, and the Scriptures he has known since he was a child. “But as for you, continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it, and how from childhood you have known the sacred writings that are able to instruct you for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.” Timothy learned the Scriptures on the knees of his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice. Many of us learned them in the same way: a grandmother reading the Bible aloud in the evening by the oil lamp, a mother teaching the first prayers, a Sunday school teacher who made the stories come alive.

Then comes one of the great sentences about the Bible: “All scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, so that everyone who belongs to God may be proficient, equipped for every good work.” The word Paul uses means “God-breathed”. The same breath that gave life to Adam and that the risen Christ breathed on the apostles is in the words of Scripture. That is why the Church reads them in every Holy Qurbono, carries the Gospel book in procession, and kisses it. The Gospel today shows Jesus himself reading Scripture with authority. He quotes Psalm 110: “David himself, by the Holy Spirit, declared, ‘The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet.”’ David himself calls him Lord; so how can he be his son?” The Messiah is David's son by birth, and yet David calls him Lord: he is more than a king of David's line; he is the Son of God, seated at the Father's right hand. Mark adds: “And the large crowd was listening to him with delight.” The word of God, opened by Christ, is not a burden but a joy.

St Boniface, whom the Panchangam remembers today, lived this love of Scripture to the end. Born in Wessex in England about 675 and named Wynfrith, he became a monk and a teacher of the Bible, and he could have stayed in his monastery all his life. But he heard the call to bring the Gospel to the Germanic peoples. Pope Gregory II sent him in 719 and gave him the name Boniface. He preached, baptized, founded monasteries and dioceses, and became the archbishop of the German lands. In old age he went back once more to Frisia, the land where he had first tried to preach. On 5 June 754, at Dokkum, a band of armed men attacked him and his companions as they waited to confirm the newly baptized. Boniface told his companions not to fight. An ancient tradition says that he raised a book of the Gospels over his head as the sword came down. In a letter he once wrote that the Church is like a great ship tossed on the sea, and that the shepherd must not abandon it but keep it on its course (Letter to Cuthbert, in substance). On this Saturday the Latin Church also honours the Immaculate Heart of Mary, who “treasured all these things in her heart”. Like Timothy's mother and grandmother, like Boniface, the Mother of God teaches us to keep God's word in our hearts and to hand it on.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul, old and in prison, writes to his young friend Timothy. He reminds him of everything he has seen and suffered, and he tells him: Indeed, all who want to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted (2 Timothy 3:12). But he also tells him where to find strength: in the people who taught him the faith, and in the Scriptures he has known from childhood.

Paul says: All scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16). The Bible is breathed by God. It teaches us, it corrects us, and it makes us ready for every good work. That is why we listen to it in every Holy Qurbono, and why we should read it at home.

In the Gospel Jesus reads the Psalms and shows that they speak of him: David himself calls him Lord; so how can he be his son? (Mark 12:37). Jesus is the Son of David, but also David's Lord, the Son of God. And the large crowd was listening to him with delight (Mark 12:37). God's word is meant to bring us joy.

Today we remember St Boniface, who carried the Gospel from England to the German peoples and died a martyr in 754, holding, so tradition says, a book of the Gospels. Let us thank God for those who taught us the faith, let us read the Bible with delight, and let us hand it on to our children. Amen.

For children

Children, who taught you your first prayers? Maybe your grandmother, or your mother, or your teacher at Sunday school. St Paul's friend Timothy learned about God from his grandmother and his mother. Paul says that the Bible is breathed by God, like when God breathed life into Adam. Today we remember St Boniface, who loved the Bible so much that he carried it to faraway countries. Ask someone at home to read you a Bible story tonight, and listen with joy!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of David and Lord of David, seated at the right hand of the Father, you opened the Scriptures to the crowds, and they listened with delight. Give us that same delight in your word. Bless the grandparents, parents and teachers who hand on the faith to children, and keep us faithful to what we have learned. Through the prayers of St Boniface, who carried your Gospel to the nations and gave his life holding it, make us bold to proclaim your word in season and out of season. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about the Scriptures. Paul reminds Timothy that he has known the sacred writings from childhood and that “All scripture is inspired by God.” In the Gospel Jesus himself reads a psalm with authority and shows that the Messiah is not only David's son but David's Lord. The key words are inspired and Lord.

2 Timothy 3:10–17 · All scripture is inspired by God

Setting.

The Second Letter to Timothy presents itself as Paul's last letter, written in chains in Rome (1:8, 1:17) when he knows that “the time of my departure has come” (4:6). As with the other Pastoral Letters, many scholars think a disciple wrote it in Paul's name; others defend Paul's own authorship. It reads as a testament, a father's words to his son in the faith.

Antioch (in Pisidia), Iconium and Lystra are the towns of Paul's first missionary journey, where he was driven out and at Lystra stoned and left for dead (Acts 13–14). Lystra was Timothy's own home town (Acts 16:1), so he may have seen these things with his own eyes.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: Timothy has followed Paul's teaching and life, and his sufferings.
  • vv. 12–13: all who want to live a godly life will be persecuted; impostors will go from bad to worse.
  • vv. 14–15: continue in what you have learned; you have known the sacred writings from childhood.
  • vv. 16–17: all Scripture is inspired by God and equips the person of God.

Key words.

  • parēkolouthēsas, you have observed: Literally “you have followed closely”. Timothy learned the faith not only from words but by watching how Paul lived and suffered.
  • hiera grammata, sacred writings: The Scriptures of Israel, which Christians call the Old Testament. Timothy learned them from his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice (1:5).
  • theopneustos, inspired by God: Literally “God-breathed”. The word is found only here in the New Testament. Scripture comes from the breath of God, the same breath that gave life to the first man (Genesis 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“Indeed, all who want to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted.” (3:12)

Paul does not promise an easy life. Faithfulness to Christ always meets resistance, whether open persecution or quiet mockery.

“continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it,” (3:14)

Faith is received from people we can trust. Knowing who taught us helps us to stand firm when new teachers come.

“All scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness,” (3:16)

Four uses: to teach the truth, to show what is wrong, to set it right, and to form a righteous life. Scripture is given not only to be studied but to make us “proficient, equipped for every good work” (3:17).

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes this verse when it teaches that the books of Scripture, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, have God as their author, and teach firmly, faithfully and without error the truth God wished to be recorded for the sake of our salvation (Dei Verbum 11; CCC 105–107). The Catechism also recalls the saying of St Jerome: “Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ” (CCC 133).

Mark 12:35–37 · David himself calls him Lord

Setting.

Jesus is teaching in the temple during the last days before his Passion. Mark has just told of a series of questions put to him: about taxes, about the resurrection, and about the greatest commandment (12:13–34). After the scribe who agreed with him, “no one dared to ask him any question” (12:34). Now Jesus asks a question of his own.

He quotes Psalm 110:1, a royal psalm traditionally ascribed to David. It is the Old Testament verse most often quoted or echoed in the New Testament. Peter uses it on the day of Pentecost to proclaim the risen Jesus as Lord (Acts 2:34–36).

Structure.

  • v. 35: the question: how can the scribes say the Messiah is the son of David?
  • v. 36: David, by the Holy Spirit, calls him “my Lord” (Psalm 110:1).
  • v. 37: so how can he be his son? The crowd listens with delight.

Key words.

  • Christos, Messiah: The Anointed One. Many in Israel expected the Messiah to be a descendant of David (2 Samuel 7:12–16).
  • kyrios, Lord: In the Hebrew of the psalm the first “Lord” is the divine name and the second is ’adōnî, “my lord”. The Greek uses kyrios for both. David calls the Messiah his lord.
  • en tō pneumati tō hagiō, by the Holy Spirit: Jesus treats the psalm as words spoken under the Holy Spirit, which agrees with today's Epistle: all Scripture is inspired by God.

Verse by verse.

“How can the scribes say that the Messiah is the son of David?” (12:35)

Jesus does not deny that the Messiah is David's son. Mark has told us that blind Bartimaeus called him “Son of David” (10:47), and Jesus accepted it. He shows that the title says too little.

“David himself, by the Holy Spirit, declared,” (12:36)

The king of Israel speaks of someone greater than himself, who will sit at the right hand of God. A father did not call his son “lord”. So the Messiah must be more than a son of David.

“David himself calls him Lord; so how can he be his son?” (12:37)

The answer is the mystery of Christ: by his birth he is son of David, and as the eternal Son he is David's Lord. Jesus leaves the question open for his hearers to ponder.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that Jesus ascribes the title “Lord” to himself in a veiled way when he disputes about Psalm 110 (CCC 447), and it teaches that being seated at the right hand of the Father means the beginning of the Messiah's kingdom (CCC 664). The Creed says the same: he “is seated at the right hand of the Father”.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Boniface, bishop and martyr, the apostle of the Germans. Born in Wessex in England about 675 and first named Wynfrith, he became a monk and then a missionary among the Germanic peoples, was made archbishop, and founded monasteries and dioceses. He was killed with his companions at Dokkum in Frisia in 754, while preparing to confirm new Christians. Like Timothy, he had learned the Scriptures from childhood, in his case in a monastery, and he carried them with him to the end.

For study and teaching

  • From whom did I first learn the Scriptures? How can I hand them on to the children in my home and parish?
  • Jesus calls himself David's Lord. In what part of my life do I still need to let Jesus be Lord?
