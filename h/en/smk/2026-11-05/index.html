Word for the day

“Now, discipline always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” Hebrews 12:11

Reflection

Hebrews speaks to Christians tired of struggling. Their trials are not a sign that God has abandoned them but that he treats them as sons and daughters: “the Lord disciplines those whom he loves.” Every good parent corrects a child; God trains us “for our good, in order that we may share his holiness.” What hurts now bears fruit later: the peaceful fruit of righteousness.

In the Gospel Jesus reveals his own sonship. The Son does nothing on his own, “but only what he sees the Father doing.” The Father loves the Son and shows him everything; he gives him power to give life and to judge, “so that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father.” Our sonship is a share in his: like him, we learn to watch the Father and do what he does.

Full homily

Dear friends, Hebrews speaks to Christians tired of struggling. Their trials are not a sign that God has abandoned them but that he treats them as sons and daughters: the Lord disciplines those whom he loves (Hebrews 12:6). Discipline is painful at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness.

In the Gospel Jesus reveals his relationship with the Father: the Son can do nothing on his own, but only what he sees the Father doing; the Father loves the Son. All should honour the Son just as they honour the Father (John 5:19–23).

The Son lived in complete dependence on the Father, and we are called to live the same way. Place one trial that tires you into the Father's hands now: what do you want to teach me through this? Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of the Father and his children. Hebrews explains hardship as the training a loving Father gives his children, so that we may share his holiness. In John, Jesus reveals himself as the Son who does nothing on his own but only what he sees the Father doing. The Son's perfect obedience is the pattern of our sonship.

Hebrews 12:4–11 · The Lord disciplines those he loves

Setting.

After the great list of the heroes of faith (chapter 11), Hebrews pictures the Christian life as a race, run with eyes fixed on Jesus, “who for the sake of the joy that was set before him endured the cross” (12:1–2). The readers are tired and facing hostility. The author now explains their struggles by quoting Proverbs 3:11–12.

Structure.

  • v. 4: you have not yet resisted to the point of blood.
  • vv. 5–6: the quotation from Proverbs: the Lord disciplines those he loves.
  • vv. 7–8: discipline is a sign that we are truly his children.
  • vv. 9–10: earthly fathers and “the Father of spirits”.
  • v. 11: painful now, fruitful later.

Key words.

  • paideia, discipline, training: From pais, child: the whole upbringing of a child, teaching as well as correction. The word and its related forms occur eight times in these verses.
  • gymnazō, to train: The word behind “gymnasium”. Hardship is pictured as an athlete's training, not as a punishment for its own sake.
  • hagiotēs, holiness: The goal of the Father's training is that “we may share his holiness” (v. 10): not only good behaviour, but a share in God's own life.

Verse by verse.

“for the Lord disciplines those whom he loves, and chastises every child whom he accepts.” (12:6)

Hebrews does not say that every suffering is sent as a punishment. It says that a believer can receive even hardship from the hands of a Father who loves.

“but he disciplines us for our good, in order that we may share his holiness.” (12:10)
“Now, discipline always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” (12:11)

An honest verse: it does not pretend that pain is pleasant. The fruit comes “later”, and it is peace.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that suffering, accepted in union with Christ, takes on a new meaning and becomes a share in his saving work (CCC 1521). St John Paul II, in his letter Salvifici Doloris, speaks of suffering as a call to conversion and to growth in goodness, and above all as a way of sharing in the sufferings of Christ (in substance).

John 5:19–23 · The Son does what he sees the Father doing

Setting.

Jesus has healed a man at the pool of Bethesda on the sabbath (5:1–18). Some of the authorities in Jerusalem objected, and John says they sought to kill him because he was “calling God his own Father, thereby making himself equal to God” (5:18). Jesus answers in a long discourse (5:19–47), of which these verses are the opening.

Structure.

  • v. 19: the Son does nothing on his own, only what he sees the Father doing.
  • v. 20: the Father loves the Son and shows him everything.
  • v. 21: as the Father raises the dead, so the Son gives life.
  • v. 22: all judgment is given to the Son.
  • v. 23: so that all may honour the Son as they honour the Father.
  • ¶ Many scholars hear behind v. 19 a small picture from daily life: an apprentice son who learns his father's trade by watching him at work and doing the same.

Key words.

  • amēn amēn, very truly: A solemn opening found only in John, always on the lips of Jesus. It marks what follows as weighty.
  • zōopoieō, to give life: The power to give life belongs to God alone. Jesus claims it for himself, both now (5:24) and at the resurrection of the dead (5:28–29).
  • krisis, judgment: The Greek word means both judgment and decision. In John, judgment happens already as people accept or refuse the Son (3:18–19).

Verse by verse.

“Very truly, I tell you, the Son can do nothing on his own, but only what he sees the Father doing; for whatever the Father does, the Son does likewise.” (5:19)

This is not weakness but perfect unity. The Son acts as the Father acts, because he is from the Father and one with him.

“just as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so also the Son gives life to whomever he wishes.” (5:21)
“so that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father.” (5:23)

The same honour for the Son as for the Father: this is the faith that the Council of Nicaea later put into the words “one in being with the Father”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that full right to pass definitive judgment belongs to Christ as Redeemer, and cites John 5:22 (CCC 679). St Augustine, preaching on these verses, explains that the Son's “seeing” the Father is not like our seeing: for the Son, to see is to be born of the Father, and so to be equal with him (in substance; Tractates on John).

Questions people ask

If the Son “can do nothing on his own”, is he less than the Father?

No. The verse speaks of unity, not of inferiority: the Son never acts apart from the Father, and “whatever the Father does, the Son does likewise”. Verse 23 asks for equal honour for both. The Church confesses the Son as true God, one in being with the Father (CCC 242).

For study and teaching

  • Is there a hardship in my life that I could begin to receive as the training of a loving Father?
  • Jesus does what he sees the Father doing. What do I “see” of the Father in prayer that I could do today?
