Word for the day

“They answered, “The emperor's.” Then he said to them, “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's.”” Matthew 22:21

Reflection

Some in Corinth wanted letters of recommendation from Paul. He answers: “You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts, to be known and read by all; and you show that you are a letter of Christ… written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts.” The best proof of the Gospel is a changed community. And Paul is humble: “Not that we are competent of ourselves… our competence is from God.”

On Republic Day the Gospel could not be more fitting. The Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Jesus: is it lawful to pay taxes to the emperor? He asks for a coin: “Whose head is this, and whose title?” “The emperor's.” “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's.” The coin bears Caesar's image; we bear God's image. Christians are loyal citizens: we pay taxes, obey just laws, vote, serve the common good and pray for those in authority. But the State is not God. Our deepest belonging is to God, and that gives us the courage to stand for justice, for the poor, and for the freedom of conscience that India's Constitution guarantees to all.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells the Corinthians: you show that you are a letter of Christ, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, on tablets of human hearts (2 Corinthians 3:3). The best proof of the Gospel is a changed community.

And he adds: our competence is from God (2 Corinthians 3:5).

In the Gospel, on this Republic Day, Jesus says: give to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's (Matthew 22:21). The coin bears Caesar's image; we bear God's.

So we are loyal citizens: we pay taxes, obey just laws, serve the common good and pray for our leaders. But the State is not God. Belonging to God gives us courage to stand for justice and for the freedom of conscience our Constitution guarantees to all. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On India's Republic Day the two readings speak of what is written and of what is owed. Paul says the Corinthians themselves are a letter of Christ, written on human hearts. Jesus looks at a coin with an emperor's head on it and says: give to the emperor what is his, and to God what is God's. A Christian is a loyal citizen whose deepest belonging is to God, in whose image each person is made.

2 Corinthians 3:1–5 · A letter of Christ

Setting.

Paul founded the Church at Corinth (Acts 18:1–11), but rival teachers have since arrived, it seems with written recommendations, and questioned his standing. Such letters were a normal and good practice in the early Church (Acts 18:27; Romans 16:1–2). Paul's point is not to despise them but to say that he has something better: the Corinthians themselves.

Structure.

  • v. 1: do we need letters of recommendation?
  • vv. 2–3: you are our letter, a letter of Christ, written by the Spirit.
  • vv. 4–5: our confidence and competence are from God.

Key words.

  • systatikai epistolai, letters of recommendation: A common kind of ancient letter that introduced the bearer to a host or a community. Paul turns the idea round: the living community is the recommendation.
  • en plaxin lithinais / en plaxin kardiais sarkinais, tablets of stone / tablets of human hearts: Literally “hearts of flesh”. Paul draws together the tablets of Sinai (Exodus 31:18) with God's promises to write the law on the heart (Jeremiah 31:33) and to give a heart of flesh (Ezekiel 36:26).
  • hikanotēs, competence: Sufficiency, being equal to a task. Paul says plainly that it does not come from himself. The same root appears three times in vv. 5–6.

Verse by verse.

“You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts, to be known and read by all;” (3:2)

A note on the text: most manuscripts read “our hearts”, which the NRSV and the POC follow; a few read “your hearts”. Either way the point stands: people read the Gospel in the life of a community before they read it in a book.

“and you show that you are a letter of Christ, prepared by us, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts.” (3:3)

Christ is the author, the Spirit the ink, Paul the hand or courier, the people the page.

“Not that we are competent of ourselves to claim anything as coming from us; our competence is from God,” (3:5)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this very verse: if God's law was written on tablets of stone by the finger of God, then the letter from Christ, entrusted to the care of the apostles, is written with the Spirit of the living God on the hearts of men (CCC 700). The New Law is written by the Holy Spirit on the heart (CCC 1965–1966). The Church does not read this as setting aside Israel's law as worthless. She reads it as the fulfilment of what the prophets foretold.

Matthew 22:15–22 · Give to the emperor, and to God

Setting.

Jesus is in Jerusalem in his last days, teaching in the Temple. Some Pharisees and Herodians, who did not normally share a cause, come together to test him. The question was painful: since AD 6 Judea had paid a direct tax to Rome, and Judas the Galilean had raised a revolt against it (Acts 5:37). To answer “Yes” would look like betraying his people's hopes; to answer “No” could be reported to the Romans as sedition. This is one group's test of Jesus, not a judgement on a people.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: the trap, with flattery first.
  • vv. 18–19: “Show me the coin.”
  • vv. 20–21: the question and the answer.
  • v. 22: they are amazed and go away.

Key words.

  • eikōn, head, image: The NRSV has “head”, the literal sense is “image”, and the POC has “രൂപം”. A denarius of the emperor Tiberius bore his head and a title calling him son of the divine Augustus. The same word is used in the Greek Bible for the image of God in humanity (Genesis 1:26–27).
  • apodote, give back, render: Not simply “give”, but to pay what is owed. The word assumes a real debt to the state, and a far greater debt to God.
  • hypokritai, hypocrites: A word from the theatre: actors behind a mask. The questioners' flattery hides their purpose, and the fact that they can produce such a coin shows that they already use it.

Verse by verse.

“Show me the coin used for the tax.” And they brought him a denarius.” (22:19)

Jesus seems to have none himself.

“Then he said to them, “Whose head is this, and whose title?”” (22:20)
“They answered, “The emperor’s.” Then he said to them, “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.”” (22:21)

The answer is neither revolt nor worship of the state. It gives the state its proper place, and thereby limits it. The second half is the larger: the coin bears Caesar's image; what bears God's image?

How the Church reads it.

Early Christians read the saying this way: the coin with Caesar's image goes to Caesar, and the human being, who bears God's image, goes to God; so Tertullian and St Augustine (in substance). The Catechism teaches that citizens have a duty to contribute to the good of society, that it is morally obligatory to pay taxes, to vote and to defend one's country (CCC 2238–2240), and it quotes this verse to teach that one must refuse obedience when civil commands are contrary to the moral order (CCC 2242). The early Letter to Diognetus, quoted in CCC 2240, says that Christians live in their own lands as resident aliens, sharing everything as citizens (in substance).

Questions people ask

Does this saying mean that religion should keep out of public life?

No. Jesus does not divide life into two separate rooms, because everything belongs finally to God. The Council teaches that the Church and the political community are independent in their own fields, and that both serve the same human persons (Gaudium et Spes 76). Christians take part in public life as citizens, guided by conscience.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Constitution of India came into force on 26 January 1950 and, among much else, protects liberty of conscience and of religion for all persons (Article 25). Today Malankara Catholics give thanks for the land we love. We pray for those who govern and those who are governed (1 Timothy 2:1–2), for justice, and for all who suffer. We serve the nation best as a “letter of Christ”, in honest work, in paying what we owe, in care for the poor, and in peace with all.

For study and teaching

  • If someone read my family's life as a letter, what would it say about Christ?
  • What do I owe my country as a citizen, and what do I owe God alone? Where do I find it hard to keep the two in order?
