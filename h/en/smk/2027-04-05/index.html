Word for the day

“Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, a descendant of David” 2 Timothy 2:8

Reflection

Christ is risen! The day after New Sunday, St Paul speaks to us from a prison cell. He writes to Timothy: “Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, a descendant of David.” This is the shortest summary of the Gospel in the New Testament, and it is written by a man in chains. He suffers hardship, “even to the point of being chained like a criminal. But the word of God is not chained.” Paul knows that he may soon die; the letter is his farewell. Yet he does not ask Timothy to remember Paul. He asks him to remember Jesus, alive. To remember, in the Bible, is not only to think of something past; it is to let the living God act now. That is why the Lord said at the Supper, “Do this in remembrance of me.” Each Holy Qurbono is this living memory: the risen Christ, of David's line, truly present with his Body and Blood. Then Paul quotes what may be an early Christian hymn: “The saying is sure: If we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him; if we deny him, he will also deny us; if we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself.” Even our unfaithfulness cannot undo his faithfulness. St Ignatius of Antioch, led in chains to Rome to die, wrote seven letters on the way that are still read today; his body was bound, but the word he preached could not be bound.

The Gospel tells the parable of the vineyard. A man plants a vineyard with care, fences it, digs a wine press, builds a tower, and leases it to tenants. When he sends servants for his share, they beat and kill them. Last of all he sends “a beloved son,” thinking, “They will respect my son.” But the tenants say, “This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.” They kill him and throw him out of the vineyard. The vineyard is a picture of God's people from Isaiah 5; the servants are the prophets; the beloved son is Jesus himself, telling his own Passion before it happens. Mark notes that those who heard it “realized that he had told this parable against them,” that is, against the leaders who were already planning his death, while the crowd listened to him gladly. The Church teaches that the guilt for the Lord's death cannot be laid on all the Jews of that time or on the Jewish people of any later time (Catechism 597; Nostra Aetate 4). The parable is a warning to every steward of God's vineyard in every age, including those who lead the Church today: the vineyard is the Lord's, not ours. And it ends with Easter: “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord’s doing, and it is amazing in our eyes.” The Son who was thrown out has been raised up as the foundation of the new temple.

How do we live this Monday of Qyomtho? Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, at the start and end of the day: say those words slowly when you wake and before you sleep. Think of the vineyard entrusted to you: your family, your work, your gifts, your share in the parish. Ask: am I giving the Lord his share, or treating it as mine? If you have been rejected or pushed aside, take heart from the stone that the builders rejected; the Lord can build on what others throw away. Pray for prisoners, especially those in prison for their faith, and for prison chaplains. And if you feel that you have been faithless since Easter, hear Paul's word: he remains faithful. Come back to him in confession and at the Holy Qurbono.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today St Paul writes from prison: Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, a descendant of David (2 Timothy 2:8). He is chained like a criminal, yet he says: the word of God is not chained.

Then he gives us a sure saying: if we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him. And the most comforting line: if we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself (2 Timothy 2:13).

In the Gospel Jesus tells of a vineyard and a beloved son who is killed and thrown out. He is speaking of himself. The warning is for every steward of God's vineyard in every age, including us. And the parable ends with Easter: the stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone (Mark 12:10).

So let us remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, when we wake and when we sleep; let us give God his share of the vineyard he has given us; and if we have been faithless, let us return to him who remains faithful. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! St Paul was in prison, but he wrote: remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead! Nobody could lock up the good news about Jesus. Jesus also told a story about a stone that builders threw away, but it became the most important stone of the whole house. That is what God did at Easter. Tonight before you sleep, can you say: Jesus, you are risen?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, raised from the dead and descended from David, you are the beloved Son sent to the vineyard, rejected and killed, and raised up as the cornerstone. Teach us to remember you each day as the living Lord. Make us faithful tenants of all you have entrusted to us, and give you the fruit that is yours. When we are faithless, you remain faithful: draw us back to you. Strengthen all who are in chains for your name, for your word is not chained. To you be glory with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The epistle returns to the passage from 2 Timothy read on New Sunday, and today we stay with its second half: a prisoner whose word is not chained, and a short hymn about dying and living with Christ. The Gospel is the parable of the tenants of the vineyard, which ends with a psalm verse that the first Christians loved: “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone.” Both readings speak of a rejection that God turns into victory.

2 Timothy 2:7–13 · The word of God is not chained

Setting.

The writer is a prisoner treated “like a criminal” (2:9), who expects to die soon (4:6). He has just given Timothy three pictures of patient effort, the soldier, the athlete and the farmer, and now asks him to think them over. Then he points to the one memory that makes all effort worthwhile: Jesus Christ, raised from the dead.

Structure.

  • v. 7: think it over; the Lord will give understanding.
  • vv. 8–10: remember Christ; Paul in chains; the word not chained; endurance for the sake of the elect.
  • vv. 11–13: the sure saying, in four lines.

Key words.

  • pistos ho logos, the saying is sure: A formula found five times in the Pastoral Letters (1 Timothy 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8). It marks a saying that the Church already knew and trusted, here perhaps part of an early baptismal hymn.
  • dedetai, is chained: From deō, to bind, the same root as the “chains” (desmoi) that Paul wears. He is bound; the word is not.
  • arneomai, deny: The word used for Peter's denial in the Gospels (Mark 14:68, 70). The saying takes it seriously, and then shows that God's faithfulness is greater than ours.

Verse by verse.

“But the word of God is not chained.” (2:9)

Prison walls could hold the apostle but not the Gospel. While he was bound, his letters, his messengers and his example went on.

“If we have died with him, we will also live with him;” (2:11)

Most likely a reference to Baptism, in which we die with Christ in order to rise with him (Romans 6:3–8).

“if we endure, we will also reign with him; if we deny him, he will also deny us;” (2:12)

An echo of Jesus' own warning: “whoever denies me before others, I also will deny before my Father in heaven” (Matthew 10:33).

“if we are faithless, he remains faithful” (2:13)

Here the pattern of the hymn breaks. We expect “he will be faithless too”. Instead God's faithfulness holds even when ours fails, “for he cannot deny himself”. This does not make denial harmless; verse 12 has just said the opposite. It is the ground of hope for every sinner who comes back.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that through Baptism the Christian is plunged into the death of Christ and rises with him (CCC 1227), which is the first line of this hymn. The Church's martyrs, who wrote no letters, proved the second line: their chains became a pulpit, and the word went on.

Mark 12:1–12 · The stone that the builders rejected

Setting.

Jesus has entered Jerusalem, cleansed the Temple, and been asked by the chief priests, scribes and elders by what authority he acts (11:27–33). He answers them with this parable, and verse 12 shows that it is aimed at them: “they realized that he had told this parable against them.” The parable concerns the leaders who were responsible for God's vineyard at that time, not the Jewish people as a whole; in fact the crowd protects Jesus here.

The opening words recall Isaiah's song of the vineyard (Isaiah 5:1–7), in which the vineyard is “the house of Israel”. Every listener would have recognized it. In Isaiah the vineyard yields wild grapes; in Jesus' parable the vineyard bears fruit, but the tenants refuse to give it to the owner.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the owner plants and equips the vineyard and leases it.
  • vv. 2–5: servant after servant is sent and mistreated.
  • vv. 6–8: the beloved son is sent last, killed, and thrown out.
  • v. 9: the owner will give the vineyard to others.
  • vv. 10–11: Psalm 118: the rejected stone becomes the cornerstone.
  • v. 12: the leaders understand, and want to arrest him.

Key words.

  • huios agapētos, beloved son: The same words the Father spoke at the Baptism (1:11) and the Transfiguration (9:7). In the parable Jesus speaks of himself as the last one sent, and as more than a servant.
  • geōrgoi, tenants: Farm workers who rented land and paid the owner part of the harvest, a common arrangement in Jesus' day.
  • kephalē gōnias, cornerstone: Literally “head of the corner”: either the great foundation stone at the corner, or the stone that crowns an arch or a wall. Either way, the most important stone in the building.

Verse by verse.

“He had still one other, a beloved son. Finally he sent him to them, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’” (12:6)

The owner's hope seems almost foolish. That is the point: God's patience goes beyond what any human owner would show.

“So they seized him, killed him, and threw him out of the vineyard.” (12:8)

Hebrews says that Jesus “suffered outside the city gate” (Hebrews 13:12).

“The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone;” (12:10)

From Psalm 118:22, a psalm sung at the great pilgrim feasts. In Hebrew there is a play of sound between “son” (ben) and “stone” (’eben), and some commentators think it lies behind the move from parable to psalm. Peter quotes the same verse about the risen Jesus (Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this parable in the light of Easter: the rejected stone has been raised and made the cornerstone of a new building (CCC 756). The Second Vatican Council teaches that what happened in Christ's Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews then living without distinction, nor against the Jews of today, and that they are not to be presented as rejected by God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). The parable is a warning for every steward in the Church, in every age, who might treat God's vineyard as his own.

Questions people ask

Does verse 9 mean that God has given up on Israel?

No. The parable is addressed to the leaders of that time (12:12), and the vineyard itself is not destroyed; what changes is who tends it. St Paul insists that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29), and the Church teaches that the Jewish people remain dear to God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). The warning is for us too: the vineyard is never ours to own.

For study and teaching

  • “The word of God is not chained.” Where in my life does the Gospel feel bound or silenced? How might God's word still be at work there?
  • The tenants wanted the vineyard for themselves. What part of my life, family, work or parish, do I treat as mine rather than as God's, entrusted to me?
