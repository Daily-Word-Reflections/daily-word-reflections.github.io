Word for the day

“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood. Let us then go to him outside the camp and bear the abuse he endured.” Hebrews 13:12–13

Reflection

Three remembrances meet on this first Friday of June. The Panchangam asks us to remember Most Rev. Geevarghese Mar Timotheos, a bishop of our Church who went to the Lord in 2019. It notes that the Latin Church celebrates today the solemnity of the Most Sacred Heart of Jesus, and June has long been kept by Catholics of every rite in Kerala as the month of the Sacred Heart. And it is the First Friday, when many of our families go to confession and receive Holy Communion in reparation and love. The readings chosen for the memory of a bishop gather all three together. The Letter to the Hebrews begins: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” We remember a bishop not only with gratitude for what he did, but with a prayer that the Lord who called him may receive him, and with a resolve to imitate what was Christlike in him. The memory of a shepherd always sends us back to the Shepherd whose heart was the source of his.

The letter then says something remarkable: “We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” The Christian altar is not a table of ordinary food; it is the place where the one sacrifice of Christ is made present. And where was that sacrifice offered? “Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood. Let us then go to him outside the camp and bear the abuse he endured.” Outside the gate, on the hill of Calvary, one of the soldiers opened his side with a spear, and blood and water flowed out. That pierced side is what the Church contemplates in the devotion to the Sacred Heart: not a sentimental picture but the wound through which we see the love of God made flesh. St Bonaventure wrote that his side was pierced so that, through the visible wound, we might see the invisible wound of love (The Tree of Life, in substance). Hebrews draws the practical conclusion: “Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name. Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” Praise and sharing: this is how a heart that has been loved answers.

The Gospel shows the same love in the shape of a steward's faithfulness: “Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time? Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” A bishop is such a steward; so is a priest, a parent, a teacher, a nurse on night duty. The wicked servant thinks “My master is delayed” and begins to treat others as he pleases. The faithful one keeps feeding the household, because he knows whose household it is. The alternative Gospels printed today show the heart of the Master who appoints the stewards: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.” “No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” On this First Friday, let us thank God for the shepherds who fed us with his word and his Body, pray for the soul of Mar Timotheos, and bring our own hearts to the pierced Heart of Christ, so that they may become a little more like his: gentle, humble, faithful, and ready to share.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Panchangam asks us to remember Most Rev. Geevarghese Mar Timotheos, a bishop of our Church who went to the Lord in 2019. It is also the First Friday, and the Latin Church celebrates today the Sacred Heart of Jesus. The Letter to the Hebrews tells us: Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith (Hebrews 13:7).

Then it says: Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever (Hebrews 13:8). Bishops and priests come and go; the Lord who sends them remains. And Hebrews shows us where his love was poured out: Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood (Hebrews 13:12). There, on Calvary, a soldier pierced his side, and blood and water came out. That open Heart is the source of every shepherd's love.

The Gospel asks: Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household? (Matthew 24:45). A bishop is such a servant, but so is every father and mother, every teacher, every one of us who has someone to care for. Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives (Matthew 24:46).

So today let us pray for the soul of Mar Timotheos and for all our shepherds. Let us bring our hearts to the Heart of Jesus in confession and Communion on this First Friday. And let us do what Hebrews asks: offer a sacrifice of praise, and do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God (Hebrews 13:16). Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

For the memory of a bishop the Church reads of faithful leaders. Hebrews asks us to remember those who spoke the word of God to us and to imitate their faith, and leads us to Jesus who suffered outside the gate and to the altar where we share in his sacrifice. In Matthew the Lord asks who is the faithful and wise servant, set over the household to give food at the proper time. Three alternative Gospels are also given: the good shepherd who lays down his life (John 10:11–18), the way, the truth and the life (John 14:1–6), and the greatest love (John 15:9–17). The key words are remember, the altar and faithful.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · Remember your leaders

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews was written to Christians, probably of Jewish background, who were tired and tempted to drift away. Its author is not named. The Church long counted it among the letters of Paul; most scholars today think it was written by another teacher of the apostolic generation, and the Church receives it as inspired Scripture. Chapter 13 gathers final counsels.

Throughout the letter the author has compared the sacrifices of the old covenant with the one sacrifice of Christ. Here he recalls the Day of Atonement, when the bodies of the animals offered for sin were burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27).

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: remember your leaders; Jesus Christ is the same for ever.
  • v. 9: do not be carried away by strange teachings.
  • vv. 10–14: our altar; Jesus suffered outside the gate; let us go out to him.
  • vv. 15–16: the sacrifice of praise and the sacrifice of sharing.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: Those who lead and guide the community. The word returns in 13:17 and 13:24. Those of verse 7 seem to have already died, since their “outcome” can be seen.
  • thysiastērion, altar: The place of sacrifice. The author speaks of an altar from which the priests of the old tent have no right to eat: the sacrifice of Christ and the table where his people share in it.
  • exō tēs pylēs, outside the gate: Jesus was crucified outside the walls of Jerusalem (John 19:17, 20), like the sin offering burned outside the camp.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

Remembering is more than gratitude. We look at how a shepherd's life ended, and we imitate his faith, not only his works.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

Leaders come and go; Christ remains. The bishop who taught us pointed to one who does not change.

“We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” (13:10)

Many Catholic interpreters see here both the cross and the Eucharistic table that makes the sacrifice of the cross present. The Christian altar is not a table of ordinary food.

“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood.” (13:12)

What the old sin offering pointed to, Christ fulfilled. To go “outside the camp” with him (13:13) is to share his rejection and his love.

“Through him, then, let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God,” (13:15)

Praise and sharing (13:16) are called sacrifices. They do not replace the sacrifice of Christ; they flow from it and are offered “through him”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names the Eucharist among other titles “the sacrifice of praise”, quoting Hebrews 13:15 (CCC 1330). It also teaches that the altar of the New Covenant is the Lord's cross, from which the sacraments of the Paschal mystery flow (CCC 1182). So the Church reads these verses at the memory of a bishop: the shepherd is remembered at the altar where he offered the Holy Qurbono, and his people are asked to imitate his faith.

Matthew 24:45–51 · The faithful and wise slave

Setting.

These verses belong to the last great discourse of Jesus in Matthew (chapters 24–25), spoken on the Mount of Olives a few days before his Passion. He has just said that no one knows the day or the hour (24:36) and that the disciples must keep awake (24:42). This parable is the first of several on readiness, followed by the ten bridesmaids and the talents (25:1–30). Luke has a similar parable (Luke 12:42–46).

Structure.

  • v. 45: the question: who is the faithful and wise slave?
  • vv. 46–47: the blessing of the one found at work.
  • vv. 48–51: the wicked slave who says “My master is delayed”.

Key words.

  • pistos kai phronimos, faithful and wise: Faithful: one who can be trusted. Wise: one who sees what the hour demands. The same pair is used of the wise bridesmaids in 25:2.
  • oiketeia, household: The master's household servants. The steward does not own them; he is set over them to serve them.
  • hypokritai, hypocrites: A word Matthew often uses. In Luke's version the place is with “the unfaithful” (Luke 12:46).

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household,” (24:45)

The steward's task is to give the others their food “at the proper time”. Authority in the Lord's household is service: feeding others with the word and the sacraments.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)

The master does not ask for spectacular deeds but for faithfulness in the ordinary task he has given.

“But if that wicked slave says to himself, ‘My master is delayed,’” (24:48)

The danger begins in the heart, with the thought that there is no hurry. Then come harshness to others and self-indulgence. The severe ending (24:51) uses the strong language of judgment to show how serious it is to abuse the trust of the Lord.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council asks the bishop to keep before his eyes the example of the Good Shepherd, who came not to be served but to serve and to lay down his life for his sheep (Lumen Gentium 27). This parable, and the alternative Gospel of the good shepherd, describe what the Church hopes for in every bishop and priest, and what every Christian is asked within his own household.

Questions people ask

Does the “sacrifice of praise” mean that Christians no longer need the sacrifice of the altar?

No. Hebrews says “We have an altar”. Our praise, our good works and our sharing are offered “through him”, that is, through Christ and his one sacrifice, which is made present in the Holy Qurbono. The Catechism calls the Eucharist itself the sacrifice of praise (CCC 1330). Our spiritual sacrifices are joined to his offering; they do not take its place.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam keeps today the memory of Most Rev. Geevarghese Mar Timotheos, a bishop of our Church who went to the Lord in 2019, with the readings appointed for the memory of a bishop. It is also the First Friday, when many families go to confession and receive Holy Communion, and June has long been kept by Catholics in Kerala as the month of the Sacred Heart; the Latin Church celebrates that solemnity today. Hebrews brings these together: we remember a shepherd and pray for him, and we go out to Jesus who sanctified the people “by his own blood”.

For study and teaching

  • Whom do I remember as a leader who spoke the word of God to me? What in their faith can I imitate?
  • In the household the Lord has given me, my family, parish or work, whom am I asked to feed “at the proper time”?
