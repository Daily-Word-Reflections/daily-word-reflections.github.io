Word for the day

“For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes.” 1 Corinthians 11:26

Reflection

Three days after Pentecost our Church keeps a memory that belongs to her very heart: the memory of Mar Yaqob Shleeha offering the Holy Qurbono for the first time. The Panchangam does not say which James is meant, but our Syriac tradition has always looked to St James of Jerusalem, called by St Paul “James the Lord's brother,” that is, a close kinsman of Jesus. The risen Lord appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7); he led the Church of Jerusalem and spoke decisively at the council of the apostles (Acts 15:13–21); ancient writers remember him as the first bishop of that city and tell of a man of such prayer that his knees grew hard as a camel's (Hegesippus, in Eusebius, Church History 2.23, in substance). He was put to death in Jerusalem about the year 62. To him the tradition ascribes the Anaphora of St James, the great Eucharistic prayer of the Church of Jerusalem, which became the pattern of the West Syriac Qurbono that we celebrate. We cannot date the morning when he first stood at the altar. But we know what the memory means: from the first days after Pentecost, the Church that had received the Spirit gathered for “the breaking of bread and the prayers” (Acts 2:42), doing what the Lord had commanded.

The Epistle gives us the oldest written account of that command. Writing to Corinth some twenty years after the Resurrection, St Paul says: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, ‘This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ In the same way he took the cup also, after supper, saying, ‘This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.’” Received and handed on: that is how the Qurbono reached us, from the upper room to Jerusalem, from Jerusalem to Antioch, from Antioch to the shores of Kerala, from one generation of priests and faithful to the next. In the Gospel St Luke lets us hear the longing in the Lord's voice: “I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer.” The Supper is not something the disciples arranged for Jesus; it is something he longed to give them. “This is my body, which is given for you.” “This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.”

St Cyril of Jerusalem, teaching the newly baptised in the very city of St James, told them not to regard the bread and wine as plain bread and wine, for they are the Body and Blood of Christ according to the Lord's own word; even if the senses suggest otherwise, let faith make you sure (Mystagogical Catecheses 4.6, in substance). This is the faith of the Catholic Church: in the Holy Qurbono the one sacrifice of the Cross is made present, and Christ, true God and true man, is truly, really and substantially present under the forms of bread and wine. “Remembrance” here is not a fond memory of something absent; it is the living memorial in which the Lord's sacrifice becomes present to us. And it looks forward too: “For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes.” Today let us thank God for every priest who has stood at the altar for us, from St James to the young priest of our own parish. Let us come to the Qurbono not as spectators but as those whom the Lord eagerly desired to feed. And if we have grown careless, arriving late, leaving early, receiving without preparation, let this memory of the first Qurbono make our next one feel like the first again.

Full homily

Dear friends, today our Church remembers Mar Yaqob Shleeha offering the Holy Qurbono for the first time. Our tradition honours St James of Jerusalem, the Lord's brother, the first bishop of that city, to whom the Anaphora of St James is ascribed, the source of the Qurbono we celebrate. After Pentecost the first Christians devoted themselves to the breaking of bread and the prayers (Acts 2:42).

St Paul tells us how the Qurbono came to us: For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you (1 Corinthians 11:23). On the night when he was betrayed, the Lord took bread and said, This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me (1 Corinthians 11:24). From the upper room, through St James and the apostles, through the centuries, this gift has been handed on to our own altar.

In the Gospel we hear the longing of Jesus: I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer (Luke 22:15). He gives the bread and says, This is my body, which is given for you; he gives the cup, the new covenant in my blood (Luke 22:19–20). What we receive is not a symbol only. It is truly his Body and his Blood, his one sacrifice made present.

For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes (1 Corinthians 11:26). Let us thank God today for every priest who has offered the Qurbono for us. Let us come early, prepare our hearts, and receive the Lord as one who longs to feed us. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what the Holy Qurbono is? It is the greatest gift Jesus gave us. At the Last Supper he said: This is my body, this is my blood, do this in memory of me. Today we remember St James, who offered the Qurbono in Jerusalem long, long ago. He prayed so much that his knees became hard! Next time you go to the Qurbono, remember: Jesus really comes to us, and he is very happy to see you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on the night you were betrayed you took bread and the cup and gave them to your apostles as your Body and Blood, and you commanded, Do this in remembrance of me. We thank you for Mar Yaqob Shleeha and for all who have offered the Holy Qurbono from the first days of your Church until now. As you eagerly desired to eat the Passover with your disciples, give us a hunger for your holy Mysteries. Make us worthy to receive them with faith and love, to proclaim your death and Resurrection, and to wait for your coming in glory. With your Father and your Holy Spirit, now and for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Three days after Pentecost our Church remembers, by an old tradition, the first Holy Qurbono offered by Mar Yaqob Shleeha, James the brother of the Lord and first bishop of Jerusalem. Both readings take us back to the night when it all began. Paul hands on to the Corinthians what he himself received: the Lord Jesus took bread and the cup and said, “Do this in remembrance of me.” Luke shows the same night from inside the upper room, where Jesus says, “I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer.” The key words are hand on, remembrance and covenant.

1 Corinthians 11:23–26 · What I received from the Lord

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus (16:8), around AD 53–55. The church in Corinth was gifted but divided, and the divisions showed even at the Lord's Supper. When they gathered, some went ahead with their own meal while others, the poor, went hungry (11:20–22). Paul does not answer first with a new rule. He reminds them what the Supper is.

These four verses are the oldest written account of the Last Supper that we have, older than the Gospels in their written form. Paul had taught it to the Corinthians when he founded their church, around AD 50, and he says that it came to him as a tradition from the Lord himself.

Structure.

  • v. 23a: the chain of tradition: received from the Lord, handed on to you.
  • vv. 23b–24: the night of betrayal; the bread, the thanksgiving, “This is my body”.
  • v. 25: the cup after supper: the new covenant in my blood.
  • v. 26: Paul's comment: every Supper proclaims the Lord's death until he comes.
  • ¶ In the verses that follow Paul draws the lesson: whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup in an unworthy manner will be answerable for the body and blood of the Lord (11:27–29).

Key words.

  • paralambanō / paradidōmi, receive / hand on: The pair of words used for passing on a sacred teaching from teacher to disciple. Paul uses the same pair in 15:3 for the Gospel of the Resurrection. The Eucharist reached Corinth as a gift handed on, not as something the community made up. There is a play on words too: the night when Jesus “was betrayed” uses the same verb. On the night he was handed over, he handed himself over for us.
  • anamnēsis, remembrance: More than a memory in the mind. In the Bible, to remember before God makes the saving event present. Israel kept the Passover as “a day of remembrance” (Exodus 12:14), and each generation was to tell what the Lord had done for it.
  • katangellete, you proclaim: The word used for preaching the Gospel. The Supper itself is a proclamation: it announces the Lord's death as the source of life, and it looks forward to his coming.

Verse by verse.

“the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread,” (11:23)

Paul names the night, not only the meal. The Eucharist is bound to the Passion. It comes from the hour when one disciple handed Jesus over and Jesus gave himself up for all.

““This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”” (11:24)

“For you” gives the meaning of the gift: a body given up on behalf of others. Among the four accounts of the Supper, only Paul and Luke keep the command “Do this”, which the Church has always understood as given to the apostles and to those who follow them in their ministry.

“This cup is the new covenant in my blood.” (11:25)

Jeremiah promised a new covenant written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34). At Sinai Moses sprinkled the people with “the blood of the covenant” (Exodus 24:8). Jesus joins the two: the covenant is new, and it is sealed in his own blood.

“For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” (11:26)

The Supper looks two ways: back to the Cross and forward to the Lord's return. The first Christians prayed in Aramaic, Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” (16:22).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is the memorial of Christ's Passover. In the liturgy this memorial is not only the recalling of past events: the sacrifice that Christ offered once for all on the Cross remains ever present (CCC 1362–1364). The Catechism points to this passage for the Lord's command to repeat his words and actions “until he comes” (CCC 1341). With the Council of Trent it teaches that at the Last Supper Christ instituted the Eucharist and made his apostles priests of the New Testament (CCC 1337). Paul's own concern is also the Church's: the Body of Christ received at the altar cannot be separated from his body the community, and above all from its poorest members. The Eucharist commits us to the poor (CCC 1397).

Luke 22:14–20 · I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you

Setting.

Luke places the Supper at the hour of the Passover meal (22:7–13). Jesus has sent Peter and John to prepare it in a large upper room. Luke's account is the longest of the four, because after the meal Jesus goes on speaking with the apostles at the table (22:21–38).

Luke's words over the bread and the cup are very close to Paul's: both have “for you”, “Do this in remembrance of me” and “the new covenant in my blood”. Scholars commonly note that Luke and Paul share one form of the tradition, and Mark and Matthew another. Luke is named as Paul's companion in Colossians 4:14 and Philemon 24.

Structure.

  • v. 14: the hour comes; Jesus takes his place with the apostles.
  • vv. 15–16: his great desire, and the Passover fulfilled in the kingdom of God.
  • vv. 17–18: a first cup, shared among them.
  • v. 19: the bread: my body given for you.
  • v. 20: the cup after supper: the new covenant in my blood, poured out for you.

Key words.

  • epithymia epethymēsa, I have eagerly desired: Literally “with desire I have desired”. The Greek copies a Hebrew way of speaking that doubles a word to make it strong. It shows the heart of Jesus as he goes to his Passion: he does not only accept this meal, he has longed for it.
  • to pascha, the Passover: The feast of Israel's deliverance from Egypt (Exodus 12). Jesus will not eat it again “until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God”. The old feast is not thrown aside; it reaches its fulfilment in him. Paul calls Christ “our paschal lamb” (1 Corinthians 5:7).
  • ekchynnomenon, poured out: A word of sacrifice, used of blood poured out at the altar. The Greek is in the present tense: the offering of the Cross is already present at the table.

Verse by verse.

“When the hour came, he took his place at the table, and the apostles with him.” (22:14)

Luke gives the Supper a solemn beginning. The word “apostles”, those who are sent, reminds us that this meal is given to men who will be sent out.

“I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer;” (22:15)

Only Luke records this saying. Before we desire the Eucharist, Christ has desired to give himself to us.

““This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”” (22:19)

Textual note: a few ancient witnesses, chiefly one Greek manuscript (Codex Bezae) and some Old Latin copies, stop after “This is my body”. Almost all manuscripts have the longer text, and the NRSV and other modern editions print it, as the Church's lectionary does.

“This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.” (22:20)

Luke mentions two cups (vv. 17 and 20). The Passover meal, as later Jewish sources describe it, had several cups of wine. Many scholars think the first cup belongs to the Passover rite, and the second, “after supper”, is the cup of the new covenant.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this account as the institution of the Eucharist and of the priesthood that serves it. The Catechism says that by celebrating the Last Supper with his apostles in the course of the Passover meal, Jesus gave the Jewish Passover its definitive meaning (CCC 1340). St Ignatius of Antioch, whom our Church honours as Mar Ignatius Noorono, wrote that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins and which the Father raised up (Letter to the Smyrnaeans 7, in substance). The Church takes the Lord at his word, “This is my body”: under the signs of bread and wine Christ is truly, really and substantially present (CCC 1374).

Questions people ask

Which James is remembered today?

James “the Lord's brother” (Galatians 1:19), who led the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; 21:18). He is not James the son of Zebedee, whose death by the sword is told in Acts 12:2 and whom our Church honoured on 30 April. The Latin tradition has usually identified the brother of the Lord with the apostle James the son of Alphaeus; Eastern traditions often keep them apart. The question has never been settled with certainty. “Brother” in the Bible can mean a close relative, and the Church holds that Mary remained ever virgin (CCC 500).

Did Mar Yaqob write the Anaphora that bears his name?

The Anaphora of St James carries his name because it comes from the Church of Jerusalem, where he was the first bishop. Many scholars think its present text took shape in Jerusalem in the fourth century, from older roots, and was then carried to Antioch and beyond. The name honours its source: the church that first devoted itself to “the breaking of bread and the prayers” after Pentecost (Acts 2:42).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Today our Church keeps the memory of the first Holy Qurbono offered by Mar Yaqob, the brother of the Lord. In the West Syriac tradition his name stands first among the anaphoras: the Anaphora of St James is honoured as the chief one, after whose pattern many others were composed. When the priest repeats the words of the Lord over the bread and the cup and the people answer “Amen”, we stand in the chain that Paul describes: what was received from the Lord is handed on. This week after Pentecost also reminds us that the Spirit who came upon the apostles is the same Spirit called down upon the gifts at every Holy Qurbono.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says he handed on what he had received. Who handed on the faith of the Holy Qurbono to me, and to whom am I handing it on?
  • Jesus “eagerly desired” to eat this Passover with his apostles. How can I prepare to meet that desire next Sunday?
  • Paul links the Lord's Supper with care for the poor of the community. Who goes hungry near my parish table?
