Word for the day

“Indeed, the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing until it divides soul from spirit, joints from marrow; it is able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.” Hebrews 4:12

Reflection

“Indeed, the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword… able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.” God's word is not a dead letter; it cuts, heals, judges and gives life. When the Gospel is proclaimed in the Qurbono, Christ himself speaks. The question is not whether the word is powerful, but whether we listen.

In Mark's Gospel Jesus begins his teaching by the sea with one word: “Listen!” Then the sower goes out to sow. The seed is the word; the soil is the human heart. “And these are the ones sown on the good soil: they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” And he ends: “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!” Listening is the first act of faith.

At the burning bush God speaks to Moses and reveals his name: “I AM WHO I AM… This is my name forever.” God is not an idea; he is the living One who speaks and sends: “I will be with you.” Jeremiah repeats God's one demand: “Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people.” To obey (in Latin ob-audire) is to listen deeply.

The Letter to the Hebrews joins the Word with mercy: “We do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathise with our weaknesses, but we have one who in every respect has been tested as we are, yet without sin. Let us therefore approach the throne of grace with boldness, so that we may receive mercy.” St Peter tells us who we have become by listening and believing: “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own people.”

Homily outline

Theme: “Let anyone with ears to hear listen.”

Jesus tells the parable of the sower. The Letter to the Hebrews calls God's word living and active. The God who revealed his name to Moses speaks to us, and calls us his own priestly people.

1. The seed and the soil

  • God's word never returns empty; the problem is never the seed.
  • The path, the rocky ground and the thorns are hearts closed by hardness, shallowness or worry.
  • Good soil hears, accepts and bears fruit with patience.
“So shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose, and succeed in the thing for which I sent it.” Isaiah 55:11
“And these are the ones sown on the good soil: they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” Mark 4:20

Apply: Ask yourself honestly which soil your heart is this week, and remove one “thorn”.

2. The word is living and active

  • God asks above all: “Obey my voice.”
  • His word judges the thoughts of the heart; it is a sword that heals.
  • In the Holy Qurbono, when the Gospel is proclaimed, Christ himself speaks (Second Vatican Council, Sacrosanctum Concilium 7).
“But this command I gave them, “Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people; and walk only in the way that I command you, so that it may be well with you.”” Jeremiah 7:23

3. A people who belong to “I AM”

  • God revealed his name to Moses: “I AM WHO I AM.”

  • This common priesthood of the faithful is lived by offering our lives with Christ, and is served by the ministerial priesthood.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the Letter to the Hebrews says: “The word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword.” When the Gospel is read in the Holy Qurbono, it is not a story from long ago. Christ himself speaks to us now. That is why we stand, why the candles and the incense honour the Book.

In Mark's Gospel Jesus begins his parable with one word: “Listen!” and ends with another: “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!” In between is the sower. The same seed falls on the path, on rock, among thorns, and on good soil. Good soil is the heart that hears the word, accepts it, and lets it bear fruit, thirty, sixty, a hundredfold.

At the burning bush God told Moses his name: “I AM WHO I AM.” The living God speaks. Jeremiah says that God asked only one thing of Israel: “Obey my voice.” To obey means to listen deeply. Much of our trouble comes from not listening: to God, to one another.

And if we are afraid that the Word will judge us, Hebrews adds: we have a high priest who understands our weakness, who was tested in every way as we are, yet without sin. “Let us therefore approach the throne of grace with boldness, so that we may receive mercy.” This week give ten minutes each day to the Gospel. Listen. And let the word bear fruit. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Eighth Sunday after Pentecost the readings are about the God who speaks and the people who hear. In the evening Gospel (Mark 4:1–12) Jesus sits in a boat on the lake and teaches the crowd in parables, beginning with the word “Listen!” and ending, “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!” Job 23:1–7 gives the voice of a man in great suffering who longs to find God and put his case before him. Jeremiah 7:21–25 repeats the Lord's first command to his people, “Obey my voice, and I will be your God.” Isaiah 65:13–16 promises that God's servants will sing for gladness of heart and will bless themselves by “the God of faithfulness”. In 1 Peter 2:4–10 the believers, coming to Christ the living stone, are built up as a spiritual house, a chosen race, a royal priesthood.

We study three readings more closely. In Exodus 3 the Lord speaks to Moses from the bush, hears the cry of his people and gives his name. Hebrews 4:12–16 says that the word of God is living and active, and that we have a high priest who sympathizes with our weakness. In the Gospel (Mark 4:1–20) the sower scatters the word on four kinds of ground, and Jesus himself explains the parable. The key words today are word, hearing and fruit.

Exodus 3:9–15 · I am who I am

Setting.

Moses, brought up in Pharaoh's house, had fled Egypt after killing an Egyptian, and for many years he had been a shepherd in Midian, tending the flock of his father-in-law Jethro. One day he leads the sheep to Horeb, “the mountain of God”, and sees a bush that burns without being consumed (3:1–3). Our verses are the middle of the conversation that follows.

The Lord has already said that he has seen the misery of his people, heard their cry and “come down to deliver them” (3:7–8). Now he turns to Moses with the word “So come” (v. 10): the God who comes down sends a man. The scene is the call of Moses, and it shows the pattern of every call in Scripture: God sees, God sends, the human being objects, and God answers with his presence.

Structure.

  • v. 9: God has heard the cry of Israel and seen the oppression.
  • v. 10: “I will send you to Pharaoh.”
  • v. 11: Moses' first objection: “Who am I?”
  • v. 12: God's answer, “I will be with you”, and a sign still in the future.
  • vv. 13–14: Moses asks for the name; God answers “I am who I am”.
  • v. 15: the name given in its covenant form: the Lord, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; “my name forever”.

Key words.

  • ṣa‘ăqâ, cry: The cry of those who are wronged and have no helper. In the Bible God always hears it: it is first heard in Genesis 4:10, the blood of Abel, and again here, and later in the Psalms. The God of Israel is the God who listens to the cry of the oppressed.
  • ’ehyeh ’ăšer ’ehyeh, I am who I am: The Hebrew can also be rendered “I will be who I will be”. It does not give a definition, but a promise and a presence: I am the God who is truly there, and who will be there for you. The Greek of the Septuagint (egō eimi ho ōn, “I am the one who is”) opened the way for later reflection on God as Being itself.
  • zēker, title, memorial: The word means that by which something is remembered and called upon. God gives a name that the people can use in prayer and worship for all generations. The Jewish people, out of reverence, do not pronounce the divine name but say Adonai, “Lord”; the Church follows this in her liturgy.

Verse by verse.

“I have also seen how the Egyptians oppress them.” (3:9)

God does not speak of oppression as a general idea. He has “seen”. For the Bible the Lord is a God who looks at human suffering and does not turn away.

“Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh, and bring the Israelites out of Egypt?” (3:11)

The first of Moses' objections. The great prophets feel their smallness; so did Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:6). God does not tell Moses that he is great enough. He says, “I will be with you.” The call does not rest on the strength of the one called.

“I will be with you;” (3:12)

The answer is a promise, and the sign follows: “you shall worship God on this mountain”. The sign will come only after the task is done. Faith goes first, and the sign comes afterwards.

“I am who I am” (3:14)

The name is both open and hidden. God is not an object that we can grasp, and he is not a distant power. He makes himself known by being with his people.

“This is my name forever, and this my title for all generations.” (3:15)

The God who is beyond all things is the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, whose faithfulness goes through the generations. Jesus draws on this very passage when he teaches that God “is God not of the dead, but of the living” (Mark 12:26–27).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in the name given at the bush the heart of God's revelation to Israel: “I am who I am” says both that God is faithful, always with his people, and that he is the mystery beyond our grasp (CCC 205–213). The Church reads the passage as pointing towards Jesus Christ. In John's Gospel Jesus says, “Before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58) and speaks of himself with the “I am” sayings (6:35; 8:12; 10:11; 11:25; 14:6). The Church also keeps the reverence of Israel for the divine name: in 2008 the Holy See asked that the name written in the Hebrew text as four letters not be pronounced in Catholic liturgy, but that the traditional “Lord” be used. The Fathers saw in Moses' reluctance a lesson in humility: God chooses those who know that they are not enough.

Hebrews 4:12–16 · The word living and active, the high priest who sympathizes

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews is a long sermon written for Jewish Christians who were growing weary and tempted to give up. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture, and the Syriac tradition reads it among the letters of Paul. The author does not give his name, and many scholars, ancient and modern, have thought that it was written by a disciple in Paul's circle; the Church has not defined the question.

Our verses end a long passage (3:7 to 4:13) built on Psalm 95: “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” The wilderness generation heard God's voice and failed to enter his rest; the readers are urged not to do likewise. Verses 12–13 close that warning, and verses 14–16 open the next theme, Christ as high priest, which will fill the middle of the letter.

Structure.

  • v. 12: the word of God is living and active, sharper than a two-edged sword.
  • v. 13: nothing in creation is hidden from God; we must give account to him.
  • v. 14: we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens; let us hold fast to our confession.
  • v. 15: he sympathizes with our weaknesses, tested as we are, yet without sin.
  • v. 16: let us approach the throne of grace with boldness.

Key words.

  • logos tou theou, word of God: In the letter it first means what God has said in Scripture, which the writer has just been quoting. The Church has also heard in it the Word who is the Son, through whom God has spoken (Hebrews 1:2). Both senses live together.
  • energēs, active, effective: The word of God is not a dead record. It does what it says: it works in those who hear it. The same word is used for the “power” by which God works (Philippians 2:13).
  • archiereus megas, great high priest: The title contains the central teaching of the letter: Jesus is the priest who offers himself and who stands in the presence of God for us. “Passed through the heavens” recalls the high priest of Israel passing through the veil on the Day of Atonement.
  • parrēsia, boldness, confidence: A word for plain, open speech before one's superiors. We may come to God's throne not trembling like slaves but with the confidence of children, because our priest has gone before us.

Verse by verse.

“Indeed, the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword,” (4:12)

The word of God cuts, but to heal, as a surgeon's knife does. It reaches the thoughts and intentions of the heart, where we cannot deceive even ourselves.

“And before him no creature is hidden, but all are naked and laid bare to the eyes of the one to whom we must render an account.” (4:13)

The first effect of the word is the truth about ourselves. Before God we are seen as we are. The comfort comes in the next verses: the one before whom we stand is the high priest who understands.

“For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses,” (4:15)

The Greek says that Jesus can “suffer with” us. He does not look at human weakness from a distance. He has entered into it.

“Let us therefore approach the throne of grace with boldness,” (4:16)

The POC reads “with hope” where the NRSV has “with boldness”, which is the sense of the Greek word. The throne of God is for those who come as the weak: there they receive mercy and grace to help.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that God speaks to us in Scripture and that “the Christian faith is not a ‘religion of the book’” but of the Word of God, living and incarnate (CCC 108; Dei Verbum 21 says the Church venerates Scripture as she venerates the Body of the Lord). The Catechism cites verse 15 to show that Jesus was truly tempted yet without sin (CCC 540), and the Council of Chalcedon says he is “like us in all things except sin” (CCC 467). Because he is both God and man, he is the one mediator who can bring us near. In the Holy Qurbono the whole of the first part is a listening to the word, and the second part an approach to the throne of grace: the Church acts out the movement of these verses every Sunday.

Mark 4:1–20 · A sower went out to sow

Setting.

In Mark 3 Jesus' family and the scribes from Jerusalem have both misjudged him (3:20–35), and the question of who truly belongs to him has been raised: “Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.” Chapter 4 now gives Jesus' first long teaching in parables. The teaching takes place by the lake, with Jesus seated in a boat as a pulpit and the crowd on the shore.

The sower was a familiar sight in Galilee. The seed was scattered by hand over the field before it was ploughed in, so some fell on the footpath that crossed the field, some on rock just below the thin soil, some among thorn roots, and some on good earth. In verses 10–20 Jesus is alone with the Twelve and others and explains the parable. Many scholars think the explanation reflects the early Church's preaching; others hold that it comes from Jesus. In either case the Church receives it as the word of Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the lake, the boat, the crowd; teaching in parables.
  • vv. 3–9: the parable of the sower: three failures and then the great harvest; “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!”
  • vv. 10–12: the disciples ask about the parables; “the secret of the kingdom of God”.
  • vv. 13–20: Jesus explains the parable: the four kinds of ground.

Key words.

  • parabolē, parable: The Greek word translates the Hebrew māšāl, which means a riddle, a proverb or a comparison. A parable is a picture from ordinary life that holds the hearer by its surprise and asks for an answer.
  • mystērion, secret, mystery: The “secret of the kingdom” is not a puzzle to be solved by the clever. It is the plan of God that is being revealed in Jesus and that can only be received. The disciples are given it, not because they are better but because they have come close to Jesus.
  • akouō, to hear, to listen: The word begins and ends the parable (4:3, 9). In the Bible to hear means to hear and obey. The whole chapter is a call to listen the right way (see also 4:23–24).

Verse by verse.

“Listen! A sower went out to sow.” (4:3)

The chapter opens with a command to listen. The Jews prayed each day, “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4); Jesus opens his teaching with the same call to the same people, but now the word is the Kingdom.

“Other seed fell into good soil and brought forth grain, growing up and increasing and yielding thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” (4:8)

A harvest of thirty- or sixty-fold would be good. A hundredfold is an amazing harvest, the kind that Scripture describes as a sign of God's blessing (Genesis 26:12). The sower is not discouraged by the waste, because the end is a harvest beyond measure.

“To you has been given the secret of the kingdom of God,” (4:11)

The secret is a gift, “given”. Those who stay close to Jesus and ask him, as the disciples do, receive the meaning. “Those outside” are not a particular people, but anyone who stays at a distance and does not ask. The saying must never be read as speaking of the Jews as a people; the crowd, the disciples and the Twelve are all Jews.

“Do you not understand this parable? Then how will you understand all the parables?” (4:13)

Jesus does not praise his disciples' understanding. In Mark they are often slow, and that is a comfort: those who follow Jesus do not have to be quick, but to keep coming back to ask.

“The sower sows the word.” (4:14)

In the early Church “the word” means the Gospel (compare Acts 6:7). The sower is Christ, and after him all who preach in his name.

“they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit,” (4:20)

The good soil is described by three actions: they hear, they accept, they bear fruit. The fruit is not for display; it is a life changed and a love that serves.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this parable the pattern of all preaching and of every response to the word of God. The Catechism teaches that Jesus invites everyone to enter the Kingdom, and that to those who accept the word the secrets of the Kingdom are opened (CCC 546). The “fruit” of the word is the sanctity of the faithful and the growth of the Church (Lumen Gentium 5). The four kinds of ground have been understood by many preachers as four ways of hearing the word within one person, rather than four kinds of people; each of us has a path, a rock, a bush of thorns and some good earth. The Fathers and the liturgies pray that God will make the heart good soil. In the Syriac tradition the Gospel is carried and honoured in procession because it is the sowing of the Word.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus say he speaks in parables “so that” people may not understand (Mark 4:12)?

The saying is hard, and Matthew softens it by writing “because” (Matthew 13:13). Jesus here quotes Isaiah 6:9–10, a passage that speaks of a people whose hearts have grown dull. Most Catholic commentators understand the verse not as God's wish to keep anyone away from the truth, but as a description of what happens when a word is heard without openness: the same parable that opens the mind of one who asks leaves another with nothing but a story. The parable is a call and an invitation. Mark himself shows that the disciples did not understand at first (4:13), and that the way is open to all who come to Jesus and ask.

Does “thirty, sixty and a hundredfold” mean that some Christians are more important than others?

No. The three numbers describe the fruitfulness of the word, not ranks among the faithful. All are called to the fullness of the Christian life and to holiness (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013), each according to his or her own path. The point of the parable is the wonder of the harvest: whatever God sows, in whatever measure it comes, is a gift beyond what the sower could have expected.

If Jesus was tempted “as we are”, could he have sinned?

The Church teaches that Jesus was truly tempted, and that the temptation was a real trial (CCC 540), and that he is “like us in all things except sin” (CCC 467). His sinlessness does not come from his being spared the struggle. It comes from the fact that he is the Son of God made man, whose human will was perfectly joined to the will of the Father. So when the Letter to the Hebrews says that he sympathizes with our weakness, it means that he has known the pressure of temptation from the inside, and has not yielded to it.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost invite the Church to listen to the Lord who sends the Spirit and speaks to his people. The Holy Qurbono has two parts: first the table of the Word, with the readings and the Gospel, and then the table of the Body and Blood. Before the readings the deacon calls the people to stand and listen with care, and the Gospel is received standing, as Christ himself speaking. Today's three readings give a good picture of the Qurbono. In Exodus the Lord says, “I will be with you”; in Hebrews he is the high priest who stands for us at the throne of grace; in the Gospel he is the sower of the word. The faithful are asked to hear the word with the same reverence with which they receive the Sacrament.

For study and teaching

  • God says to Moses, “I will be with you.” Where in my life do I need to hear this promise, and what task has God asked of me that I have thought too great for me?
  • Hebrews says the word of God lays bare “the thoughts and intentions of the heart”. When I hear the Scriptures at the Holy Qurbono, do I let the word look at me, or do I only look at it?
  • Which kind of ground is my heart this week: the footpath, the rock, the thorns or the good earth? What do I need to clear away?
  • The sower scatters his seed generously, with no fear of waste. How can I speak of Christ in my family or parish with the same generosity and hope?
