Word for the day

“And he answered them, “Go and tell John what you have seen and heard: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised,” Luke 7:22

Reflection

Hebrews opens with a sweeping vision: “Long ago God spoke to our ancestors in many and various ways by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son,” through whom he made the worlds. The Son is “the reflection of God's glory and the exact imprint of God's very being”; having made purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. The child of Bethlehem is God's final and complete Word.

John the Baptist, in prison, sends disciples to ask Jesus: “Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?” Jesus does not answer with a title but with signs: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor hear good news, all fulfilling Isaiah. “And blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.” Even great saints have dark hours; Jesus answers them with his works.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews begins majestically: long ago God spoke to our ancestors in many and various ways by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom he also created the worlds (Hebrews 1:1–2).

God spoke through many prophets, each a partial word. In Jesus he speaks his full and final Word. The Son is the reflection of God's glory and the exact imprint of God's very being, and he sustains all things by his powerful word (Hebrews 1:3).

In the Gospel John the Baptist, from prison, sends his disciples to ask: are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another? Jesus answers: go and tell John what you have seen and heard: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news brought to them (Luke 7:19–22).

God has spoken his final Word in Jesus, and that Word acts: he heals, raises and brings good news to the poor. The signs of the Messiah are works of mercy.

At Yaldo we will see this Word made flesh as a child. The One who sustains the universe will be held in Mary's arms. St Ephrem sang of this wonder in his hymns of the Nativity.

During this fast, let us listen more to the Son: read the Gospel daily. And let us do the works of the Messiah: visit the sick, help the poor, bring good news. Then our lives will answer the question: yes, he has come. Amen.

For children

Children, God speaks to us through Jesus. When we read the Gospel, we hear Jesus speaking to us!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Son through whom the Father speaks, you answered John's doubt with signs of mercy. Answer our questions, open our eyes to your works, and keep us from taking offence at your ways. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask who Jesus is. Hebrews answers from the heights: he is the Son through whom God has spoken his last word. Luke answers from the road: John the Baptist, in prison, asks, “Are you the one who is to come?”, and Jesus points to what he is doing for the blind, the lame and the poor. The glory of the Son and his care for the lowly belong to one Person.

Hebrews 1:1–4 · God has spoken to us by a Son

Setting.

This single long and carefully built sentence in Greek opens the Letter to the Hebrews, a sermon written for tired Christians who were tempted to drift away. There is no greeting and no name of an author. The preacher begins with God speaking, and in four verses he says who Christ is before he says anything else.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2a: two times, two ways of speaking: long ago by the prophets, now by a Son.
  • vv. 2b–3: seven statements about the Son: heir, agent of creation, radiance of glory, imprint of God's being, sustainer of all, purifier from sin, seated at the right hand.
  • v. 4: he is greater than the angels, which the rest of the chapter will develop.

Key words.

  • polymerōs kai polytropōs, in many and various ways: Two rhythmic Greek adverbs: in many parts and in many manners. God's word in the prophets was true but given in pieces. The Son is not one more piece.
  • apaugasma, reflection: Brightness streaming out from a source of light. The Son is the light that shines from the Father, and is never apart from him, as the Creed says: “Light from Light” (CCC 241).
  • charaktēr, exact imprint: The mark a seal or a stamp leaves in wax or on a coin. The Son shows exactly what the Father is. The word translated “very being” is hypostasis, a term that later became important in the Church's teaching on the Trinity, though here it is not yet used in that later technical sense.

Verse by verse.

“Long ago God spoke to our ancestors in many and various ways by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son,” (1:1–2)

The Greek has no “the” here: literally “by a Son”. The contrast is between kinds of speakers. The prophets were servants who carried a message. This speaker is himself the message.

“He is the reflection of God’s glory and the exact imprint of God’s very being, and he sustains all things by his powerful word.” (1:3)

The one who holds up the universe is also the one who “made purification for sins”. Then he “sat down”, because that work is finished.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses to teach that Christ is the Father's one, perfect and final Word (CCC 65). In the same paragraph it gives the words of St John of the Cross: in giving us his Son, God spoke everything to us at once, and he has no more to say. The Church expects no new public revelation before the Lord's return (CCC 66).

Luke 7:18–23 · Are you the one who is to come?

Setting.

Herod has shut John in prison (Luke 3:20). John's disciples tell him “all these things”: just before this, Jesus has healed a centurion's servant and raised a widow's son at Nain (7:1–17). John had announced a stronger one who would come with fire and a winnowing fork (3:16–17). What he now hears is mercy, and he sends two disciples to ask.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–20: John's question, carried by two messengers.
  • v. 21: Jesus' answer begins in deeds.
  • vv. 22–23: the answer in words, with a beatitude at the end.

Key words.

  • ho erchomenos, the one who is to come: A way of speaking of the awaited one sent by God (compare Psalm 118:26; Malachi 3:1). John had used the same verb in 3:16.
  • skandalizō, take offense: To stumble over something placed in the path. Jesus knows that a Messiah of mercy, who leaves his herald in prison, can be a stumbling block for those who expect something else.

Verse by verse.

““Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?”” (7:19)

Did John himself doubt? St John Chrysostom held that he asked for the sake of his disciples, to send them to Jesus (in substance). Many modern commentators think the question is John's own, the honest question of a faithful man in a dark prison. Either way, it is a question brought to the right person.

“the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news brought to them.” (7:22)

Jesus does not say yes. He lets Isaiah answer (Isaiah 29:18; 35:5–6; 61:1). The list ends not with the raising of the dead but with the poor, as if that were the greatest sign.

“And blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.” (7:23)

This is a gentle word for John, and a word for all whose prayers seem to bring no release.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs of Jesus show that the Kingdom is present in him and attest that he is the promised Messiah, and that they can also become an occasion of offense (CCC 547–548). They are not given to satisfy curiosity but to call to faith. Good news for the poor is a mark of the Kingdom (CCC 544).

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews says God has spoken “by a Son”. How much of my day is shaped by listening to him?
  • What question would I send to Jesus from my own prison, and where might he be answering it already?
