Word for the day

“Whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me. Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” Matthew 10:38–39

Reflection

On the morning of Pentecost Peter stands up and explains what the crowds are seeing: it is the fulfilment of Joel: “In the last days it will be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh, and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy… Then everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” The Spirit is not reserved to a few; he is poured out on young and old, men and women, all who call on the Lord.

St Paul tells us what this means for daily life: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set you free from the law of sin and of death… If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will give life to your mortal bodies also through his Spirit that dwells in you.” The Spirit who raised Jesus lives in us.

In the evening Gospel Jesus spends the night in prayer on the mountain, then chooses the Twelve and proclaims: “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh.” The Beatitudes are the portrait of a life led by the Spirit, often hidden, often poor, always blessed.

Leviticus gives the deepest command: “You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy.” Jesus shows the way: “Whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me… whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple… none of these will lose their reward.” Holiness is the Cross carried with love, and the small cup of water given in Christ's name.

Homily outline

Theme: “Called after a night of prayer.”

Jesus spends the night in prayer, chooses the Twelve, and proclaims the Beatitudes. The Spirit poured out at Pentecost makes this way of life possible for all of us: chosen, blessed, and called to holiness.

1. Chosen after prayer

  • Jesus spends the whole night in prayer before choosing the apostles.
  • He calls each by name and names them apostles, “sent ones”.
  • As God was with David, so he is with those he calls.
“And David became greater and greater, for the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him.” 2 Samuel 5:10
“Now during those days he went out to the mountain to pray; and he spent the night in prayer to God. And when day came, he called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles.” Luke 6:12–13

Apply: Before an important decision this week, spend extra time in prayer.

2. The happiness of the kingdom

  • Jesus calls the poor, the hungry and those who weep “blessed”.
  • Their hope is in the kingdom that God promised, a kingdom that will never be destroyed.
  • The Beatitudes turn the world's values upside down.
“For he is the living God, enduring forever. His kingdom shall never be destroyed, and his dominion has no end.” Daniel 6:26
“Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh.” Luke 6:20–21

Apply: Thank God for one blessing hidden in a present difficulty.

3. Be holy: the cross and the cup of water

  • God's command to Israel: “You shall be holy, for I am holy.”
  • Holiness means taking up the cross and following Jesus.
  • It also means small kindnesses: even a cup of cold water given in his name.
“You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy.” Leviticus 19:2
“Whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple—truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward.” Matthew 10:42

Apply: Do one hidden act of kindness each day this week.

Prayer, poverty of spirit and humble love are the marks of those whom Christ calls.

Let the Spirit of Pentecost make us holy in great crosses and small kindnesses.

Take-home resolution: Read the Beatitudes (Luke 6:20–26) each morning this week.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on the day of Pentecost Peter stood up and said: this is what the prophet Joel foretold: “I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh… everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” Not only on priests and prophets, but on sons and daughters, old and young. On you.

And what does the Spirit do in us? St Paul says: “There is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. The law of the Spirit of life has set you free.” The same Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead lives in us and will raise our mortal bodies. We are not slaves of sin; we are temples of the Spirit.

In the Gospel Jesus prays all night, chooses the Twelve, and then looks at his disciples and says: Blessed are you who are poor, blessed are you who are hungry, blessed are you who weep. This is the strange happiness of the Spirit: not the happiness of the rich and comfortable, but of those who trust God.

God says in Leviticus: “You shall be holy, for I am holy.” Holiness is not only for saints in pictures. Jesus shows two ways: to take up the cross and follow him, and to give a cup of cold water to a little one in his name. Big crosses and small kindnesses. Let us do one hidden act of kindness each day this week. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus once prayed all night long! And in the morning he chose twelve friends to be his apostles.

Then he said: “Happy are the poor, happy are the hungry, happy are those who cry, because God will take care of them.”

Jesus also said that even giving a cup of water in his name is precious. What kind thing can you do today?

Prayer

Spirit of life, poured out on all flesh, set us free from sin and death and make us holy as the Father is holy. Teach us the joy of the Beatitudes, give us strength to carry the cross, and let no cup of water given in Christ's name be forgotten. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Second Sunday after Pentecost the readings show what the Spirit does with those whom God calls. The evening Gospel (Luke 6:12–23) tells how Jesus spent the night in prayer and then chose the Twelve, and how he proclaimed the Beatitudes: “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God.” In 2 Samuel 5:1–10 the tribes come to David at Hebron and recall the Lord's word to him: “It is you who shall be shepherd of my people Israel.” In Baruch 1:2–8 the exiles in Babylon weep, fast and pray, and send gifts to Jerusalem. In Daniel 6:25–28, after Daniel has come safely out of the lions' den, King Darius confesses the God of Daniel, “the living God”, whose kingdom shall never be destroyed. In Acts 2:14–21 Peter explains Pentecost with the words of Joel: “I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh.”

We study three readings more closely. Leviticus gives the great command, “You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy”, and shows that holiness reaches from the family table to the altar. Paul tells the Romans that there is no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus, because the Spirit of the one who raised Jesus now dwells in them. In Matthew, Jesus ends his sending of the Twelve with the cost and the reward of discipleship: whoever loses his life for his sake will find it, and even a cup of cold water given to a disciple will not lose its reward. The key words are holy, Spirit and welcome.

Leviticus 19:1–8 · You shall be holy, for I am holy

Setting.

Scholars call chapters 17–26 of Leviticus the Holiness Code, because of the refrain that runs through them: be holy, for I the Lord am holy. Leviticus took its final form, many scholars think, among the priests of Jerusalem, drawing on older laws. In the book itself these words are given by God to Moses at Sinai, for the whole people.

Chapter 19 is a remarkable mixture. Worship and daily life sit side by side: honour for parents, the sabbath, the ban on idols, the rules for sacrifice, care for the poor at harvest, honest weights, respect for the deaf and the blind, and at its centre the commandment “you shall love your neighbor as yourself” (19:18), which Jesus calls the second great commandment (Mark 12:31). For Leviticus, holiness is not one department of life. It is the whole of it.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the command to the whole congregation: be holy, because God is holy.
  • v. 3: reverence for mother and father; keeping the sabbath.
  • v. 4: no idols, no cast images.
  • vv. 5–8: the sacrifice of well-being: eaten on the first or second day, burned on the third.
  • ¶ Verses 3 and 4 end with the signature “I am the Lord your God”, and verse 2 with “I the Lord your God am holy”.

Key words.

  • qādôš, holy: The root idea is “set apart”, belonging to God. Only God is holy in himself. Israel becomes holy by belonging to him and living in a way that shows it.
  • tîrā’û, you shall revere: The Hebrew verb is the one used for the fear of God. Respect for parents is almost a religious duty. Unusually, the mother is named first, unlike the commandment in Exodus 20:12.
  • zevaḥ šᵉlāmîm, sacrifice of well-being: Also called the peace offering. Part of it was burned on the altar, and the rest was eaten by the worshippers in a sacred meal. It was the sacrifice of communion: God and his people sharing one table.

Verse by verse.

“You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy.” (19:2)

Holiness is not first a moral achievement. It is a share in what God is. St Peter quotes this verse to the Christians of Asia Minor: “be holy yourselves in all your conduct” (1 Peter 1:15–16).

“You shall each revere your mother and father, and you shall keep my sabbaths:” (19:3)

The first concrete sign of holiness is the home. The family and the day of rest are where reverence for God is first learned.

“When you offer a sacrifice of well-being to the Lord, offer it in such a way that it is acceptable in your behalf.” (19:5)

The worshipper is responsible for how he worships. The gift must be offered in the way God has given, not in a way of our own invention.

“It shall be eaten on the same day you offer it, or on the next day; and anything left over until the third day shall be consumed in fire.” (19:6)

The same rule is given in Leviticus 7:16–18. Many commentators think it kept the holy meal close to the offering and stopped sacred food from being treated as common. Verse 7 calls meat eaten on the third day piggûl, something spoiled and unclean.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in Leviticus 19:2 the universal call to holiness. The Second Vatican Council teaches that all the faithful, of whatever state or rank, are called to the fullness of Christian life and the perfection of charity (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013). Christian readers have also seen in the sacrifice of well-being, offered to God and then shared at a holy meal, a distant sign of the Holy Qurbono, where the one sacrifice of Christ becomes the table of communion. The reverence that Leviticus demands for holy food teaches us how to approach the Body and Blood of the Lord.

Romans 8:1–11 · No condemnation for those in Christ Jesus

Setting.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth around AD 57–58, to a church he had not yet visited. Chapter 7 ends with the cry of a person who knows God's law but cannot keep it: “Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?” and the answer, “Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (7:24–25). Chapter 8 unfolds that answer.

The word “Spirit” occurs about twenty times in chapter 8, more than in any other chapter of Paul. After the great feast of Pentecost, the Church reads this chapter to learn what life in the Spirit means day by day.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: no condemnation: God has done in his Son what the law could not do.
  • vv. 5–8: two ways of setting the mind: on the flesh, which leads to death, or on the Spirit, which is life and peace.
  • vv. 9–11: the Spirit dwells in you, and the one who raised Jesus will give life to your mortal bodies.

Key words.

  • katakrima, condemnation: A legal word: the sentence passed on the guilty, with its penalty. Paul does not say there is no sin. He says the sentence has been lifted for those who are in Christ.
  • sarx, flesh: Not the body as such, and not sexuality only. In Paul it means human nature closed in on itself, relying on its own strength apart from God. The body itself is promised resurrection in verse 11.
  • phronēma, the mind that is set: The direction of the whole person: what we think about, desire and pursue. Life according to the Spirit is a matter of where the mind and heart are turned.

Verse by verse.

“There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” (8:1)

“Now” is decisive. Something has changed in history, through the cross and resurrection, and in the believer, through Baptism.

“For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set you free from the law of sin and of death.” (8:2)

The “you” is singular in the best manuscripts; some read “me”. The POC keeps the singular “you”. Paul speaks to each believer personally.

“by sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and to deal with sin, he condemned sin in the flesh,” (8:3)

“To deal with sin” renders the Greek peri hamartias, the phrase the Greek Old Testament uses for the sin offering in Leviticus. The NRSV notes the other possible rendering, “as a sin offering”. Christ is the true offering that the old sacrifices pointed to.

“If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ from the dead will give life to your mortal bodies also through his Spirit that dwells in you.” (8:11)

The whole Trinity is in this verse: the Father who raises, the Son who was raised, the Spirit who dwells in us. The hope is not only for the soul but for the body.

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent took up verse 1 to teach that in those who are reborn in Baptism God hates nothing, and that there is no condemnation for those truly buried with Christ, even though an inclination to sin, called concupiscence, remains in them for the struggle (Decree on Original Sin, 5; see CCC 1264). The Catechism quotes verse 11 when it teaches the resurrection of the body: God will give life to our mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in us (CCC 989). Pentecost is therefore not only a feast of the past. The same Spirit lives in every baptized person.

Matthew 10:38–42 · Whoever welcomes you welcomes me

Setting.

Matthew arranges the teaching of Jesus in five great discourses. Chapter 10 is the second, the missionary discourse. Jesus names the Twelve (10:1–4), sends them first to “the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:6), and warns them of persecution, of division even within families, and of the cost of following him (10:16–37). Our verses close the discourse; the next verse says, “Now when Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples…” (11:1).

The passage moves from the cost of discipleship to the reward of those who receive disciples. Behind verse 40 stands a principle well known in Judaism and later written down in the Mishnah: one who is sent is like the one who sends him.

Structure.

  • v. 38: taking up the cross.
  • v. 39: finding and losing life.
  • v. 40: to welcome the disciple is to welcome Christ, and the Father.
  • vv. 41–42: rewards: for welcoming a prophet, a righteous person, or one of the little ones.

Key words.

  • psychē, life: The Greek word can mean life, soul or self. The one who clings to his own life as a possession loses it; the one who spends it for Christ receives it back, now and in eternity.
  • dechomai, welcome, receive: The word for receiving a guest. It occurs four times in verse 40 alone. The mission of the Twelve depends on homes that open their doors.
  • mikroi, little ones: Here the disciples themselves, the humble and unimportant messengers of Christ (compare 18:6). The smallest kindness to them is counted as done to him.

Verse by verse.

“and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” (10:38)

This is the first time Matthew's Gospel mentions the cross. The disciples do not yet know how Jesus will die; Matthew's readers do.

“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” (10:39)

“For my sake” is the key. Jesus does not ask for loss for its own sake but for love of him. This saying is found in all four Gospels in some form (Mark 8:35; Luke 9:24; John 12:25).

“Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” (10:40)

A chain of sending: the Father sends the Son, the Son sends the apostles. To welcome the messenger is to welcome God himself.

“and whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple” (10:42)

Cold water in a hot land is the simplest gift there is. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this verse, points out that it costs nothing, so that no one can excuse himself on grounds of poverty (Homilies on Matthew, in substance).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus is the Father's emissary and that he sends his apostles as the Father sent him, so that whoever receives them receives him (CCC 858). It also teaches that Jesus calls his disciples to take up their cross and follow him, because he wants to associate with his redeeming sacrifice those who are its first beneficiaries (CCC 618). These verses explain why the Church has always honoured those who welcome her missionaries and pastors: the family that houses a priest, the parish that supports a seminarian, the parents who give a son or daughter to God, all share in the reward of the one they welcome.

Questions people ask

If there is “no condemnation” for those in Christ, can a baptized Christian still be lost?

Paul's promise is for those who remain “in Christ”. In the same chapter he warns: “if you live according to the flesh, you will die” (8:13). The Church teaches that grave sin freely chosen can destroy charity and the life of grace in the soul (CCC 1861), and that Christ gave the sacrament of Reconciliation for those who fall after Baptism (CCC 1446). Our assurance is hope, not presumption: we trust in God's faithfulness and keep close to him (CCC 2005).

Why does Leviticus put a rule about leftover sacrificial meat next to “You shall be holy”?

Because for Leviticus holiness touches everything: the home, the sabbath, the altar and the table. The rule teaches that what is given to God is not to be handled carelessly. Christians apply the same lesson to the Holy Qurbono, approaching the Holy Mysteries with faith, reverence and a prepared heart.

Does “losing one's life” mean that Christians should look for suffering or death?

No. Jesus asks us to give our lives for his sake, not to seek pain for its own sake. The early Church honoured the martyrs but did not praise those who rushed to give themselves up; the account of St Polycarp's death says so plainly (Martyrdom of Polycarp 4). For most Christians, losing one's life for Christ means the daily gift of self: in a family, in service, in a vocation faithfully lived.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church keeps this Second Sunday after Pentecost as Vocations Day. In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost unfold the life of the Church that the Spirit has gathered, and today's readings fit the day closely: Jesus prays all night before he chooses the Twelve; David is called to be shepherd of God's people; the Spirit is poured out on sons and daughters, young and old; Titus, in yesterday's Epistle, is told to appoint elders in every town. Every vocation, to the priesthood, to religious life, to marriage and to mission, begins with God's call and is carried by the prayer of the Church. Jesus himself told us what to do: “ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest” (Matthew 9:38). Today every family is invited to pray for vocations by name, and to welcome those who answer, as the Gospel says, with at least a cup of cold water.

For study and teaching

  • Leviticus says holiness begins with reverence for mother and father and with the sabbath. What would make my home and my Sunday more holy?
  • Paul says there is no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus. Is there an old guilt I still carry that I need to bring to the sacrament of Reconciliation and then leave behind?
  • Whom has the Lord sent to me, and how have I welcomed them: priests, religious, catechists, or simply people in need?
  • On this Vocations Day, whom can I encourage by name to listen to God's call, and how can our family support a vocation in our parish?
