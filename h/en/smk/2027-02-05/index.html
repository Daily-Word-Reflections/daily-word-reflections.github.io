Word for the day

“If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” John 15:18

Reflection

Paul looks back at Israel in the wilderness. They had every gift: “our ancestors were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea… and all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank from the spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ.” Yet “God was not pleased with most of them.” They desired evil, worshipped idols, tested the Lord and grumbled. “These things happened to them to serve as an example, and they were written down to instruct us.” Baptism and the Eucharist are not a charm that saves us without our cooperation. “So if you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall.” And at once Paul adds the comfort: “God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out so that you may be able to endure it.”

In the Gospel Jesus prepares his friends for another kind of testing, the hostility of the world: “If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you… Servants are not greater than their master. If they persecuted me, they will persecute you.” The disciples are hated not for their faults but because they belong to him: “I have chosen you out of the world.” On this Friday, the day of the Cross, we remember Christians in India and across the world who suffer for the name of Jesus. As the Great Fast approaches, Paul's warning and Jesus' promise belong together: watch, because you can fall; take heart, because God is faithful and Christ has gone ahead of you. St John Chrysostom says that Paul names the way out together with the trial, so that no one may despair (in substance).

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul reminds us that Israel passed through the sea, ate the manna and drank from the rock, and the rock was Christ. Yet most of them fell in the wilderness (1 Corinthians 10:4–5).

These things were written down to instruct us. So if you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall (1 Corinthians 10:11–12). Grace is not a charm; it calls for watchfulness.

But Paul adds at once: God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out (1 Corinthians 10:13).

Jesus tells us not to be surprised if the world hates us: it hated him first (John 15:18). On this Friday, let us pray for persecuted Christians, and enter the coming Fast watchful and full of trust. Amen.

For children

Children, sometimes it is hard to do what is right. But God is faithful! He never gives us a test that is too big, and he always shows us a way out. Ask him for help when you are tempted.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you were hated without a cause and you went before us on the way of the Cross. Keep us watchful so that we do not fall, and faithful when we are tested. Strengthen all who suffer for your name today, and give them the way out that you have promised. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two warnings for a Church about to begin its Fast. Paul reminds the Corinthians that a people can receive every gift of God and still fall, and he adds that God is faithful in every testing. Jesus tells his disciples not to be surprised if the world does not love them. Neither reading frightens for its own sake: both end with the Lord's faithfulness to his own.

1 Corinthians 10:1–13 · Written down to instruct us

Setting.

Paul writes from Ephesus (16:8), probably about the year 54 or 55, to a Church in a great port city. In chapters 8–10 he answers a practical question: may Christians eat food offered to idols, and go to meals in temples? Some in Corinth felt that their Baptism and the Eucharist made them safe. So Paul retells the story of Israel in the desert. His own people, he says, had their “baptism” and their “spiritual food”, and yet many fell.

He calls them “our ancestors” even when writing to many who were Gentiles. Israel's story is the family story of all Christians.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: all received the gifts: cloud, sea, food, drink, rock. The word “all” comes five times.
  • v. 5: but most of them fell in the wilderness.
  • vv. 6–10: four warnings, from Exodus and Numbers: idolatry, immorality, testing the Lord, complaining.
  • vv. 11–12: this was written for us; watch out.
  • v. 13: God's faithfulness in testing.

Key words.

  • typos, example, type: An impression left by a seal, then a pattern. From this word comes the Christian reading of the Old Testament called typology, in which events foreshadow Christ.
  • peirasmos, testing: Paul uses the noun and the verb for both the desert and present life. It can mean both a trial from outside and a temptation from within.
  • ekbasis, the way out: A way through, like a pass out of a narrow valley. God does not always remove the test, but he makes a path.

Verse by verse.

“and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank from the spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ.” (10:4)

Exodus and Numbers tell of water from a rock (Exodus 17:6; Numbers 20:11). Some Jewish writings of the time knew a tradition of a rock or a well that went along with Israel. Paul takes it up and says who was really giving them drink.

“We must not put Christ to the test, as some of them did, and were destroyed by serpents.” (10:9)

A note on the text. Manuscripts differ here. Some have “Christ”, as the NRSV does; others have “the Lord”, as the Malayalam POC does. The story is in Numbers 21:4–9.

“No testing has overtaken you that is not common to everyone. God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out so that you may be able to endure it.” (10:13)

After these warnings comes this word of comfort. Our trial is not unique, and we do not face it alone. What is promised is not the absence of the trial, but the power to endure it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws on this chapter when it teaches the spiritual sense of Scripture: the crossing of the Red Sea is a sign of Christ's victory and of Christian Baptism (CCC 117; see also 128–129). It also teaches: “What God commands he makes possible by his grace” (CCC 2082). St Augustine says God does not command the impossible, but in commanding he tells you to do what you can, and to ask for what you cannot (in substance), words which the Council of Trent took up.

John 15:18–25 · If the world hates you

Setting.

We are at the table on the night before the Passion. Jesus has just said, “love one another” (15:17), and now he speaks of what love will meet outside. In John, “the world” (kosmos) can mean what God so loves (3:16). Here it means human life when it is closed against God.

A word on “they”, and on “their law” in v. 25. Jesus and his first disciples were Jews, and these words are not about the Jewish people. Many scholars think that John's wording also reflects painful later disputes between his community and synagogue leaders. The Church teaches that the Jews are not to be held responsible, as a people, for the death of Jesus (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). This passage speaks of anyone, in any time, who shuts out the light.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19: the world's hatred, and the reason for it: you were chosen out of it.
  • vv. 20–21: the servant shares the master's lot.
  • vv. 22–24: having seen and heard, they have no excuse.
  • v. 25: the Scripture is fulfilled.

Key words.

  • kosmos, world: One of John's great words. God loves the world and sends his Son to save it (3:16–17); here it is the world as a system closed to God. So a disciple is sent into the world, and does not belong to it (17:14–18).
  • miseō, to hate: The word comes seven times in these eight verses. Jesus says it plainly, so that it will not take his disciples by surprise (16:1, 4).
  • dōrean, without a cause: “For nothing”, the same word used elsewhere for a gift given without charge. It is the sad twin of grace. Grace loves us for nothing; here people hate him for nothing.

Verse by verse.

““If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” (15:18)

This is a comfort, not a threat. The disciple who is opposed is standing where the Lord stood.

“If they persecuted me, they will persecute you; if they kept my word, they will keep yours also.” (15:20)

Both halves count. Some will reject the witness, and some will receive it. The apostle's word has the same fate as his Master's.

“It was to fulfill the word that is written in their law, ‘They hated me without a cause.’” (15:25)

The phrase comes from the Psalms (35:19; 69:4). “Law” here means Scripture as a whole. Psalm 69 is one of the Psalms the New Testament most often applies to the Passion.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council says that some Christians have been called, and will always be called, to give the supreme witness of love before all people and especially before persecutors (Lumen Gentium 42). St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to die in Rome, wrote that Christianity shows its greatness most when it is hated by the world (in substance; Letter to the Romans 3). In our day, too, we remember all who suffer for Christ in many lands, and we pray for them and for those who have wronged them.

Questions people ask

Paul says twenty-three thousand fell. Numbers says twenty-four thousand. Why the difference?

Paul seems to be recalling Numbers 25:9, which has “twenty-four thousand”. Interpreters offer several explanations: a slip of memory in dictation, a round figure, or a blend with another verse. The Church's teaching on the truth of Scripture does not depend on such figures. It looks at what God wished to teach for our salvation (Dei Verbum 11). Paul's point is not arithmetic, but that privilege does not protect from sin.

For study and teaching

  • “If you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall.” Where do I feel most safe from sin, and might that be the very place to watch?
  • Have I ever been laughed at or left out because of my faith? How does John 15:18 change the way I carry that?
