Word for the day

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Hebrews 13:8

Reflection

Hebrews asks us to remember our leaders, those who spoke the word of God to us, and to imitate their faith. Today the Church remembers the bishops of the Council of Nicaea (AD 325), whom tradition numbers as 318. They confessed the Son “one in being with the Father”, the faith we still sing in the Creed. Their teaching stands because Christ himself does not change: “the same yesterday and today and forever.”

In the Gospel Jesus praises the faithful and wise servant whom the master puts in charge of his household “to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time.” That is the work of every shepherd in the Church, and of every parent at home: to feed others faithfully, not to lord it over them, because the master may come at any hour.

Full homily

Dear friends, Hebrews asks us to remember our leaders, those who spoke the word of God to us, and to imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and for ever (Hebrews 13:7–8). Today the Church remembers the Fathers of the Council of Nicaea (AD 325), whom tradition numbers as 318. They confessed that the Son is of one being with the Father; the Creed we pray at every Holy Qurbono comes from them.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks of the faithful and wise servant whom the master puts in charge of his household: blessed is that servant whom his master will find at work when he arrives (Matthew 24:45–46). The Fathers of Nicaea were such servants; many bore in their bodies the wounds of persecution.

Faith is both an inheritance and a responsibility. This week pray the Creed slowly, meditating on its meaning, and explain one line of it to your children. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church remembers the 318 Fathers of the Council of Nicaea, and the readings fit the day. Hebrews tells us to remember the leaders who spoke God's word to us and to imitate their faith, because “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” In the Gospel, the faithful servant is the one who feeds the household at the proper time, until the master comes.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · Remember your leaders; Christ does not change

Setting.

Chapter 13 closes the long sermon of Hebrews with practical counsel: love, hospitality, marriage, money, and the community's leaders. The “leaders” of v. 7 seem to be those who first preached to them and have already died, perhaps as martyrs, since the readers are told to consider “the outcome” of their life. The living leaders are mentioned in v. 17.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: remember your leaders; Christ is unchanging.
  • vv. 9–10: do not be carried away by strange teachings; “we have an altar”.
  • vv. 11–14: as the sin offerings were burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27), Jesus suffered outside the city gate; let us go out to him.
  • vv. 15–16: the sacrifices Christians offer: praise, doing good, sharing.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: Those who guide and go ahead. The same word is used for the leaders who are still alive in vv. 17 and 24.
  • thysiastērion, altar: “We have an altar” (v. 10). Many interpreters see here the cross of Christ; Catholic tradition has also seen the Eucharistic table, where we eat of the one sacrifice.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: An Old Testament phrase (Psalm 50:14, 23 in the Greek). Hebrews adds “the fruit of lips” from Hosea 14:2.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

Christians are asked not only to remember their teachers but to look at how their lives ended, and to copy their faith, not merely their words.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

Leaders pass; Christ remains. This is why the faith confessed at Nicaea is still our faith: it is faith in the same Christ.

“Let us then go to him outside the camp and bear the abuse he endured.” (13:13)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists “the sacrifice of praise” (Hebrews 13:15) among the names of the Eucharist, which makes present the one sacrifice of Christ (CCC 1330). It also teaches that the bishops, as successors of the apostles, hand on the faith received, so that the Church remains in the teaching of the apostles (CCC 77, 857).

Matthew 24:45–51 · The faithful and wise servant

Setting.

This short parable belongs to Jesus' last discourse about the end of the age (Matthew 24–25). The question is not when the master will come, but what he will find his servant doing when he comes. Luke has the same parable and adds that Peter asked, “Lord, are you telling this parable for us or for everyone?” (Luke 12:41).

Structure.

  • v. 45: the question: who is the faithful and wise servant?
  • vv. 46–47: the blessing of the one found at work.
  • vv. 48–49: the wicked servant who says, “My master is delayed.”
  • vv. 50–51: the master's sudden coming and the servant's ruin.

Key words.

  • pistos kai phronimos, faithful and wise: Faithful toward the master, wise in caring for the household. The same word “wise” describes the wise bridesmaids (25:2) and the man who built on rock (7:24).
  • chronizei, is delayed: The servant's mistake is to treat delay as absence. The Lord's patience is not a sign that he will not come.
  • dichotomēsei, will cut in pieces: A deliberately shocking image of total punishment, which many scholars take as “cut off” from the household. The point is the seriousness of a trust betrayed.

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” (24:45)

The servant is a servant among servants. Authority in the household is for feeding others, not for ruling them.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)
“But if that wicked slave says to himself, ‘My master is delayed,’” (24:48)

The fall begins in the heart, with a private sentence: there is plenty of time. Then come violence toward others and self-indulgence (v. 49).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this parable first for her pastors, and then for every Christian who has been given others to care for. The Catechism teaches that ministry in the Church is service: those who receive it become “slaves of Christ” for the sake of the people (CCC 876). It also recalls the Council's call to watch constantly, since we know neither the day nor the hour (CCC 1036; Lumen Gentium 48).

Questions people ask

Who were the 318 Fathers?

The bishops who met at Nicaea in AD 325, the first ecumenical council, and confessed that the Son is “one in being with the Father” against the teaching of Arius. The number 318 is the traditional count; St Ambrose noted that it is also the number of Abraham's servants in Genesis 14:14 (in substance). Their Creed, completed at Constantinople in 381, is the one we profess today.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the Holy Qurbono the Malankara Church professes the Nicene Creed, the faith of the 318 Fathers as completed at Constantinople. When we say “one in being with the Father”, we stand with Nicaea and with the faithful leaders whom Hebrews tells us to remember.

For study and teaching

  • Who first spoke the word of God to me? How can I imitate their faith, and not only remember them?
  • In my home, parish or work, whom has the Lord put in my care “to give them their food at the proper time”?
