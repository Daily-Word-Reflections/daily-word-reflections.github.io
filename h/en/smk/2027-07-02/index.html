Word for the day

“so that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father.” John 10:38

Reflection

Today is the First Friday of the month. Every Friday recalls the Cross, and many Catholic parishes give the first Friday of each month to special prayer, the Holy Qurbono and devotion to the Heart of Jesus, the heart that loved us to the end. Today's Epistle shows what such a heart looks like in those who follow him. St Paul writes to Corinth about the churches of Macedonia, the communities of Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea, the first that he founded on European soil. They were small, poor, and already suffering. And Paul says of them: “for during a severe ordeal of affliction, their abundant joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part.” Joy and poverty side by side, and out of them a flood of giving. They “voluntarily gave according to their means, and even beyond their means.” Usually it is the collector who has to beg. Here the givers do the begging: they were “begging us earnestly for the privilege of sharing in this ministry to the saints.”

Something more precious than money is named in the next verse: “they gave themselves first to the Lord and, by the will of God, to us.” The gift of self comes first and the gift of money follows from it. A parish that has given itself to the Lord is never short of gifts; a parish that gives only money, without itself, soon finds that even the money is hard to find. Paul does not command. He appeals to their pride in what is best in them: “Now as you excel in everything, in faith, in speech, in knowledge, in utmost eagerness, and in our love for you, so we want you to excel also in this generous undertaking.” A few verses later he gives the reason that stands behind all Christian giving: “For you know the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich” (2 Corinthians 8:9). Jesus gave himself first. The First Friday keeps this before our eyes: the Heart that was pierced on a Friday gave everything.

The Gospel carries us to the Temple at Jerusalem, in winter, at the Feast of the Dedication (John 10:22). Jesus has said, “The Father and I are one,” and some of his hearers pick up stones. When John writes “the Jews” in such a scene he means those in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus on that occasion; we must remember that Jesus himself, his Mother, the Apostles and the first Christians were all Jews, and the Church teaches that what happened in the Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews of that time, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 597). Jesus answers with calm: “I have shown you many good works from the Father. For which of these are you going to stone me?” The works are his credentials: the sick healed, the hungry fed, the sinner forgiven. Then he argues from their own Scripture: “Is it not written in your law, ‘I said, you are gods’?” If the Scripture could call those to whom the word of God came “gods”, can anyone say that “the one whom the Father has sanctified and sent into the world is blaspheming because I said, ‘I am God's Son’?” The Fathers of the Church, especially in the East, drew from this the great truth that the Son of God became human so that we might share in his own life: “the Son of God became man so that we might become God,” wrote St Athanasius (On the Incarnation 54, in substance; Catechism 460).

Jesus ends with an invitation: “If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me. But if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works, so that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father.” Believe the works. The same test is put to us. The Macedonians' joy in their poverty was such a work: a sign that Christ was alive in them. Few people in our days are argued into faith; many are drawn when they see a neighbour who gives more than he can afford and ask where that comes from. John says that they tried to arrest Jesus again “but he escaped from their hands.” His hour had not yet come. Later in the same discourse he says, “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:18), and on a Friday he did. Today we ask for a heart like his, and for works that point to him.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the First Friday, and we look to the Heart of Jesus, the Heart that gave everything. St Paul tells the Corinthians about the churches of Macedonia. They were poor and in affliction, and yet their abundant joy and their extreme poverty overflowed in a wealth of generosity (2 Corinthians 8:2). They gave according to their means, and even beyond their means, and they begged for the privilege of giving (2 Corinthians 8:3–4).

What was best of all is that they gave themselves first to the Lord (2 Corinthians 8:5). That is where all true giving begins. And Paul reminds us why: Jesus Christ, though he was rich, became poor for our sake, so that by his poverty we might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). He gave himself first.

In the Gospel Jesus is threatened with stones, and he answers calmly: I have shown you many good works from the Father; for which of these are you going to stone me? (John 10:32). He asks that people believe the works, so that they may know and understand that the Father is in him and he is in the Father (John 10:38). We too are to be known by our works. Many who do not read the Scriptures read our lives.

So today let us offer ourselves to the Lord first, and then let us give, with joy, even beyond our means, something that costs us. It will be a work that points to the Father. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know that today is the First Friday? We remember that Jesus loves us so much that he gave everything for us, even his life on the Cross. St Paul tells about some people who were very poor, but so happy in Jesus that they shared what little they had. And best of all, they gave themselves to the Lord. Today, do one kind thing without telling anyone: give a toy, help at home, or say a prayer for someone who is sad. That is how we show the Heart of Jesus to others.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, though you were rich, for our sake you became poor, so that by your poverty we might become rich. On this First Friday we look to your pierced Heart, which gave everything. Teach us to give ourselves first to you, and then whatever we have for the sake of your saints and your poor. Give us the joy of the churches of Macedonia, who found in their poverty a wealth of generosity. Let our works be so full of your love that those who see them may believe that you are in the Father and the Father is in you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what proves that something comes from God. In Corinthians the proof is a gift: the poor churches of Macedonia, in their affliction, “gave themselves first to the Lord”, and their giving overflowed. In John, when Jesus is accused of blasphemy, he points to his works: if they are the Father's works, believe them, “so that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father.” In both, what is real shows itself in what is done. The key words are grace and works.

2 Corinthians 8:1–7 · They gave themselves first to the Lord

Setting.

Paul is raising a collection among his Gentile churches for the poor Christians of Jerusalem, called “the saints” (Romans 15:25–27; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4; Galatians 2:10). It is a sign of the unity of the Church: Gentile believers share their goods with the mother Church from which the gospel came. In 2 Corinthians 8–9 he writes to Corinth, which had begun the collection a year earlier (8:10), to help them finish it.

He writes from Macedonia, probably in AD 55–56. The churches of Macedonia, at Philippi, Thessalonica and Beroea, were themselves poor and persecuted. Paul holds them up as an example, not to shame the Corinthians but to move them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: God's grace in Macedonia: joy and poverty overflow in generosity.
  • vv. 3–5: they gave willingly, beyond their means, and begged to take part; first they gave themselves.
  • v. 6: Titus is asked to complete the work at Corinth.
  • v. 7: Paul asks the Corinthians, who excel in everything else, to excel in this too.

Key words.

  • charis, grace: Paul begins by calling the Macedonians' gift “the grace of God” given to them. In his eyes generosity is not first a human achievement but a gift of God working in people. The same word runs through chapters 8 and 9, meaning grace, gift, favour and thanks.
  • koinōnia kai diakonia, sharing and ministry: The collection is called a “sharing” (communion) and a “service”. These are words of the Church's life, used also of the Eucharist and of the ministry of deacons. Giving to the poor is a part of the life of the Body of Christ, not an extra.
  • haplotēs, generosity: The word has the sense of a single, undivided heart: open-handed and without calculation. It is Paul's word for the gift that comes from a simple and wholehearted giver.

Verse by verse.

“their abundant joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part.” (8:2)

Joy and poverty together should not produce wealth, but here they do. The Macedonians are poor in money and rich in heart, a reversal Paul sees again and again in the Gospel.

“they voluntarily gave according to their means, and even beyond their means,” (8:3)

The measure of giving is not only what is left over. Paul never forces the gift; he appeals to love and to a willing heart (8:8).

“they gave themselves first to the Lord and, by the will of God, to us,” (8:5)

The order is the heart of the passage: first the person, then the gift. Money given without the giver is only money; money given with the giver is love.

“so we want you to excel also in this generous undertaking.” (8:7)

Paul lists the Corinthians' gifts: faith, speech, knowledge, eagerness, love. To these he adds one more: generosity to those in need.

How the Church reads it.

From the very beginning, the Catechism recalls, Christians have brought with the bread and wine of the Eucharist gifts to share with those in need, and the collection of the Church continues Paul's custom (CCC 1351). Giving to the poor is part of the Church's worship and not only her charity. Because the gift in this passage begins with the grace of God, the Church can teach that she is called to love the poor with a love that comes from Christ.

John 10:31–39 · Believe the works

Setting.

The scene takes place in Jerusalem at the Feast of the Dedication (10:22), a winter feast that recalls the cleansing and rededication of the temple under Judas Maccabeus in 164 BC (1 Maccabees 4:36–59). Jesus is walking in the portico of Solomon. The people there press him: if he is the Messiah, let him say so plainly (10:24). He answers that his works testify for him, and he says, “The Father and I are one” (10:30). Today's verses follow at once.

In John “the Jews” in these scenes means those among the leaders and the crowd in Jerusalem who opposed him. Jesus, his mother and his disciples were all Jews, and Jesus turns to the Law and the Psalms of his own people to answer them.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–33: they take up stones; Jesus asks for which good work; they answer: blasphemy, because he makes himself God.
  • vv. 34–36: Jesus answers from Scripture: if the word of God calls human beings “gods”, can the one sanctified and sent by the Father be accused of blasphemy?
  • vv. 37–38: believe the works, so that you may know that the Father is in him and he in the Father.
  • v. 39: they try to arrest him, but he escapes.

Key words.

  • erga, works: In John the “works” of Jesus are his miracles seen as signs and, more deeply, everything the Father gives him to do (5:36; 14:10–11). They are not only wonders; they show the Father at work.
  • hēgiasen, sanctified: To make holy, to set apart for God. It is a word used in the Greek Old Testament for consecrating the temple and its vessels, and Jesus speaks it at the feast of the Dedication. Jesus is the one consecrated by the Father and sent into the world, the true temple.
  • ginōskete, know: In John, to know is not only to understand a fact but to enter into a relationship. Jesus wants them to know the Father through the Son.

Verse by verse.

“I have shown you many good works from the Father. For which of these are you going to stone me?” (10:32)

Jesus does not argue first; he asks. His good works, the healing of the blind man, the giving of life, speak before any claim.

“Is it not written in your law, ‘I said, you are gods’?” (10:34)

Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6. In the psalm God addresses the judges of Israel, to whom the word of God was entrusted, and calls them “gods”, a title of dignity. Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater: if the Scripture can use that name of those who received the word, how much more of him whom the Father sent.

“and the scripture cannot be annulled” (10:35)

A strong statement about Scripture: it cannot be set aside. Jesus treats the Scriptures of Israel as the word of God.

“the one whom the Father has sanctified and sent into the world” (10:36)

Jesus does not weaken his claim; he deepens it. He is the Son whom the Father set apart and sent, and the word “sent” runs through the whole Gospel.

“believe the works, so that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father.” (10:38)

The works lead to faith in the Father and the Son, who dwell in each other. This is why the Church calls the miracles of Jesus signs.

“Then they tried to arrest him again, but he escaped from their hands.” (10:39)

His hour has not yet come (7:30; 8:20). When it comes, he will lay down his life of his own accord (10:17–18).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs worked by Jesus testify that the Father sent him; they invite faith in him, and they bear witness that he is the Son of God (CCC 548, in substance). The Church reads this passage as a revelation of the unity of the Father and the Son, and therefore of the divinity of Christ, which the Council of Nicaea later set out in the word “one in being with the Father”. At the same time, the Church notes how Jesus himself honours the Scriptures: he argues from the Psalms, and says that the Scripture cannot be annulled.

For study and teaching

  • The Macedonians gave themselves first to the Lord, and then their money. What would it mean for me to give myself, and not only something I own, to a person or a cause in need?
  • Jesus asks, “For which of these are you going to stone me?” When others misjudge me, do I let my good works speak, or do I argue back?
