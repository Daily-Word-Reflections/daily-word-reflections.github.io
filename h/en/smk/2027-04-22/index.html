Word for the day

“let us run with perseverance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus the pioneer and perfecter of our faith” Hebrews 12:1–2

Reflection

Three and a half weeks after Qyomtho, the joy of Easter has to learn to walk. The first rush of the feast is over; ordinary days have come back, with work, exams, illness and bills. The Letter to the Hebrews speaks to exactly this moment. It has just finished its long roll-call of the faithful of old, Abel and Noah, Abraham and Sarah, Moses and Rahab and the prophets, and now it turns to us: “Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight and the sin that clings so closely, and let us run with perseverance the race that is set before us.” The picture is a stadium. The runners are on the track, and the stands are full of those who have already finished. St John Chrysostom pictures the saints as spectators who once ran the same course and now cheer us on, so that we may not be ashamed to slacken before such a crowd (Homilies on Hebrews 28, in substance). In the Holy Qurbono our Church names them every time: the Mother of God, the prophets and apostles, the martyrs and confessors, the holy fathers and all the faithful departed. We never run alone.

But the runner does not keep looking at the stands. Hebrews tells us where to fix our eyes: “looking to Jesus the pioneer and perfecter of our faith, who for the sake of the joy that was set before him endured the cross, disregarding its shame, and has taken his seat at the right hand of the throne of God.” Jesus is the pioneer, the one who has already run the whole course, through the Cross and the tomb, and has broken the tape on Easter morning. He is also the perfecter: he will bring our faltering faith to its finish. “Consider him who endured such hostility against himself from sinners, so that you may not grow weary or lose heart.” The Gospel takes up the same danger of losing heart. “Then Jesus told them a parable about their need to pray always and not to lose heart.” A widow, with no money, no husband and no influence, keeps coming to a judge “who neither feared God nor had respect for people,” saying, “Grant me justice against my opponent.” In the end he gives in, only to be rid of her. Then the Lord draws the lesson from the lesser to the greater: if even this judge yields, “will not God grant justice to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night?” God is not the unjust judge; he is the Father. The point of the parable is not that we must wear God down, but that we must not let ourselves be worn down. St Augustine wrote to the widow Proba that to pray always is to keep desire for God alive in the heart without ceasing, and that our set times of prayer rekindle that desire when it cools (Letter 130, in substance).

Our Church gives us a rhythm for this persevering prayer: the seven times of prayer of the Shehimo, from the Evening prayer through the night to the Ninth Hour, so that the whole day is carried to God. Few families can pray all seven, but every family can keep two: the evening prayer together before supper and a short prayer on waking. Jesus ends the parable with a question that is meant for each of us: “And yet, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?” Faith is found in people who keep running and keep asking. If there is a prayer you stopped making because nothing seemed to happen, a child far from the Church, a marriage under strain, a healing not yet given, take it up again today. Bring it to the Holy Qurbono, where the risen Lord who finished the race gives himself as food for runners who are tired. And remember the cloud of witnesses: ask one saint you love to run beside you this week.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews pictures a great stadium. We are on the track, and the stands are full: since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight and the sin that clings so closely, and let us run with perseverance the race that is set before us (Hebrews 12:1).

Where do we look while we run? Looking to Jesus the pioneer and perfecter of our faith, who for the sake of the joy that was set before him endured the cross (Hebrews 12:2). He has already finished the course; on Easter morning he broke through the tape of death. Consider him, says Hebrews, so that you may not grow weary or lose heart (Hebrews 12:3).

In the Gospel Jesus tells a parable about our need to pray always and not to lose heart (Luke 18:1). A poor widow keeps coming to a judge who fears neither God nor people, until he gives her justice just to be rid of her. If even he gives in, will not God grant justice to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night? (Luke 18:7). Our God is not that judge. He is our Father.

Jesus ends with a question: when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth? (Luke 18:8). Let us answer it with our lives. Take up again the prayer you dropped. Keep the evening prayer at home. Come to the Holy Qurbono for strength, and run with the saints. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Have you ever run in a race at school sports day? When you are tired, the people cheering help you keep going. The Bible says the saints in heaven are like a big crowd cheering for us. And Jesus is at the finish line! Jesus also told a story about a lady who kept asking and asking until she got help. He wants us to keep praying and never give up. What will you keep praying for this week?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, pioneer and perfecter of our faith, for the joy set before you you endured the Cross and now you are seated at the right hand of the Father. Keep our eyes fixed on you. Surrounded by the cloud of witnesses, by your holy Mother and all the saints, help us to lay aside every weight and the sin that clings to us, and to run with perseverance. Teach us to pray always and not to lose heart, trusting that the Father hears the cry of his children day and night. When you come, may you find faith in us. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Thursday's readings are about not losing heart. In the NRSV both use the same words: Hebrews tells us to consider Jesus “so that you may not grow weary or lose heart” (12:3), and Luke introduces the parable of the widow with “their need to pray always and not to lose heart” (18:1). One reading points to the runners who have finished before us and to Jesus at the finish; the other to a woman who would not stop asking.

Hebrews 12:1–3 · Looking to Jesus

Setting.

Chapter 11 has just listed the heroes of faith, from Abel to the prophets and martyrs, and ended by saying that they “did not receive what was promised” (11:39). Now the writer draws the conclusion with “Therefore”. The picture is a stadium: the runners on the track, the crowd around them, the finishing line ahead.

Structure.

  • v. 1: surrounded by witnesses, lay aside every weight and run.
  • v. 2: look to Jesus, who endured the Cross and is now seated at God's right hand.
  • v. 3: consider him, so as not to grow weary.

Key words.

  • nephos martyrōn, cloud of witnesses: A great crowd. They are witnesses first because they bore witness to God by their lives; the picture of spectators watching a race is also present.
  • agōn, race: A contest, a struggle; the word from which “agony” comes. The Christian life is pictured as an athletic contest that needs endurance.
  • archēgos kai teleiōtēs, pioneer and perfecter: Jesus is the one who goes ahead and opens the way, and the one who brings faith to its goal. He has run the whole course himself.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses,” (12:1)

The saints are not distant figures but companions who have finished their race and now encourage ours.

“who for the sake of the joy that was set before him endured the cross, disregarding its shame,” (12:2)

The small Greek word anti can mean “for the sake of” or “instead of”. The NRSV takes the first: he endured for the joy ahead. Others read it as: he set aside the joy that was his and chose the Cross, and the Malayalam Bible follows this reading. Both are true of Christ (Philippians 2:6–8).

“Consider him who endured such hostility against himself from sinners,” (12:3)

The cure for weariness is not to look at ourselves but at him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it speaks of the witnesses of faith: we must turn to them, and above all to Jesus, “the pioneer and perfecter of our faith” (CCC 165). It also teaches that the witnesses who have gone before us into the Kingdom, especially those the Church recognizes as saints, share in the living tradition of prayer and pray for us (CCC 2683).

Luke 18:1–8 · Pray always and do not lose heart

Setting.

The parable follows Jesus' teaching on the coming of the Son of Man (17:20–37). Luke tells us its meaning before he tells the story. In Israel a widow was the classic example of a person without protection, and the Law commanded special care for widows (Exodus 22:22; Deuteronomy 10:18). Judges were to judge fairly and take no bribes (Deuteronomy 16:18–20). This judge, Jesus says, neither feared God nor had respect for people (18:2).

Structure.

  • v. 1: the purpose of the parable.
  • vv. 2–5: the judge and the widow.
  • vv. 6–8a: the lesson: how much more will God do justice.
  • v. 8b: the question about faith.

Key words.

  • enkakein, lose heart: To grow weary, to give up. Paul uses the same verb when he says, “we do not lose heart” (2 Corinthians 4:16).
  • ekdikēson me, grant me justice: Give a just verdict for me, vindicate me. She is not asking for revenge but for her rights.
  • hypōpiazē, wear me out: Literally “strike under the eye”, a boxing term (Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 9:27). The judge says, half in jest, that she will end up giving him a black eye.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus told them a parable about their need to pray always and not to lose heart.” (18:1)

The parable is not about wearing God down. It is about our need: we stop praying before God stops listening.

“And will not God grant justice to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night?” (18:7)

The argument goes from the lesser to the greater. If even this judge gives in, how much more will God, who is just and who loves his chosen ones.

“And yet, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”” (18:8)

The question turns back on the hearer. God's faithfulness is not in doubt; ours is.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names this as one of the three chief parables on prayer in Luke, centred on one quality of prayer: we must pray always, without ceasing, with the patience of faith (CCC 2613). The Syriac Churches have always loved praying through the day; in our tradition the Shehimo, the prayer of the hours, is one way of answering this command to pray always.

For study and teaching

  • What “weight” do I need to lay aside so that I can run more lightly toward Christ?
  • For what intention have I stopped praying because the answer seemed slow? Can I take it up again this week?
