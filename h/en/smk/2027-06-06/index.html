Word for the day

“I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” John 6:35

Reflection

Jesus says: “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty… Anyone who comes to me I will never drive away… This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” And: “No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father who sent me.” Faith itself is a gift: the Father draws, the Spirit moves the heart.

In the evening Gospel Jesus is rejected in his own town: “Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” Yet he does not stop. He sends the Twelve two by two, with authority over unclean spirits. “So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent. They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” The mission of the Church begins in weakness and is carried by the Spirit's power.

In the book of Daniel three young men stand before a golden statue and a blazing furnace. They say to the king: “If our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the furnace of blazing fire and out of your hand, O king, let him deliver us. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods and we will not worship the golden statue that you have set up.” This is faith that does not bargain: God first, whatever happens.

St Paul writes: “So then, whenever we have an opportunity, let us work for the good of all, and especially for those of the family of faith… May I never boast of anything except the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ… a new creation is everything!” In Baptism we became a new creation; the bread of life keeps us alive as God's new people.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Jesus went to his own town and was rejected. “Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown.” Did he give up? No. He called the Twelve and sent them out two by two. They preached repentance, cast out demons, and anointed the sick with oil. The mission of the Church began with rejection, and went on in the power of God.

In the Gospel of John Jesus says: “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry… anyone who comes to me I will never drive away.” What a promise! No sinner who comes to him is turned away. And he says: “No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father.” If you are here today, the Father has drawn you.

In the book of Daniel three young men refuse to worship the golden statue. They say: our God can save us from the fire; “but if not,” we still will not worship your gods. This is mature faith. It does not say “God, if you give me this, I will believe.” It says “God, whatever happens, I belong to you.” Our Malankara Church, born in 1930 with Mar Ivanios, knows this faith: they left property and buildings, but kept communion.

St Paul says: let us do good to all, and boast only in the Cross; what matters is a new creation. This week, go two by two: visit a sick or lonely person with someone from your family. And come often to the bread of life. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus sent his friends out two by two, like partners, to tell people about God and to help sick people.

In the Bible, three brave young men would not bow to a golden statue, even when the king was angry. They loved God more than anything!

Jesus says: “I am the bread of life.” Who can you visit this week with your mum, dad or friend?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, bread of life, draw us to yourself and never drive us away. Make us faithful like the three young men in the furnace, send us two by two to heal and proclaim repentance, and make us a new creation for the good of all. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Third Sunday after Pentecost the readings speak of hunger and bread, of guilt and forgiveness, and of a faith that stays firm. In the evening Gospel (Mark 6:4–13) Jesus is not honoured in his home town, yet he sends the Twelve out two by two; they preach repentance and anoint the sick with oil. Judges 1:8–11 tells how the people of Judah went up to take the land. In Daniel 3:1–18 three young men refuse to worship the golden statue: “But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods.” In Jeremiah 3:1–5 the Lord confronts an unfaithful people who still call him “My Father”. In Acts 13:26–41 Paul preaches at Antioch in Pisidia: “through this man forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you.”

We study three readings more closely. In Genesis, Joseph's brothers come to Egypt for grain in a famine and, without knowing it, stand before the brother they sold; for the first time they admit their guilt. Paul ends his letter to the Galatians in his own large handwriting: he will boast only in the cross, for “a new creation is everything!” In John, Jesus says, “I am the bread of life”, and promises that whoever comes to him will never be driven away. The key words are bread, the cross and drawn.

Genesis 42:18–25 · We are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother

Setting.

The story of Joseph fills the last chapters of Genesis (37–50). Years earlier his brothers, jealous of their father's love for him, threw him into a pit and sold him to traders going down to Egypt (37:18–28). In Egypt, after slavery and prison, Joseph interpreted Pharaoh's dreams, foresaw seven years of plenty and seven of famine, and was set over the land to store grain (41).

Now the famine has reached Canaan, and Jacob sends ten of his sons to buy grain. They bow before the governor of Egypt without recognising him. Joseph recognises them. He speaks harshly, accuses them of being spies and keeps them in custody for three days (42:6–17). Today's verses follow. Joseph is testing them: have they changed? Will they abandon another brother as they abandoned him?

Structure.

  • vv. 18–20: Joseph's new terms: one brother stays bound; the rest take grain home and bring back Benjamin.
  • vv. 21–22: the brothers confess their guilt for what they did to Joseph; Reuben recalls his warning.
  • vv. 23–24: Joseph, who understands them, turns away and weeps; Simeon is bound before their eyes.
  • v. 25: their sacks are filled with grain and each man's money is secretly returned.
  • ¶ The scene moves from fear to conscience, and from conscience towards the reconciliation of chapter 45.

Key words.

  • ’et-hā’ĕlōhîm ’ănî yārē’, I fear God: Joseph tells them, without revealing who he is, that he fears God. In Genesis the fear of God is the root of right conduct (compare 20:11). The brothers, who once showed no pity, are now in the hands of a man who does.
  • ’ăšēmîm, guilty: The brothers say literally “truly we are guilty”. The word is used for guilt that must be answered for. About twenty years later their conscience wakes.
  • mēlîṣ, interpreter: Joseph speaks through an interpreter, so the brothers think he cannot understand them. Their private confession is heard by the very one they wronged.

Verse by verse.

“Do this and you will live, for I fear God:” (42:18)

Joseph softens his first demand that one brother go and the rest stay in prison (42:16). The one who holds their life in his hands chooses mercy.

“Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother; we saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen.” (42:21)

The first time Genesis tells us that Joseph pleaded with them from the pit is here, in the brothers' own confession. Guilt long buried rises when trouble comes. True repentance begins when we see the anguish of the one we hurt.

“They did not know that Joseph understood them, since he spoke with them through an interpreter.” (42:23)

God hears even what we think no one hears. The brothers' confession is not spoken to Joseph, yet it reaches him.

“He turned away from them and wept;” (42:24)

This is the first of several times Joseph weeps (43:30; 45:2, 14–15; 50:17). His harshness is a test, not revenge. Behind the stern face of the governor is a brother's heart.

“to return every man’s money to his sack,” (42:25)

A hidden gift. The brothers will be frightened when they find it (42:28), but it is a sign of grace: they receive bread without paying for it.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers often saw in Joseph a figure of Christ: rejected and sold by his brothers, humbled and then raised up, he feeds the hungry and forgives those who wronged him. St Ambrose wrote a whole work, On Joseph, in this spirit. The Catechism quotes Joseph's later words to his brothers, “You meant evil against me; but God meant it for good”, to show how God's providence can draw good even from sin (CCC 312; Genesis 50:20). The bread that Joseph gives in the famine points to the bread of life in today's Gospel.

Galatians 6:10–15 · A new creation is everything

Setting.

These verses close Paul's letter to the Galatians. Throughout the letter he has argued that Gentile believers do not need to be circumcised in order to belong fully to God's people, because they have received the Spirit through faith in Christ. Some teachers were urging circumcision on them.

At verse 11 Paul takes the pen from the scribe who wrote the letter at his dictation and adds a final paragraph in his own hand, as he does elsewhere (1 Corinthians 16:21; 2 Thessalonians 3:17). The dispute is among believers in Christ about what to ask of Gentile converts; it is not an attack on the Jewish people, to whom Paul himself belonged.

Structure.

  • v. 10: let us do good to all, especially to the family of faith.
  • v. 11: Paul writes in his own hand.
  • vv. 12–13: the motives of those who urge circumcision.
  • vv. 14–15: Paul boasts only in the cross; what counts is a new creation.

Key words.

  • kairos, opportunity: The right time, the moment given by God. While we have time, we are to do good.
  • kauchasthai, to boast: To glory in, to put one's confidence in. Paul refuses to glory in anything that he or others can achieve, only in what Christ has done on the cross.
  • kainē ktisis, new creation: The same words appear in 2 Corinthians 5:17: “if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation”. In Christ God begins again what he began in Genesis.

Verse by verse.

“let us work for the good of all, and especially for those of the family of faith.” (6:10)

Charity has no limits, “the good of all”, yet the Church is a family with a special duty to its own members, above all the poor among them.

“See what large letters I make when I am writing in my own hand!” (6:11)

Some think the large letters show Paul's poor eyesight (compare 4:15); others that he writes large for emphasis. Either way, the apostle signs his letter with his own hand and his own heart.

“May I never boast of anything except the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world.” (6:14)

The cross, once a sign of shame, has become Paul's only glory. Through it the old world of pride and rivalry has lost its hold on him.

“but a new creation is everything!” (6:15)

Neither being circumcised nor being uncircumcised makes a person new. Only God's grace does, given in Baptism and lived by the Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Baptism makes the neophyte “a new creature”, an adopted son of God and a partaker of the divine nature (CCC 1265). The Church signs the Christian with the cross at Baptism and at every blessing, so that Paul's words become our own: our glory is the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ.

John 6:35–46 · I am the bread of life

Setting.

Chapter 6 of John begins with the feeding of the five thousand near the Sea of Galilee, when the Passover was near (6:4). The next day the crowd finds Jesus at Capernaum (6:24, 59). They ask for a sign and recall that their ancestors ate manna in the wilderness (6:30–31). Jesus answers that the true bread from heaven is given by his Father, and then says plainly: “I am the bread of life.”

Many interpreters see two movements in the discourse. In verses 35–50 Jesus is the bread to be received by faith: whoever comes to him and believes. From verse 51 he speaks of eating his flesh and drinking his blood, words the Church has always understood of the Eucharist. Today's passage belongs to the first part. In verse 41 John says “the Jews” began to complain; here the term stands for the hearers in the synagogue at Capernaum who could not accept his claim, not for the Jewish people as such. Jesus, his disciples and many who believed in him were Jews.

Structure.

  • v. 35: “I am the bread of life”: whoever comes and believes will not hunger or thirst.
  • vv. 36–40: the Father's will: that none be lost, and that all who believe have eternal life and be raised on the last day.
  • vv. 41–42: the hearers complain: is he not the son of Joseph?
  • vv. 43–46: no one can come unless drawn by the Father; all shall be taught by God.

Key words.

  • egō eimi ho artos tēs zōēs, I am the bread of life: The first of the great “I am” sayings in John with an image: bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine. Bread is the food of every day and of every home. Jesus gives himself as that which keeps us alive.
  • gongyzein, to complain: To murmur, to grumble. It recalls how Israel complained in the wilderness before the manna was given (Exodus 16:2, 7–8).
  • helkyō, to draw: The Father “draws” people to Jesus. John uses the same verb when Jesus says that, lifted up on the cross, he will draw all people to himself (12:32). Faith is God's gift before it is our act.

Verse by verse.

“I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” (6:35)

“Comes” and “believes” say the same thing in two ways. The hunger meant is the deep hunger of the heart, which no other food can satisfy.

“anyone who comes to me I will never drive away;” (6:37)

One of the most consoling verses of the Gospel. No one who comes to Jesus, however weak or sinful, will be turned out.

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” (6:40)

The promise “I will raise them up on the last day” returns four times in this chapter (6:39, 40, 44, 54). Eternal life begins now in faith and is completed in the resurrection of the body.

“Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?” (6:42)

The hearers think they know where he comes from. They see only his earthly family, not that he has come down from heaven. The evening Gospel tells the same story in his home town: familiarity becomes a stumbling block.

“No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father who sent me;” (6:44)

This does not mean that God draws some and refuses others. The Church teaches that God wills all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and the next verse explains how the Father draws: by teaching every heart that listens.

“And they shall all be taught by God.” (6:45)

Jesus recalls the prophets, especially Isaiah 54:13: “All your children shall be taught by the Lord.” In the new covenant God himself teaches his people from within, by his Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that St John reports the words of Jesus in the synagogue at Capernaum which prepare for the institution of the Eucharist: Christ calls himself the bread of life, come down from heaven (CCC 1338). It also teaches that faith is a gift of God, a supernatural virtue infused by him, and at the same time a truly human act (CCC 153–154). St Augustine, preaching on verse 44, says that the Father draws us not by force but by delight, as a sheep is drawn by a green branch held out to it (Tractates on John 26, in substance). The promise of resurrection “on the last day” is taught in CCC 1001.

Questions people ask

If no one can come to Jesus unless the Father draws him, are some people simply not chosen?

No. The Church teaches that God “desires everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth” (1 Timothy 2:4). The Father's drawing is the grace offered to every human heart. Jesus himself explains it in the next verse: everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to him. We can resist that grace, but we can never come to Christ without it. That is why we pray for those who do not yet believe.

Why did Joseph treat his brothers so harshly if he loved them?

Joseph was testing whether they had changed. About twenty years earlier they had sold a brother and lied to their father. Now he sees whether they will abandon Simeon and Benjamin in the same way. The test leads them to confess their guilt, and in chapter 44 Judah offers himself as a slave in place of Benjamin. Joseph's tears (42:24) show that his heart was never closed. Forgiveness does not ignore the truth; it helps the guilty to face it and be healed.

Is the bread of life only faith in Jesus, or is it the Eucharist?

Both, and they belong together. In the first part of the discourse Jesus is received by faith; from verse 51 he speaks of giving his flesh to eat. The Church has always read the whole chapter in the light of the Eucharist (CCC 1338). We come to the Holy Qurbono first by believing, and in receiving the Body and Blood of Christ our faith is fed.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost unfold the life of the Church that the Spirit has gathered. This Sunday's readings are full of signs of that life: the Twelve sent out two by two, the anointing of the sick with oil, which the Catechism sees as a hint of the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick (CCC 1511; Mark 6:13), forgiveness proclaimed through Jesus, and the bread of life. In every Holy Qurbono the Malankara faithful receive the bread that came down from heaven. Like Joseph in the famine, the Church does not keep the bread for herself: she gives it to all who come hungry, and Jesus promises that whoever comes will never be driven away.

For study and teaching

  • Joseph's brothers remembered their guilt only when trouble came. Is there a wrong I have done that I have buried and need to bring to the sacrament of Reconciliation?
  • Paul says, “May I never boast of anything except the cross.” What do I secretly boast in, and how can the cross release me from it?
  • What hunger do I bring to Jesus, the bread of life, when I come to the Holy Qurbono?
  • Jesus promises never to drive away anyone who comes to him. Is there someone in my family or parish who feels driven away, and how can I help them come back?
