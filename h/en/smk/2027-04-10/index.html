Word for the day

“Whoever listens to you listens to me, and whoever rejects you rejects me, and whoever rejects me rejects the one who sent me.” Luke 10:16

Reflection

Christ is risen! Yesterday Paul told how the risen Lord called him; today he tells how he kept communion with the Church. “Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me. I went up in response to a revelation.” Fourteen years of preaching lay behind him, and still he laid his message before the leaders of the Church: “Then I laid before them (though only in a private meeting with the acknowledged leaders) the gospel that I proclaim among the Gentiles, in order to make sure that I was not running, or had not run, in vain.” Here is a great lesson for every gifted Christian. Paul had seen the risen Lord himself, yet he did not run alone. He submitted his preaching to the apostles, so that his work would be one with theirs. The Gospel is not a private discovery; it is received, guarded and handed on in the communion of the Church. St Irenaeus, writing a century later, pointed to this unbroken handing on through the bishops in the apostolic Churches as the sign of the true faith (in substance).

The question that troubled the young Church was whether Gentile believers like Titus had to be circumcised and keep the whole Law of Moses to belong to Christ. Paul's answer was clear, and the leaders in Jerusalem agreed with him: “But even Titus, who was with me, was not compelled to be circumcised, though he was a Greek.” Against “false believers secretly brought in, who slipped in to spy on the freedom we have in Christ Jesus,” Paul stood firm, “so that the truth of the gospel might always remain with you.” This freedom is not a freedom to do as we like. It is the freedom of children who are saved by the grace of the risen Christ, not by their own achievements, and who now live by his Spirit. Paul speaks here about what was required of Gentile converts, not against the Jewish people, whose covenant with God the Church honours (Catechism 839). And he adds a word every parish can use: “God shows no partiality.” In Christ there are no first-class and second-class members.

The Gospel is sober. Jesus grieves over the towns where he did his mighty works: “Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the deeds of power done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes.” Those towns had seen everything and changed nothing. The danger is not far from us. We have heard the Easter proclamation, received the Holy Qurbono, seen the light of the Resurrection. Then Jesus gives his messengers an astonishing dignity: “Whoever listens to you listens to me, and whoever rejects you rejects me, and whoever rejects me rejects the one who sent me.” When the Church preaches the Gospel faithfully, it is Christ who speaks (Catechism 87). On Saturdays many of our families turn in prayer to the holy Mother of God, the Virgin Mary, who heard the word of God and kept it in her heart. Ask her today for a listening heart. Tomorrow, the Second Sunday after Qyomtho, come to the Holy Qurbono ready to listen, and ask the risen Lord to let his word change something in you this week.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! After fourteen years of preaching, St Paul went up to Jerusalem and laid his gospel before the leaders of the Church, in order to make sure that I was not running, or had not run, in vain (Galatians 2:2). Even a great apostle does not run alone.

He defended the freedom we have in Christ: Titus was not compelled to be circumcised. We are saved by the grace of the risen Lord, and God shows no partiality (Galatians 2:6).

In the Gospel Jesus grieves: woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! They saw his mighty works and did not repent. We too have seen much this Easter. Have we changed?

Whoever listens to you listens to me (Luke 10:16). Christ speaks through his Church. Like the holy Mother of God, who kept the word in her heart, let us listen, and let his word change something in us this week. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Jesus once said to his friends: when people listen to you, they are listening to me. So when your parents or your Sunday school teacher tell you about Jesus, it is really Jesus speaking to you. Listening means doing what we hear. What will you do this week that Jesus asks?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen and alive, you speak to us through your Church and send messengers in your name. Do not let us be like the towns that saw your mighty works and did not repent. Keep us in the communion of your apostles, guard in us the truth of the Gospel and the freedom you have won for us, and deliver us from all partiality. By the prayers of your holy Mother, give us hearts that listen and are changed. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After fourteen years Paul goes up to Jerusalem with Barnabas and Titus, and lays his gospel before the leaders, “so that the truth of the gospel might always remain with you.” In Luke, Jesus mourns over the lakeside towns that saw his mighty works and did not repent, and then gives his messengers a great dignity: “Whoever listens to you listens to me.” Both readings show the gospel carried by people who are sent, and who remain in communion with the Lord who sends them.

Galatians 2:1–6 · That the truth of the gospel might remain

Setting.

Paul continues his account. After his first short visit to Peter (1:18), fourteen years pass. Now he goes up again, and this time the question is the one troubling the Galatians: must Gentile believers be circumcised?

Scholars discuss how this visit fits the story in Acts. Many identify it with the meeting at Jerusalem in Acts 15, where the apostles and elders decided that Gentiles were not to be burdened with circumcision; others link it with the earlier visit to bring help in a time of famine (Acts 11:29–30). Either way, Paul's point is that the leaders in Jerusalem recognized his gospel.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: after fourteen years, with Barnabas and Titus, Paul goes up in response to a revelation and lays his gospel before the leaders.
  • v. 3: Titus, a Greek, is not compelled to be circumcised.
  • vv. 4–5: false believers try to bring them into bondage; Paul does not give way.
  • v. 6: the leaders add nothing to his gospel; God shows no partiality.

Key words.

  • hoi dokountes, the acknowledged leaders: Literally “those who are reckoned”, those of repute. In verse 9 Paul names them: James, Cephas and John.
  • hē alētheia tou euangeliou, the truth of the gospel: Not a formula but the gospel's real content: that in Christ the Gentiles are received by grace through faith, without first having to become Jews.
  • prosōpon lambanei, shows partiality: Literally “receives a face”, a Hebrew way of speaking for judging by appearance or rank. God does not.

Verse by verse.

“Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me.” (2:1)

Barnabas was a Jewish believer from Cyprus (Acts 4:36); Titus was a Greek. The two companions are the question in person.

“Then I laid before them (though only in a private meeting with the acknowledged leaders) the gospel that I proclaim among the Gentiles, in order to make sure that I was not running, or had not run, in vain.” (2:2)

Paul, who received his gospel by revelation, still seeks communion with the apostles. He knows that a gospel cut off from the Church would be running in vain.

“we did not submit to them even for a moment, so that the truth of the gospel might always remain with you.” (2:5)

The “false believers” of verse 4 were members of the Church who insisted on circumcision; Paul's quarrel is with them, not with the Jewish people. His firmness is for the sake of others: “with you”. He defends the Galatians' place in the family of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen in this meeting, together with Acts 15, a pattern for settling questions of faith: the apostles meet, listen and decide together under the Holy Spirit. The Catechism teaches that the college of bishops, united with the successor of Peter, carries on this care for the whole Church (CCC 880–883). Paul's words also stand behind the Church's teaching that our justification comes from the grace of God (CCC 1996), a grace that bears fruit in “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6).

Luke 10:13–16 · Whoever listens to you listens to me

Setting.

Jesus is on his way to Jerusalem and has sent out seventy messengers ahead of him (10:1). He has told them what to do when a town does not receive them (10:10–12). Now he turns to the towns beside the Sea of Galilee, where so many of his mighty works had been done.

Chorazin lay in the hills a few kilometres north of Capernaum. Bethsaida, the town of Andrew, Peter and Philip (John 1:44), lay near the place where the Jordan enters the lake. Tyre and Sidon were Gentile cities on the coast, often rebuked by the prophets (Isaiah 23; Ezekiel 26–28).

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: woe to Chorazin and Bethsaida; Tyre and Sidon would have repented.
  • v. 15: Capernaum will be brought down to Hades.
  • v. 16: whoever listens to you listens to me; whoever rejects you rejects me and the one who sent me.

Key words.

  • ouai, woe: A cry of grief as much as of warning, the word of a lament. Jesus mourns over these towns as he warns them.
  • dynameis, deeds of power: Literally “powers”, the word the first three Gospels use for the miracles. They were signs calling for a change of heart.
  • metanoeō, repent: To change one's mind and turn one's life around. Sackcloth and ashes were the outward signs of such turning (Jonah 3:6).

Verse by verse.

“Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida!” (10:13)

Chorazin is named only here and in Matthew's parallel (Matthew 11:21), and no miracle there is recorded. We learn from this verse how much Jesus did that is not written (John 21:25).

“And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? No, you will be brought down to Hades.” (10:15)

The words recall Isaiah 14:13–15, the taunt against the proud king of Babylon. Capernaum, the town Jesus made his home, is warned not to be proud of its privilege.

“Whoever listens to you listens to me, and whoever rejects you rejects me, and whoever rejects me rejects the one who sent me.” (10:16)

A chain of sending, from the Father to Jesus to his messengers. The Easter Church lives by it: the risen Lord still speaks through those he sends.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes verse 16 when it teaches that the bishops have succeeded the apostles as shepherds of the Church, so that whoever hears them hears Christ (Lumen Gentium 20). The Catechism recalls the same words when it explains why the faithful receive the teaching of their pastors with docility (CCC 87). This is not a claim to human greatness: the messengers carry a word that is not their own.

Questions people ask

Why did Paul refuse to have Titus circumcised, when Acts says that he had Timothy circumcised (Acts 16:3)?

The two cases were different. Titus was a Greek, and some were demanding his circumcision as necessary for salvation; to give way would have denied that we are saved by the grace of Christ. Timothy had a Jewish mother, and Paul had him circumcised by his own choice, so that he could work among Jews without giving offence (Acts 16:1–3). Paul was firm on the principle and flexible in love (1 Corinthians 9:20).

For study and teaching

  • Paul defended “the truth of the gospel” with firmness and in communion with the apostles. Where do I need both courage and communion in my faith?
  • Jesus says, “Whoever listens to you listens to me.” How do I listen to the pastors and teachers of the Church in my life?
