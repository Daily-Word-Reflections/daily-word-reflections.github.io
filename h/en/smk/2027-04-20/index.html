Word for the day

“No slave can serve two masters; for a slave will either hate the one and love the other, or be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and wealth.” Luke 16:13

Reflection

Christ is risen! Today's Gospel is plain speech about money. “If then you have not been faithful with the dishonest wealth, who will entrust to you the true riches?” Then the sentence everyone remembers: “No slave can serve two masters... You cannot serve God and wealth.” Jesus does not say that money is evil; he says it makes a poor master. Wealth asks for the whole heart, and so does God; one of them must give way. Luke adds that some who heard this “were lovers of money” and ridiculed him, and Jesus answers: “God knows your hearts; for what is prized by human beings is an abomination in the sight of God.” The warning is for every listener in every age, and the Catechism repeats the bold words of St John Chrysostom: not to let the poor share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life; the goods we possess are not ours, but theirs (Catechism 2446). The risen Lord does not ask us to despise the world; he asks us to use what passes so as to gain what lasts.

Then Jesus speaks of the law: “it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away, than for one stroke of a letter in the law to be dropped,” and he gives one example where he restores God's first plan: “Anyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery.” Jesus takes marriage back to the beginning, when God made man and woman to become “one flesh” (Genesis 2:24). The Church holds that a valid, consummated marriage between baptized persons cannot be dissolved by any human power (Catechism 1614–1615, 2382). This teaching is a word of protection, especially for wives who in the ancient world could be sent away with a single document. It is never a reason to treat with coldness those whose marriages have broken down. Many were abandoned against their will; many carry wounds. The Church is asked to accompany them with patience and love, to help them examine their situation in truth, and to keep them close to the life of the parish (Pope Francis, Amoris Laetitia, 2016).

The Letter to the Hebrews today sounds severe: “if we willfully persist in sin after having received the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins,” and “It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.” What does it mean? The Letter has spent chapters showing that Christ's one sacrifice is the only sacrifice for sin. Whoever deliberately rejects it, trampling on “the Son of God” and outraging “the Spirit of grace,” turns away from the only remedy there is. In the third century some, like the followers of Novatian, read these lines as if the Church could never forgive grave sin after Baptism. The Church rejected that reading and kept the door of Penance open: there is no offence, however serious, that the Church cannot forgive (Catechism 982). On the very evening of his Resurrection the Lord breathed on the apostles and gave them power to forgive sins (John 20:22–23). The fearful thing is not the hand of God but refusing it. King David once chose: “let us fall into the hand of the Lord, for his mercy is great” (2 Samuel 24:14). Let that be our choice too, in the confessional this Easter.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today Jesus says plainly: no slave can serve two masters... You cannot serve God and wealth (Luke 16:13). Money is a good servant and a poor master. It asks for the whole heart, and so does God.

St John Chrysostom said that not to share our goods with the poor is to steal from them, for what we hold is also theirs (in substance). The risen Lord asks us to use what passes so as to gain what lasts.

Jesus also restores marriage to the beginning: what God has joined, no one may separate. The Church holds this firmly, and she is also asked to accompany with love those whose marriages have broken, many of whom were hurt against their will.

The Letter to the Hebrews warns: it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. But the fearful thing is to refuse his mercy. On Easter evening Jesus gave the apostles power to forgive sins. Like King David, let us choose to fall into the hand of the Lord, for his mercy is great. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: nobody can have two bosses. You cannot serve God and money. Money is useful, but God must always be number one. Can you think of one thing you could share today, a sweet, a toy, a coin, with someone who has less? When we share, we show that God is our boss, not money.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you alone are our Master. Deliver us from the love of money and teach us to use the goods of this world in faithfulness and generosity. Bless husbands and wives with faithful love, and surround with your compassion those whose marriages have been broken. Keep us from ever spurning the blood of the covenant, and when we fall, bring us back to the mercy you gave your Church on the evening of your Resurrection. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Tuesday sets two hard sayings side by side. Hebrews warns those who have known Christ not to turn their backs on him on purpose, and Luke gathers sayings of Jesus about money, the Law and marriage. Neither reading lets faith become a matter of words alone. What we have received must shape what we choose.

Hebrews 10:26–31 · Do not spurn the blood of the covenant

Setting.

The encouragement of 10:19–25 is followed at once by a warning, as happened earlier in the letter (6:4–8). The writer speaks to people who have been baptized and are tempted to give up the faith altogether and go back, perhaps under pressure from outside. He does not speak of the ordinary weakness of believers, but of a deliberate turning away from Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: deliberate sin after knowing the truth leaves no other sacrifice.
  • vv. 28–29: from the lesser to the greater: if the Law of Moses was serious, how much more the covenant in Christ's blood.
  • vv. 30–31: two sayings from the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:35–36), and the conclusion.

Key words.

  • hekousiōs, willfully: Deliberately; and the verb that follows is in a tense of continuing action, which the NRSV renders “persist”. The writer has in mind not weakness but a settled choice to reject Christ, as v. 29 describes.
  • katapatēsas, spurned: Literally “trampled underfoot”. It pictures treating the Son of God as worthless.
  • to pneuma tēs charitos, the Spirit of grace: The Spirit who gives grace. To insult him is to refuse the very gift that heals.

Verse by verse.

“For if we willfully persist in sin after having received the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins,” (10:26)

The sentence does not say that God refuses to forgive. It says that if Christ's sacrifice is rejected, there is no other to turn to, since the old sacrifices have been fulfilled in his.

“profaned the blood of the covenant by which they were sanctified, and outraged the Spirit of grace?” (10:29)

The Son, the blood of his covenant and the Spirit: the sin in view is turning against the whole gift of God.

“It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.” (10:31)

David once chose to fall into the hand of the Lord rather than into human hands, because his mercy is great (2 Samuel 24:14). The same hands are fearful to those who despise him and a refuge to those who turn back.

How the Church reads it.

In the third century the followers of Novatian used verses like these to say that Christians who had denied the faith under persecution could never be received back. The Church rejected this. St Ambrose, writing On Penance against them, defended the Church's power to forgive the repentant (in substance), and the Catechism teaches that there is no offence, however serious, that the Church cannot forgive (CCC 982). The sin that cannot be forgiven is the one that refuses to repent and so refuses God's mercy (CCC 1864). The warning is real, and it is given so that no one reaches that point.

Luke 16:11–18 · You cannot serve God and wealth

Setting.

These verses follow the parable of the dishonest manager (16:1–8), and Luke gathers several sayings around it. The thread is faithfulness: with money, with the Law, and in marriage. Luke notes that some Pharisees who heard this “were lovers of money” and ridiculed Jesus. He is describing those listeners, not the Pharisees as a whole, many of whom were devout; Luke also tells of Pharisees who warned Jesus of danger (13:31).

Structure.

  • vv. 11–13: faithfulness with money, and the two masters.
  • vv. 14–15: the ridicule, and God who knows the heart.
  • vv. 16–17: the Law and the prophets, and the kingdom.
  • v. 18: marriage.

Key words.

  • mamōnas, wealth: An Aramaic word for property or riches. Jesus speaks of it almost as a rival lord, which is why older translations kept the name “mammon”.
  • bdelygma, abomination: A strong biblical word for what is repugnant to God, often used of idols. To prize wealth above God is a kind of idolatry.
  • keraia, stroke: Literally “a little horn”: the tiny stroke that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another.

Verse by verse.

“You cannot serve God and wealth.” (16:13)

A slave belonged wholly to one owner. Jesus does not say “should not” but “cannot”.

“but God knows your hearts;” (16:15)

Public reputation and God's knowledge are set side by side. What counts is what he sees.

“But it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away, than for one stroke of a letter in the law to be dropped.” (16:17)

The kingdom does not cancel the Law; it fulfils it. The very next verse shows Jesus asking more than the practice of his day, not less.

“Anyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery,” (16:18)

Luke gives the saying with no exception (compare Mark 10:11–12). Moses had allowed a certificate of divorce (Deuteronomy 24:1); Jesus goes back to the beginning (Mark 10:6–9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists money among the things that can become an idol when honoured in place of God (CCC 2113), and it quotes “You cannot serve God and mammon” against any system that makes profit the only rule (CCC 2424). On marriage it teaches that the Lord insisted on the original intention of the Creator, who willed that marriage be indissoluble (CCC 2382). It also notes that one spouse may be the innocent victim of a divorce (CCC 2386); the Church is called to stand beside those who suffer when a marriage breaks.

Questions people ask

What does “everyone tries to enter it by force” mean?

The Greek verb biazetai is hard to translate, and Matthew has a similar saying (Matthew 11:12). It may mean that people press eagerly into the kingdom, or that they are strongly urged to enter it. Scholars are divided. What is clear is that since John the Baptist something new is happening: the good news of the kingdom is being proclaimed.

For study and teaching

  • Is there a part of my life where I have quietly decided not to let Christ in? What would it mean to bring it to him in Confession?
  • Where does money compete with God for my loyalty: in time, in worry, in what I am proud of?
