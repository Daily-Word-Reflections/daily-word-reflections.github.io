Word for the day

“But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” Ephesians 2:4–5

Reflection

Christ is risen! In the Easter season the Church reads St Paul's letter to the Ephesians, which speaks of Easter as something that has already happened to us. “You were dead through the trespasses and sins in which you once lived.” Paul does not soften the picture: without Christ, we were following “the course of this world,” ruled by our passions, and “by nature children of wrath, like everyone else.” Then come two words that change everything: “But God.” “But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ… and raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus.” What God did for Jesus on Easter morning, he has begun in us through Baptism. We were buried with him, we were raised with him, and in hope we are already seated with him. “For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not the result of works, so that no one may boast.” Salvation is pure gift; no one earns the first grace (Catechism 1996–1998, 2010).

Yet Paul at once adds: “For we are what he has made us, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand to be our way of life.” The Catholic faith holds both together: we are saved by grace, not by our own works; and grace is not idle, it makes us capable of works of love that God himself has prepared for us. The Gospel shows this in a small household scene. Jesus enters Simon's house after the synagogue. Simon's mother-in-law has a high fever. “Then he stood over her and rebuked the fever, and it left her. Immediately she got up and began to serve them.” The Greek word for “got up” is the same family of words used for the Resurrection; healed by the Lord, she rises and serves. St Jerome, preaching on this scene in Mark's Gospel, prayed that the Lord would come into our house too, and with a word heal the fever of our sins, so that we might rise at once and serve him (in substance). Then, at sunset, the whole town brings its sick, and “he laid his hands on each of them and cured them.” Not the crowd in general, but each one. He silences the demons who shout his name, because he will not be proclaimed by them; he will be known through the Cross and the empty tomb.

How do we live this Tuesday of Qyomtho? First, thank God for the grace of Baptism: you were dead, but God made you alive with Christ. If you know the date of your Baptism, mark it in your calendar this year. Second, ask the Lord to rebuke whatever fever burns in you: anger, envy, anxiety, an addiction. Name it before him and, if it is sin, bring it to confession. Third, rise and serve. Simon's mother-in-law did not wait to feel stronger; she began at once. Do one good work today that God has prepared for you: help at home without being asked, visit someone sick, take food to a family in need. Bring a sick person you know to the Lord in prayer at the Holy Qurbono, as the people of Capernaum did at sunset.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! St Paul tells us today what Easter has already done in us. You were dead through your trespasses. But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us, made us alive together with Christ (Ephesians 2:4–5).

By grace you have been saved through faith; it is the gift of God. And then: we are what he has made us, created in Christ Jesus for good works (Ephesians 2:10). Grace is pure gift, and grace makes us able to love.

In the Gospel Jesus enters Simon's house, stands over the sick woman, and rebukes the fever. Immediately she got up and began to serve them (Luke 4:39). At sunset he lays his hands on each of the sick and heals them. He knows each one by name.

So let us thank God for our Baptism, ask him to rebuke whatever fever burns in us, and rise at once to serve. Let us bring our sick to him at this Holy Qurbono. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! One day Jesus went to Peter's house, and Peter's mother-in-law was very sick with a fever. Jesus told the fever to go away, and it did! At once she got up and made food for everyone. When Jesus helps us, we can help others too. What can you do to help at home today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, when we were dead through our trespasses the Father, rich in mercy, made us alive together with you. Come into our house as you came into Simon's house. Stand over us and rebuke every fever of sin and fear, and raise us up to serve you at once. Lay your hands on each of the sick we bring to you, and heal them. Make us live the good works you have prepared for us, so that no one may boast except in your grace. To you be glory with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ephesians describes Easter happening in us: we were dead through our trespasses, and God, “rich in mercy”, made us alive together with Christ, raised us up with him and seated us with him. In the Gospel Jesus rebukes a fever, and Simon's mother-in-law gets up at once and serves. Both readings have the same movement: lying down, raised up, and then service. We are saved by grace, and grace sets us to work.

Ephesians 2:1–10 · By grace you have been saved

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter of praise and teaching that bears Paul's name, addressed to Christians of Gentile background. Many scholars think it comes from a disciple who gathered Paul's thought after his death; many others hold that Paul wrote it from prison (3:1; 4:1). Chapter 1 has just praised the power God showed when he raised Christ from the dead and seated him at his right hand (1:20). Chapter 2 says that the same power has been at work in the readers.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: what we were: dead, following the course of this world, children of wrath.
  • vv. 4–7: but God: made alive with Christ, raised with him, seated with him.
  • vv. 8–9: saved by grace, through faith, as a gift, not as the result of works.
  • v. 10: God's handiwork, created for good works.

Key words.

  • synezōopoiēsen, made us alive together with: One of three verbs that Ephesians forms with “together with” (syn-): made alive with, raised with, seated with (vv. 5–6). What happened to Christ at Easter is shared with those who belong to him.
  • charis, grace: God's favour, given without our deserving it. The word appears three times in this short passage (vv. 5, 7, 8).
  • poiēma, what he has made: From the verb “to make”; our word “poem” comes from it. Christians are God's handiwork, his work of art, made new in Christ.

Verse by verse.

“But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us” (2:4)

The turning point of the passage is two words: “But God”. Everything before them is human failure; everything after them is God's initiative.

“For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God” (2:8)

Grace is the source, and faith is the way we receive it. The whole gift, faith included, comes from God.

“For we are what he has made us, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand to be our way of life.” (2:10)

Works do not earn salvation, but they are its purpose. God prepared them beforehand, and by his grace we walk in them, so that the good we do is both his gift and truly ours.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that our justification comes from the grace of God, the undeserved help he gives us to respond to his call (CCC 1996), and that our merit before God comes from his having chosen to associate us with the work of his grace, so that the merit of good works is to be credited first to the grace of God (CCC 2008). The Council of Trent taught that nothing that comes before justification, whether faith or works, merits the grace of justification (Session 6, chapter 8). In 1999 the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation affirmed together, in their Joint Declaration, that by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit of ours, we are accepted by God.

Luke 4:38–41 · She got up and served

Setting.

Jesus has just taught in the synagogue at Capernaum on the sabbath and delivered a man from an unclean spirit (4:31–37). Now he goes to Simon's house. Luke has not yet told the call of Simon (5:1–11), so this healing in his home comes before Simon leaves his nets. Simon was married: Paul mentions that Cephas was accompanied by a believing wife (1 Corinthians 9:5).

The sabbath ended at sunset. Only then could people carry their sick through the streets, and so the whole town comes “as the sun was setting”.

Structure.

  • vv. 38–39: in Simon's house: the fever rebuked, the woman raised, her service.
  • v. 40: at sunset: the sick brought, hands laid on each one.
  • v. 41: demons driven out and silenced.

Key words.

  • epetimēsen, rebuked: Luke uses the same verb for the demon in 4:35 and for the demons in 4:41. The fever is treated like a hostile power, and Jesus speaks to it with authority.
  • diēkonei, began to serve: The verb from which “deacon” comes. Her healing is complete: she takes up her place in the household and serves Jesus and the others. The tense suggests service that goes on.
  • epitheis tas cheiras, laying his hands: Only Luke notes that Jesus laid his hands on “each of them” (v. 40). No one is healed as a face in a crowd.

Verse by verse.

“Then he stood over her and rebuked the fever, and it left her.” (4:39)

Mark says that Jesus took her by the hand and lifted her up (Mark 1:31). Luke shows the Lord standing over the sick bed and speaking a word of command.

“Immediately she got up and began to serve them.” (4:39)

The Greek for “got up” (anastasa) is from the same family as the word for resurrection. Read in Easter week, she is a picture of every Christian: raised up in order to serve.

“and he laid his hands on each of them and cured them.” (4:40)

A long evening, one person at a time.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ the physician, moved by so much suffering, not only lets himself be touched by the sick but makes their miseries his own, and that he often uses signs such as the laying on of hands (CCC 1503–1504). The Church continues this care in the Anointing of the Sick, which St James describes (James 5:14–15; CCC 1510).

For study and teaching

  • Ephesians says that God prepared good works beforehand for me to walk in. What good work is waiting for me this week?
  • Simon's mother-in-law got up and served at once. When God has lifted me out of an illness or a trouble, how have I used that new strength?
