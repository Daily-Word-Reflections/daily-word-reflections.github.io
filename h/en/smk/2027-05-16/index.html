Word for the day

“All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.” Acts 2:4

Reflection

“When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place. And suddenly from heaven there came a sound like the rush of a violent wind… Divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them. All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit.” Fifty days after Easter, the promise of the Father is fulfilled. The Church, gathered in prayer with Mary, receives the Spirit and is born into mission.

The crowds from every nation hear the apostles “in our own languages… speaking about God's deeds of power.” At Babel human pride scattered the nations in confusion of tongues; at Pentecost the Spirit gathers them in one faith, each hearing in his own language. The Church is catholic from her first day: one Spirit, many peoples, many languages, including ours.

The Old Testament readings were longing for this day. Moses wished: “Would that all the LORD's people were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit on them!” Ezekiel promised: “A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you… I will put my spirit within you.” Now it happens. The Spirit who rested on a few prophets is poured out on all the baptised.

Jesus had promised: “I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Advocate, to be with you forever… I will not leave you orphaned.” St Paul explains that the one Spirit gives many gifts, “to each… for the common good.” Each of us received this Spirit in Baptism and Chrismation. Pentecost asks: are we living by the Spirit's power, or as if he had never come?

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today is the birthday of the Church. Fifty days after Easter, the apostles were gathered in prayer with Mary. Suddenly there came a sound like a mighty wind, and tongues like fire rested on each of them. They were filled with the Holy Spirit, and the frightened men who had locked the doors went out into the streets and proclaimed Christ.

People from every nation heard them, each in his own language, speaking of the wonders of God. At Babel, pride had scattered humanity into many languages that could not understand one another. At Pentecost the Spirit gathers the nations into one faith, without erasing their languages. That is why we can pray the Holy Qurbono in Syriac and Malayalam, in Tamil and Hindi and English: one Spirit, many tongues.

Moses once said: “Would that all the Lord's people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his spirit on them!” Ezekiel promised: “A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you.” Today it happens. And it happened to you on the day of your Baptism, when you were anointed with holy Myron. The Spirit is not only for bishops and saints; he lives in you.

St Paul says the one Spirit gives many gifts, each for the common good. What is your gift? Music, teaching, caring for the sick, organising, praying? Offer it. And let us pray today with the whole Church: Come, Holy Spirit, fill the hearts of your faithful, and kindle in us the fire of your love. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, on this feast of Pentecost the Gospel takes us back to the upper room on the night before Jesus died. He promised: I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Advocate, to be with you forever (John 14:16). An advocate is one who stands beside us, defends us, encourages us. Jesus had been that for his disciples. Now he promises another, the Holy Spirit.

Notice where the Spirit will be. Jesus says: you know him, because he abides with you, and he will be in you (John 14:17). Ezekiel had heard God say: I will put my spirit within you (Ezekiel 36:27). The Spirit is not only above us or around us. He lives within the baptised. That is why Jesus speaks of love: if you love me, you will keep my commandments (John 14:15). Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them (John 14:23).

And what does the Spirit do? The Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you (John 14:26). On the day of Pentecost the apostles suddenly understood what Jesus had taught them. Isaiah had foretold the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might (Isaiah 11:2). The Spirit is the Church's living memory of Jesus.

We meet the Spirit at the heart of every Holy Qurbono. The priest calls upon the Holy Spirit to come down upon the bread and wine, and by his power they become the Body and Blood of Christ. The same Spirit who came upon the apostles comes upon our altar and upon us, to make us one body in Christ.

So let us welcome the Advocate today. Each morning this week, let us pray: Holy Spirit, dwell in me today. Before we read the Gospel, let us ask him to teach us. And let us keep the word of Jesus out of love, so that the Father and the Son may make their home in us. Come, Holy Spirit. Amen.

God shares his Spirit with all his people and gives us new hearts.

We must guard that gift and never grieve the Spirit by unrepented sin.

Take-home resolution: This week I will ask for one fruit of the Spirit, share the burden of my parish, and guard the gift of the Spirit by confession.

Dear friends, among today's readings are two old stories about the Spirit of God. In the first, Moses is exhausted. He cannot carry the whole people alone. So the Lord came down in the cloud and spoke to him, and took some of the spirit that was on him and put it on the seventy elders (Numbers 11:25). God shares his Spirit so that the burden is shared.

At Pentecost that sharing reaches everyone. Saint Peter stands up and quotes the prophet Joel: I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh, and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy (Acts 2:17). Not only the seventy, not only the apostles, but every baptised person receives the Spirit. Each of us is called to carry part of the Church's burden: in our families, in our parishes, in our work.

The second story is about Saul. When Samuel anointed him, God gave him another heart, and the spirit of God possessed him (1 Samuel 10:9–10). That is what the Spirit does: he changes the heart from within. Saint Paul describes the new heart: the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22–23).

But Saul's story has a sad ending. Through pride and disobedience he turned away from God, and later we read: the spirit of the Lord departed from Saul (1 Samuel 16:14). This is a serious warning. The gift of the Spirit can be refused by serious sin that we do not repent of. Saint Paul says: do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, with which you were marked with a seal for the day of redemption (Ephesians 4:30). And David, after his sin, prayed: do not take your holy spirit from me (Psalm 51:11). David repented, and God restored him.

So on this Pentecost let us thank God for the Spirit given to us in Baptism and Myron. Let us share the burden of our parish. Let us ask for one fruit of the Spirit this week and practise it. And if we have grieved the Spirit, let us not wait: a good confession opens the door again. Come, Holy Spirit, and never leave us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sunday of Pentecost every reading speaks of the Spirit of God coming to rest on his people. In the evening Gospel (John 14:25–31) Jesus promises that “the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything”. In Numbers 11:16–35 the Lord takes some of the spirit that was on Moses and puts it on the seventy elders, and Moses cries, “Would that all the Lord’s people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his spirit on them!” Joshua 18:1–7 shows the whole congregation gathered at Shiloh around the tent of meeting, where each tribe receives its share by lot and the Levites receive the priesthood as their heritage. In 1 Samuel 10:9–16 the spirit of God comes upon Saul and he is changed, so that people ask, “Is Saul also among the prophets?”

We study four readings more closely, because on this feast the Acts of the Apostles cannot be passed over. Ezekiel promises a new heart and a new spirit. Acts tells how the promise was fulfilled on the day of Pentecost in Jerusalem. Paul teaches that the one Spirit gives many gifts, to each for the common good. In John, Jesus promises another Advocate and says that the Father and he will come and make their home with those who love him. The key words are spirit, gift and home.

Ezekiel 36:22–38 · A new heart and a new spirit

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest taken to Babylon with the first group of exiles in 597 BC. He prophesied there before and after the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC. Chapters 1–24 announce the judgment; after the news of the city's fall (33:21), the book turns to hope. Chapter 36 promises that the people will return to their land, and chapter 37 follows with the vision of the dry bones that live again when the breath of God enters them.

The starting point is surprising. God does not act because Israel has earned it, but “for the sake of my holy name”. In the ancient world a defeated people seemed to prove that its god was weak. The exile had made the nations think little of the Lord. God will now show who he is by renewing his people from within.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–23: not for your sake, but for my holy name.
  • vv. 24–28: gathering, cleansing, a new heart, my spirit within you, my people and your God.
  • vv. 29–30: abundance of grain and fruit.
  • vv. 31–32: remembrance and shame that lead to repentance.
  • vv. 33–38: rebuilt towns, a land like Eden, and cities full of people like the flocks at the festivals.
  • ¶ The centre of the passage is vv. 26–27: the gift of a new heart and of God's own spirit.

Key words.

  • lēb ḥādāš, a new heart: In Hebrew thought the heart is not only the seat of feeling but the centre of thought, will and decision. A new heart means a new person, able at last to choose what God wills.
  • rûaḥ, spirit: The same word means wind, breath and spirit. In v. 26 God gives “a new spirit”; in v. 27 he goes further: “my spirit”. In the next chapter the rûaḥ blows over the valley of dry bones and they live (37:9–10).
  • lēb hā’eben, lēb bāśār, heart of stone, heart of flesh: A heart of stone is hard, cold and unable to respond. A heart of flesh is living and tender. The phrase is not about weakness but about life.

Verse by verse.

“It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name,” (36:22)

Salvation begins with God's faithfulness to himself, not with our merits. That is good news: what depends on God cannot fail.

“I will sprinkle clean water upon you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses,” (36:25)

The sprinkling recalls the purification rites of the Law (Numbers 19). Christians have always heard Baptism in these words.

“A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you; and I will remove from your body the heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh.” (36:26)

Jeremiah had promised a new covenant written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34). Ezekiel adds how it will happen: by the gift of the spirit.

“you shall be my people, and I will be your God.” (36:28)

The covenant formula, the heart of the whole Old Testament. The new heart is given so that the covenant may at last be lived.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names Ezekiel 36 among the prophetic oracles that directly concern the sending of the Holy Spirit, in which God speaks to the heart of his people in the language of promise (CCC 715). The Church sees the promise fulfilled at Pentecost and in every Baptism, when water and the Spirit make a person new. The first beneficiaries of the promise were the people of Israel, and the Church never forgets that God's gifts to them are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; CCC 839).

Acts 2:1–13 · When the day of Pentecost had come

Setting.

Pentecost, Greek for “fiftieth”, is the Jewish feast of Weeks (Shavuot), kept fifty days after Passover at the end of the grain harvest (Leviticus 23:15–21; Deuteronomy 16:9–12). It was one of the three pilgrim feasts, so Jerusalem was full of Jews from many lands. Later Jewish tradition linked this feast with the giving of the Law at Sinai, and some scholars think the link was already known in the time of the apostles.

Luke has prepared this moment. The risen Lord told the disciples to wait in the city for “the promise of the Father” (Acts 1:4), and the apostles devoted themselves to prayer, “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (1:14).

Structure.

  • v. 1: the day and the gathering.
  • vv. 2–4: wind, fire and the gift of the Spirit; they speak in other languages.
  • vv. 5–11: devout Jews from every nation hear in their own languages.
  • vv. 12–13: two reactions: wonder and mockery.
  • ¶ Peter's sermon follows (2:14–41), explaining the event from the prophet Joel.

Key words.

  • pnoē biaia, a violent wind: Pnoē, breath or blast of wind, is close to pneuma, spirit. As at creation (Genesis 2:7) and in Ezekiel's valley (Ezekiel 37), the breath of God gives life.
  • glōssai hōsei pyros, tongues as of fire: Fire is a sign of God's presence: the burning bush, the pillar of fire, the fire on Sinai (Exodus 3:2; 13:21; 19:18). John the Baptist had said that the Messiah would baptize “with the Holy Spirit and fire” (Luke 3:16).
  • dialektos, native language: The word in vv. 6 and 8 means the speech of a people or region. Luke stresses that each heard in his own tongue. The Gospel does not erase languages and cultures; it speaks in all of them.

Verse by verse.

“When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place.” (2:1)

Literally “when the day was being fulfilled”. The feast reaches its true meaning. The disciples are together, in unity and prayer, when the gift comes.

“Divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them.” (2:3)

One fire, divided among many, resting on each. The Spirit is given to the whole community and to every person in it.

“All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.” (2:4)

Luke means real languages that the hearers understood, as vv. 6–11 show. This differs from the gift of tongues Paul discusses in 1 Corinthians 14, which needed an interpreter.

“in our own languages we hear them speaking about God’s deeds of power.” (2:11)

The list of nations runs from Parthia in the east to Rome in the west. The first thing the Spirit does is to make the Church speak of God's great works.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that on the day of Pentecost, when the seven weeks of Easter had come to an end, Christ's Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, who is manifested, given and communicated as a divine person (CCC 731). Many Fathers saw here Babel undone: where pride had scattered the nations in confusion of speech (Genesis 11:9), the Spirit gathers them in one faith, each in his own language (for example St Augustine, in substance). The Church is catholic from her first day.

1 Corinthians 12:1–11 · Varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, around AD 54–55 (16:8). The church at Corinth was gifted but divided: rival parties, disorder at the Lord's Supper, and pride over spiritual gifts, especially speaking in tongues. Chapters 12–14 deal with these gifts. Chapter 12 gives the principle, chapter 13 the more excellent way of love, and chapter 14 practical order in worship.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the test of the Spirit: “Jesus is Lord”.
  • vv. 4–6: one Spirit, one Lord, one God in many gifts, services and activities.
  • v. 7: each gift is for the common good.
  • vv. 8–10: a list of gifts.
  • v. 11: one Spirit gives to each as he chooses.

Key words.

  • pneumatika, spiritual gifts: The word in v. 1 can mean “spiritual things” or “spiritual people”. The Corinthians may have used it proudly of themselves. Paul quickly turns to another word, charismata.
  • charismata, gifts: From charis, grace. A charism is a gift of grace, not a prize for holiness. It is given, never earned.
  • pros to sympheron, for the common good: Literally “for what is profitable”. Gifts are not trophies for display; they are tools for building up the body. Paul develops this with the picture of the body in vv. 12–27.

Verse by verse.

“no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit.” (12:3)

“Jesus is Lord” is perhaps the oldest Christian creed. To say it truly, with the heart, is already a sign of the Spirit at work.

“Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit;” (12:4)

Verses 4–6 name the Spirit, the Lord and God. The Church has long seen in this the pattern of the Holy Trinity: one God acting in many ways.

“To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good.” (12:7)

“To each”: no baptized person is without a gift. “For the common good”: no gift is only for oneself.

“All these are activated by one and the same Spirit, who allots to each one individually just as the Spirit chooses.” (12:11)

The Spirit is not a force we control but a person who chooses. Gifts are received with gratitude, not demanded.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the Holy Spirit with v. 3: no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit (CCC 683). It teaches that charisms, whether extraordinary or simple and humble, are graces of the Holy Spirit that benefit the Church, and that they must be received with gratitude and tested by the pastors of the Church, with charity as the true measure (CCC 799–801). The Second Vatican Council says the Spirit distributes special graces among the faithful of every rank (Lumen Gentium 12).

John 14:15–24 · Another Advocate, to be with you for ever

Setting.

These words belong to Jesus' farewell discourse at the Last Supper (John 13–17). He has washed the disciples' feet, foretold Judas' betrayal and Peter's denial, and told them he is going to the Father. They are troubled. Into their fear he speaks a promise: they will not be left alone. The Spirit is promised five times in these chapters (14:16–17; 14:26; 15:26; 16:7–11; 16:13–15).

Structure.

  • v. 15: love shown in keeping the commandments.
  • vv. 16–17: another Advocate, the Spirit of truth.
  • vv. 18–20: “I will not leave you orphaned; I am coming to you.”
  • v. 21: love, obedience and revelation.
  • vv. 22–24: Judas' question and Jesus' answer: we will make our home with them.

Key words.

  • paraklētos, Advocate: Literally “one called to the side” of another: a helper, defender, counsellor or comforter. Jesus says “another” Advocate, because he himself has been the first; 1 John 2:1 calls Jesus our paraklētos with the Father.
  • orphanous, orphaned: Disciples were often called the “children” of their teacher (13:33). Jesus will not leave his own like children without a father.
  • monē, home: The same word as the “dwelling places” in the Father's house (14:2). There the disciples go to the Father; here the Father and the Son come to the disciple. Heaven begins in the heart that loves.

Verse by verse.

“If you love me, you will keep my commandments.” (14:15)

Love is not only a feeling; it is shown in faithfulness. And keeping his word is itself a fruit of love.

“And I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Advocate, to be with you forever.” (14:16)

The Son asks, the Father gives, the Spirit comes. The three Persons of the Trinity appear together in one sentence.

“I will not leave you orphaned; I am coming to you.” (14:18)

Jesus comes to his disciples in his Resurrection, in the Spirit and at the end of time. All three are in view.

“and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” (14:23)

The goal of the whole Christian life: the indwelling of the Holy Trinity in the soul. Judas (not Iscariot) asked why Jesus would show himself to them and not to the world. The answer: he shows himself to those who love.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains the name Paraclete as “he who is called to one's side” and notes that Jesus calls the Spirit “another Paraclete” (CCC 692, 243). It quotes v. 23 to say that the ultimate end of the whole divine economy is that God's creatures enter into the perfect unity of the Trinity, and that even now we are called to be a dwelling for the Holy Trinity (CCC 260). Pentecost is not only an event in Jerusalem long ago. It is the beginning of God's life in each believer.

Questions people ask

Were the tongues at Pentecost foreign languages or a kind of ecstatic speech?

In Acts 2 Luke clearly means real languages, understood by people from many nations (2:6–11). Paul in 1 Corinthians 14 speaks of a gift of tongues that no one understood without an interpreter. The two are related but not the same. In both cases the Spirit is the giver, and in both the gift is meant to build up the Church.

If the Spirit is “another” Advocate, is he less than Jesus?

No. The Church confesses that the Holy Spirit is one God with the Father and the Son, “with the Father and the Son he is worshipped and glorified”, as the Nicene Creed says. “Another” means a distinct Person, not a lesser one. Jesus was the Advocate visible among the disciples; the Spirit is the Advocate within them.

Are gifts like healing and prophecy still given today?

Yes. The Catechism teaches that charisms, extraordinary or simple and humble, continue to be given for the good of the Church (CCC 799–800). Their genuineness is discerned by the Church, and charity remains the greatest gift (1 Corinthians 13; CCC 800–801). Most gifts are quiet: a catechist's patience, a parent's wisdom, a gift for consoling the sick.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

On this Sunday our Church celebrates the Pentecost service of kneeling. In three parts the Church kneels and prays to the Father, to the Son and to the Holy Spirit, asking the Spirit to come upon the people as on the apostles. The season of Qyomtho ends and the season of Pentecost begins. In the Syriac tradition the Spirit is also at the heart of every Holy Qurbono: in the invocation of the Holy Spirit (the epiclesis) the priest prays that the Spirit may come upon the bread and wine and make them the Body and Blood of Christ. In our Church the newly baptized are anointed with holy Myron at once, so that each of us received the Spirit as a gift from the first day of our Christian life.
