Word for the day

“But blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear.” Matthew 13:16

Reflection

Paul is in chains, and some preachers are using his imprisonment to make themselves look better. His reaction is astonishing: “What does it matter? Just this, that Christ is proclaimed in every way, whether out of false motives or true; and in that I rejoice.” He is free of rivalry because his only concern is that Christ be exalted in his body, “whether by life or by death.”

The disciples ask why Jesus speaks in parables. Parables are an invitation: those who want to understand lean in and ask; those who do not, hear without hearing. Isaiah foresaw a people whose hearts had grown dull. “But blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear. Truly I tell you, many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see.” We are among those who now see; what we do with that gift is our responsibility.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul is in chains, and some preachers are using his imprisonment to make themselves look better. His reaction is astonishing: what does it matter? Just this, that Christ is proclaimed in every way, and in that I rejoice. My hope is that Christ will be exalted now as always in my body, whether by life or by death (Philippians 1:18–20).

In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples: blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear. Many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see, but did not see it (Matthew 13:16–17).

We have been given the blessing the prophets longed for: to know Christ. That is greater than our own reputation. Today let us rejoice whenever Christ is proclaimed, even through others, and give thanks instead of being jealous. Amen.

For children

Children, when someone else does well, be happy instead of jealous. Praise a friend today!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, free us from rivalry, so that we rejoice whenever you are proclaimed. Open our eyes to see and our ears to hear, and be exalted in our bodies, in life and in death. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are about seeing and proclaiming. Paul, in chains, rejoices that Christ is proclaimed, even by those who preach out of rivalry. Jesus tells his disciples that they are blessed because they see and hear what prophets longed for. The Gospel is a gift to be received with open eyes and handed on with joy, whatever it costs the messenger.

Philippians 1:15–20 · Christ is proclaimed, and in that I rejoice

Setting.

Paul writes to the church at Philippi, the first church he founded in Europe and the one closest to his heart. He is in prison, most likely in Rome (or possibly Ephesus), waiting for a verdict that could mean death. Some local preachers have become bolder because of his imprisonment, but some are using it to compete with him.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: two kinds of preachers: some from love, others from rivalry.
  • v. 18: Paul's surprising verdict: Christ is proclaimed, and he rejoices.
  • vv. 19–20: his hope: by their prayers and the Spirit, all will turn to his salvation; Christ will be exalted in his body, by life or by death.

Key words.

  • eritheia, selfish ambition, rivalry: Originally the self-seeking of someone who works only for pay or for party. Paul uses it again in 2:3: “Do nothing from selfish ambition”.
  • sōtēria, deliverance, salvation: Paul borrows the words of Job 13:16 in the Greek Old Testament, “this will turn out for my salvation”. Like Job, he trusts that God will vindicate him, whatever the human verdict.
  • apokaradokia, eager expectation: A vivid word: to watch with head stretched forward. Paul uses it also in Romans 8:19 of creation waiting for God's children to be revealed.

Verse by verse.

“What does it matter? Just this, that Christ is proclaimed in every way, whether out of false motives or true; and in that I rejoice.” (1:18)

Paul cares more about Christ being preached than about who gets the credit. He does not approve of rivalry, but he refuses to let it rob him of joy.

“It is my eager expectation and hope that I will not be put to shame in any way, but that by my speaking with all boldness, Christ will be exalted now as always in my body, whether by life or by death.” (1:20)

“In my body”: Paul glorifies Christ not only with words but with his whole person, chains included.

How the Church reads it.

The next verse, “For to me, living is Christ and dying is gain” (1:21), is quoted by the Catechism in its teaching on Christian death (CCC 1010). The Church sees in Paul the model of the apostle who rejoices in the Gospel's progress more than in his own success, and who makes even prison a pulpit.

Matthew 13:10–17 · Blessed are your eyes, for they see

Setting.

Matthew 13 gathers seven parables of the Kingdom. After the parable of the sower (13:1–9), the disciples ask why Jesus speaks to the crowds in parables. His answer, before he explains the sower (13:18–23), quotes Isaiah 6:9–10, the words God gave the prophet at his call.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the disciples' question.
  • vv. 11–12: the gift: to know the secrets of the Kingdom; to the one who has, more is given.
  • vv. 13–15: the reason: they see and do not perceive; Isaiah's prophecy is fulfilled.
  • vv. 16–17: the blessing on the disciples, who see what prophets longed to see.

Key words.

  • mystēria, secrets, mysteries: Not puzzles but God's plan, once hidden and now revealed in Jesus. It is given, not earned.
  • parabolē, parable: Behind it stands the Hebrew māšāl, which covers proverbs, riddles and comparisons. A parable both reveals and hides: it opens itself to those who come closer.
  • epachynthē, has grown dull: Literally “has grown fat”: a heart thickened and insensitive, no longer able to feel God's word.

Verse by verse.

“He answered, “To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been given.” (13:11)
“The reason I speak to them in parables is that ‘seeing they do not perceive, and hearing they do not listen, nor do they understand.’” (13:13)

Mark 4:12 says “in order that” they may not see; Matthew says “because” they do not see. Matthew stresses that the hardness comes first, from the hearers, and that parables meet it with mercy, inviting them to look again.

“But blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear.” (13:16)

The disciples are not cleverer than the crowds. They are blessed because they stay near Jesus and ask him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus' invitation to the Kingdom comes in parables, which call for a decision: one must give everything, and words are not enough. To those who stay “outside”, everything remains enigmatic (CCC 546). The Church hears verse 16 as a word to every believer who hears the Gospel and receives the sacraments: what prophets and righteous people longed for is given to us.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus speak in parables so that people will not understand?

No. In Matthew the reason is that their hearts are already dull (13:15). Parables are a gentle way to draw the listener to come closer and ask, as the disciples did.

For study and teaching

  • Paul rejoices that Christ is preached even by rivals. Where do I need to let go of credit for the good I do?
  • What have I been given to “see and hear” that the prophets longed for? How can I thank God for it today?
