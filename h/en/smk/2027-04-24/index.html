Word for the day

“But the tax collector, standing far off, would not even look up to heaven, but was beating his breast and saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’” Luke 18:13

Reflection

Two men go up to the temple to pray, and Jesus lets us listen to both. The Pharisee, “standing by himself, was praying thus, ‘God, I thank you that I am not like other people: thieves, rogues, adulterers, or even like this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of all my income.’” Every word of his list may be true. He really fasts; he really gives. What is wrong is the direction of his gaze: he looks at himself, and then sideways at the tax collector, and hardly at God at all. Luke tells us at the start whom the parable is for: “some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt.” The tax collector, “standing far off, would not even look up to heaven, but was beating his breast and saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’” He has no list. He has only a need, and he names it. And Jesus gives the verdict: “this man went down to his home justified rather than the other; for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted.” St Augustine said the Pharisee came to the Physician and showed him only his healthy limbs, while the tax collector uncovered his wounds, and so only he was healed (in substance).

The Letter to the Hebrews shows what God does with the humble. It begins with a sober line that follows on from yesterday's feast of a martyr: “In your struggle against sin you have not yet resisted to the point of shedding your blood.” Then it speaks of discipline, quoting Proverbs: “My child, do not regard lightly the discipline of the Lord, or lose heart when you are punished by him; for the Lord disciplines those whom he loves.” God corrects us not as an angry master but as a father: “he disciplines us for our good, in order that we may share his holiness.” It hurts at the time, Hebrews admits honestly, “but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” So “lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees.” The reading then gives three warnings. “Pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.” Watch “that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble.” And do not become like Esau, “who sold his birthright for a single meal.” The tax collector let God's discipline reach him, and it healed him. The Pharisee had no room for it; he had already passed judgment on himself and on his neighbour.

Our Church gives us the tax collector's prayer on our lips again and again. In the Holy Qurbono and in every hour of the Shehimo we sing “Kurielaison,” Lord, have mercy, and we bow and make the sign of the Cross. These are not empty repetitions; they are the tax collector beating his breast. This Saturday, as the week ends, is a good day to prepare for Sunday by making a good confession, or at least an honest examination of conscience. Ask yourself the Pharisee's question in reverse: not “how am I better than others?” but “where do I need God's mercy?” Is there a root of bitterness in your family, an old quarrel that keeps sending up shoots? Pursue peace: make one call, send one message, offer one word of reconciliation before you go to the Qurbono tomorrow. The risen Lord, who showed his wounds to Thomas, is not ashamed of ours.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Two men went up to the temple to pray. The Pharisee prayed: God, I thank you that I am not like other people (Luke 18:11), and he gave God a list of his fasts and his gifts. The tax collector stood far off, beat his breast and said: God, be merciful to me, a sinner! (Luke 18:13).

Jesus says the second man went home justified, for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted (Luke 18:14). The Pharisee showed God only his healthy side. The tax collector showed God his wounds, and God healed him.

Hebrews tells us how God heals: by the discipline of a Father. The Lord disciplines those whom he loves (Hebrews 12:6), for our good, in order that we may share his holiness (Hebrews 12:10). So pursue peace with everyone, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord (Hebrews 12:14), and let no root of bitterness spring up.

In every Qurbono we pray the tax collector's prayer: Kurielaison, Lord, have mercy. Let us mean it. Today, prepare for Sunday: make a good confession if you can, and take one step to make peace with someone. The risen Lord, who was not ashamed to show his wounds, is not ashamed of ours. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Jesus told a story about two men praying. One said: God, I am so good, better than everyone! The other said: God, I have done wrong things, please forgive me. Which one do you think made God happy? The second one! God loves it when we are honest and say sorry. In church we say Kurielaison, which means Lord, have mercy. Tonight, before you sleep, tell Jesus sorry for one thing, and he will give you a big hug of forgiveness.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you rose from the dead and showed your wounds to your disciples. Deliver us from the pride that trusts in itself and looks down on others. Give us the humility of the tax collector, who stood far off and asked only for mercy. When the Father disciplines us, let us not lose heart, but receive it as the love that makes us share his holiness. Pull up every root of bitterness in our hearts and homes, help us to pursue peace with everyone, and bring us to see your face. Lord, have mercy on us. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Saturday closes the week with two lessons in humility before God. Hebrews asks Christians to accept hardship as a father's training and to pursue peace and holiness. In Luke two men pray in the temple, and only one goes home justified. Both readings ask who will see the Lord: the one who lets God correct him, and the one who asks for mercy.

Hebrews 12:4–17 · The Lord disciplines those whom he loves

Setting.

The readers have suffered, but “not yet” to the point of blood (v. 4). The writer helps them to understand their hardships by quoting Proverbs 3:11–12. In the ancient world it was taken for granted that a good father trains his children, with teaching and correction together. The writer does not say that every suffering is a punishment for a particular sin (compare John 9:3). He says that God can use every hardship to form his children.

Structure.

  • v. 4: the struggle against sin.
  • vv. 5–6: the word of Proverbs.
  • vv. 7–11: discipline as a father's training, and its peaceful fruit.
  • vv. 12–13: strengthen weak knees, make straight paths.
  • vv. 14–17: pursue peace and holiness; the warning of Esau.

Key words.

  • paideia, discipline: The whole upbringing of a child: teaching, training and correction. The word comes from pais, child.
  • hagiasmos, holiness: Being made holy, set apart for God. Verse 10 says that God trains us so “that we may share his holiness”.
  • bebēlos, godless: Profane, the opposite of holy: one who treats sacred things as common. Esau treated his birthright, the gift of God's promise, as worth one meal.

Verse by verse.

“for the Lord disciplines those whom he loves,” (12:6)

Proverbs 3:12. The discipline is the proof of the love, not its opposite.

“Now, discipline always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” (12:11)

“Trained” is an athletic word, gegymnasmenois, from which “gymnasium” comes. The fruit comes “later”.

“See to it that no one becomes like Esau, an immoral and godless person, who sold his birthright for a single meal.” (12:16)

Genesis 25:29–34. Esau traded a lasting gift for a passing hunger. Verse 17 recalls his tears when the blessing went to Jacob (Genesis 27:34–38).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the way of perfection passes by way of the Cross: there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle, and spiritual progress involves discipline and self-denial that lead gradually to the peace and joy of the Beatitudes (CCC 2015). Verse 14, holiness “without which no one will see the Lord”, echoes the Beatitude of the pure in heart, who will see God (Matthew 5:8).

Luke 18:9–14 · God, be merciful to me, a sinner

Setting.

Luke says plainly to whom the parable is told: “to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt” (18:9). Two men go up to the temple to pray. The Pharisee's practices go beyond what the Law required: the Law commanded fasting on the Day of Atonement, and he fasts twice a week; he tithes all he receives. What he says about himself is true. The trouble is the comparison and the contempt. The parable does not portray all Pharisees; it portrays a temptation that every devout person, Christians first of all, can fall into.

Structure.

  • v. 9: to whom the parable is told.
  • vv. 10–12: the Pharisee's prayer.
  • v. 13: the tax collector's prayer.
  • v. 14: the verdict, and the saying about exalting and humbling.

Key words.

  • exouthenountas, regarded with contempt: Treating others as nothing. This, more than his fasting, is what Jesus exposes.
  • hilasthēti, be merciful: A plea for atonement. The word is related to hilastērion, the mercy seat over the ark (Hebrews 9:5). He asks God to cover his sin.
  • dedikaiōmenos, justified: Made right with God. It is God's verdict, not the man's own claim.

Verse by verse.

“God, I thank you that I am not like other people:” (18:11)

The prayer begins with thanks, but it is really about himself. He asks for nothing, and so receives nothing.

“God, be merciful to me, a sinner!” (18:13)

He stands far off, does not look up, and beats his breast. His prayer is short and true. The Greek says “the sinner”, as if there were no other.

“for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted.” (18:14)

The same saying closes the parable of the places at table (14:11). In both, God is the one who lifts up.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls humility the foundation of prayer (CCC 2559), and names this parable as the third of Luke's parables on prayer, the prayer of the humble heart (CCC 2613). The cry of the tax collector, joined with that of the blind men, lives on in the Jesus Prayer of the Eastern Churches, “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on us sinners” (CCC 2667). In our Holy Qurbono the people answer again and again, “Kurielaison”, Lord, have mercy: the prayer of the tax collector on the lips of the whole Church.

Questions people ask

What does Hebrews mean when it says Esau “found no chance to repent”?

Genesis tells that Esau wept and begged for a blessing after Jacob had received it (Genesis 27:34–38). Many interpreters understand Hebrews to mean that he could not undo what was done: the birthright he had despised was gone, though his tears were real. Hebrews does not teach that God refuses a sinner who truly repents; the whole letter invites the readers to come near the throne of grace (4:16). It warns against trading a lasting gift for a passing appetite.

For study and teaching

  • What hardship in my life might God be using to train me? Can I ask for the “peaceful fruit” it may bring?
  • When I pray, do I compare myself with others? Can I pray the tax collector's prayer slowly today?
