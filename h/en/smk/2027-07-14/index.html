Word for the day

“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.” 1 Corinthians 3:6

Reflection

The Church at Corinth had split into parties. “What I mean is that each of you says, ‘I belong to Paul,’ or ‘I belong to Apollos,’ or ‘I belong to Cephas,’ or ‘I belong to Christ’” (1 Corinthians 1:12). Today St Paul answers with a picture that every farmer in Kerala understands: “What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you came to believe, as the Lord assigned to each. I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. So neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth.” In these weeks the seedlings of paddy are being transplanted into the flooded fields and the coconut saplings are going into the wet earth. Anyone who has done that work knows that the planter and the person who waters each have a task, and that both together cannot make a single seed grow. Life comes from God, and the servants are only servants. Paul does not make light of their labor: “The one who plants and the one who waters have a common purpose, and each will receive wages according to the labor of each.” The work is real, the reward is real, and the praise belongs to the Lord of the field.

Paul then gives two pictures of the Church: “For we are God's servants, working together; you are God's field, God's building.” A field and a building: a field that must be tended, patiently, season after season; a building that is raised stone upon stone. He goes on to say that he himself laid a foundation and another is building on it, and that “no one can lay any foundation other than the one that has been laid; that foundation is Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 3:10–11). The faith we received in India was planted and watered by many hands: the Apostle Thomas, according to tradition, who first sowed the Gospel on our shores; the mothers who taught prayers; the priests who kept the Qurbono alive in hard times. Tomorrow the Church of the Syro-Malankara tradition remembers one of those planters, Archbishop Mar Ivanios, who died on 15 July 1953. He founded the Bethany Ashram in 1919 and the Bethany Sisters in 1925, was received with Mar Theophilos and their companions into full communion with the Catholic Church on 20 September 1930, keeping the Syriac liturgy of the Malankara tradition, and in 1932 became the first Archbishop of Trivandrum. He planted in poverty and in tears, and the growth, in eparchies across India and beyond, has been God's. Tonight is a good evening to thank him, and to ask which part of the field has been given to us to plant or to water.

The Gospel brings us two men who could not see. “As Jesus went on from there, two blind men followed him, crying loudly, ‘Have mercy on us, Son of David!’” They cannot see the road, but they see who he is, for “Son of David” is the title of the promised Messiah, and the prophet had said that “the eyes of the blind shall be opened” (Isaiah 35:5). They follow him all the way into a house, and there Jesus asks them one question: “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” They said to him, “Yes, Lord.” “Then he touched their eyes and said, ‘According to your faith let it be done to you.’ And their eyes were opened.” Faith here is not a payment that buys a miracle; it is an open hand. Then Jesus sternly orders them: “See that no one knows of this.” Probably he wished to avoid becoming a mere wonder-worker before his hour had come. But the two men “went away and spread the news about him throughout that district.” Their gratitude was too great to keep quiet. The cry of the blind men is the cry that rises in every Holy Qurbono: Lord, have mercy. And like the Corinthians whose eyes were dim with party spirit, we too need our eyes opened to see that all growth is God's gift, and that we are one field, one building, one body in the one Lord.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul speaks to a divided community: some said, I belong to Paul, and others, I belong to Apollos. And Paul answers with a picture from the field: I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. So neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth (1 Corinthians 3:6–7). In these weeks of rain, our fields are being planted. We know that no farmer makes the seed grow. He prepares the soil, he plants, he waters, and then he waits for God.

Paul adds: you are God's field, God's building (1 Corinthians 3:9). A field needs care in every season; a building rises stone by stone. The faith we have received was planted and watered by many hands that we never saw: the Apostle Thomas, the first Christians of our land, parents and grandparents, priests who kept the Qurbono alive. Tomorrow the Church remembers Archbishop Mar Ivanios, who died on 15 July 1953. He worked and prayed for the unity of the Church and for the Malankara tradition, and he began in poverty. What he planted has grown, and the growth is God's.

In the Gospel, two blind men follow Jesus, crying, Have mercy on us, Son of David! (Matthew 9:27). They could not see the road, but they saw who he was. Jesus asks them: Do you believe that I am able to do this? Yes, Lord, they say. And he touches their eyes: According to your faith let it be done to you (Matthew 9:29). Faith is an open hand that receives. And when they were healed, they could not keep silent about him.

So tonight, let us thank someone who planted our faith. Let us do our part in the field faithfully, and give the glory to the Lord who gives the growth. And let us say, with the blind men, Lord, have mercy on us. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever planted a seed and watered it every day? You cannot make the seed grow yourself; only God does that. But you plant it and you water it, and God does the rest. St Paul says that we are all workers in God's garden: some plant, some water, and God makes it grow. Somebody planted our faith: our grandparents, our parents, the priests, the catechism teachers. Tomorrow we remember Archbishop Mar Ivanios, who planted something very important in our Church. In the Gospel two men who were blind cried, Jesus, have mercy on us! And Jesus opened their eyes. This week, plant something small, a seed or a flower, and water it, and say a thank-you prayer for the people who planted your faith.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the only foundation, and from you comes all growth. We thank you for those who planted and watered the faith in our land, and especially for Archbishop Mar Ivanios, whose memory we shall keep tomorrow. Make us faithful servants in your field, glad to plant and glad to water, and never proud of the harvest. Open our eyes, as you opened the eyes of the two blind men, to see you and to see one another as members of one building. Lord, have mercy on us. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today ask where the power in a Christian life comes from. In Corinth the believers were boasting about their teachers, “I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos”; Paul answers that teachers only plant and water, and that “God gave the growth.” In the Gospel two blind men follow Jesus and cry for mercy, and he answers “According to your faith let it be done to you.” In both, the work belongs to God, and our part is to serve him and trust him. The key words are growth and faith.

1 Corinthians 3:5–9 · God gave the growth

Setting.

Paul founded the Church in Corinth about AD 50–51 and stayed there for a year and a half (Acts 18:1–11). Afterwards Apollos came, a learned Jew from Alexandria, powerful in the Scriptures and in speech (Acts 18:24–28). When Paul writes from Ephesus a few years later, the community has split into parties: “I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos”, “I belong to Cephas”, “I belong to Christ” (1:12). Some people compared the teachers like favourite athletes.

In 3:1–4 Paul has told them that this rivalry shows how immature they still are. In our verses he gives a picture from the farm to put the teachers in their place, and to put God in his.

Structure.

  • v. 5: who are Apollos and Paul? Servants, each with the task the Lord gave.
  • v. 6: Paul planted, Apollos watered, God gave the growth.
  • v. 7: so the one who plants and the one who waters are nothing beside God.
  • v. 8: planter and waterer share one purpose, and each will receive a reward for his own labour.
  • v. 9: we are God’s servants working together; you are God’s field and God’s building.

Key words.

  • diakonoi, servants: The word means one who serves at table or carries out a task for another. Paul and Apollos are not rival masters but servants. Our word “deacon” comes from it.
  • auxanō, to make grow: Paul uses it twice, in verses 6 and 7. In verse 6 the Greek tense (“God was giving the growth”) suggests that God keeps on giving it, long after the planting and the watering are done.
  • theou synergoi, God’s servants working together: The Greek says literally “God’s fellow workers”. Paul does not make us equals of God; he says that God has chosen to carry out his work through human helpers, and that they labour together under him.

Verse by verse.

“What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you came to believe, as the Lord assigned to each.” (3:5)

Paul asks “what”, not “who”. The question reduces the teachers to their role. Each has a task the Lord has assigned, and none of them has a task of his own choosing.

“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.” (3:6)

Paul’s own work was the first preaching and the founding of the community; the work of Apollos was to teach and strengthen it. Both were necessary, and both were small beside the living power that makes a seed grow.

“So neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth.” (3:7)

This is not an insult to Paul or Apollos. It releases them, and us, from the weight of thinking that the Church lives by the talent of its leaders.

“The one who plants and the one who waters have a common purpose, and each will receive wages according to the labor of each.” (3:8)

The Greek says that planter and waterer “are one”. There is no ranking among them. The reward is real, and it is given to each “according to the labor”. The Church teaches that even this merit is itself a gift of God’s grace (CCC 2008).

“you are God’s field, God’s building.” (3:9)

Two images follow. The field is the Church seen as a living, growing thing; the building, which Paul develops in 3:10–17, shows her as a house with one foundation, Jesus Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always taken these verses as a lesson on how she grows. The Catechism says that the Church is God’s “tilled field”, and that the building of God is built on Christ the cornerstone (CCC 755–756). The Second Vatican Council gathers images such as the field and the building to describe the Church (Lumen Gentium 6). Priests, parents and catechists take comfort from verse 6: they sow and water, but the growth is God’s work, and the harvest will be seen in his time. And because the planter and the waterer have “a common purpose”, rivalry among those who serve in a parish is a sign that something has been forgotten.

Matthew 9:27–31 · According to your faith

Setting.

This is the second of a series of healings that Matthew gathers in chapters 8–9. It comes straight after the raising of the daughter of the synagogue leader and the healing of the woman with the flow of blood (9:18–26). Matthew likes pairs: he mentions two blind men here, and two again at Jericho (20:29–34), where Mark tells of one, Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46). The next story, a mute man who speaks, closes the series (9:32–34).

The cry of the blind men, “Son of David”, is the first time in Matthew that anyone other than the narrator gives Jesus this title in a healing story. It is the title of the promised king from David’s line, and the prophets said that in his time “the eyes of the blind shall be opened” (Isaiah 35:5).

Structure.

  • v. 27: two blind men follow Jesus, crying for mercy to the Son of David.
  • v. 28: in the house Jesus asks, “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” and they answer, “Yes, Lord.”
  • v. 29: he touches their eyes: “According to your faith let it be done to you.”
  • v. 30a: their eyes are opened.
  • vv. 30b–31: he orders silence, and they spread his fame throughout the district.

Key words.

  • eleēson, have mercy: The Greek of the cry is eleēson hēmas, “have mercy on us”, the same word as in the “Kyrie eleison” of the liturgy. It asks for help from someone who has the power and the kindness to give it.
  • pisteuete, do you believe: Jesus puts faith before healing. He asks first whether they trust that he is able, and their “Yes, Lord” shows that they call him more than a healer: they call him “Lord”.
  • enebrimēthē, he sternly ordered: A strong verb, used of a stern warning (see also Mark 1:43). Jesus does not want his healing to be known as the work of a wonder-worker before people understand who he is and what road he will take.

Verse by verse.

“two blind men followed him, crying loudly, “Have mercy on us, Son of David!”” (9:27)

The men cannot see, but they know who he is. The prayer is short and complete: a title that confesses his identity, and a request that trusts his kindness.

““Do you believe that I am able to do this?” They said to him, “Yes, Lord.”” (9:28)

Jesus’ question is not a test of their cleverness. It invites them to say, out loud, what they hope for. Their answer is simple, and nothing more is demanded.

““According to your faith let it be done to you.”” (9:29)

These words do not make faith a kind of magic power that forces God’s hand. Jesus is the one who heals; their faith opens their hands to receive what only he can give.

“Then Jesus sternly ordered them, “See that no one knows of this.”” (9:30)

In Matthew Jesus often asks for silence about his miracles (8:4; 12:16). He does not wish to be seen only as a wonder-worker, or as an earthly king, before the cross and the resurrection have been revealed.

“But they went away and spread the news about him throughout that district.” (9:31)

They do not obey. It is hard to blame people who have just received their sight for speaking, and Matthew records it without a word of reproach. Good news cannot be locked in.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ miracles are signs that invite faith in him; to those who come to him in faith, he grants what they ask (CCC 548). The Fathers of the Church often read the healing of the blind as a picture of the enlightenment of Baptism, when the eyes of the heart are opened to see Christ (in substance). In the Syriac liturgy the cry of the two men continues in every Holy Qurbono, when the people sing again and again “Kurielaison”, “Lord, have mercy”. The cry is not only for physical sight. We ask that he open our eyes to see his will, and the neighbour in need.

Questions people ask

If healing comes “according to your faith”, does a person who is not healed lack faith?

No. Jesus’ words are a promise to these two men, not a rule that every sick person will be cured if his or her faith is strong enough. The Church teaches that God hears every prayer, but his answer is his own: sometimes healing, sometimes strength to carry the cross, and always his closeness. Saint Paul prayed three times that a “thorn” be taken from him, and was told, “My grace is sufficient for you” (2 Corinthians 12:7–9). To say that an illness shows a lack of faith would add suffering to the sick.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that he planted and Apollos watered, and that God gave the growth. Who has planted or watered in my faith, and for whom do I do so?
  • The two blind men were asked, “Do you believe?” What would I answer if Jesus asked me today, “Do you believe that I am able to do this?”, about the greatest need in my life?
