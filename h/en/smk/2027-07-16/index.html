Word for the day

“But he said, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!”” Luke 11:28

Reflection

Today the Church remembers Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the two readings the Panchangam gives us look at the Mother of the Lord from two sides. The Epistle looks at her from the side of God's plan: “But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” This is the only place where St Paul mentions the mother of Jesus, and he does it without her name, in four words that hold the whole mystery: born of a woman. The Son of God did not come as an angel or a vision. He took flesh from a woman, and so truly that the Church, at the Council of Ephesus in 431, declared that she who bore him is to be called Mother of God, Theotokos. In our Syriac tradition the same truth is sung in the title Yoldath Aloho, she who gave birth to God (Catechism 495). St Cyril of Alexandria, who defended that title, asked how anyone who confesses that our Lord Jesus Christ is God could hesitate to call the Holy Virgin who bore him the Mother of God (Letter to the Monks of Egypt, in substance). And all this is for us: “And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, ‘Abba! Father!’ So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if a child then also an heir, through God.” The Son who was born of a woman makes us children of the Father.

The Gospel looks at Mary from the side of the heart. “While he was saying this, a woman in the crowd raised her voice and said to him, ‘Blessed is the womb that bore you and the breasts that nursed you!’” It is a simple woman's praise, the kind every mother in Kerala would understand: she blesses the mother because of the Son. And Jesus answers: “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” Some have heard this as if he were setting his mother aside, but the Church has always understood it the other way. The word “rather” does not take the blessing from Mary; it shows where her blessing comes from. Who ever heard the word of God and kept it as she did? “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). “Mary treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart” (Luke 2:19). Elizabeth had already told her, “blessed is she who believed” (Luke 1:45), and she herself had sung, “from now on all generations will call me blessed” (Luke 1:48). St Augustine said that Mary was more blessed in receiving the faith of Christ than in conceiving the flesh of Christ (On Holy Virginity 3, in substance). The Second Vatican Council, quoting this very passage, says that the Blessed Virgin herself, in the course of her Son's preaching, received the words by which he declared blessed those who hear and keep the word of God, as she was faithfully doing (Lumen Gentium 58, in substance). So the woman in the crowd and Jesus do not disagree. Mary is the first and best of those who hear and keep.

Why Carmel? Mount Carmel is the green headland on the coast of the Holy Land near Haifa, and its name means garden or orchard. The prophets sang of its beauty (Isaiah 35:2). It is above all the mountain of the prophet Elijah, whom we shall remember on 20 July. After three years of drought, Elijah went up to the top of Carmel, “bowed himself down upon the earth and put his face between his knees,” and sent his servant to look toward the sea. “There is nothing.” Seven times he sent him. “At the seventh time he said, ‘Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person's hand is rising out of the sea.’ ... In a little while the heavens grew black with clouds and wind; there was a heavy rain” (1 Kings 18:42–45). Anyone who has watched a Kerala sky in the first days of the monsoon, a small dark cloud at the edge of the sea growing into the rains, understands why the Christian imagination saw in that little cloud a sign of the Virgin Mary: from her, small and humble, came the One who is the rain of grace for a dry world. In the twelfth century hermits from the West settled on Mount Carmel, near the place remembered as the prophet's, and built a chapel dedicated to Our Lady. They became the Carmelite Order, and their patroness is Our Lady of Mount Carmel, whose feast is kept on 16 July.

The Carmelites spread her devotion in the sign of the scapular, two small pieces of brown cloth worn on the shoulders. According to a pious tradition, Our Lady gave it to St Simon Stock, an English Carmelite, on 16 July 1251; historians cannot confirm the details, but the devotion is old and has been blessed by the Church. The Church teaches that the scapular, like any sacramental, is a sign: of belonging to Mary, and of the wish to follow her, to hear the word of God and keep it. It cannot replace a life of faith, and it is the Son, not the cloth, who saves us. Here in Kerala, Carmel is a familiar name on churches, schools and convents, and the Carmelite family has served our land since 1831, when the first monastery of the Carmelites of Mary Immaculate was founded at Mannanam; one of its founders, St Kuriakose Elias Chavara, was canonised in 2014. And on the cross the Lord gave her to us: “Woman, here is your son.” “Here is your mother.” (John 19:26–27). Today, near the beginning of Karkidakam, when the rain is heaviest, let us ask the Mother of God to teach us the one blessedness her Son named: to hear the word of God and obey it.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we remember Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the Gospel gives us a scene full of warmth. A woman in the crowd calls out, Blessed is the womb that bore you and the breasts that nursed you! (Luke 11:27). She is praising Jesus' mother. And Jesus answers: Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it! (Luke 11:28). He does not take away Mary's blessing; he shows us its secret. Who heard the word of God like Mary? Let it be with me according to your word (Luke 1:38). She kept all these things in her heart (Luke 2:19).

In the Epistle, St Paul tells us what her yes made possible: God sent his Son, born of a woman, so that we might receive adoption as children (Galatians 4:4–5). The Son of God was born of the Virgin Mary; that is why the Church calls her Mother of God, and in our own Syriac language, Yoldath Aloho. And because he came, we are no longer slaves but children, and we cry Abba, Father. Everything in the Gospel begins with her yes.

Mount Carmel is a mountain by the sea. There the prophet Elijah bowed down and prayed after three years of drought. His servant looked at the sea seven times, and the seventh time he saw a little cloud no bigger than a person's hand (1 Kings 18:44). Soon there was a heavy rain. Many believers have seen in that little cloud a sign of Mary, small and humble, from whom came the Son who is the rain of grace. The Carmelites took her as their patroness and gave us the scapular, a sign of belonging to her and of the wish to follow her. The sign is not a charm; it points to the Son.

So in these days of rain, let us ask Our Lady of Mount Carmel to obtain for us the blessedness her Son spoke of: to hear the word of God and to obey it. Let us choose one word of the Gospel to keep this week. Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember Our Lady of Mount Carmel. Carmel is a green mountain by the sea where the prophet Elijah once prayed for rain. His servant looked at the sky seven times, and the last time he saw a little cloud, as small as your hand. And soon the rain came! Mary is like that little cloud, small and humble, and she brought us Jesus, who brings God's love like rain. Jesus tells us that people are happy when they hear God's word and do it, and Mary always did. Some people wear a little brown cloth called the scapular to remember to follow Mary. This week, choose one thing Jesus asks, like being kind or telling the truth, and do it all week. And say a Hail Mary every day.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, born of a woman in the fullness of time, you gave us the Virgin Mary to be your Mother and ours. On this feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel we thank you for her faith: she heard your word and kept it, and she pondered it in her heart. Through her prayers make us children who cry Abba, Father, and disciples who hear and obey. As the prophet Elijah saw the little cloud rise from the sea and the rain come to the dry land, so let the grace you have given through her refresh your Church and our country. Bless all who wear her sign and all the Carmelite family, and lead us by the path of her faith to you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today our calendar remembers Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the two readings look at the Mother of the Lord from two sides. Paul says that “when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman”: God’s plan has a place in it for a mother. In Luke a woman in the crowd cries, “Blessed is the womb that bore you!” and Jesus answers, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” Read together, the two readings show that Mary is blessed twice, as the mother of the Son of God and as the first and best hearer of his word. The key words are fullness of time and blessed.

Galatians 4:4–7 · God sent his Son, born of a woman

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Churches of Galatia, in the centre of what is now Turkey, to answer teachers who said that Gentile Christians must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to belong fully to God’s people. His answer, from chapter 3 onwards, is that all who are baptized into Christ are children of Abraham and heirs of the promise (3:26–29).

In 4:1–3 Paul compares the time before Christ to the time of a child who is heir to a great estate but is no better than a slave until the day his father has set. Our verses describe that day. The key event is that “God sent his Son”, and the result is that we are no longer slaves but children.

Structure.

  • v. 4: when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law.
  • v. 5: purpose: to redeem those under the law, that we might receive adoption.
  • v. 6: because we are children, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying “Abba! Father!”
  • v. 7: so no longer a slave but a child, and an heir through God.

Key words.

  • plērōma tou chronou, fullness of time: The time was “full” as a cup is full, ready to receive. God had prepared his people through the Law and the Prophets, and in his time he sent his Son.
  • exapesteilen, he sent out: Paul uses the same verb for the sending of the Son (v. 4) and for the sending of the Spirit of the Son (v. 6). The Father sends both, and the two sendings belong together: the Son in our flesh, the Spirit in our hearts.
  • huiothesia, adoption as children: A word from Greek and Roman law, for the full acceptance of a child as heir. Paul uses it for the new relationship that God gives us in Christ. We are not children by nature, as the Son is; we are made children by grace.
  • abba, Abba, Father: An Aramaic word from the language of Jesus, the word he used in his own prayer in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36). Paul keeps it in his Greek letters because it was treasured in the early community as the voice of the Spirit.

Verse by verse.

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law,” (4:4)

Paul’s phrase “born of a woman” says that the Son of God really became human, with a human mother. Paul does not name Mary here, but the Church has long seen her in these words. “Born under the law” means that Jesus was a Jew, who lived under the covenant given at Sinai.

“in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” (4:5)

The Son comes into our condition so that we may come into his. The Fathers of the Church said this in a brief saying, that the Son of God became man so that men might become sons of God (CCC 460, in substance).

“And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!”” (4:6)

The Spirit is the guarantee of sonship. Prayer is the sign: whoever says “Abba” in the Spirit is speaking as a child of the Father.

“So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if a child then also an heir, through God.” (4:7)

Paul says “you” in the singular. Each of us is addressed. We are not heirs because we earned it but “through God”, by his gift.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses verse 4 to speak of the Incarnation (CCC 422) and of Mary’s place in it: God sent forth his Son, “born of a woman”, and for that wished the willing co-operation of a creature (CCC 488). The Second Vatican Council begins its teaching on Mary with these words of Paul (Lumen Gentium 52). The Church also teaches that Mary is truly the Mother of God, because the child she bore is the Son of God in person. In the Syriac tradition this is the faith expressed in the title Yoldath Aloho, “Mother of God”. For Paul and for the Church, what is said about Mary is said for the sake of the Son.

Luke 11:27–28 · Blessed rather are those who hear

Setting.

Jesus has just driven out a demon that made a man mute, and some say that he does it by the power of Beelzebul (11:14–26). In the midst of this argument a woman in the crowd cries out in praise. Only Luke tells the story.

The woman’s cry is a blessing for the mother of Jesus. It is the kind of blessing that belongs to Luke’s Gospel: Elizabeth had said to Mary, “Blessed is she who believed” (1:45), and Mary herself had said in the Magnificat that “all generations will call me blessed” (1:48).

Structure.

  • v. 27: a woman in the crowd blesses the womb that bore Jesus and the breasts that nursed him.
  • v. 28: Jesus answers: blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and keep it.

Key words.

  • makaria, blessed, happy: The word the Bible uses for the person whom God has favoured (Psalm 1:1; Matthew 5:3–11). It is joy that comes from God, and not simply good fortune.
  • menoun, rather, indeed: This small word can mean “no, rather” or “yes, and more”. Many interpreters hear the second meaning here: Jesus does not take back the woman’s blessing, but he completes it and says what makes it greatest.
  • akouontes … phylassontes, hearing and keeping: To “keep” is to guard, as one guards a treasure. Luke uses closely related words of Mary, who “treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart” (2:19; see also 2:51).

Verse by verse.

“a woman in the crowd raised her voice and said to him, “Blessed is the womb that bore you and the breasts that nursed you!”” (11:27)

The woman speaks from ordinary human admiration for a mother. Her words are warm and true, and Jesus does not rebuke her for them.

“But he said, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!”” (11:28)

Jesus points to what makes his mother greatest. She was blessed because she heard the word of God, said “let it be with me according to your word” (1:38), and kept it to the cross. The beatitude is open to every disciple who does the same.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this Gospel as praise of Mary, not as a lesson against her. The Second Vatican Council quotes it: Jesus “declared blessed those who heard and kept the word of God, as she was faithfully doing” (Lumen Gentium 58). The Catechism calls Mary the most perfect example of the obedience of faith (CCC 148). St Augustine says in the same spirit that Mary was more blessed in receiving the faith of Christ than in conceiving the flesh of Christ (On Holy Virginity 3, in substance). Her motherhood and her faith are not two things: she became the mother because she believed, and she believed with all her being. In this we are invited to follow her.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus play down his mother here?

No. Luke has already shown Mary’s greatness (1:28–45), and the same Luke tells how she stood by her Son to the end (Acts 1:14). Jesus is not correcting the woman’s respect for his mother but saying what that respect should rest on. The Church honours Mary as Mother of God, and honours her most when it follows her example of hearing and keeping the word.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel comes from the Carmelite hermits who settled on Mount Carmel in the Holy Land in the twelfth century and built a chapel in honour of Mary. The same mountain is where the prophet Elijah called on the Lord (1 Kings 18), and we remember him on 20 July. In the Syriac tradition we call Mary Yoldath Aloho, the Mother of God, and the Fast of the Assumption (Shunoyo Nombu), which prepares us for her feast, begins on 1 August. Today’s readings ask us to love her as the first listener to the word.

For study and teaching

  • God sent his Son “born of a woman”. What does it mean to me that the Son of God had a human mother?
  • Mary was blessed for hearing and keeping the word of God. Which word of God have I heard recently that I have not yet “kept”?
