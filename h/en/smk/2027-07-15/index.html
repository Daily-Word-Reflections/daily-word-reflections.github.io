Word for the day

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” Hebrews 13:7

Reflection

The Letter to the Hebrews says: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Today the Syro-Malankara Church remembers her father, Archbishop Mar Ivanios, who died on 15 July 1953. We remember him not to look back, but to imitate his faith.

Geevarghese, born in 1882 at Mavelikara, was a brilliant scholar and teacher. Drawn to prayer and to the monastic life, he founded the Bethany Ashram (Order of the Imitation of Christ) in 1919 and the Bethany Sisters in 1925. Longing for the unity of the Church, he searched for the communion of the Malankara Church with the See of Peter. On 20 September 1930, with Bishop Mar Theophilos and a few companions, he was received into full communion with the Catholic Church, keeping the venerable West Syriac liturgy. In 1932 he became the first Archbishop of Trivandrum.

Like Abraham and Moses, he left security behind. The reunion cost him churches, properties and friends; he began again in poverty, in small chapels and simple houses. Like the servant in the parable who traded with his talents, he did not bury what he had received: his learning, his liturgy, his love for the Malankara tradition became a gift to the universal Church. “Well done, good and trustworthy slave; you have been trustworthy in a few things, I will put you in charge of many things.”

Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus could be his: “Keep watch over yourselves and over all the flock, of which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to shepherd the church of God that he obtained with the blood of his own Son… remembering the words of the Lord Jesus, for he himself said, ‘It is more blessed to give than to receive.’” Today we thank God for him, pray for the unity of all Christians, and ask God, if it is his will, to glorify his servant.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the Letter to the Hebrews tells us: “Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” Today our Church remembers her father, Archbishop Mar Ivanios, who went to God on 15 July 1953.

He was a scholar and teacher, but above all a man of prayer. He founded Bethany, the communities of the Imitation of Christ. And he carried a great longing: that the ancient Malankara Church be one with the Church of Peter. On 20 September 1930, with Mar Theophilos and a few others, he entered full communion with the Catholic Church, keeping the beautiful liturgy of our fathers.

It cost him a great deal. He left behind churches, properties and friendships. He began again in small chapels and sheds. But he trusted the promise God made to Moses: “I will be with you.” Like the faithful servant in the parable, he did not bury his talents. Today our Church, spread across India and the world, is the harvest of that sacrifice.

So let us imitate his faith: love for prayer, love for the liturgy, love for the unity of the Church. Let us pray for our Major Archbishop and bishops, for all Christians of Kerala to be one, and, if it is God's will, for the glorification of his servant Mar Ivanios. “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today our Church remembers Archbishop Geevarghese Mar Ivanios, who died on 15 July 1953. He is the founder and first Metropolitan Archbishop of the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, and the Holy Qurbono is offered today for the people. His cause for canonisation is in progress. The Lectionary gives a rich set of readings for a shepherd of the Church: the evening Gospel of the talents (Matthew 25:14–30), the sending of Moses with the promise “I will be with you” (Exodus 3:9–12), the death of Moses on Mount Nebo (Deuteronomy 34:1–8), the joy of the Lord’s servants (Isaiah 65:13–16), and Paul’s farewell to the elders of Ephesus, “Keep watch over yourselves and over all the flock” (Acts 20:25–35). The Panchangam also remembers Mar Kuriakose and Morth Yulithi (Sts Cyriacus and Julitta), the child and his mother who, according to tradition, were martyred at Tarsus.

We study three readings more closely. Ben Sira tells us to honour the Lord’s ministers and then to stretch out our hand to the poor, the mourning and the sick. The Letter to the Hebrews asks us to remember our leaders, to imitate their faith, and to follow Jesus “outside the camp”. In the Gospel the master puts one servant in charge of the household “to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time”, and blesses the servant who is found at work when he arrives. The key words are shepherd, faithfulness and the Lord who is the same.

Sirach 7:29–36 · Honour his ministers, and remember the poor

Setting.

The Book of Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus) was written in Hebrew by Jesus ben Sira, a teacher in Jerusalem, around 180 BC. His grandson translated it into Greek in Egypt, about 132 BC, as he tells us in his own preface. The Catholic and the Orthodox Churches receive it among the inspired books of the Old Testament; the Council of Trent listed it in the canon. It is a book of practical wisdom, as the Book of Proverbs is, and it shows what life in Israel looked like in the time before the Maccabees.

Chapter 7 gathers short sayings on how to treat other people: friends, family, servants, the priest and the poor. Our verses place two duties side by side: reverence for the Lord and his priests, and tenderness for the poor, the grieving and the sick. For Ben Sira, worship and mercy belong together.

Structure.

  • vv. 29–31: fear the Lord and honour his priests; give them the portion that the Law commands.
  • v. 32: stretch out your hand to the poor.
  • vv. 33–35: be generous to the living, faithful to the dead, a companion to those who weep, a visitor to the sick.
  • v. 36: in all you do, remember the end of your life.
  • ¶ The passage moves from the temple to the street, from the altar to the sickbed, and ends by reminding us that all of life is lived in view of its end.

Key words.

  • yir’at YHWH, fear of the Lord: In the wisdom books “fear” is not terror but reverent awe before God: the beginning of wisdom. It is a love that knows who God is and who we are.
  • kohănîm, priests: Verse 31 lists the portions that the Law gave to the priests of the Temple (see Leviticus 7:31–34; Numbers 18:8–20), who had no land of their own. The principle that God’s ministers should be supported by the community remains in the New Testament (1 Corinthians 9:13–14; 1 Timothy 5:17–18).
  • ta eschata, the last things, the end: The Greek of verse 36 says literally “remember your last things”. From this word the Church’s teaching on death, judgment and the world to come is called eschatology.

Verse by verse.

“With all your soul fear the Lord, and revere his priests.” (7:29)

Reverence for God is placed first, and reverence for his ministers follows from it. The priest is honoured because of the Lord whom he serves, not for his own sake.

“Fear the Lord and honor the priest, and give him his portion, as you have been commanded” (7:31)

Ben Sira says that the support of the ministers is not a favour but an obligation, “as you have been commanded”. Worship is not carried on without a community that provides for it.

“Stretch out your hand to the poor, so that your blessing may be complete.” (7:32)

Blessing is “complete” only when it reaches the poor. Another verse of the book says that almsgiving atones for sin (3:30). The same Church that honours the priest is bound to the poor.

“Do not avoid those who weep, but mourn with those who mourn.” (7:34)

Paul gives the same advice in other words: “Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15). Ben Sira asks for presence, not only advice.

“In all you do, remember the end of your life, and then you will never sin.” (7:36)

Remembering death is not meant to frighten but to give things their weight. A person who knows how short life is, and who will receive it back from God, is slower to waste it on sin.

How the Church reads it.

The Church receives Sirach as a book that teaches how faith and daily conduct fit together. Its list of mercies (visiting the sick, comforting the grieving, burying the dead) stands behind the Church’s tradition of the works of mercy (CCC 2447). The call to remember the end of life agrees with the Catechism’s counsel to prepare ourselves for the hour of death by prayer and by the sacraments (CCC 1014). St Athanasius, in his Festal Letter of AD 367, recommended Sirach, with a few other books, for those newly joining the Church who wished to be instructed in the word of godliness (in substance). Today, with Mar Ivanios in mind, we see a bishop’s double duty in these verses: to honour the altar, and to stand beside the poor.

Hebrews 13:7–16 · Jesus Christ is the same

Setting.

Hebrews is a long homily, which ends with a short letter-like conclusion. The writer does not name himself. The Eastern Churches, including the Syriac tradition, long attached the letter to Paul, while many scholars today think that it came from someone in Paul’s circle. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture in either case. It was written to Christians of Jewish background who were tempted to give up their faith under pressure, and the writer shows that Christ has fulfilled the worship that God gave to Israel.

Chapter 13 gives practical counsels: love one another, show hospitality, honour marriage, be content (13:1–6). Our passage then turns to the leaders of the community, and to the “altar” which Christians have.

Structure.

  • v. 7: remember your leaders, who spoke God’s word to you; imitate their faith.
  • v. 8: Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today and for ever.
  • v. 9: do not be carried away by strange teachings; let the heart be strengthened by grace.
  • vv. 10–12: we have an altar; the bodies of the sin-offerings were burned outside the camp, and Jesus suffered outside the city gate to sanctify the people with his own blood.
  • vv. 13–14: let us go to him outside the camp; here we have no lasting city.
  • vv. 15–16: through him let us offer a sacrifice of praise, and not neglect to do good and to share.

Key words.

  • hēgoumenoi, leaders: Literally “those who lead”. Verse 7 speaks of leaders who have died or gone from the community (they “spoke the word of God to you”), and verse 17 of those who now lead and keep watch over souls. The Church is held together by a chain of witnesses who pass on one faith.
  • thysiastērion, altar: The word used in the Greek Old Testament for the altar of sacrifice. For many Fathers and for the liturgical tradition, “we have an altar” points to the altar of the Eucharist, where the sacrifice of Christ is made present.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: An expression from the Greek Old Testament (Leviticus 7:12; Psalm 50:14, 23), which translates the Hebrew tôdâ, “thanksgiving”. The thank-offering was a meal of joy shared in the Temple. The Greek word for thanksgiving, eucharistia, gives the Eucharist its name.

Verse by verse.

“Remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.” (13:7)

The writer asks the community to look at the “outcome” of their leaders’ lives, a lifetime of service ending in faithfulness. We are asked to imitate their faith, not their personalities.

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” (13:8)

Leaders come and go, but Christ does not. The Church can change her language and her customs, but she cannot change her Lord. This sentence stands between the memory of the leaders and the warning against strange teaching.

“We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” (13:10)

The writer is speaking of the Old Covenant’s sin-offerings, which were burned outside the camp, so that the priests could not eat of them. He does not despise the Temple, which he elsewhere calls a shadow of heavenly things (8:5); he says that Christ’s sacrifice is the one to which those shadows pointed. The tradition of the Church reads “altar” as the Eucharistic altar.

“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood.” (13:12)

In the sacrifice of the Day of Atonement the bodies of the sin-offerings were burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27). Jesus was crucified outside the walls of Jerusalem (John 19:20). The letter sees that as an act of love for all who stand outside.

“For here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.” (13:14)

This is the pilgrim’s verse. Christians love their homeland and serve it, but their final home is the city of God (Hebrews 11:10; Revelation 21).

“let us continually offer a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name.” (13:15)

Praise flows from the one sacrifice of Christ, which we offer and receive at the altar. It does not replace that sacrifice; it is our response to it, and it goes out into the day.

“Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” (13:16)

The writer adds to praise the work of mercy. A life of praise and a life of sharing are one sacrifice. They are the fruit of the grace of verse 9, which strengthens the heart.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in verse 15 a name for the Eucharist itself: the Catechism lists “sacrifice of praise” (Hebrews 13:15) among the names of the Holy Qurbono (CCC 1330), and teaches that the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice (CCC 1367). The “spiritual sacrifices” of praise and of mercy flow from the Eucharist and lead back to it; they do not replace it. Verse 7 shows the Church’s respect for her pastors, and verse 8 her conviction that the faith handed down is the faith of the living Christ.

Matthew 24:45–51 · The faithful and wise servant

Setting.

These verses come from Jesus’ last great discourse in Matthew (chapters 24–25), given on the Mount of Olives in the last days before his Passion. After describing the signs of the end, Jesus says that no one knows the day or the hour (24:36), and then gives a series of short stories on being ready: Noah’s days, the householder and the thief, and our parable. In chapter 25 the discourse continues with the ten bridesmaids, the talents (the evening Gospel today) and the last judgment.

The story is about a servant whom the master leaves in charge of the household while he is away. The servant’s task is a very simple one: to see that those under his care have food “at the proper time”. The master’s delay is the test. Matthew’s readers, waiting for the Lord’s return, understood that this was their own situation.

Structure.

  • v. 45: the question: who is the faithful and wise servant put in charge of the household?
  • vv. 46–47: blessed is the servant found at work; he will be given charge of all the master’s possessions.
  • vv. 48–49: the wicked servant: “My master is delayed”; he beats his fellow servants and eats and drinks with drunkards.
  • vv. 50–51: the master comes unexpectedly, and the servant is severely punished.
  • ¶ A question opens the parable, and the two answers (the faithful and the unfaithful servant) show what is at stake.

Key words.

  • phronimos, wise, sensible: The same word is used of the man who builds his house on rock (7:24) and of the five bridesmaids who bring oil (25:2). Biblical wisdom is practical: to do the right thing in the right way because one expects the Lord.
  • trophē en kairō, food at the proper time: The servant’s task is to feed the household. The picture suits shepherds of the Church: to give the people the word and the sacraments when they need them, not when it suits the shepherd.
  • chronizei, is delayed: The verb is the same one used in the parable of the bridesmaids, where the bridegroom “was delayed” (25:5). The temptation is to say, “My master is delayed” (24:48), and to behave as if there were no master.

Verse by verse.

“Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom his master has put in charge of his household, to give the other slaves their allowance of food at the proper time?” (24:45)

Jesus asks a question rather than makes a statement, and so each hearer must ask, “Am I that servant?” The trust given to the servant is a gift; the servant is a “slave” (doulos), not the owner.

“Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives.” (24:46)

The servant is not found anxious or idle. He is “at work”, doing quietly what he was asked to do. Faithfulness is not noisy; it is ordinary tasks done in the master’s name until he comes.

“Truly I tell you, he will put that one in charge of all his possessions.” (24:47)

The reward is greater responsibility, a share in the master’s own concerns. In the Kingdom, faithfulness in a small trust opens into a greater one (see 25:21).

“But if that wicked slave says to himself, ‘My master is delayed,’” (24:48)

The sin begins in the heart, “says to himself”, before it appears in action. Whoever has stopped expecting the Lord soon begins to treat others badly.

“the master of that slave will come on a day when he does not expect him and at an hour that he does not know.” (24:50)

The warning echoes 24:36 and 24:42–44. The point is not to calculate dates but to live every day as one who is accountable.

“where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.” (24:51)

These are Matthew’s words for the loss of the Kingdom (see 8:12; 13:42, 50; 22:13; 25:30). The harsh image (“cut him in pieces”) is the language of warning. It shows how seriously Jesus takes the abuse of trust, above all by those who have charge of others.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read this parable especially of those who are put in charge in her household: bishops, priests, parents and teachers, and, in a wider way, every Christian, who must give an account of what he or she has been given (CCC 1036). The Catechism also reminds us that God wishes all to be saved, and that no one is predestined to damnation; a person must by his or her own choice turn away from God for that to happen (CCC 1037). The parable is therefore a call to vigilance, not a threat made to frighten us. In the Holy Qurbono we pray for our shepherds by name, because the faithfulness described here is hard and needs grace.

Questions people ask

Why do we read Sirach, which is not in every Bible?

The Catholic and Orthodox Churches have received Sirach as inspired Scripture since early times; the Greek Old Testament, which the apostolic Church used, contained it, and the Council of Trent confirmed it in the canon. Some Christian communities place it among books they read for edification but not for doctrine, and the Jewish canon does not include it, although Jewish teachers quoted from it for centuries. We read it with respect for all, and because the Church has found in it much wisdom for daily life.

Does “We have an altar” mean that Christians offer only a sacrifice of praise?

No. Hebrews says that Christ gave himself once for all (9:26–28; 10:10), and the Church believes that this one sacrifice is made present in the Holy Qurbono, so that we share in it (CCC 1367). The “sacrifice of praise” is the thanksgiving of the Eucharist (eucharistia means thanksgiving), and it spreads into the praise and mercy of our daily life. Our praise and our good works are offered through Christ; they depend on his sacrifice and do not replace it.

How can a loving master punish a servant so severely?

Jesus is describing the seriousness of unfaithfulness in strong, traditional language. The parable aims to wake the hearer, not to make God look cruel. The Church teaches that God does not will anyone’s ruin, and that the judgment follows a person’s own choice to refuse him (CCC 1037). The servant of the parable is not condemned for a small mistake but for beating his fellow servants and living in selfish indulgence, after he forgot that he had a master.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Mar Ivanios was born in 1882 in Mavelikara, and was named Geevarghese. Before 1930 he was a teacher and a man of prayer, and he founded the Bethany Ashram and the Bethany Sisters, communities of prayer and service. On 20 September 1930 he and a few companions were received into full communion with the Catholic Church, asking to keep their own Syriac liturgy and the traditions of the Malankara Church. In 1932 the hierarchy of the new Church was established, and he became its first Metropolitan Archbishop, at Thiruvananthapuram. He died on 15 July 1953, and his tomb is at St Mary’s Cathedral, Pattom.

The day’s readings help us to read his life. The leaders of Hebrews leave a faith to be imitated; Jesus Christ is “the same yesterday and today and forever”. The servant of the Gospel gives food at the proper time: he cared for the poor and the sick, and he opened schools and homes. And Paul’s words in Acts 20:28, “Keep watch over yourselves and over all the flock”, are the words of an apostle to the elders of Ephesus. We honour all who love and serve the Church in every part of the Body of Christ; and in the Qurbono we pray, as Christ prayed, “that they may all be one” (John 17:21).

For study and teaching

  • Ben Sira asks us to honour the Lord’s ministers and also to stretch out a hand to the poor. Which of the two is easier for me, and what would the other ask of me this week?
  • The letter asks me to remember those who first spoke the word of God to me. Who were they? What have I never thanked them for?
  • “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” What in my life, or in the life of the parish, changes constantly? Where do I need this fixed point?
  • The faithful servant is found “at work”. What is the ordinary task that the Lord has entrusted to me, and what does faithfulness in it look like today?
