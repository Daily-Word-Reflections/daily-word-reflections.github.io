Word for the day

“all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another;” 2 Corinthians 3:18

Reflection

St Paul puts two meetings with God side by side. When Moses came down from the mountain his face shone, and he covered it with a veil. Paul reads that veil as a sign: the glory on Moses' face was real, but it was passing, and it pointed ahead to a glory that does not fade. He writes as a son of Israel, with grief and love and no contempt; the Church still honours the Law and the Prophets as the word of God, and asks only to read them with Christ in view, for “only in Christ is it set aside.” Then comes the sentence at the heart of today's Epistle: “but when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.” Turning is the whole work of the Christian life. Each morning we turn from our worries, our screens and our small resentments towards the face of the Lord, and as we turn, something is lifted from our eyes.

Paul goes on: “And all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another; for this comes from the Lord, the Spirit.” This is a promise about prayer. We become like what we look at. A child who watches a screen all day begins to talk like the screen; a believer who looks every day at the face of Christ, in the Gospel, at the altar and in the poor, slowly begins to look like him. The change is not our own achievement. It “comes from the Lord, the Spirit,” who works quietly, from one degree of glory to the next, as a monsoon slowly turns a brown hillside green.

Today the Panchangam sets before us a man who spent his life looking at that face. Youssef Makhlouf was born in 1828 in the mountain village of Bekaa Kafra in Lebanon. At twenty-three he left home to become a monk at the monastery of St Maron at Annaya, and he took the name Sharbel, the name of an early Syriac martyr. He was ordained priest in 1859. In 1875, with the permission of his superiors, he went to the small hermitage of Sts Peter and Paul above the monastery, where he lived for twenty-three years in poverty, fasting, silence and prayer. According to his biographers he was stricken with a stroke while celebrating the Holy Qurbono in December 1898 and died on Christmas Eve; Pope Paul VI beatified him in 1965 and canonised him in 1977. His Church, the Maronite Church, shares with our own the Antiochene, West Syriac heritage, and its holy Qurbono is prayed in the same family of ancient rites. His feast is kept on 24 July because 24 December is the vigil of Christmas.

In the Gospel three would-be disciples meet the Lord on the road. To the first, who promises to follow wherever he goes, Jesus says that foxes have holes and birds of the air have nests, “but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head”: whoever follows him must be ready for a life whose only security is the Lord himself. To the second he says, “Follow me,” and when the man asks first to bury his father he answers, “Let the dead bury their own dead; but as for you, go and proclaim the kingdom of God.” These are hard words, and they do not despise the fourth commandment; they say that when God calls, nothing may stand in front of him. To the third he says, “No one who puts a hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God.” Every farmer in the wet paddy knows that a ploughman who looks back cuts a crooked furrow. Not everyone is called to a hermitage, and the Church does not ask a father, a nurse or a teacher to leave home; but she asks every one of us to put a hand to the plow and not to look back, and to let the Lord come first in the house, the shop and the hospital ward.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul tells us that there is a veil over our hearts that only Christ can lift: when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed (2 Corinthians 3:16). Moses covered his shining face with a veil; but we, with unveiled faces, see the glory of the Lord as in a mirror, and we are changed into his image. We become like what we look at, so what are we looking at?

The Panchangam remembers St Sharbel, a Maronite monk of Lebanon. He left home at twenty-three, became a monk, then a priest, and for twenty-three years lived as a hermit above the monastery of Annaya, in poverty, fasting and prayer. He was stricken while celebrating the Holy Qurbono and died on Christmas Eve 1898. Pope Paul VI canonised him in 1977. His Church belongs, like ours, to the West Syriac family.

In the Gospel Jesus asks for a following that does not turn back. The Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head (Luke 9:58). No one who puts a hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God (Luke 9:62). Few are called to a hermitage, but everyone is called to keep the Lord first.

So let us turn to the Lord every morning, give him a quiet half hour in the week, and finish what we have begun in his name. Let us not look back. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever walked in the wet rain with your umbrella and looked at the puddles? You see your own face in the water as in a mirror. St Paul says that when we look at Jesus, it is like looking into a mirror that makes us look more and more like him. St Sharbel loved to look at Jesus in prayer, so much that his whole life shone. Every day, say a little prayer and look at the cross. Jesus will make your heart shine too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the glory of the Father and the face in which we see him. Take away the veil that lies over our hearts, and turn our eyes to you every day. Through the prayers of St Sharbel, who sought you in silence and in the Holy Qurbono, teach us to love prayer, to follow you without looking back, and to be changed from one degree of glory to another by your Holy Spirit. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about turning towards the Lord and not looking back. In 2 Corinthians Paul says that when anyone turns to the Lord the veil is taken away, and that with unveiled faces we are being transformed into the Lord's image. In Luke, Jesus meets three would-be disciples on the road and tells them what following him will cost. Today the Panchangam remembers St Sharbel, the monk and hermit of Lebanon (1828–1898), who left everything to follow Christ. The key words are veil, image and follow.

2 Corinthians 3:12–18 · With unveiled faces

Setting.

In 2 Corinthians 3 Paul defends his ministry by comparing it with the ministry of Moses. He writes to Corinthians who have been troubled by rival teachers, and he calls the Corinthians themselves his “letter of recommendation”, written by the Spirit on human hearts (3:1–3). Then he reads the story of Exodus 34:29–35, where Moses came down from Sinai with his face shining, and put a veil over it. Paul gives that old story a new meaning.

Paul himself was a Jew and a loyal one, and he did not say that the old covenant was bad. He calls Moses' ministry glorious (3:7). His point is that what came with Christ is more glorious still, and that to read the Law of Moses without Christ is to read with a veil over the mind. The Church has always said that the gifts and call of God to Israel are irrevocable (Romans 11:29).

Structure.

  • v. 12: because of such a hope we act with great boldness.
  • v. 13: unlike Moses, who veiled his face.
  • vv. 14–15: the veil remains on minds when the old covenant is read, since only in Christ is it set aside.
  • v. 16: when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.
  • vv. 17–18: the Lord is present as the Spirit, where there is freedom; with unveiled faces we are transformed into his image.

Key words.

  • parrēsia, boldness: Plain, open speech without fear. The apostles have nothing to hide, because the glory they proclaim is not fading. Boldness is the mark of a Christian witness (Acts 4:13, 31).
  • katoptrizomenoi, seeing as in a mirror: The word can mean “beholding as in a mirror” or “reflecting like a mirror”. Both ideas are true: we see the Lord's glory, and, like polished metal, we begin to shine with it.
  • metamorphoumetha, we are being transformed: The verb used for the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:2). The change is slow and continuous: “from one degree of glory to another”.

Verse by verse.

“Since, then, we have such a hope, we act with great boldness,” (3:12)

Hope is the source of boldness. The apostle is not bold because he is clever or strong, but because what he has seen in Christ is lasting.

“but when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.” (3:16)

The sentence echoes Exodus 34:34, where Moses removes the veil whenever he enters the presence of the Lord. Paul now says this is true of every person who turns to the Lord.

“Now the Lord is the Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom.” (3:17)

Paul does not mean that the Son and the Spirit are the same person. He means that the Lord who met Moses is now present among us through the Spirit, who gives the freedom of the children of God.

“And all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another;” (3:18)

We are transformed by looking at the Lord. The more we look at him in the Gospel, in prayer and in the Holy Qurbono, the more we come to resemble him.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen in this passage the goal of Christian life. The Catechism says that the Son of God became man so that we might share in the divine nature (CCC 460; 2 Peter 1:4). The Syriac Fathers, and Mar Aphrem above all, speak of the glory that Adam lost as a “robe of glory”, which Christ restores to the baptized (in substance). On the Jewish people, the Church teaches that the Old Covenant has never been revoked (CCC 121), and that the Jews remain most dear to God (Nostra Aetate 4). Paul's “veil” is a mystery of reading Scripture, not a verdict on any people.

Luke 9:57–62 · Follow me, and do not look back

Setting.

In Luke 9:51 Jesus “set his face to go to Jerusalem”. From there Luke tells the long journey to Jerusalem (9:51 to about 19:28), in which Jesus teaches what it means to follow him on the road to the Cross. Just before our verses a Samaritan village has refused to receive him (9:52–56). Now, on the road, three people come forward or are called, and each receives a hard answer.

The first is eager but has not counted the cost; the second is called by Jesus himself but asks for a delay; the third offers to follow after a farewell. The answers sound severe, but they show how serious the call is. The last scene recalls Elijah's call of Elisha, who was ploughing with twelve yoke of oxen. Elisha asked to kiss his father and mother, and Elijah allowed it (1 Kings 19:19–21). Jesus asks for even more.

Structure.

  • vv. 57–58: the first man: “I will follow you wherever you go”; Jesus: the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.
  • vv. 59–60: the second: “first let me go and bury my father”; Jesus: proclaim the kingdom of God.
  • vv. 61–62: the third: “let me first say farewell”; Jesus: whoever looks back from the plough is not fit for the kingdom.

Key words.

  • akolouthein, to follow: In the Gospels to follow Jesus means to be a disciple: to walk behind him, to learn from him, to share his road. It is not a feeling but a way of life.
  • euthetos, fit: Literally “well placed” or “useful”. A ploughman who keeps looking back cannot plough a straight furrow. The kingdom needs the whole attention of the one who serves it.
  • diangelle, proclaim: The word means to announce far and wide. To the man whose first thought was a funeral, Jesus gives another urgent task: the kingdom must be announced.

Verse by verse.

“Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests; but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.” (9:58)

Jesus has no house of his own on the road. He asks his disciples to share a life that is poor and on the move, which is also how he lived.

“But Jesus said to him, “Let the dead bury their own dead; but as for you, go and proclaim the kingdom of God.”” (9:60)

The saying is hard. Burying the dead is a duty of mercy (Tobit 1:17–19), and Jesus does not despise it. Many commentators think the man's father was perhaps not yet dead, and that the request meant waiting for the father's death. In either case, “the dead” are those who do not yet know the life of the kingdom, and the call of God comes first.

“No one who puts a hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God.” (9:62)

Lot's wife looked back (Genesis 19:26). To plough a straight furrow, one must look ahead. Following Christ means to move forward with full attention.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these three answers as teaching on vocation. The Catechism says that family ties are important but not absolute, and that as a child grows to maturity, the vocation that comes from God asserts itself more clearly (CCC 2232). It also teaches that Christ proposes the evangelical counsels to every disciple in various ways (CCC 915), and that burying the dead is one of the corporal works of mercy (CCC 2447). Neither call cancels the other: the Gospel asks for first place in the heart, and everything else is then loved rightly.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus say we should not bury our dead or say goodbye to our families?

No. The Church honours the dead and teaches respect for the body (CCC 2300), and Jesus himself was buried with care. These sayings are strong, even shocking, on purpose, to show that the call of the kingdom cannot be delayed and must have first place. The same Lord commands the love of father and mother (Mark 7:10–13). The Gospel asks for the right order of love, not for the loss of the other loves.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

St Sharbel, born Youssef Makhlouf in 1828 at Bekaa Kafra in northern Lebanon, left his village at twenty-three to become a monk of the Lebanese Maronite Order, taking the name of an early martyr of Edessa. He lived many years in the monastery of St Maron at Annaya, was ordained priest in 1859, and from 1875 lived as a hermit in prayer, silence and manual work. He died on Christmas Eve 1898 after a stroke suffered while celebrating the Holy Qurbono. Pope Paul VI beatified him in 1965 and canonized him in 1977. Pilgrims of many faiths still come to his tomb at Annaya. The Maronite Church, like our own Church, belongs to the West Syriac tradition, and in Sharbel the Church sees the monk who followed the Lord without looking back.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that with unveiled faces we are transformed by looking at the Lord. How much of my day is spent looking at him: in Scripture, in prayer, in the Holy Qurbono?
  • Jesus gave three people three hard answers. Which of the three, the eager one, the one who asked for a delay, or the one who wanted to look back, is closest to me today?
