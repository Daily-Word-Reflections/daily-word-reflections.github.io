Word for the day

“Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.” Romans 5:1

Reflection

Christ is risen! The first gift of the risen Lord to his frightened disciples was peace: “Peace be with you” (John 20:19). Today St Paul explains what that peace is. “Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand; and we boast in our hope of sharing the glory of God.” Peace with God is not first a calm feeling. It is a new relationship: the enmity of sin is ended, the door is open, and we stand in grace. Christ “was handed over to death for our trespasses and was raised for our justification,” Paul has just written (Romans 4:25). Easter is the reason we can stand before God unafraid. Then Paul says something surprising: “And not only that, but we also boast in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” Suffering does not cancel the peace of Easter; in Christ it becomes a road to hope.

Today the Panchangam commemorates St Julius, Bishop of Rome from 337 to 352. His years fell in the storm that followed the Council of Nicaea, when many powerful men sought to soften the faith that the Son is truly God, of one being with the Father. St Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria who defended that faith, was driven from his see, and he came to Rome. Julius received him, examined his case with a synod in Rome, declared him innocent, and wrote a firm letter to the Eastern bishops who had condemned him, asking why the matter had not been referred to Rome as custom required. Julius did not fear the anger of emperors or bishops; he welcomed the persecuted shepherd. The Gospel of the day reads like a portrait of him: “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” In welcoming Athanasius, Julius welcomed Christ, and helped to keep the faith of Nicaea, which we still confess in the Creed at every Holy Qurbono.

Jesus ends with the smallest gift: “and whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple, truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward.” The Fathers loved this verse. St John Chrysostom pointed out that the Lord said “cold” water, so that no one could say he was too poor even to light a fire (in substance). Welcome is within everyone's reach. It may be a refugee family in the next street, a migrant worker from the north at your bus stop, a priest newly transferred to your parish, a student far from home. To welcome them in Christ's name is to welcome Christ, and the Father who sent him. Pray today for the Pope and for all who suffer for the true faith. Then give one cup of cold water: a meal, a phone call, a seat, a word of welcome to someone who needs it. That is the peace of Easter going out from your heart.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! The first word of the risen Lord to his disciples was peace. St Paul tells us what it means: since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 5:1). We stand in grace, unafraid.

Even suffering is changed. Suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:3–5).

Today we remember St Julius, Pope. When St Athanasius was driven out of Alexandria for defending the faith that the Son is truly God, Julius welcomed him in Rome and stood up for him. Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, says Jesus (Matthew 10:40).

Welcome is within everyone's reach: even a cup of cold water given in his name will not lose its reward. Let us give someone that cup this week: a meal, a visit, a word of welcome. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Jesus said that if we give even a glass of cold water to someone in his name, God will never forget it. Long ago Pope Julius welcomed a bishop who had been chased away. Can you welcome someone this week, maybe a new child in your class? When you welcome them, you are welcoming Jesus!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you have given us peace with the Father and poured God's love into our hearts through the Holy Spirit. By the prayers of St Julius, who welcomed your persecuted servant Athanasius and guarded the faith of Nicaea, keep our Pope and our bishops firm in the truth. Turn our sufferings into endurance and hope, and make us quick to welcome the least of your brothers and sisters, even with a cup of cold water. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church remembers St Julius, Bishop of Rome from 337 to 352. St Paul tells us what the Easter peace is: “since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,” and even suffering becomes a road to a hope that does not disappoint. In Matthew, Jesus says that whoever welcomes his messengers welcomes him, and that not even a cup of cold water given to a little one will be forgotten. St Julius lived both readings: he stood firm in the faith of Nicaea and welcomed St Athanasius when he was driven from his see.

Romans 5:1–5 · We have peace with God

Setting.

In Romans 1–4 Paul has shown that all, Jews and Gentiles alike, need the grace of God, and that God makes people righteous through faith in Christ, as he reckoned Abraham righteous through faith (4:3). Chapter 4 ends with an Easter confession: Jesus “was handed over to death for our trespasses and was raised for our justification” (4:25). Chapter 5 begins with what follows from this.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: justified by faith, we have peace with God, access to grace and the hope of glory.
  • vv. 3–4: we boast even in sufferings: suffering, endurance, character, hope.
  • v. 5: hope does not disappoint, for God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit.

Key words.

  • dikaiōthentes, justified: Made righteous, set right with God. In the Catholic faith this is not only a verdict: God forgives sin and truly renews the person within (CCC 1989).
  • dokimē, character: Literally the quality of something that has been tested and proved, like metal that has passed through the fire.
  • ekkechytai, has been poured: The tense tells of something done that goes on: the love was poured and is still flowing. The image recalls God's promise, “I will pour out my spirit on all flesh” (Joel 2:28).

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,” (5:1)

A note on the text. Many ancient manuscripts read “let us have peace” instead of “we have peace”; in Greek the two differ by a single letter. Most editors prefer “we have”, as the NRSV does, while the Malayalam POC follows the other reading. Both are true: peace is a gift given, and a gift to be lived.

“through whom we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand;” (5:2)

Grace is not a passing visit but ground to stand on.

“and hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” (5:5)

Hope does not put us to shame, for it rests not on our own strength but on God's love already given. “God's love” here is first God's love for us, and it becomes our love for him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 5 when it teaches that God is Love and that love is his first gift, poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit (CCC 733). It calls justification the most excellent work of God's love (CCC 1994). St Augustine returned to this verse again and again, above all in his work On the Spirit and the Letter, where it is the key to his teaching that grace is the love of God given to us (in substance).

Matthew 10:40–42 · Whoever welcomes you welcomes me

Setting.

These verses close the missionary discourse of Matthew 10, in which Jesus sends the Twelve to proclaim that the Kingdom of heaven has come near (10:7). He has warned them of rejection and persecution (10:16–39). He ends with the other side: the welcome they will receive, and its reward.

Jewish teaching of the time knew the principle that a person's envoy is like the person himself (Mishnah, Berakhot 5:5). Jesus takes it further: the one who receives his messenger receives him, and through him the Father.

Structure.

  • v. 40: to welcome you is to welcome me and the one who sent me.
  • v. 41: to welcome a prophet or a righteous person brings their reward.
  • v. 42: even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones will not lose its reward.

Key words.

  • dechomai, welcome: To receive a guest into one's house. For travelling missionaries, welcome meant a roof, a meal and a hearing.
  • mikroi, little ones: In Matthew these are the disciples themselves, ordinary believers without rank (18:6, 10, 14). The least disciple carries the Lord's honour.
  • potērion psychrou, a cup of cold water: The smallest gift a poor home could offer. In a hot land, cool water from a deep well was itself a kindness.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” (10:40)

Hospitality becomes a meeting with God. The door opened to the messenger is opened to the Father.

“and whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple” (10:42)

“In the name of a disciple” means because he is a disciple, for Christ's sake. The gift is small, but the reason gives it weight.

“truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward.” (10:42)

The reward is not a wage earned but the generosity of God, who notices what everyone else forgets.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that our merit before God comes from his grace, which goes before us and goes with us, so that every reward is first his gift (CCC 2008–2011). St Julius is a living commentary on verse 40. When St Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, was driven from his see for holding to the faith of Nicaea, Julius received him at Rome, and a council held there under Julius declared him innocent. In welcoming the persecuted bishop, the Bishop of Rome welcomed Christ.

Questions people ask

Paul says that we are justified by faith. Does that make good works unimportant?

No. The Catholic Church teaches that we are justified by grace, received through faith and Baptism, and that this grace makes us truly righteous and bears fruit in works of love. Good works done in grace are pleasing to God and merit reward, but only because God's grace first made them possible (CCC 2008, 2010). Today's Gospel shows it: even a cup of cold water given for Christ's sake is not forgotten.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

St Julius I was Bishop of Rome from 337 to 352, in the years of the great struggle over the faith of Nicaea. His letter to the bishops gathered at Antioch, which St Athanasius preserved, pleads for fairness and for the communion of the Churches. The Syro-Malankara Catholic Church, Antiochene in heritage and in full communion with the successor of Peter, can see in him a Pope who cared for the faith and the bishops of the East.
