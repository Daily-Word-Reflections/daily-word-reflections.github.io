Word for the day

“For truly I tell you, if you have faith the size of a mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move; and nothing will be impossible for you.” Matthew 17:20

Reflection

The Epistle opens with one of the most moving sentences St Paul ever wrote: “Brothers and sisters, my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved.” He is speaking of his own people, the people of Israel. A chapter earlier he has said that he would wish himself cut off from Christ for their sake, and he has listed with pride what belongs to them: the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, the promises, the patriarchs, and, according to the flesh, the Messiah himself. Paul never stopped being a son of Israel, and he never stopped loving his people. When he says, “I can testify that they have a zeal for God, but it is not enlightened,” he is not sneering; he is remembering his own past, when his zeal had made him a persecutor. The Church has learned from Paul to speak of the Jewish people with reverence and gratitude. The Second Vatican Council, recalling these very chapters of Romans, teaches that the Jews remain most dear to God because of their fathers, for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable (Nostra Aetate 4). No Christian may speak of them with contempt.

Then Paul gives the heart of his Gospel: “For, being ignorant of the righteousness that comes from God, and seeking to establish their own, they have not submitted to God's righteousness. For Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.” The word “end” here means both the finishing point and the goal, as the finishing line is the end of a race. Our Malayalam Bible renders it: Christ has fulfilled the law. The whole law was running towards him. This is a warning for us, not for others. It is very possible to be zealous for God and still to be trying to establish our own righteousness: to fast, pray, give and serve as a way of being someone in God's eyes and in the parish's eyes. In a few days we begin the Apostles' Fast. Paul tells us in advance how to keep it: not to establish our own righteousness, but to submit to God's, receiving from Christ what we could never make ourselves.

The Gospel brings us down from the mountain of the Transfiguration into a crowd and a family in distress. A father kneels before Jesus: “Lord, have mercy on my son, for he is an epileptic and he suffers terribly; he often falls into the fire and often into the water. And I brought him to your disciples, but they could not cure him.” Every parent who has sat up at night with a sick child, or watched a son or daughter fall into the fire and the water of addiction, depression or bad company, knows this prayer. Jesus heals the boy at once. When the disciples ask privately why they had failed, he answers: “Because of your little faith. For truly I tell you, if you have faith the size of a mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move; and nothing will be impossible for you.” Many ancient manuscripts add a further verse, printed as a footnote in our English Bible: this kind goes out only by prayer and fasting. Whatever its place in the text, the Church has always heard in this Gospel a call to the faith that prays and fasts. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, remarked that the mustard seed looks small but is the most powerful of seeds; so faith, small to look at, carries the power of God within it (Homilies on Matthew 57, in substance). On this Saturday, let parents bring their children to Jesus by name, and let all of us ask for the small, real faith that moves mountains.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul begins today with words from his heart: Brothers and sisters, my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved (Romans 10:1). He is speaking of his own people, Israel. Paul loved them all his life, and the Church teaches us to speak of the Jewish people with reverence and gratitude, for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.

Paul says that some had a zeal for God, but it is not enlightened (Romans 10:2). They were trying to establish their own righteousness. That danger is ours too. We can pray and fast and serve to make ourselves important. But Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes (Romans 10:4). We receive righteousness from him.

In the Gospel a father kneels before Jesus: Lord, have mercy on my son (Matthew 17:15). The disciples could not heal the boy, but Jesus did. Then Jesus tells them: if you have faith the size of a mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move (Matthew 17:20). A small faith, a real faith, is enough, because God is great.

Next Wednesday we begin the Apostles' Fast. Let us keep it humbly, not to make ourselves righteous, but to open our hearts to Christ. Let us bring our children and our families to Jesus by name, and pray for the salvation of those we love. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever seen a mustard seed? It is tiny, smaller than a grain of rice! Jesus said that if we have faith even as small as a mustard seed, God can do great things through us. In the Gospel a father brought his sick son to Jesus, and Jesus made him well. When someone in your family is sick or sad, bring them to Jesus in your prayers. Your faith may be small, but God is very big!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the end of the law and the righteousness of all who believe. Give us Paul's heart, full of love and prayer for others, and keep us from every contempt for the people of the covenant, to whom your gifts and calling remain. Take from us the zeal that seeks to establish our own righteousness, and teach us to receive everything from you. Have mercy on our sons and daughters who fall into the fire and the water; heal them and bring them home. Give us faith the size of a mustard seed, and as the Apostles' Fast draws near, teach us to pray and to fast with humble hearts. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two prayers stand at the heart of today's readings. Paul prays with all his heart for his own people, Israel, that they may be saved. A father kneels before Jesus and begs, “Lord, have mercy on my son.” Both readings ask what true faith is: not a righteousness of our own making, but trust in what God does in Christ, even if that trust is as small as a mustard seed. The key words are righteousness and faith.

Romans 10:1–4 · My heart's desire and prayer for them

Setting.

Romans 9–11 is Paul's long reflection on his own people. He has said that he has “great sorrow and unceasing anguish” in his heart for them (9:2), and he has listed with pride what belongs to them: “the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises” (9:4). He will end this section with hope: “all Israel will be saved” (11:26), for “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29). Today's verses must be read within that whole.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Paul's prayer for their salvation.
  • v. 2: a zeal for God, but not enlightened.
  • v. 3: seeking to establish their own righteousness, not submitting to God's.
  • v. 4: Christ, the end of the law, so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.

Key words.

  • zēlos, zeal: Paul knows this zeal from inside. He was once “far more zealous for the traditions of my ancestors” (Galatians 1:14), and “as to zeal, a persecutor of the church” (Philippians 3:6). He does not mock it; he honours it, and grieves that it has missed its goal.
  • dikaiosynē theou, the righteousness of God: God's own saving justice, by which he makes sinners righteous as a gift. “Their own” righteousness is one that a person tries to establish by his own observance.
  • telos, end: The word can mean an end as a stopping point and an end as a goal. Catholic interpreters generally stress the second: Christ is the goal toward which the law was leading, and in him it is fulfilled (compare Matthew 5:17: “I have come not to abolish but to fulfill”).

Verse by verse.

“Brothers and sisters, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved.” (10:1)

The first word Paul says about his people here is a prayer. That remains the Christian way to speak of the Jewish people.

“I can testify that they have a zeal for God, but it is not enlightened.” (10:2)

“Not enlightened” is literally “not according to knowledge” (ou kat' epignōsin). Paul speaks with respect: he bears witness to their zeal before he names what is lacking.

“For Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.” (10:4)

“Everyone”: Jew and Gentile alike. A few verses later Paul says, “there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; the same Lord is Lord of all” (10:12).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses with the whole of Romans 9–11. She teaches that the Jews remain most dear to God because of their fathers, for he does not repent of the gifts he makes or of the calls he issues (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839), and she prays for the Jewish people as “the first to hear the word of God” (CCC 839). The Catechism quotes verse 4 when it teaches that Christ is the end of the law, because he alone teaches and bestows the justice of God (CCC 1953). Paul's prayer in verse 1 remains a model for the Church: to speak of others first in prayer.

Matthew 17:14–21 · Faith the size of a mustard seed

Setting.

This scene follows straight after the Transfiguration (17:1–8). Peter, James and John have seen Jesus in glory on the mountain; at its foot the other disciples have failed to heal a boy. Mark tells the same story at greater length (Mark 9:14–29), with the father's cry, “I believe; help my unbelief!” (Mark 9:24).

Matthew's word for the boy's illness, selēniazetai, means literally “moon-struck”, because people linked such seizures to the moon; the NRSV renders it “epileptic”. The Gospel speaks both of an illness and of a demon (17:18), in the language of its time. The Church today carefully distinguishes illness, which is the concern of medicine, from the presence of the Evil One, before any exorcism is performed (CCC 1673).

Structure.

  • vv. 14–16: the father kneels; the disciples could not cure his son.
  • v. 17: “You faithless and perverse generation … bring him here to me.”
  • v. 18: Jesus rebukes the demon; the boy is cured at once.
  • vv. 19–20: “Why could we not cast it out?” Faith the size of a mustard seed.
  • ¶ Verse 21 (“But this kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting”) is missing from the oldest manuscripts and the NRSV gives it only in a footnote; it was probably added from Mark 9:29. The POC joins the end of verse 20 to the number 21.

Key words.

  • oligopistia, little faith: Matthew's favourite reproach to the disciples: the noun here, and the related adjective oligopistoi, “you of little faith” (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). It is not no faith, but weak and divided faith, faith that looks at the storm instead of at the Lord.
  • kokkos sinapeōs, mustard seed: Proverbially the smallest of seeds (13:31–32). Jesus does not ask for great faith but for real faith: a small seed that is alive can move what seems immovable.
  • genea apistos kai diestrammenē, faithless and perverse generation: The words echo the Song of Moses about Israel in the wilderness, “a perverse and crooked generation” (Deuteronomy 32:5). The reproach falls on the whole generation, the disciples included, and not on the suffering father.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, have mercy on my son,” (17:15)

The father's prayer is the prayer of every parent, and it is close to the Church's oldest cry, Kyrie eleison, which the Holy Qurbono repeats many times.

“Why could we not cast it out?” (17:19)

The disciples ask in private. It is a model for bringing our failures to the Lord honestly.

“if you have faith the size of a mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move;” (17:20)

“Moving mountains” was a proverbial image for doing what seems impossible. The mountain of the Transfiguration stands just behind them.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this Gospel the power of faith joined to prayer. Mark's form of the saying, “This kind can come out only through prayer” (Mark 9:29), and the later reading that adds fasting, have shaped the Church's practice of meeting evil with prayer and fasting. For the Syro-Malankara faithful the Apostles' Fast begins on Wednesday 16 June: a time to bring the “mountains” of our lives to the Lord in that spirit. The father also teaches parents to bring their children to Christ in prayer, even when others have failed to help.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus mean that with enough faith we can get anything we want?

No. Faith is trust in God, not power over him. Jesus himself prayed, “yet not what I want but what you want” (Matthew 26:39). The promise that “nothing will be impossible” rests on God, of whom the angel said to Mary, “nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37). Mustard-seed faith places the mountain in God's hands and leaves the way it moves to him.

For study and teaching

  • Paul's first words about those who had not accepted Christ are a prayer. How do I speak of people who do not share my faith?
  • What “mountain” in my life or my family seems immovable? How can I bring it to the Lord with mustard-seed faith, prayer and fasting, as the Apostles' Fast draws near?
