Word for the day

“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Reflection

St Paul has been answering a question about meat that had been offered to idols, and at the end he lifts the whole discussion to a height: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” Nothing is too small for this. The meal, the work, the journey, the hours of the day that no one sees can be offered to God and made into praise. Paul then adds a rule for the common life: “Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God, just as I try to please everyone in everything I do, not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved.” He seeks the good of others, in the hope that they will be saved. And the model is not himself but the Lord: “Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.” An imitator is not a copy but a pupil, one who has watched someone close to Christ and has learned how to walk.

The Gospel is harder. Large crowds are travelling with Jesus, and he turns and speaks to them without softening anything: “Whoever comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and even life itself, cannot be my disciple. Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” The word “hate” is the strong speech of the East, which the Lord himself explains elsewhere: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me” (Matthew 10:37). He asks for the first place, not for the destruction of the family; the love of father and mother is a commandment, but no love may stand in the way of Christ. Then he gives two small stories: the builder who “does not first sit down and estimate the cost” and is laughed at when he cannot finish the tower, and the king who counts his ten thousand against twenty thousand and sends for the terms of peace. The Lord is not driving the crowd away; he wants them to choose with open eyes. Following him is neither a mood nor a bargain. “None of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.”

Both readings come alive in St Ignatius of Loyola. Íñigo López was born in 1491 in the Basque country of Spain, the youngest of a large noble family, and trained to be a soldier. In 1521 a cannonball broke his leg at the siege of Pamplona. During the long weeks of recovery he asked for adventure stories, and in the castle there were only a life of Christ and the lives of the saints. He noticed that when he dreamed of fame he felt empty afterwards, but when he thought of doing for God what the saints had done he stayed content. That was the beginning of his conversion. At Manresa he prayed and wrote the notes that became the Spiritual Exercises, he went as a pilgrim to Jerusalem, and in Paris he gathered companions, among them Francis Xavier and Peter Faber. On the hill of Montmartre in 1534 they made their vows together. Pope Paul III approved the Society of Jesus in 1540, and Ignatius, elected its first Superior General, spent his last years in Rome writing letters and its constitutions. He died on 31 July 1556 and was canonised in 1622 together with Francis Xavier. His motto, taken from today's Epistle, is Ad maiorem Dei gloriam: to the greater glory of God.

The Exercises begin with the sentence that sums up what Paul says: human beings are created to praise, reverence and serve God, and by this means to save their souls. Everything else is for the sake of that end. Ignatius taught people to find God in all things, to look back each evening on the day in a short prayer called the examen, and to weigh in prayer the choices of their lives; this is how he put into practice Jesus' call to estimate the cost before building. His friend Xavier reached Goa in 1542 and travelled the coast of India. He went among the fishing villages in the south, including the shore of Travancore, and he is known to have prayed at Mylapore, where the tradition of the Thomas Christians places the tomb of the Apostle Thomas, so the first Jesuit of India honoured the same Apostle whom we remembered on 3 July. Many of our own children and grandchildren have studied in schools and colleges that Jesuits founded in Kerala. The lesson of Ignatius is for every one of us, in every state of life: ask what the Lord wants, count the cost, and then give the work, the meal and the ordinary day to the greater glory of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul gives us a sentence that can fill every day: whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God (1 Corinthians 10:31). Nothing is too small, the meal, the work, the journey. And he adds: not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved; be imitators of me, as I am of Christ (1 Corinthians 10:33–11:1).

In the Gospel Jesus speaks to large crowds, and he is not gentle. Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple (Luke 14:27). He asks us to count the cost, like a builder and a king. He wants the crowd to choose with open eyes. The strong word about hating father and mother means that no love may stand before Christ; elsewhere he says that whoever loves father or mother more than him is not worthy of him.

Today the Panchangam remembers St Ignatius of Loyola. He was a soldier, wounded at Pamplona in 1521. In bed he read about Jesus and the saints, and he gave his life to God. He wrote the Spiritual Exercises, gathered companions, among them Francis Xavier, and founded the Society of Jesus in 1540. He died in 1556. His motto is Ad maiorem Dei gloriam: to the greater glory of God. Xavier brought the Gospel to the coast of India.

So let us begin each task with the words, To the greater glory of God. Let us count the cost of our promises, and let us carry the cross after the Lord. Amen.

For children

Children, do you remember how to say your prayer before you eat? St Paul says that even eating and drinking can be done for the glory of God. St Ignatius, whom we remember today, was a soldier who got hurt and read about Jesus while he was in bed. He promised to do everything for the greater glory of God. Before you start your homework or your game, say very quietly, “For you, Jesus.” Every little thing becomes a gift.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you called St Ignatius from the battlefield to follow you, and you made him a servant of your greater glory. Teach us to do everything, eating and drinking and all our work, for the glory of God. Help us to count the cost and to carry the cross, that we may be your true disciples and imitators of you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the single aim that gives order to the whole of life. Paul says that whatever we do, we should do it for the glory of God, and that he himself imitates Christ. In Luke, Jesus speaks to large crowds and says that to follow him costs everything, as a builder or a king must count the cost before beginning. The day also names St Ignatius of Loyola, whose motto was the greater glory of God. The key words are for the glory of God and count the cost.

1 Corinthians 10:31–11:1 · Do everything for the glory of God

Setting.

Paul closes a long answer (8:1–11:1) to the Corinthians’ question about eating meat that has been offered to idols. His answer is balanced: idols are nothing, but the weak may be hurt if the strong use their freedom without love. Many in the city bought meat from temple markets, and a meal in a friend’s house could easily raise the question.

Our verses give the summary. All of life, the plainest acts such as eating and drinking, is to be offered to God, and in whatever he does Paul seeks the good of others so that they may be saved. The chapter division is unfortunate: the first verse of chapter 11 belongs to the argument of chapter 10.

Structure.

  • v. 31: whether you eat or drink, do everything for the glory of God.
  • v. 32: give no offense to Jews, to Greeks or to the church of God.
  • v. 33: Paul’s own practice: not seeking his own advantage but that of many.
  • 11:1: be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.

Key words.

  • doxa, glory: The weight and radiance of God. To do something “for the glory of God” is to let his goodness show in it.
  • aproskopoi, giving no offense: Literally “not causing to stumble”. The word recalls the stumbling block that Paul fears to put before a weaker believer (8:9).
  • mimētai, imitators: Paul asks to be imitated only as he imitates Christ. The standard is Christ, and the apostle is a pointer.

Verse by verse.

“do everything for the glory of God.” (10:31)

Nothing is too small. Eating, drinking and the ordinary acts of the day can be given to God.

“Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God” (10:32)

Paul names the three groups of his world: Jews, Gentiles and the Church. He wants to cause no needless stumbling to any.

“Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.” (11:1)

Christianity is learned by following people who follow Christ. Parents, catechists and pastors are all asked to be this kind of example.

How the Church reads it.

The Church takes up Paul’s verse as the principle of the whole Christian life: every act, offered to God, becomes prayer. The Catechism teaches that the glory of God consists in the manifestation and communication of his goodness, for which the world was created (CCC 294). It also puts before us the saints as models and intercessors (CCC 828). In the Eastern tradition, the whole day, with its meals and its work, is surrounded with the prayers of the hours.

Luke 14:25–33 · The cost of discipleship

Setting.

Jesus is on the road to Jerusalem, and large crowds travel with him (14:25). He turns and tells them what following him means. The language is deliberately sharp. Just before this he has told of the great banquet to which many make excuses (14:15–24), and now he warns those who join the journey that it is not a casual walk.

“Hate” in verse 26 is Semitic speech: to love less by comparison. Matthew gives the sense: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me” (10:37). Jesus does not teach contempt for family, which the fourth commandment honours. He asks that no bond come before him. Then come two small parables, the builder and the king, both about thinking ahead before starting.

Structure.

  • v. 25: large crowds with him on the road.
  • vv. 26–27: no one can be a disciple who puts even family or life before him, or who will not carry the cross.
  • vv. 28–30: the tower builder: count the cost first.
  • vv. 31–32: the king: weigh your strength before going to war.
  • v. 33: so none can be a disciple who does not give up all possessions.

Key words.

  • miseō, to hate: In biblical Hebrew and Aramaic “hate” can mean “love less” or “prefer something else”, as in Genesis 29:30–31 where Leah is “hated” in comparison with Rachel.
  • psēphizō, to calculate: Literally to count with pebbles. The builder sits down and reckons honestly. Discipleship is not an impulse; it is a considered, lasting decision.
  • apotassomai, to say farewell, to give up: A word used for taking leave of someone. Disciples take leave of everything that would rule them.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and even life itself, cannot be my disciple.” (14:26)

The sentence is shocking on purpose, to show the place of Christ. Those who love him most also love their families best, because they love them in him.

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” (14:27)

Every disciple has a cross. Jesus does not hide it. It is carried behind him, never alone.

“which of you, intending to build a tower, does not first sit down and estimate the cost” (14:28)

Jesus encourages planning. A well-built life asks for clear thought and honest prayer.

“none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” (14:33)

To give up is to hold nothing as one’s own security. Some are called to leave everything; all are called to hold everything lightly.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that detachment from riches is necessary for entering the kingdom of heaven (CCC 2544), and that the bonds of the family are not absolute, for the child’s first loyalty is to God (CCC 2232). The tradition has understood the words as the call of every Christian, and as the source of the counsels of poverty, chastity and obedience in the religious life. The two parables remind us that the response to Christ is made with open eyes. The cross that Jesus asks us to carry is in the end the road to joy.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus really ask us to hate our families?

No. The word is a Semitic way of saying “love less than”, as Matthew’s parallel shows (10:37). Jesus himself honoured his mother and gave her to the beloved disciple from the cross (John 19:26–27). What he asks is that no loyalty, even the dearest, stand in the way of following him. In practice, those who put Christ first become better sons, daughters, spouses and parents.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The day also names St Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), a Basque soldier who was wounded in battle, read the lives of Christ and the saints during his recovery, and gave his life to God. He founded the Society of Jesus, whose motto is “for the greater glory of God”. His own life shows both of today’s readings: the call to do everything for God’s glory and the cost of leaving the old way of living for Christ. Many Jesuit missionaries, among them St Francis Xavier, a companion of Ignatius, came to India.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks us to do everything for the glory of God, even eating and drinking. What ordinary part of my day could I deliberately offer to God this week?
  • Jesus asks the builder to count the cost. What might I need to give up or hold more lightly so that I can follow him more fully and with an undivided heart?
