Word for the day

“But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering” Hebrews 12:22

Reflection

Yesterday the Church remembered St Mark the Evangelist, and today, fittingly, the Gospel comes from his book. Jesus is teaching by the Sea of Galilee, and the crowd is so large that “he got into a boat on the sea and sat there, while the whole crowd was beside the sea on the land.” The boat becomes a pulpit. And his first word is a command: “Listen!” Then comes a story every farmer in Kerala would understand. “A sower went out to sow.” Some seed falls on the path and the birds eat it; some on rocky ground, where it springs up quickly but withers in the sun “since it had no root”; some among thorns, which choke it; and some “into good soil and brought forth grain, growing up and increasing and yielding thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” The sower is generous to the point of seeming careless: he throws seed everywhere, even where it may not grow. So God scatters his word on every heart, never first asking whether we deserve it. The question the parable leaves with us is not about the sower, nor about the seed, which is always good, but about the soil. St Ephrem the Syrian, our own teacher in the Syriac tradition, marvelled that no one can grasp even one word of the Lord fully: we are like the thirsty who drink from a spring, and what we leave behind is far more than what we take (Commentary on the Diatessaron 1, in substance).

The Letter to the Hebrews tells us where that word is spoken most solemnly: in the worship of the Church. It sets two mountains side by side. The first is Sinai, where Israel received the Law: “a blazing fire, and darkness, and gloom, and a tempest, and the sound of a trumpet, and a voice whose words made the hearers beg that not another word be spoken to them.” Even Moses said, “I tremble with fear.” The second mountain is the one we have come to: “But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering, and to the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven, and to God the judge of all, and to the spirits of the righteous made perfect, and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel.” This is a picture of the Holy Qurbono. When our Church celebrates the holy mysteries, the veil is drawn back and we stand with the angels and the saints, singing with the Seraphim, “Holy, holy, holy.” The blood of Abel cried out from the ground for vengeance; the blood of Jesus, offered on the Cross and made present on the altar, speaks a better word: mercy, forgiveness, peace.

Put the two readings together and a practical question appears: what kind of soil do we bring to the Qurbono? Some of us arrive like the path, trampled by a hurried week, and the word is gone before we reach the door. Some are like the rocky ground: moved for a moment by a good homily, but with no root of daily prayer. Many are like the thorny ground: we hear, but the worries of money, exams, health and phones grow up around the word and choke it. The remedy is simple and old. Come a few minutes early and be still. Read the Sunday Gospel at home on Saturday night. After the Qurbono, keep one sentence from the readings and repeat it through the week. In this Qyomtho season the risen Lord is still walking beside us and opening the Scriptures, as he did on the road to Emmaus. Give him good soil, and he promises a harvest: thirty, sixty and a hundredfold.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Yesterday we honoured St Mark, and today we hear his Gospel. Jesus sits in a boat and teaches the crowd on the shore. His first word is: Listen! A sower went out to sow (Mark 4:3).

The seed falls on the path, on rocky ground, among thorns, and on good soil. Only the good soil yields grain, thirty and sixty and a hundredfold (Mark 4:8). The sower throws seed everywhere. God gives his word to every heart. The question is not the seed, which is always good, but the soil.

Hebrews tells us where the word is sown most solemnly. We have not come to the fire and fear of Sinai. You have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering (Hebrews 12:22), and to Jesus, whose sprinkled blood speaks a better word than the blood of Abel (Hebrews 12:24). That is the Holy Qurbono: heaven and earth singing together.

So let us prepare the soil. Read the Gospel before Sunday. Come early and be still. Keep one word from the readings all week. Pull out one thorn of worry by giving it to God in prayer. And the Lord promises a harvest. Let anyone with ears to hear listen! (Mark 4:9). Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Have you ever planted a seed? Jesus told a story about a farmer who threw seeds everywhere. Birds ate some; some dried up on the rocks; some were choked by thorns. But the seeds on good soil grew big and tall! God's words are like seeds, and your heart is like the soil. When you listen in church and remember what you heard, your heart becomes good soil. This week, try to remember one thing Jesus said, and tell it to someone at home.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, sower of the word of life, you scatter your seed generously on every heart. Break up the hard paths of our hurry, give root to our good intentions, and pull out the thorns of worry that choke your word. Make us good soil, that we may bear fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold. You have brought us not to a mountain of fear but to Mount Zion, to the angels in festal gathering and to your sprinkled blood that speaks of mercy. Let us stand in awe and joy at your holy Qurbono. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Yesterday was the feast of St Mark, and today's Gospel comes from his book: the parable of the sower, which begins and ends with a call to listen. The Epistle tells us where God's word is now heard most fully. We have not come to the terrors of Sinai but to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant. The key words are come and listen.

Hebrews 12:18–24 · You have come to Mount Zion

Setting.

Hebrews is a long sermon in written form, addressed to Christians who were growing tired and tempted to fall back. Its author is unknown; the early Church already debated whether Paul wrote it. Chapter 12 urges the readers to run the race with their eyes on Jesus (12:1–2). Today's verses set two mountains side by side, Sinai, where the Law was given (Exodus 19–20; Deuteronomy 4–5), and Zion, the city of God.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–21: what you have not come to: fire, darkness, trumpet, a voice no one could bear.
  • vv. 22–24: what you have come to: the city of the living God, angels, the firstborn, God, the righteous, Jesus and his blood.

Key words.

  • proselēlythate, you have come: The verb is in a tense that speaks of arrival with lasting effect: you have come and you are here. It is also the word Hebrews uses for drawing near to God in worship (4:16; 10:22).
  • ekklēsia prōtotokōn, assembly of the firstborn: Ekklēsia is the word for the Church. The firstborn belonged to God in Israel (Exodus 13:2). Here all the faithful, “enrolled in heaven”, share that title in Christ, “the firstborn” (1:6).
  • mesitēs, mediator: The one who stands between two parties and binds them. Moses was mediator at Sinai; Jesus is mediator of the new covenant, and his own blood seals it (9:15).

Verse by verse.

“Indeed, so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, “I tremble with fear.”” (12:21)

These words are not found in the Sinai story of Exodus. They echo Deuteronomy 9:19, where Moses fears God's anger after the golden calf. Even the great mediator of the old covenant trembled.

“But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering,” (12:22)

The Christian is not waiting outside. In worship he is already joined to the festival of heaven, though still on the way.

“and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel.” (12:24)

Abel's blood cried out from the ground for justice (Genesis 4:10). The blood of Jesus speaks of mercy and forgiveness.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as a description of her worship. The Second Vatican Council says that in the earthly liturgy we share in a foretaste of the heavenly liturgy celebrated in the holy city of Jerusalem, toward which we journey as pilgrims (Sacrosanctum Concilium 8; CCC 1090). The contrast is not between a cruel God and a kind one. The same God spoke at Sinai and speaks in his Son. What is new is that, through Jesus, the distance has been crossed.

Mark 4:1–9 · A sower went out to sow

Setting.

Mark gathers several parables in chapter 4. Jesus has already met growing opposition (3:6, 22) and has chosen the Twelve (3:13–19). Now he teaches from a boat, with the crowd on the shore. The explanation of the parable comes later, given to the disciples (4:13–20); today we hear the story as the crowd heard it.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the setting: the boat, the crowd, teaching in parables.
  • vv. 3–8: the sower and four kinds of ground.
  • v. 9: the call to hear.

Key words.

  • parabolē, parable: Behind it is the Hebrew māshāl, which covers proverbs, riddles and stories. A parable draws the hearer in and leaves him to find the meaning, so it reveals to some and hides from others (4:11–12).
  • akouete, listen!: The parable opens with this command and closes with “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!” Israel's great prayer begins the same way: “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4).

Verse by verse.

“Listen! A sower went out to sow.” (4:3)

The sower scatters seed everywhere, even on the path and among stones. God does not first measure the soil before he speaks.

“Other seed fell on rocky ground, where it did not have much soil, and it sprang up quickly, since it had no depth of soil.” (4:5)

Quick growth is not the same as deep growth. Jesus later explains that these are people who receive the word with joy but fall away when trouble comes (4:16–17).

“Other seed fell into good soil and brought forth grain, growing up and increasing and yielding thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” (4:8)

After three losses, a harvest beyond all expectation. Even Isaac's blessed harvest is called a hundredfold (Genesis 26:12). The parable ends in hope.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears the parable as a call to examine the soil of the heart. The Catechism uses it to speak of the Kingdom: the word is like seed sown in a field, and those who hear it with faith have received the Kingdom itself (CCC 543). The Second Vatican Council quotes Jesus' saying that the word of the Lord is like a seed sown in a field (Lumen Gentium 5). For a family, the parable asks practical questions: what birds, rocks and thorns stop the word from growing in our home?

Questions people ask

Is the parable saying that some people are simply bad soil and cannot change?

No. A parable is a call, not a verdict. Jesus speaks so that the hearers will become good soil. The paths can be broken up, the stones removed and the thorns pulled out, and the grace of God is the farmer who does it with our consent.

For study and teaching

  • Which of the four kinds of ground best describes how I heard last Sunday's word?
  • Hebrews says we have already come to the heavenly Jerusalem. How would I take part in the Holy Qurbono differently if I believed that?
