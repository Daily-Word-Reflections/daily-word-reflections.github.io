Word for the day

“For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen.” Romans 11:36

Reflection

Today's Epistle is the end of a long argument. In chapters 9 to 11 of his Letter to the Romans, St Paul wrestles with a sorrow that lay close to his heart: most of his own people, the Jews, had not accepted Jesus as the Messiah, while many Gentiles had. Had God's promise failed? Paul will not allow it: “I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin.” And a few verses before today's passage he writes: “for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.” The Church has taken these words very seriously. The Second Vatican Council, in Nostra Aetate 4, recalled that the Jewish people remain most dear to God, who does not repent of the gifts he makes or of the calls he issues (in substance), and that the Church can never forget that she received the revelation of the Old Testament through them. Against this background we hear today's verses: “Just as you were once disobedient to God but have now received mercy because of their disobedience, so they have now been disobedient in order that, by the mercy shown to you, they too may now receive mercy.” Paul sees history as one great movement of mercy in which no one has a claim and no one may boast, and he ends the thought with a sentence that holds the whole of the gospel: “For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all.”

Then Paul, who has been reasoning for three chapters, simply stops reasoning and begins to sing: “O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!” St John Chrysostom, commenting on this passage, remarked that when Paul's argument reaches the mystery of God's plan he does not try to measure it; he adores (Homilies on Romans, in substance). The questions he asks, “Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?” and “Or who has given a gift to him, to receive a gift in return?”, remind us that God owes us nothing and that every good thing is a gift. Then comes the line the Church loves to pray: “For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen.” From him, because he is the source of all that exists; through him, because he sustains and redeems it; to him, because he is the end for which everything was made. In these weeks of the monsoon, when the rain pours on the hills and the paddy fields drink and turn green again, it is easy to feel this. No one gave the rain a gift first. All of it comes from him, and all of it is meant to rise again in thanksgiving. The great prayers of the Holy Qurbono end exactly as Paul does, with glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and the people's Amen.

The Gospel is short and strange. “On the following day, when they came from Bethany, he was hungry. Seeing in the distance a fig tree in leaf, he went to see whether perhaps he would find anything on it. When he came to it, he found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs. He said to it, ‘May no one ever eat fruit from you again.’ And his disciples heard it.” St Mark places this story on either side of the cleansing of the Temple, and the two scenes are meant to be read together (Mark 11:15–17 and 11:20–24). The prophets often compared Israel to a fig tree from which God hoped for fruit (Hosea 9:10; Jeremiah 8:13), and Jesus here performs a prophetic sign, as the prophets did: an acted parable against religion that shows leaves and no fruit, splendid worship without justice, prayer and love. It is a warning for every believer, for each of us as much as for anyone, and it is no verdict on a people; Romans 11, which we heard first, forbids any Christian to think so. St Augustine, preaching on the fig tree, said that leaves without fruit are words without deeds (Sermon 89, in substance). Notice too that Jesus is hungry. He comes looking for us, hungry for our love, and the next morning the tree is found withered to its roots and he says to the disciples simply: “Have faith in God.” Let us ask ourselves today what fruit he would find in us, and let us give thanks that the God who is merciful to all keeps coming to us as one who hopes.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul comes to the end of a long argument, and he ends it with a song: O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways! (Romans 11:33). He has been thinking about the people of Israel, about the Gentiles, and about the way God's plan moves through history. And at the end he does not say: now I understand it all. He says: now I can only praise.

He tells us what he has learned: God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all (Romans 11:32). Nobody can stand before God and say, I deserve this. We all live by mercy: those who came first and those who came later, Jew and Gentile, bishop and child. And the Church reminds us, with Paul, that the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). So no Christian may ever speak of the Jewish people with contempt or pride.

Then the Gospel gives us a picture to examine our own hearts. Jesus is hungry; he sees a fig tree in leaf and goes to it, but finds nothing but leaves (Mark 11:13). A tree that looks full of life and gives no fruit: that can be a person, a home, a parish. Fine words, fine feasts, fine decorations, and no prayer, no justice, no mercy. Jesus is not asking for more show. He is asking for fruit.

So let us ask ourselves where our leaves are, and with God's grace let us begin to bear fruit. And let us do what Paul does: let us thank God from whom, and through whom, and to whom are all things. To him be the glory for ever. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever gone to the mango tree hoping for a mango, and found only leaves? Jesus once did that with a fig tree! He was hungry, and the tree had lots of beautiful green leaves, but no figs. Jesus wants us to be like a tree full of fruit: not only looking nice, but being kind, sharing, praying and helping. And St Paul tells us to thank God for everything, because everything comes from him. This week, thank God for your food before you eat it, and do one kind thing that is like a fruit: share a sweet, help at home, or visit a lonely neighbour.

Prayer

Lord God, from you and through you and to you are all things. We bless you for the depth of your riches and wisdom and knowledge, and for the mercy you have shown to all. Keep us from boasting, since everything we have is your gift; keep us from leaves without fruit, and teach us to bear the fruit of prayer, justice and love. Remember your ancient people, whose gifts and calling you have never taken back, and gather all peoples in your mercy. To you be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about God's patience and what he looks for. In the Epistle Paul ends his long reflection on Israel and the nations (Romans 9–11) with a hymn: God has shut up all in disobedience only to show mercy to all, and his ways are beyond our searching out. In the Gospel, on the Monday of his last week, Jesus is hungry and finds a fig tree with leaves but no fruit. The key words are mercy and fruit.

Romans 11:30–36 · The depth of the riches and wisdom of God

Setting.

Chapters 9 to 11 of Romans form one argument. Paul is deeply grieved that so many of his own people, Israel, have not accepted the Gospel (9:1–5), and he asks whether God's word has failed. His answer is no: God has not rejected his people (11:1), and “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29). Gentiles who now believe are like wild olive branches grafted into Israel's own olive tree (11:17–24).

Our passage comes at the end. It sums up the whole history in two short sentences about mercy, and then Paul, who has been reasoning at length, can reason no more and breaks into praise.

Structure.

  • vv. 30–31: Gentiles received mercy when Israel was disobedient; Israel too will receive mercy through the mercy shown to the Gentiles.
  • v. 32: God has shut up all in disobedience, to show mercy to all.
  • v. 33: wonder at the depth of God.
  • vv. 34–35: two questions drawn from Isaiah and Job: who has known his mind, who has given him a gift first?
  • v. 36: all things are from him, through him and to him; glory for ever.

Key words.

  • eleos / eleēthōsin, mercy; may receive mercy: The word at the centre of the passage, used four times in verses 30–32. The mercy of God is not earned and not ended: it goes first to one, then to the other, so that no one may boast.
  • synekleisen, he has shut up, imprisoned: A word for shutting a catch of fish inside a net. Paul uses it in Galatians 3:22 as well. It does not mean that God causes sin. It means that God allows all, Jew and Gentile, to find that none can rescue himself, so that all must receive salvation as a gift.
  • anexichniastoi, unsearchable (literally, that cannot be tracked): A hunter's word: the footprints of God's ways cannot be followed to the end. Human reasoning must give way to adoration.

Verse by verse.

“For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all.” (11:32)

This is the summary of the whole letter. Everyone, Jew and Gentile, has need of mercy (compare 3:9–24), and God's purpose in the whole of history is mercy. It is not a statement that all will be saved without conversion, but of God's will that mercy be offered to everyone.

“O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!” (11:33)

Paul can say no more by argument. “Riches” of God's generosity, “wisdom” of his plan and “knowledge” of the heart: the thought opens into worship.

“For from him and through him and to him are all things.” (11:36)

Creation comes from God, is held through him, and goes to him as its goal. The Fathers heard in the three little words a trace of the Trinity; this is a reading in the Church's tradition and not the main sense of Paul's sentence.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses as her guide in speaking of the Jewish people. The Second Vatican Council, quoting Paul, teaches that the Jews “remain most dear to God” because of their ancestors, “for he does not repent of the gifts he makes nor of the calls he issues” (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). The Church awaits, with hope, the day when “all Israel” and the nations together will be gathered into God's people (CCC 674). She also confesses that God's ways are unsearchable. The Catechism says that no one can fully know why God permitted evil, and that only at the end shall we see the whole of his plan (CCC 309–314). Paul's hymn is the best prayer for that: not an explanation, but adoration. The doxology in verse 36 is the shape of every Eucharistic prayer, which gives thanks to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit.

Mark 11:12–14 · A fig tree with leaves and no fruit

Setting.

This short passage is the first part of one of Mark's “sandwiches” (a story with another set inside it). Mark opens one story, inserts another, and closes the first, so that each explains the other. Here the cursing of the fig tree (11:12–14) is followed by Jesus' cleansing of the Temple (11:15–19), and then by the finding of the withered tree the next morning (11:20–25).

It is the Monday of Jesus' last week. On Sunday he rode into Jerusalem, looked about the Temple, and went out to Bethany (11:11). Now, on the way back into the city, he is hungry.

Structure.

  • v. 12: Jesus is hungry on the road from Bethany.
  • v. 13: a tree in leaf in the distance; nothing but leaves; it was not the season for figs.
  • v. 14: “May no one ever eat fruit from you again”; the disciples hear it.

Key words.

  • peina, to be hungry: Mark names Jesus' hunger in plain words. The Son of God is truly man, and he knows hunger as we do. Elsewhere he is asleep in the boat (4:38).
  • sykē, fig tree: In the Old Testament the fig tree often stands for the people of God and for peace (Micah 4:4). Its failure to bear fruit is a figure used by the prophets to warn: “no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree” (Jeremiah 8:13; compare Hosea 9:10).
  • ou gar ēn kairos sykōn, for it was not the season for figs: A hard clause. Mark says that figs were not in season; yet Jesus looks for fruit. Readers explain it in different ways, but all agree that Mark wants us to see that the story is an acted parable and not an event of nature.

Verse by verse.

“On the following day, when they came from Bethany, he was hungry.” (11:12)

Bethany is the home of Lazarus, Martha and Mary, where Jesus has found a resting place during these days. He leaves it to go to Jerusalem, where the Passion awaits.

“he found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs.” (11:13)

A tree in leaf seems to promise fruit; the leaves make a show. The tree makes a promise that it does not keep. Many readers have seen here the warning against religion that has the outward look of life but no fruit of love.

“May no one ever eat fruit from you again.” (11:14)

Jesus does not show anger at a tree. His words are a prophet's sign, like those of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, who also acted out God's message. The next verses explain what the sign is about: the Temple, which should be “a house of prayer for all the nations” (11:17), but has become a place of trade.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers did not see in this scene a cruelty to a tree, but a warning to every soul. St Augustine, preaching on the barren fig tree, took the leaves without fruit to be words without deeds (Sermon 89, in substance). The Church has also understood the sign as a call to the leaders and people of Jesus' own time, and to all who hold a sacred charge, to bear the fruit that God asks. It must never be read as a rejection of the Jewish people: the Temple, the prophets and the Scriptures are the Church's inheritance as well, and Jesus acts here as a prophet within Israel. The Church prays that every Christian may bear “much fruit” (John 15:8) and teaches that faith without love and good works is dead (James 2:17).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus curse a tree that could not have had fruit yet?

The question is old, and Mark does not answer it directly. The key is that Mark tells the story as part of a pair, with the Temple cleansing between its two halves. Jesus is not angry at a tree; he is acting out a prophet's sign about a people and a Temple that were full of religious show but were not bearing the fruit God looked for. Some readers also note that in spring a fig tree in leaf may have early green fruit, so that a leafy tree promised something. Whichever explanation is taken, the teaching is the same: God looks for fruit.

For study and teaching

  • Paul ends by praising God and not by explaining him. What in my own life can I only entrust to God's “unsearchable ways”?
  • A tree with leaves and no fruit. Where in my own life is there a show of religion without the fruit of love?
