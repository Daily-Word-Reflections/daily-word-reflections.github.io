Readings

Word for the day

“For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen.” Romans 11:36

Reflection

Today's Epistle is the end of a long argument. In chapters 9 to 11 of his Letter to the Romans, St Paul wrestles with a sorrow that lay close to his heart: most of his own people, the Jews, had not accepted Jesus as the Messiah, while many Gentiles had. Had God's promise failed? Paul will not allow it: “I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin.” And a few verses before today's passage he writes: “for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.” The Church has taken these words very seriously. The Second Vatican Council, in Nostra Aetate 4, recalled that the Jewish people remain most dear to God, who does not repent of the gifts he makes or of the calls he issues (in substance), and that the Church can never forget that she received the revelation of the Old Testament through them. Against this background we hear today's verses: “Just as you were once disobedient to God but have now received mercy because of their disobedience, so they have now been disobedient in order that, by the mercy shown to you, they too may now receive mercy.” Paul sees history as one great movement of mercy in which no one has a claim and no one may boast, and he ends the thought with a sentence that holds the whole of the gospel: “For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all.”

Then Paul, who has been reasoning for three chapters, simply stops reasoning and begins to sing: “O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!” St John Chrysostom, commenting on this passage, remarked that when Paul's argument reaches the mystery of God's plan he does not try to measure it; he adores (Homilies on Romans, in substance). The questions he asks, “Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?” and “Or who has given a gift to him, to receive a gift in return?”, remind us that God owes us nothing and that every good thing is a gift. Then comes the line the Church loves to pray: “For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen.” From him, because he is the source of all that exists; through him, because he sustains and redeems it; to him, because he is the end for which everything was made. In these weeks of the monsoon, when the rain pours on the hills and the paddy fields drink and turn green again, it is easy to feel this. No one gave the rain a gift first. All of it comes from him, and all of it is meant to rise again in thanksgiving. The great prayers of the Holy Qurbono end exactly as Paul does, with glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and the people's Amen.

The Gospel is short and strange. “On the following day, when they came from Bethany, he was hungry. Seeing in the distance a fig tree in leaf, he went to see whether perhaps he would find anything on it. When he came to it, he found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs. He said to it, ‘May no one ever eat fruit from you again.’ And his disciples heard it.” St Mark places this story on either side of the cleansing of the Temple, and the two scenes are meant to be read together (Mark 11:15–17 and 11:20–24). The prophets often compared Israel to a fig tree from which God hoped for fruit (Hosea 9:10; Jeremiah 8:13), and Jesus here performs a prophetic sign, as the prophets did: an acted parable against religion that shows leaves and no fruit, splendid worship without justice, prayer and love. It is a warning for every believer, for each of us as much as for anyone, and it is no verdict on a people; Romans 11, which we heard first, forbids any Christian to think so. St Augustine, preaching on the fig tree, said that leaves without fruit are words without deeds (Sermon 89, in substance). Notice too that Jesus is hungry. He comes looking for us, hungry for our love, and the next morning the tree is found withered to its roots and he says to the disciples simply: “Have faith in God.” Let us ask ourselves today what fruit he would find in us, and let us give thanks that the God who is merciful to all keeps coming to us as one who hopes.

Homily outline

Theme: “For from him and through him and to him are all things.”

On Friday of the seventh week after Pentecost: God's mercy for all, the depth of his wisdom, and the fig tree that shows leaves but no fruit.

1. Mercy for all

  • Paul's sorrow for his own people.
  • The gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.
  • No one has a claim and no one may boast.
“The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.” Psalm 103:8
“who desires everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.” 1 Timothy 2:4

Apply: Pray today for someone you find hard to love, and ask God to show them the mercy he has shown you.

2. The depth of God

  • Paul reasons for three chapters, then adores.
  • God owes us nothing: all is gift.
  • The doxology of the Holy Qurbono.
“For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways and my thoughts than your thoughts.” Isaiah 55:9
“For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then we will see face to face.” 1 Corinthians 13:12

Apply: Add a sentence of praise to your prayer at meals this week.

3. Leaves and fruit

  • The fig tree and the Temple, read together.
  • Hosea and Jeremiah: Israel as the fig tree God hoped for.
  • Words without deeds are leaves.
“When I wanted to gather them, says the Lord, there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree; even the leaves are withered, and what I gave them has passed away from them.” Jeremiah 8:13
“My Father is glorified by this, that you bear much fruit and become my disciples.” John 15:8

Apply: Name one place in your life where there are only leaves and decide what fruit you will bear there.

God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all: we live by mercy, and mercy leaves no room for boasting.

He comes to us hungry, looking for the fruit of prayer, justice and love.

Take-home resolution: Praise God for his mercy, and bear fruit instead of leaves.

Full homily

Dear friends, today St Paul comes to the end of a long argument, and he ends it with a song: O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways! (Romans 11:33). He has been thinking about the people of Israel, about the Gentiles, and about the way God's plan moves through history. And at the end he does not say: now I understand it all. He says: now I can only praise.

He tells us what he has learned: God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all (Romans 11:32). Nobody can stand before God and say, I deserve this. We all live by mercy: those who came first and those who came later, Jew and Gentile, bishop and child. And the Church reminds us, with Paul, that the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). So no Christian may ever speak of the Jewish people with contempt or pride.

Then the Gospel gives us a picture to examine our own hearts. Jesus is hungry; he sees a fig tree in leaf and goes to it, but finds nothing but leaves (Mark 11:13). A tree that looks full of life and gives no fruit: that can be a person, a home, a parish. Fine words, fine feasts, fine decorations, and no prayer, no justice, no mercy. Jesus is not asking for more show. He is asking for fruit.

So let us ask ourselves where our leaves are, and with God's grace let us begin to bear fruit. And let us do what Paul does: let us thank God from whom, and through whom, and to whom are all things. To him be the glory for ever. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever gone to the mango tree hoping for a mango, and found only leaves? Jesus once did that with a fig tree! He was hungry, and the tree had lots of beautiful green leaves, but no figs. Jesus wants us to be like a tree full of fruit: not only looking nice, but being kind, sharing, praying and helping. And St Paul tells us to thank God for everything, because everything comes from him. This week, thank God for your food before you eat it, and do one kind thing that is like a fruit: share a sweet, help at home, or visit a lonely neighbour.

Prayer

Lord God, from you and through you and to you are all things. We bless you for the depth of your riches and wisdom and knowledge, and for the mercy you have shown to all. Keep us from boasting, since everything we have is your gift; keep us from leaves without fruit, and teach us to bear the fruit of prayer, justice and love. Remember your ancient people, whose gifts and calling you have never taken back, and gather all peoples in your mercy. To you be glory for ever. Amen.
