Readings

Word for the day

“I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.” Matthew 13:35

Reflection

The Gospel gives us two very short parables, and both begin with something small. “The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in his field; it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.” The kitchens of Kerala know the mustard seed, tiny and black, which crackles in hot coconut oil. And then: “The kingdom of heaven is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” Three measures is a great quantity of flour, enough to feed a large household, and the yeast is hidden in it, working unseen through the night, as the batter rises in our own kitchens before the morning appam. Both parables say the same thing: God's kingdom does not enter the world with trumpets. It begins small and hidden, in a handful of fishermen, in a crucified carpenter, in a loaf of bread broken in an upper room, and from there it grows until it shelters the nations and leavens everything. Matthew then tells us why Jesus spoke in parables: “This was to fulfill what had been spoken through the prophet: ‘I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.’”

A prophet is the right companion for that verse, and today the Panchangam remembers Daniel. Daniel was a young Jew taken into exile in Babylon, trained for the king's service, who nevertheless “resolved that he would not defile himself with the royal rations of food and wine.” When King Nebuchadnezzar dreamed a dream that no wise man of Babylon could tell or explain, Daniel prayed, and then he blessed God: “He reveals deep and hidden things; he knows what is in the darkness, and light dwells with him.” This is what Matthew means by proclaiming what has been hidden. In the dream Daniel saw a great statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay, the pride of the empires of the earth, and “a stone was cut out, not by human hands,” and it struck the statue and broke it in pieces. “But the stone that struck the statue became a great mountain and filled the whole earth.” The Fathers saw in this stone Christ himself, small at his coming and unregarded, and yet the beginning of a kingdom that fills the earth. Daniel says plainly what the kingdom is: “the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed.” In the next vision the king saw a mighty tree, so high that it could be seen to the ends of the earth, with “the birds of the air” nesting in its branches; and that was the tree of an empire's pride, which God cut down. The tree of Jesus' parable grows from the smallest seed, in humility, and is the one that does not fall.

Daniel's life shows what it means to live as a seed in the soil of a great empire. He prayed in the same way every day, “three times a day,” with the windows of his upper room open toward Jerusalem, even when the king's decree made this dangerous, and he would not hide his prayer. The Syriac tradition, which prays the hours seven times a day, has always seen in Daniel a teacher of the praying hours. He read the scriptures with attention: “I, Daniel, perceived in the books the number of years” of Jeremiah, and he turned to the Lord “by prayer and supplication with fasting and sackcloth and ashes.” Notice the way he prays: “we have sinned and done wrong.” He, the blameless one, takes the sins of his people on his own lips. That is exactly what today's Epistle describes: “We who are strong ought to put up with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves. Each of us must please our neighbor for the good purpose of building up the neighbor. For Christ did not please himself.” Daniel did not please himself; he bore the failings of his people before God.

Paul adds the reason why the Church reads the prophets: “For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, so that by steadfastness and by the encouragement of the scriptures we might have hope.” The scriptures teach us to be steadfast when the seed seems small and the yeast seems hidden. Daniel saw, in another vision, “one like a human being coming with the clouds of heaven,” to whom was given “dominion and glory and kingship, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him,” a kingship that “shall never be destroyed.” Jesus took that figure to himself when he spoke of the Son of Man. So the small seed of the Gospel and the great kingdom of Daniel are one reality. Paul ends: “May the God of steadfastness and encouragement grant you to live in harmony with one another, in accordance with Christ Jesus, so that together you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.” The seed grows when it grows in harmony, with one voice.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel Jesus gives two short parables. The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed, the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches (Matthew 13:31–32). And it is like yeast that a woman mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened (Matthew 13:33). God's kingdom begins small and hidden, and it grows.

Matthew says that Jesus spoke in parables to fulfil what had been spoken through the prophet: I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world (Matthew 13:35). Today the Church remembers the prophet Daniel, who blessed the God who reveals deep and hidden things (Daniel 2:22). Daniel saw a stone cut out, not by human hands, which became a great mountain and filled the whole earth (Daniel 2:35). The Fathers saw in that stone Christ himself.

Daniel lived as a believer in a great empire. Three times a day he knelt to pray with the windows of his room open toward Jerusalem (Daniel 6:10). He prayed for his people: we have sinned and done wrong (Daniel 9:5). That is what St Paul means when he says today: We who are strong ought to put up with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves (Romans 15:1). And the scriptures were written so that by steadfastness and by their encouragement we might have hope (Romans 15:4).

Let us trust the small seeds of God in our lives, a little prayer, a little kindness, and let us pray together with one voice, so that his kingdom may grow in us. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know how tiny a mustard seed is? Smaller than a pin's head! But when it is planted it grows into a big plant where little birds come to rest. Jesus says God's kingdom is like that. Today we remember Daniel, who prayed three times every day even when it was dangerous, and God took care of him. When you say a little prayer, or do a little kindness, it is like a tiny seed. God makes it grow.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the stone cut out without human hands, the seed that grows into the tree that shelters all, the yeast hidden in the flour of the world. We thank you for the prophet Daniel, who prayed in Babylon with his windows open toward Jerusalem, who bore the sins of his people on his lips, and who saw your kingdom coming. Teach us to trust the small beginnings of your grace, to pray at every hour, and to bear with one another as you did not please yourself. Keep us steadfast and encouraged by the scriptures, and let us with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers Daniel the Prophet, whose book speaks of a kingdom that begins as a small stone and fills the whole earth. In the Gospel Jesus tells two short parables of the kingdom: a mustard seed and a pinch of yeast, both small, hidden and growing. In the epistle Paul asks the strong in faith to bear with the weak, as Christ did. The key words are seed, yeast and steadfastness.

Romans 15:1–6 · Not to please ourselves

Setting.

From 14:1 Paul has been speaking to a church divided over food and holy days. Some Christians in Rome, probably many of them Jewish believers, felt bound to avoid certain foods and keep certain days; others, probably many of them Gentiles, felt at liberty to eat anything. Paul names the first group “the weak” in faith and the second “the strong”, and he places himself with the strong (15:1; compare 14:14). Our verses are the conclusion of that long section.

He does not tell the strong to prove they are right. He asks them to carry the weaker, as Christ carried them. The whole passage ends with a prayer for harmony and for one voice of praise (15:5–6), so that the Roman Church, made up of different people, would worship together.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the strong must bear with the weak and not please themselves.
  • v. 2: each should please the neighbour for the neighbour's good, to build up.
  • v. 3: the example of Christ, who bore insults.
  • v. 4: Scripture was written for our instruction, to give hope.
  • vv. 5–6: a prayer that, in harmony, they may glorify God with one voice.

Key words.

  • bastazein, to bear, to put up with: The word can mean to carry a load. Paul asks the strong to take up the weight of the weak, as one carries another's burden (Galatians 6:2).
  • oikodomē, building up: A building image that Paul loves. The test of an action in the Church is whether it builds up the neighbour (14:19; 1 Corinthians 14:26).
  • hypomonē, steadfastness: Patient endurance under pressure. It is a gift of “the God of steadfastness and encouragement” (15:5), and Scripture itself feeds it.

Verse by verse.

“We who are strong ought to put up with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves.” (15:1)

Strength in faith is for service. It is not a reason to look down on others. A strong conscience that has no patience for a weaker one has forgotten what it was given for.

“For Christ did not please himself; but, as it is written, “The insults of those who insult you have fallen on me.”” (15:3)

Paul quotes Psalm 69:9; the New Testament often applies Psalm 69 to the Passion (John 2:17; 15:25; 19:28–29). Christ took on himself the insults that were meant for God. Our patience with the weak is a small share in that.

“For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, so that by steadfastness and by the encouragement of the scriptures we might have hope.” (15:4)

“Former days” means the Old Testament, which Paul reads as written for Christians too. Hope is not a feeling that things will turn out well. It is built from steadfastness and from the comfort that Scripture gives.

“so that together you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.” (15:6)

The goal of the whole argument is worship. Differences over food and days must not break the one voice that rises in the Holy Qurbono.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that Sacred Scripture is the food of the soul, “the pure and perennial source of spiritual life”, and that the Church should give her children easy access to it (Dei Verbum 21–22; CCC 131–133). Paul's words in verse 4 are one of the Church's oldest statements that the whole Bible is meant for our instruction. Today's verses also speak to every parish. The Church teaches that the variety of gifts and peoples within her does not oppose her unity (CCC 814), and that charity is what holds it together. The strong are asked to give way for the sake of the weak, and the weak are not asked to be silent, but to be loved.

Matthew 13:31–35 · The kingdom small, hidden and growing

Setting.

Chapter 13 gathers the parables of the kingdom. Jesus has told the Sower, the Weeds and the Wheat, and now tells the Mustard Seed and the Yeast, two short parables that go together as a pair, one with a man in a field, the other with a woman in a kitchen. Matthew explains why Jesus teaches in parables (13:10–17, 34–35): the parables reveal the kingdom to those who are open to hear and hide it from those who are not.

The mustard plant of Palestine is a garden herb that can grow to the height of a small tree. Jesus speaks as people spoke: it was the smallest seed that a farmer would sow in his field, and he is not giving a lesson in botany. The picture of a tree where “the birds of the air” nest recalls Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4:12, where a great tree stands for a mighty kingdom sheltering the nations. There the tree is an empire; here it starts from the smallest seed.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–32: the mustard seed: the smallest seed becomes the largest plant, and birds nest in it.
  • v. 33: the yeast: a woman hides it in three measures of flour until it is all leavened.
  • vv. 34–35: Jesus speaks to the crowds only in parables, as the psalmist said: things hidden since the foundation of the world.

Key words.

  • kokkos sinapeōs, mustard seed: A tiny seed, used in Jewish speech for anything very small. Jesus also uses it for faith that can move mountains (Matthew 17:20).
  • zymē, yeast, leaven: Usually in the Bible a sign of corruption that spreads (1 Corinthians 5:6–8; Mark 8:15). Jesus surprises his hearers by using it for the kingdom, which also works from within and cannot be stopped.
  • enekrypsen, hid: Literally “hid in”. The woman does not stir the yeast in sight; she hides it in the flour. The kingdom works quietly, from the inside.

Verse by verse.

“The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in his field;” (13:31)

The kingdom does not begin with power. It begins with a Child in Bethlehem, a small group of disciples, a Cross. Jesus invites us to trust that what is small in God's hand will grow.

“it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree,” (13:32)

The contrast is the point: from the smallest to the greatest. The later growth of the Church out of a handful of apostles, as Matthew's first readers could see, is a living illustration.

“The kingdom of heaven is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” (13:33)

Three measures is a very large batch, more than twenty litres of flour. The little yeast works until the whole is changed. The kingdom is not only something to be reached at the end of time; it is already at work in the world.

“I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.” (13:35)

Matthew quotes Psalm 78:2, the psalm that tells Israel's story from Egypt to David. Jesus is the teacher who now tells what has been hidden since the beginning.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church “receives the mission to proclaim and to establish among all peoples the kingdom of Christ and of God, and she is, on earth, the seed and the beginning of that kingdom” (Lumen Gentium 5; CCC 764). Many of the Fathers read the mustard seed as Christ himself, buried in the earth and rising to become a tree (in substance), and the yeast as the Gospel quietly at work in the world. The kingdom is not a worldly power that wins by force. It spreads by the Word, by prayer, by love hidden in daily life, and by holy lives of people nobody noticed.

Questions people ask

Is the mustard seed really the smallest of all seeds?

In botany it is not; orchid seeds are smaller. Jesus uses the way ordinary people spoke. For the farmers of Galilee, the mustard seed was the smallest seed they sowed, and the proverb “as small as a mustard seed” was familiar. The point of the parable is the contrast between a tiny beginning and a surprising end, not a lesson in botany.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks the strong to carry the weak. Whom has God given me strength to carry, in my family or parish, and do I carry them gladly?
  • Both parables are about small, hidden things that grow. What small thing, a prayer or a kind act, is God asking me to plant today without seeing the result?
