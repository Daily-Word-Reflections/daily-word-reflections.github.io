Word for the day

“For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost.” Luke 19:10

Reflection

Four days after Suloko, the Church is in the upper room. The Lord has gone up to the Father, the Spirit has not yet come, and the Panchangam reminds us that in these ten days no wedding celebrations are held: the whole Church holds its breath and prays. It is fitting that today we remember one of the men who sat in that very room. St Luke gives the list of those who waited: “Peter, and John, and James, and Andrew, Philip and Thomas, Bartholomew and Matthew, James son of Alphaeus, and Simon the Zealot, and Judas son of James.” This is Mar Shemun Shleeha, whom our Church calls “the zealous one.” St Matthew and St Mark call him “the Cananaean,” from an Aramaic word for zeal; St Luke translates it as “the Zealot.” Whether Simon had belonged to the party of armed patriots who longed to drive out the Romans, or whether the name simply marked a man burning with zeal for God's Law, the Gospels do not say, and scholars still discuss it. What happened to him after Pentecost is told differently by different Churches: some ancient traditions send him with St Jude to preach in Persia and to die there as a martyr; others speak of Egypt or other lands. We hold to what is sure: Jesus chose him, Jesus changed the direction of his zeal, and on the day of Pentecost he was among those on whom the fire came down.

The Gospel shows us why such a man could follow Jesus. In Jericho there lived Zacchaeus, “a chief tax collector” who “was rich.” Tax collectors worked for the occupying power and often took more than was due; a zealot would have counted him a traitor to his people. Yet Zacchaeus, short in stature and long in curiosity, “ran ahead and climbed a sycamore tree to see him.” Jesus stopped beneath the tree: “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today.” The crowd grumbled, “He has gone to be the guest of one who is a sinner.” But grace had already done its work. Zacchaeus stood and said, “Look, half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much.” Then came the great word: “Today salvation has come to this house, because he too is a son of Abraham. For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost.” Think of the company of the Twelve: Simon the Zealot and Matthew the tax collector sat at the same table and ate the same bread. Only Jesus could make brothers of such men. St Augustine says that Zacchaeus climbed the sycamore as we must climb the Cross: the humble wood that seems foolish to the proud is the place from which we see the Lord passing by (Sermon 174, in substance). Simon's zeal did not die when he met Jesus; it was turned from hatred of the sinner to love of the lost.

St Paul's words to the Corinthians come from the same season of the year. “I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost,” he writes, “for a wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.” Paul counted the days towards Pentecost, as we do now. He saw a door open, and he saw opposition too, and he did not run from either. Then he speaks with tenderness of his younger helper: “If Timothy comes, see that he has nothing to fear among you, for he is doing the work of the Lord just as I am; therefore let no one despise him.” And of Apollos, who would not come just then: “He will come when he has the opportunity.” Here is zeal that has learned patience and gentleness. It does not crush the young or force the reluctant; it waits for God's time. That is the lesson of these ten days. The apostles did not rush out of the upper room on the day after the Ascension to begin their own campaign. They waited, “constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus.” Holy zeal begins on its knees. Then the Spirit opens the wide door.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! We are living in the ten days between Suloko and Pentecost. The apostles are in the upper room, waiting and praying, and today our Church remembers one of them: Mar Shemun Shleeha, St Simon the Zealot. We know little about his life, but his name tells us something important. He was a man of zeal, a man with fire in his heart. Before he met Jesus, that fire may have burned against the Romans and against anyone who served them. After he met Jesus, it burned for the Kingdom of God.

In the Gospel we meet the very kind of man a zealot would have despised: Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector, rich from other people's money. The crowd blocked his way, so he ran ahead and climbed a sycamore tree. And Jesus stopped. He looked up and said, “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today.” Notice: Jesus did not wait for Zacchaeus to become good before he came to his house. He came first, and his coming made Zacchaeus good. The man who had taken now began to give: half of his possessions to the poor, and four times back to anyone he had cheated.

Now picture the table of the Twelve. Simon the Zealot on one side, Matthew the former tax collector on the other. In the world they would have been enemies. At the table of Jesus they were brothers. This is what the Holy Spirit will do at Pentecost: not take away our zeal, but purify it. The anger goes; the fire stays. St Paul, waiting in Ephesus “until Pentecost,” shows us what purified zeal looks like. He sees a wide door and many adversaries, and he is not afraid. He tells the Corinthians to protect young Timothy and to let Apollos come in his own time. That is zeal with patience, zeal with gentleness.

So in these days of waiting, let us ask ourselves: is there a Zacchaeus in my life, someone I have written off? Let us pray for that person by name until Pentecost. And let us let Jesus say to us too, “I must stay at your house today.” He will say it at this altar, when he comes to us in his Body and Blood. Let us hurry down and welcome him with joy.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Have you ever climbed a tree to see something far away? In today's Gospel a short man named Zacchaeus climbed a tree to see Jesus, because the crowd was too big. Nobody liked Zacchaeus, because he took too much money from people. But Jesus stopped right under the tree, looked up and called him by his name! Jesus went to his house, and Zacchaeus was so happy that he decided to give money to the poor and pay back everyone he had cheated. Today we also remember St Simon, one of Jesus' twelve friends. He had a fiery heart, and Jesus taught him to use that fire for love. This week, be kind to someone others leave out. That is what Jesus did.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you came to seek out and to save the lost, and you stopped beneath the sycamore tree to call Zacchaeus by name. You chose Mar Shemun the Zealot and turned the fire of his heart towards your Kingdom. In these days of waiting between your Ascension and the coming of the Holy Spirit, gather us with your apostles and with your holy Mother in prayer. Purify our zeal from anger and pride, make it patient and gentle, and open for us the wide door of your work. By the prayers of Mar Shemun Shleeha, let salvation come to our homes today, and make us glad to welcome you. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Shemun Shleeha, St Simon the Apostle, called the Zealot, the readings show two kinds of zeal turned toward Christ. In the Epistle Paul plans his journeys with zeal for the Gospel: a wide door has opened in Ephesus, and he will stay there until Pentecost. In the Gospel a rich tax collector, eager to see Jesus, runs ahead and climbs a tree, and the Lord invites himself into his house. Simon's zeal, whatever it was before, became zeal for the Lord who came “to seek out and to save the lost”. The key words are door, today and seek.

1 Corinthians 16:5–12 · A wide door for effective work has opened

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, as verse 8 shows, probably around AD 53 to 55. He had founded the church in Corinth some years before (Acts 18:1–11). The letter answers many questions and troubles: divisions, a case of incest, lawsuits, marriage, food offered to idols, the Lord's Supper, spiritual gifts, the resurrection.

Chapter 16 is the practical close of the letter: the collection for the poor of Jerusalem, travel plans, news of fellow workers, and greetings. Even these plain lines show how the apostles worked: as a team, under God's providence, with care for one another.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: Paul will come after passing through Macedonia, and hopes to stay some time.
  • vv. 8–9: he will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost: a wide door, many adversaries.
  • vv. 10–11: receive Timothy well and send him back in peace.
  • v. 12: Apollos will come when he has the opportunity.

Key words.

  • pentēkostē, Pentecost: The fiftieth day, the Jewish Feast of Weeks, counted seven weeks from the offering of the first sheaf (Leviticus 23:15–16; Deuteronomy 16:9–10). Paul still reckons his year by Israel's feasts. For Christians the same day had become the day of the Spirit (Acts 2:1).
  • thyra, door: Paul often speaks of an open door for the Gospel (2 Corinthians 2:12; Colossians 4:3), and Luke uses the same image of the door of faith opened to the Gentiles (Acts 14:27). The door is opened by God; the apostle's task is to go through it.
  • energēs, effective: Work that is active and bears fruit. The open door does not mean an easy road. In the same verse Paul says that “there are many adversaries”.

Verse by verse.

“if the Lord permits.” (16:7)

Paul makes plans, and good ones, but always under God's will. James gives the same counsel: “If the Lord wishes, we will live and do this or that” (James 4:15).

“But I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost,” (16:8)

These few words fix the place of writing and show that Paul kept the rhythm of the Jewish year.

“for a wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.” (16:9)

Opportunity and opposition come together. Acts tells of the riot of the silversmiths in Ephesus, who feared that Paul's preaching would ruin the trade in images of Artemis (Acts 19:23–41).

“for he is doing the work of the Lord just as I am;” (16:10)

Timothy was young (1 Timothy 4:12) and perhaps timid. Paul asks the Corinthians to respect him as they would the apostle himself, because the work is the Lord's.

“He will come when he has the opportunity.” (16:12)

Some in Corinth had set Apollos against Paul (1:12; 3:4–6). Paul speaks of him warmly as “our brother”. The apostles did not compete.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in these lines that mission is the work of many, sent and supported by a community. The Catechism teaches that on the day of Pentecost, when the seven weeks of Easter had come to an end, Christ's Passover was fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit (CCC 731). Paul's waiting in Ephesus “until Pentecost” fits this season well: the Church in these days waits and prays, and then goes through the doors God opens. Paul's respect for Timothy and Apollos also teaches the Church's ministers to support one another rather than compete.

Luke 19:1–10 · Today salvation has come to this house

Setting.

This is Jesus' last stop before he goes up to Jerusalem. Luke has told the long journey since 9:51, and just before this, near Jericho, Jesus has healed a blind beggar who cried out, “Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!” (18:35–43). The story of Zacchaeus is found only in Luke.

Jericho, near the Jordan, was a rich city on an important road, a natural place for collecting taxes and tolls. Tax collectors worked for the Roman authorities through contractors, and many grew rich by charging more than was due. They were despised by their neighbours as collaborators and cheats.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: Zacchaeus wants to see Jesus and climbs a sycamore tree.
  • vv. 5–6: Jesus calls him by name and comes to his house; Zacchaeus welcomes him with joy.
  • v. 7: the crowd grumbles.
  • v. 8: Zacchaeus gives half to the poor and promises fourfold restitution.
  • vv. 9–10: salvation today; the Son of Man came to seek and save the lost.

Key words.

  • architelōnēs, chief tax collector: A word found nowhere else in the New Testament. Zacchaeus was not just a tax collector but the head of others, and rich. Luke has just said how hard it is for the rich to enter the Kingdom (18:24–27); here is a rich man who enters.
  • sēmeron, today: A key word of Luke's Gospel: “to you is born this day a Saviour” (2:11), “Today this scripture has been fulfilled” (4:21), “today you will be with me in Paradise” (23:43). Salvation is not only for later. It comes today.
  • zētein, to seek: Zacchaeus “was trying to see” (literally, was seeking to see) who Jesus was (v. 3). At the end we learn that the Son of Man came to seek the lost (v. 10). The man who sought was himself being sought.

Verse by verse.

“He was trying to see who Jesus was,” (19:3)

Perhaps curiosity, perhaps something deeper. God can use even a small desire to begin a great conversion.

“Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today.” (19:5)

Jesus calls him by name before Zacchaeus has said anything. The word “must” is the language of God's plan in Luke: this visit is part of the Saviour's mission.

“Look, half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor;” (19:8)

The law required one who stole a sheep to repay four sheep (Exodus 22:1). Zacchaeus goes beyond what anyone would demand. Some scholars, noting that the Greek verbs are in the present tense, read his words as a description of what he already did; many others see a conversion here, the fruit of Jesus' welcome.

“Today salvation has come to this house, because he too is a son of Abraham.” (19:9)

The man the crowd had written off is restored to the people of God. Salvation comes to “this house”: conversion touches a whole household.

“For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost.” (19:10)

The words recall Ezekiel 34, where God promises to seek out his lost sheep himself. In Jesus the Shepherd has come.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses Zacchaeus to teach that justice requires restitution of what has been taken wrongly: “if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much” (CCC 2412). It also notes that Jesus' welcome of sinners at table expresses in an astonishing way God's forgiveness and their return to the people of God (CCC 1443). The order matters: Jesus first comes to Zacchaeus' house, and then Zacchaeus changes. Grace comes first, and conversion is our answer to it.

Questions people ask

Who was Simon the Zealot?

He is one of the Twelve, named in every list of the apostles. Luke calls him “the Zealot” (Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13); Mark and Matthew call him “the Cananaean” (Mark 3:18; Matthew 10:4), from an Aramaic word that also means zealous, not from the land of Canaan. It may mean that he had belonged to a group zealous for the Law and for Israel's freedom, or simply that he was a man of great zeal. Later traditions about where he preached differ, and we cannot be sure of them.

Was it a sin to be a tax collector?

The work itself was not forbidden. When tax collectors asked John the Baptist what to do, he did not tell them to leave their work but to collect no more than was due (Luke 3:12–13). The sin was the fraud and greed that often went with it. Zacchaeus' conversion shows itself in honesty and generosity.

Why did the crowd grumble?

They saw Jesus going to the house of a man everyone knew as a sinner, and they thought it made Jesus unclean or approved the man's ways. Luke shows the same grumbling when Jesus eats with sinners (15:2). Jesus' answer is always the same: he came for the lost.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church honours Mar Shemun Shleeha today, as the Byzantine Churches also do; the Latin Church keeps his feast with St Jude on 28 October. Shleeha means one who is sent. We know little of Simon's life, but we know the most important thing: Jesus chose him, and his zeal was turned into zeal for the Kingdom. In the Holy Qurbono the Lord comes to our house as he came to the house of Zacchaeus, and Holy Communion is meant to bear fruit in honesty, generosity and the making good of wrongs. We ask Mar Shemun to pray that our zeal may always be the zeal of love.

For study and teaching

  • What “door” has God opened for me to share the faith, and what “adversaries” make me hesitate?
  • Zacchaeus climbed a tree to see Jesus. What effort am I willing to make to see him more clearly?
  • Is there anything I have taken wrongly, in money, time or reputation, that I need to give back?
  • Simon was called the Zealot. What am I zealous about, and how can that zeal serve Christ?
