Readings

Word for the day

“So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” Romans 10:17

Reflection

In the first week of the season of Pentecost our Church gathers four saints into one feast: the Four Evangelists, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, through whose writing the good news of Jesus reaches every generation. Matthew was the tax collector whom Jesus called from his booth (Matthew 9:9). Mark, ancient writers tell us, set down the preaching of St Peter, whose interpreter he had been (Papias, in Eusebius, Church History 3.39, in substance). Luke was the physician and companion of St Paul (Colossians 4:14), who also wrote the Acts of the Apostles. John is the disciple whom Jesus loved, who “has testified to these things and has written them” (John 21:24). Four men, four styles, four points of view, and one Lord. St Irenaeus, writing in the second century, already insisted that the Gospel is fourfold and yet one, held together by one Spirit, like the four winds or the four pillars of a single house (Against Heresies 3.11.8, in substance). The Church gave each evangelist a sign from the four living creatures that Ezekiel saw around the throne of God: the human face for Matthew, the lion for Mark, the ox for Luke, the eagle for John.

The Epistle explains why the evangelists matter. St Paul builds a chain of questions: “But how are they to call on one in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in one of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone to proclaim him? And how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent? As it is written, ‘How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!’” Then the conclusion: “So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” Nobody invents faith for himself. It comes by hearing, and we hear because someone was sent and someone wrote. Paul goes on to speak with sorrow and hope of his own people Israel, who had heard and not yet believed; elsewhere he insists that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). He also applies to the preachers a line from the psalm about the heavens: “Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.” The four Gospels are that voice. They have gone out in hundreds of languages; in our own Syriac tradition the Gospels were read for some centuries in a single woven harmony, until in the fifth century bishops such as Rabbula of Edessa saw to it that the four separate Gospels were read in the churches. Today they are read in Malayalam at our altars.

The Gospel is the last page of St Matthew. The eleven go to the mountain in Galilee: “When they saw him, they worshiped him; but some doubted.” Even at the end, the evangelist is honest about the disciples' weakness. Jesus does not wait for their doubts to vanish. He comes near and gives them everything: “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything that I have commanded you. And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” He is with us in the Holy Qurbono, and he is with us in the word. That is why, in our Qurbono, the Gospel is not simply read like a notice: it is called the Evangelion, it is proclaimed by the priest, and the people stand to hear the Lord himself speak. St Jerome said that ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ (Commentary on Isaiah, prologue, in substance). On this feast, take one of the four Gospels and begin to read it through, a chapter a day. Mark can be read in under three weeks. Let the evangelists do for you what they have done for the whole Church: bring you face to face with Jesus.

Theme: “So faith comes from what is heard.”

On the feast of the Four Evangelists: four voices and one Gospel, the word that is heard and believed, and the Lord who sends and stays with us.

Full homily

Dear friends, today our Church honours the Four Evangelists, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. Matthew the tax collector, Mark who wrote down Peter's preaching, Luke the physician and companion of Paul, John the beloved disciple. Four voices, one Lord. St Irenaeus said that the Gospel is fourfold, held together by one Spirit.

St Paul tells us why they matter: how are they to believe in one of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone to proclaim him? (Romans 10:14). So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ (Romans 10:17). Someone was sent, someone wrote, someone read it to us. How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news! (Romans 10:15).

In the Gospel the risen Lord meets the eleven on the mountain. They worshiped him; but some doubted (Matthew 28:17). Even so, he sends them: Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, and he promises: I am with you always, to the end of the age (Matthew 28:19–20). He is with us in the Qurbono and in the word. That is why we stand when the Evangelion is proclaimed.

Let us honour the evangelists by reading what they wrote. Take one Gospel and read a chapter a day. Read it with your family. Then share it: faith comes from what is heard, and someone near you is waiting to hear. Amen.

For children

Children, who wrote the stories of Jesus that we hear in church? Four friends of Jesus: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John! We call them the evangelists, which means people who bring good news. In pictures, Matthew has a man, Mark a lion, Luke an ox and John an eagle. At the end of Matthew's book, Jesus says: I am with you always. Whenever you hear the Gospel, Jesus is talking to you! Can you ask your parents to read you one story from a Gospel tonight?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, living Word of the Father, you gave your Church the four holy Gospels through Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, so that all who hear may believe and have life in your name. We thank you for the evangelists and for all who have handed on your word in every language, and in ours. Open our ears to hear you, our hearts to believe, and our lips to proclaim you. Send us, as you sent the eleven, to make disciples, and keep your promise to be with us always, to the end of the age. With your Father and your Holy Spirit, now and for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today our Church honours the four Evangelists together: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. The Epistle traces the chain by which faith reaches anyone at all: someone is sent, someone proclaims, someone hears, someone believes and calls on the Lord. “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!” The Gospel is the last page of Matthew, where the risen Lord sends the eleven to all nations and promises, “I am with you always.” The four Evangelists stand in that sending: what the apostles preached, they wrote down for every generation. The key words are sent, hear and with you.

Romans 10:14–20 · How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news

Setting.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth, around AD 57, to a church he had not yet visited. Chapters 9–11 wrestle with a question that grieved him deeply: why had most of his own people, Israel, not accepted Jesus as the Messiah? He begins with “great sorrow and unceasing anguish” (9:2). He ends by saying that “all Israel will be saved” (11:26) and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29).

Just before today's verses Paul has quoted the prophet Joel: “Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved” (10:13). Now he traces, link by link, the chain that makes such calling possible.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: the chain: call, believe, hear, proclaim, be sent; Isaiah 52:7.
  • vv. 16–17: not all have obeyed; faith comes from what is heard (Isaiah 53:1).
  • v. 18: the message has gone out to all the earth (Psalm 19:4).
  • vv. 19–20: Moses and Isaiah: God found by those who did not seek him (Deuteronomy 32:21; Isaiah 65:1).
  • ¶ The next verse (10:21) shows God holding out his hands “all day long” to his people. The passage ends not in rejection but in God's patience, and chapter 11 opens: “has God rejected his people? By no means!”

Key words.

  • apostellō, to send: From this verb comes the word apostle. Preaching in the Church is not self-appointed: “how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent?” The Evangelists wrote within this sending, and their books carry the preaching of the apostles.
  • euangelizomai, bring good news: The verb of Isaiah 52:7 in the Greek Old Testament, used of the messenger running over the mountains to Jerusalem to say, “Your God reigns.” Our word Evangelist comes from the same root.
  • akoē, what is heard: The same word means both the act of hearing and the message that is heard. Faith begins as an answer to a word that comes to us from outside. Paul adds “through the word of Christ”: the message is about Christ, and in it Christ himself speaks.

Verse by verse.

“And how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent? As it is written, “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!”” (10:15)

In Isaiah the feet belong to a runner bringing news of peace to Jerusalem after the exile. They are beautiful not for their shape but for the news they carry, and for the long road they have walked.

“So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” (10:17)

Faith is personal, but it is never private in its origin. No one gives it to himself. It comes through a voice: a parent, a catechist, a priest, a book of the Gospels read aloud.

“Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.” (10:18)

Psalm 19 says this of the heavens, which declare God's glory without speech. Paul applies it to the apostles' preaching. He quotes the Greek Old Testament, “their voice”; the Hebrew text has “their line”.

“I have been found by those who did not seek me; I have shown myself to those who did not ask for me.” (10:20)

From Isaiah 65:1. Paul reads it of the Gentiles who came to faith without having looked for it. God goes ahead of every search.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as the charter of her preaching. The Catechism quotes verses 14–15 to show that no one can give himself the mandate to preach the Gospel: the minister of Christ is sent and speaks with an authority he has received (CCC 875). The Second Vatican Council teaches that among all the Scriptures the Gospels have a special place, because they are the principal witness to the life and teaching of the incarnate Word, and that the Church has always held that the four Gospels are of apostolic origin (Dei Verbum 18). Paul's sorrow for his own people is also the Church's: God has not rejected Israel, and the Church relates to the Jewish people as to those to whom God first spoke (CCC 839).

Matthew 28:16–20 · Go therefore and make disciples of all nations

Setting.

These are the last verses of Matthew's Gospel. On Easter morning the risen Lord met the women and gave them a message for “my brothers”: go to Galilee, and there they will see me (28:10). Galilee is where Jesus began his preaching, “Galilee of the Gentiles” (4:15). Matthew does not describe the Ascension. His Gospel ends with a promise of presence.

Matthew's Gospel began with the name Emmanuel, “God is with us” (1:23). It ends with “I am with you always”. The whole book lies between these two sayings.

Structure.

  • v. 16: the eleven go to the mountain in Galilee.
  • v. 17: worship, and doubt.
  • v. 18: all authority given to Jesus.
  • vv. 19–20a: go, make disciples, baptize, teach.
  • v. 20b: I am with you always.

Key words.

  • mathēteusate, make disciples: The one main command in the sentence; going, baptizing and teaching serve it. A disciple is a learner who follows the master and lives with him. The mission is not only to win agreement but to form lives.
  • panta ta ethnē, all nations: During his ministry Jesus sent the Twelve first to “the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:6). Now the circle opens to every people. Israel is not left out. The promise to Abraham, that in him “all the families of the earth shall be blessed”, is reaching its goal (Genesis 12:3).
  • eis to onoma, in the name: Baptism is “in the name”, singular, of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. The Greek suggests being plunged into, and belonging to, the one God who is three persons.

Verse by verse.

“When they saw him, they worshiped him; but some doubted.” (28:17)

Matthew is honest. On the mountain of the risen Lord, worship and hesitation stand side by side. The verb for doubt is the one Jesus used to Peter sinking in the waves: “why did you doubt?” (14:31). Jesus does not send the hesitant away. In the next verse he comes near to them.

“All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.” (28:18)

Daniel saw one like a son of man, to whom was given dominion over all peoples (Daniel 7:13–14). The risen Jesus speaks as that Son of Man. The mission rests on his authority, not on the strength of the eleven.

“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,” (28:19)

The clearest naming of the Trinity in the Gospels. The early Church baptized with these words; the Didache, a very early Christian handbook, already gives them.

“And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” (28:20)

Not “I will be” but “I am”. Many readers hear in it an echo of God's name revealed to Moses, “I am who I am” (Exodus 3:14). The Church goes out with the Lord already at her side.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses as the origin of the Church's missionary mandate (CCC 849), and teaches that we are baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, not in their names, for there is only one God (CCC 233). St Irenaeus of Lyons, writing around AD 180, held that there can be neither more nor fewer than four Gospels, as there are four winds and four quarters of the world, and he linked them with the four living creatures around God's throne (Against Heresies 3.11.8, in substance; Revelation 4:6–7). St Jerome later gave the symbols that Christian art still uses: the man for Matthew, the lion for Mark, the ox for Luke and the eagle for John.

Questions people ask

Why four Gospels and not one?

The early Church received four written witnesses to the one Gospel of Jesus Christ, each rooted in the apostles' preaching. Their differences in order and detail are like four witnesses describing the same events. In the second century Tatian wove them into a single account, the Diatessaron, which was read in Syriac-speaking churches for a long time; Mar Aprem wrote a commentary on it. In the end the Church kept the four, because each brings out something of the one Lord in its own way (Dei Verbum 18–19; CCC 125–127).

Who were the Evangelists?

Matthew and John are counted by the Church's tradition among the Twelve. Early writers link Mark with Peter and Luke with Paul. Many scholars today think the Gospels as we have them were written down between about AD 65 and 100, drawing on the preaching and tradition of the apostles. The Church holds them to be of apostolic origin and to hand on faithfully what Jesus really did and taught (Dei Verbum 18–19).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church honours the four Evangelists on one day. In the Holy Qurbono the reading of the Gospel is surrounded with signs of honour: lighted candles, incense, and the people standing to listen. The Gospel is announced with the name of the apostle or evangelist who wrote it, and the people answer with praise to the Lord who sent him. The Syriac Churches once read the four Gospels woven into one, in the Diatessaron, and then returned to the four separate books. Today we thank God for the four voices through which the one Gospel has reached Kerala and our own homes.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that faith comes from what is heard. Through whose voice did I first hear the Gospel, and have I thanked God for that person?
  • Matthew is honest that “some doubted”. What doubt do I bring to the Lord, and what does it mean to me that he still comes near?
  • Which Gospel do I know best, and which least? How could our family read one Gospel through, slowly, in the coming months?
