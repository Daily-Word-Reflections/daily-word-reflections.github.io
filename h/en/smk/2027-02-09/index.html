Word for the day

“I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” John 10:11

Reflection

Abram has believed, but he still asks: “O Lord God, how am I to know that I shall possess it?” God does not scold him. He asks for a sacrifice: a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove and a pigeon. Abram cuts the animals in two and waits, and “when birds of prey came down on the carcasses, Abram drove them away.” Then darkness falls, and God speaks of the long road ahead: his descendants will be strangers and slaves for four hundred years, and then come out free. Faith is not the end of questions; it is staying beside the sacrifice and driving away whatever would snatch it. In the Fast our prayer, fasting and almsgiving are such an offering, and the birds of prey are many: distraction, discouragement, the voice that says it is useless.

Paul tells us why we can keep watch without fear: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set you free from the law of sin and of death.” The Fast is not an attempt to earn God's love by our own strength; it is letting the Spirit who dwells in us set our minds “on the things of the Spirit”, which are “life and peace.” And in the Gospel Jesus shows whose sacrifice saves us: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” Unlike the hired hand, he does not run when the wolf comes. He knows his own, and he will bring the other sheep too, “so there will be one flock, one shepherd.” St Gregory the Great says that the Good Shepherd first did what he taught: he laid down his life (in substance).

Full homily

Dear friends, Abram believed God and yet asked: how am I to know? God answered with a covenant. Abram prepared the sacrifice, and when birds of prey came down on the carcasses, Abram drove them away (Genesis 15:8, 11).

Our Fast is an offering, and birds of prey come to snatch it: distraction, discouragement, the voice that says it is useless. Like Abram, we must keep watch.

But we do not keep watch in fear. There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1). The Spirit of life dwells in us.

And Jesus says: I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). His sacrifice saves us; ours is only to stay close to him and listen to his voice. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus is the Good Shepherd. He knows your name, he takes care of you, and he even gave his life for you. In the Fast, let us listen to his voice by reading the Bible.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, you laid down your life for us and took away our condemnation. Pour your Spirit into our hearts, so that we may keep watch over the offering of this Fast and drive away all that would steal it. Gather all your sheep into one flock, and keep us close to your voice. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Abram has believed; now he asks how he can know. God answers with a rite and with a hard word: his descendants will suffer before they are delivered. The epistle (Romans 8:1–11) speaks to people who still struggle in the flesh: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus” (8:1). In the Gospel the God who made promises to Abram comes in person as the shepherd who lays down his life. Faith does not remove the dark. It knows who is with us in it.

Genesis 15:7–16 · How am I to know?

Setting.

The scene continues the same chapter. Abram has believed the promise of a son (15:6), and God now turns to the promise of land. The animals he is told to bring belong to a covenant rite. In the ancient Near East those making a solemn pact could cut animals in two and pass between the parts; Jeremiah 34:18 is the clearest witness in the Bible. The passing itself comes in vv. 17–21. Here the pieces are laid out and Abram waits.

Structure.

  • v. 7: God names himself as the one who brought Abram from Ur.
  • v. 8: Abram's question.
  • vv. 9–11: the animals are cut in two, and Abram drives away the birds of prey.
  • v. 12: deep sleep and terrifying darkness.
  • vv. 13–16: God foretells slavery in a foreign land, judgment on the oppressor, and the return.

Key words.

  • tardēmāh, deep sleep: The same word as the sleep God sent on Adam (Genesis 2:21). It is more than tiredness: a sleep that God sends when he is about to do something the person cannot do or watch for himself.
  • gēr, alien: A foreigner living in a land not his own, without the rights of its people. Abraham's descendants will be strangers, and the Law later tells Israel to care for the stranger because of this memory (Exodus 22:21).
  • ‘innû, they shall oppress: From the verb ‘ānāh, to afflict or humble. The same verb describes what Egypt did to Israel in Exodus 1:11–12. God sees that history before it begins.

Verse by verse.

“O Lord God, how am I to know that I shall possess it?” (15:8)

This comes just after “he believed”. Faith is not silence. A believer may ask God how, and God does not rebuke Abram; he answers.

“And when birds of prey came down on the carcasses, Abram drove them away.” (15:11)

A small, plain act. All Abram can do is keep watch over what he has laid before God until God acts. It is a good picture of the patient side of a fast.

“your offspring shall be aliens in a land that is not theirs, and shall be slaves there, and they shall be oppressed for four hundred years” (15:13)

God does not hide the long dark from Abram. The number is a round one: Exodus 12:40 gives 430 years. Verse 16 adds that the land is given in God's own time and with his justice: “the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete”.

How the Church reads it.

St Stephen recalls this promise in his speech (Acts 7:6–7), and the Letter to the Hebrews sees Abraham as a pilgrim who “set out, not knowing where he was going” (Hebrews 11:8). The Catechism teaches that God in his providence can draw good even from the consequences of evil, though we often see it only with time (CCC 312). The Church reads this passage as a school of hope: God's promise stands even when the road to it passes through suffering.

John 10:11–16 · The good shepherd

Setting.

John 10 follows the healing of the man born blind (John 9), and it is addressed to those who should have cared for him. The picture has deep roots. In Ezekiel 34 God speaks against shepherds who feed themselves and then says, “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep” (34:15). When Jesus says “I am the good shepherd”, he stands where Psalm 23 and Ezekiel place God himself.

Structure.

  • v. 11: the shepherd lays down his life.
  • vv. 12–13: the hired hand runs away.
  • vv. 14–15: mutual knowledge, like that of the Father and the Son.
  • v. 16: other sheep, one flock, one shepherd.

Key words.

  • kalos, good: More than morally good: fine, noble, true to what a shepherd should be. This is the shepherd as he was always meant to be.
  • misthōtos, hired hand: A man working for wages. He is not called wicked; he simply does not own the sheep, and so when danger comes he has no reason to stay.
  • tithēmi tēn psychēn, to lay down one's life: John alone uses this phrase in the Gospels, and repeats it (10:15, 17–18; 13:37; 15:13). Jesus' death is not something that happens to him against his will; he gives his life.

Verse by verse.

“I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” (10:11)

In ordinary life a shepherd risks himself but hopes to live, since a dead shepherd leaves the flock unprotected. Here the shepherd's death is the life of the sheep.

“I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father.” (10:14–15)

To know here means a bond of love, not information. The disciple is drawn into the very relationship of the Son and the Father.

“So there will be one flock, one shepherd.” (10:16)

The “other sheep” are commonly understood as the Gentiles who would come to believe (compare John 11:52). The unity comes from the one voice they hear.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, following Vatican II, calls the Church a flock of which God foretold that he would be the shepherd (CCC 754; Lumen Gentium 6). It also says that Christ always gives his Church the gift of unity, and that she must always pray and work to preserve and perfect it (CCC 820). St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, asks his people whether they know the shepherd not only by faith but by love and by what they do (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 14).

For study and teaching

  • Abram asks God, “how am I to know?” What honest question do I need to bring to God in prayer this week?
  • The hired hand leaves because the sheep are not his own. Who has been given to my care, and where am I tempted to run away?
