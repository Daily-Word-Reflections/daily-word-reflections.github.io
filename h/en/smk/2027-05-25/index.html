Word for the day

“So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” 2 Corinthians 4:16

Reflection

Today the Panchangam names a saint called Sophia and tells us nothing more. Several holy women of that name are honoured in the calendars of East and West, and we need not guess which one our Church has in mind. The name itself is enough to pray with. Sophia is the Greek word for wisdom, and the readings of the day are a lesson in the wisdom that looks beyond what can be seen. St Paul writes to the Corinthians from the middle of great hardship. He has been afflicted, perplexed, persecuted and struck down, and his body carries the marks. Yet he says: “So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” Every one of us knows the outer nature that wastes away: the knees that complain on the church steps, the eyes that need stronger glasses, the strength that is not what it was. Paul does not pretend otherwise. But he sees something growing while the body declines: an inner person, renewed day by day by the Spirit who was poured out at Pentecost. Old age, illness and loss are not the end of the story for a Christian; they can be the very place where the inner person grows young.

Paul continues: “For this slight momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure, because we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen; for what can be seen is temporary, but what cannot be seen is eternal.” He calls his sufferings slight and momentary, not because they were small, but because he has placed them on a scale beside the glory that is coming, and the glory is heavier. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter, says that Paul makes affliction light by setting it beside the reward, and short by setting it beside eternity (Homilies on 2 Corinthians, in substance). That is true wisdom, the wisdom the name Sophia speaks of: to judge things by their weight in eternity. The house, the land, the results in the examination, the praise of the neighbours, all these can be seen, and all are temporary. Faith, hope and love, the soul of a child, a kindness done in secret, the grace of the Holy Qurbono, these cannot be seen, and they remain. A wise person spends the visible to gain the invisible.

The Gospel tells how to do that. Jesus is near Jerusalem, and his hearers think the kingdom is about to appear at once. So he tells of a nobleman who goes to a distant country to receive royal power and then return. Before leaving he gives ten servants a pound each and says: “Do business with these until I come back.” The time between his going and his return is our time, the time of the Church after the Ascension and Pentecost. When he comes back, one servant has made ten pounds and hears, “Well done, good slave! Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities.” Another wrapped his pound in a cloth, because he was afraid. The parable ends with hard words about the citizens who said, “We do not want this man to rule over us.” Jesus is not teaching violence; he is going up to Jerusalem to die for those who reject him, and on the Cross he will pray for them. The harsh ending tells us how serious it is to refuse the true King. St Gregory the Great explained that the gift entrusted to each of us is whatever we have received: let the one who has understanding take care not to keep silent, the one who has means not to be slow in mercy, the one who has a skill to share its use (Homilies on the Gospels 9, in substance). The pound is the faith, the gifts of the Spirit and the time given to us. Do not bury it. Trade with it until he comes.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Panchangam remembers St Sophia. Her name means wisdom, and St Paul shows us what Christian wisdom looks like: so we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day (2 Corinthians 4:16).

Paul calls his many sufferings a slight momentary affliction, because they are preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure (2 Corinthians 4:17). He has put them on a scale next to heaven. And he tells us where to look: what can be seen is temporary, but what cannot be seen is eternal (2 Corinthians 4:18).

In the Gospel Jesus tells of a nobleman who gave his servants a pound each and said: do business with these until I come back (Luke 19:13). Two used it well and were praised: because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities (Luke 19:17). One hid it in a cloth because he was afraid. The Lord has given each of us faith, gifts and time. This is the time between his Ascension and his return.

Let us not lose heart when the body grows weak, and let us not bury what we have received. A visit to a sick person, a song in the choir, an hour with a child learning the catechism: these are the pounds we trade for the kingdom. With St Sophia, let us choose the wisdom that looks at what cannot be seen. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a king who gave his helpers some money and said: use it well until I come back. Two helpers worked hard and made more. One was scared and hid the money in a cloth. God has given each of you something special: maybe you sing well, or draw, or you are good at making friends. Do not hide it! Use it to make someone happy and to make God happy too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you went away to receive your kingdom and you will come again in glory. While we wait, you have entrusted to us the gifts of your grace. Renew our inner nature day by day by your Holy Spirit, even when our outer nature grows weak. Give us the true wisdom that looks at what cannot be seen, and keep us from the fear that buries what you have given. With St Sophia and all your saints, let us be found trustworthy in small things, so that at your coming we may hear your word of welcome. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today are about the time in between: the time after the Lord has gone to the Father and before he returns. Paul tells the Corinthians how to live it when the body grows weak: “we do not lose heart”, because something within is being renewed day by day. In Luke the nobleman goes away to receive royal power and leaves his servants with one pound each and a single command: “Do business with these until I come back.” The key words are renewal and faithfulness.

2 Corinthians 4:16–18 · We do not lose heart

Setting.

Paul wrote 2 Corinthians from Macedonia, probably around AD 55–57, in a hard season of his ministry. In chapter 4 he speaks of the Gospel as a treasure carried “in clay jars” (4:7), and of himself as “afflicted in every way, but not crushed” (4:8). Just before our verses he gives the ground of his hope: the one who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also (4:14). Today's three verses draw the conclusion.

Structure.

  • v. 16: we do not lose heart: the outer nature wastes away, the inner nature is renewed.
  • v. 17: light and passing affliction set beside an eternal weight of glory.
  • v. 18: what is seen and passes, and what is unseen and lasts.

Key words.

  • ouk enkakoumen, we do not lose heart: The same words open the chapter (4:1). To lose heart is to give up in weariness. Paul does not say he never feels tired. He says he does not give in to it.
  • ho exō / ho esō anthrōpos, the outer / the inner nature: Literally “the outer person” and “the inner person”. Paul is not dividing body from soul as if the body did not matter. The outer person is our life as it can be seen and as it ages; the inner person is the life that God is making new in us.
  • baros doxēs, weight of glory: In Hebrew the word for glory, kābôd, also carries the sense of weight. Paul puts the two on a scale: the affliction is light, the glory is heavy.

Verse by verse.

“Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” (4:16)

“Day by day” is the rhythm of grace. Renewal is not only a single event at Baptism. It goes on in every day that is lived with Christ, even in old age and sickness.

“For this slight momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure,” (4:17)

Paul had been beaten, shipwrecked and imprisoned (11:23–27). He calls it “slight” and “momentary” only because he has set it beside eternity. In Romans he says the same: the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory to be revealed (Romans 8:18).

“because we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen;” (4:18)

The verb means to keep one's eyes fixed on a goal, as a runner looks at the finish.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these verses the virtue of hope, by which we desire eternal life as our happiness and rely not on our own strength but on the grace of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1817). They are often read at funerals and in the care of the sick, because they say what faith sees when the body fails. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter, says that Paul makes affliction light by setting it beside the reward, and short by setting it beside eternity (Homilies on 2 Corinthians, in substance).

Luke 19:11–27 · Do business with these until I come back

Setting.

Jesus is now near Jerusalem. He has just stayed in the house of Zacchaeus at Jericho (19:1–10), and the next scene is his entry into the city (19:28–40). Luke says why he tells this parable: the crowd “supposed that the kingdom of God was to appear immediately” (19:11). The parable answers that there will be a time of absence first, and a time of work.

The story would have sounded familiar. When Herod the Great died in 4 BC, his son Archelaus went to Rome to have his kingship confirmed by the emperor, and a delegation followed him to oppose it (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 17). Jesus uses this kind of event as the frame of his story. Matthew has a similar parable with talents (Matthew 25:14–30), but in Luke every servant receives the same sum: one mina, about a hundred days' wages.

Structure.

  • v. 11: why the parable is told.
  • vv. 12–14: the nobleman leaves; ten servants receive a pound each; the citizens refuse him.
  • vv. 15–19: he returns as king; two servants are rewarded with cities.
  • vv. 20–26: the third servant, who hid the pound out of fear.
  • v. 27: judgment on those who refused his rule.

Key words.

  • mna, pound: A Greek sum of money worth a hundred drachmas. It is not a fortune. The servants are tested on something modest, so that faithfulness, not natural ability, is what counts.
  • pragmateusasthe, do business: To trade, to put to work. The gift is not given to be kept safe but to be used and to grow.
  • pistos, trustworthy, faithful: The master's praise is not “successful” but “trustworthy”. The same word is used of the faithful steward in 12:42 and of being faithful in a very little in 16:10.

Verse by verse.

“Do business with these until I come back.” (19:13)

These words describe the life of the Church between the Ascension and the Lord's return. Luke opens Acts with the same horizon: “This Jesus… will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven” (Acts 1:11).

“Well done, good slave! Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities.” (19:17)

The reward is far beyond the work. A single pound becomes authority over ten cities. God is never outdone in generosity.

“for I was afraid of you, because you are a harsh man;” (19:21)

The third servant does nothing wrong with the money; he simply does nothing. What paralyses him is a false picture of his master. He is judged “by your own words” (19:22).

“I tell you, to all those who have, more will be given;” (19:26)

A law of the spiritual life: grace used grows, grace buried withers.

How the Church reads it.

Many interpreters see in the one pound the gift that every Christian receives alike, such as the word of God and the grace of Baptism, and in the cities the share in Christ's reign that he gives to the faithful. The harsh last verse belongs to the world of the story, where kings punished rebels. It is a warning that to refuse Christ's kingship has consequences, not a picture of a cruel Jesus: in the very next scene he weeps over Jerusalem (19:41). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church is a pilgrim, already sharing in the last age, who waits for the Lord's return and works while she waits (Lumen Gentium 48).

Questions people ask

Is God really a harsh master who reaps where he did not sow?

No. Those are the frightened servant's words, not the truth about the master, who in fact gives cities for a pound. The parable shows how fear can freeze a believer. St John writes: “There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear” (1 John 4:18). We work for the Lord not as slaves who dread him but as children who trust him.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Sophia, without saying which of the holy women of that name is meant. The name itself is a word to pray with: sophia is Greek for wisdom. Today's readings show what that wisdom is: to weigh things by eternity, and to use faithfully, while we wait, what the Lord has entrusted to us.

For study and teaching

  • What is the “one pound” the Lord has given me, and am I trading with it or keeping it wrapped in a cloth?
  • Paul's outer nature was wasting away. Where do I see my own inner nature being renewed day by day, and how can the Holy Qurbono feed that renewal?
