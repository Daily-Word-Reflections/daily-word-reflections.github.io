Word for the day

“A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots.” Isaiah 11:1

Reflection

On the last Sunday before Yaldo the Church reads names: the long genealogy of Jesus, and the list of Jacob's family going down to Egypt. These lists can seem dry, but they say something essential: God's salvation enters real history, through real families, over many generations.

The prophets used the image of a tree. The royal house of David had been cut down like a stump; but Isaiah says, “A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots.” Jeremiah repeats it: “I will raise up for David a righteous Branch.” When everything seemed dead, God was preparing new life from the old root.

Luke brings the promise to a dusty road. Because of a decree of Caesar Augustus, “Joseph also went from the town of Nazareth in Galilee to Judea, to the city of David called Bethlehem… He went to be registered with Mary, to whom he was engaged and who was expecting a child.” The emperor thinks he is counting his subjects; in fact he is bringing the Son of David to the town where the prophet Micah said the ruler of Israel would be born.

Peter in Acts recalls God's word to Abraham: “In your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Acts 3:25). The genealogy does not end with Jesus; it continues in us, the Church, who by Baptism are grafted into this family tree. As Yaldo approaches, we prepare not only to remember a birth, but to welcome the Saviour into our own family history.

Homily outline

Theme: “The shoot from the stump of Jesse.”

The Sunday before Christmas gives us a long list of names: the genealogy of Jesus. It looks dry, but it tells a great truth: God keeps his promises through real families, with their saints and their sinners, until the Saviour is born.

  • From a cut-down stump, Isaiah says, a new shoot will grow: no family history is beyond God's hope.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on this last Sunday before Yaldo, the Church gives us a list of names: the family of Jacob going down to Egypt, and the genealogy of Jesus. Some of us may wonder why. But these names tell us something beautiful: God did not save the world from a distance. He came through a family, generation after generation.

Isaiah uses a picture every farmer in Kerala knows. A tree is cut down; only a stump remains. Then one day a green shoot appears. “A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse.” When the house of David seemed finished, God was preparing the Saviour. When something in our life seems cut down, God can still make it sprout.

Then St Luke tells us how Mary and Joseph came to Bethlehem. Not by their own plan, but because the emperor ordered a census. A very inconvenient journey for a woman expecting a child! Yet God used it so that his Son would be born in the city of David, as the prophet had promised. God can use even the inconvenient events of our lives.

This week, pray for your family tree: for grandparents and great-grandparents who passed on the faith. Make your Christmas Confession. And on Christmas Eve, gather your family and read the Gospel of the Nativity together. Let us go to Bethlehem, the house of bread, to welcome the Saviour. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know the names of your grandparents? Jesus had grandparents and great-great-grandparents too! The Bible gives a long list of them, all the way back to Abraham and King David.

Mary and Joseph had to walk a long way to Bethlehem. It was hard, but God had a plan: Jesus would be born in the town of King David.

This Christmas, let's thank God for our family. Let's say together: “Thank you, God, for my family!”

Prayer

Lord God, from the stump of Jesse you made a Branch to grow, and through many generations you prepared the birth of your Son. Bless our families and our ancestors, make your Word live in our homes, and lead us like Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem, to welcome the Saviour. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This is the Sixth Sunday of Suboro, the last before Yaldo, and the Church spends it among names and family trees. The Evening Gospel (Matthew 1:1–17) gives the genealogy of Jesus, from Abraham through David and through the exile to “Joseph the husband of Mary”. The Old Testament list of Jacob's household going down to Egypt (Genesis 46:8–27) has Judah, Perez and Hezron in it, the same names that stand at the head of Matthew's line. These lists are not dull. They say that God's salvation enters real history through real families, some faithful and some not.

The other readings follow the promise along that line. God promises Solomon a throne, and warns that the house can be lost (1 Kings 9:3–7). The prophets looked beyond that loss: Isaiah saw a shoot from the stump (Isaiah 11:1–9), and Jeremiah, as the kingdom was collapsing, announced “a righteous Branch” for David (Jeremiah 23:5–8). In Acts (3:20–26) Peter returns to the oldest word of all, spoken to Abraham: “in your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed”.

We study three readings closely: Isaiah 11:1–9, the Old Testament lesson closest to the Gospel; Hebrews 7:14–17, the Pauline reading, which says what a family tree could not give Jesus; and Luke 2:1–5, where the promise walks down a road toward Bethlehem. The key words are root, descent and registered.

Isaiah 11:1–9 · A shoot from the stump of Jesse

Setting.

Isaiah preached in Jerusalem in the eighth century BC, when Assyria was swallowing up the nations around Judah and the kings of David's line were often a disappointment. The chapter before ends with a terrible image: the Lord “will hack down the thickets of the forest with an ax” (10:34). Then comes today's first verse, in the same landscape of felled trees.

Notice that the prophet says Jesse, not David. Going behind the king to his father, a farmer of Bethlehem (1 Samuel 16:1), suggests that God will begin again from the beginning, from small and humble things.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3a: the shoot and the Spirit who rests on him.
  • vv. 3b–5: his justice, especially for the poor.
  • vv. 6–8: creation at peace: predators and their prey, a child among snakes.
  • v. 9: no more harm on God's holy mountain; the earth full of the knowledge of the Lord.

Key words.

  • gēza‘, stump: What is left after the tree is cut. It looks dead. Isaiah had already spoken of a holy seed in a stump (6:13).
  • nēṣer, branch: A green shoot. Many scholars see a play on this word behind Matthew 2:23, where Jesus of Nazareth “will be called a Nazorean”. Jeremiah 23:5 uses another Hebrew word (ṣemaḥ) for the same hope.
  • rûaḥ YHWH, the spirit of the Lord: The Spirit who came on Israel's judges and on David (1 Samuel 16:13). Here the Spirit rests on the king and stays. The Hebrew names six qualities in three pairs. The Greek and Latin Bibles render “fear of the Lord” in two different ways (piety and fear), and from this the Church's tradition counts the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit.
  • da‘at YHWH, knowledge of the Lord: In Hebrew “knowing” means more than having information. It is a relationship of faithful love. For Isaiah that knowledge will one day cover the earth.

Verse by verse.

“A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots.” (11:1)

Hope in Isaiah starts where everything seems finished. St Ambrose and St Jerome read the “shoot” (Latin virga) as the Virgin Mary (virgo), and the flower that rises from it as Christ (in substance). The word play exists only in Latin, but the thought has fed Christian prayer ever since.

“The spirit of the Lord shall rest on him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord.” (11:2)

In the Gospels the Spirit descends and rests on Jesus at his baptism (Luke 3:22), and he applies Isaiah's promise of the anointed one to himself in Nazareth (Luke 4:18, from Isaiah 61).

“but with righteousness he shall judge the poor, and decide with equity for the meek of the earth;” (11:4)

The first act of this king is to do justice for those who have no one else. “The rod of his mouth” in the same verse means that he rules by his word. He needs no army.

“The wolf shall live with the lamb, the leopard shall lie down with the kid,” (11:6)

Eden's peace comes back, and the enmity between serpent and human is also healed (v. 8). See the Questions below.

“and a little child shall lead them.” (11:6)

Isaiah probably means only that the world will be so safe that a child can herd these animals. Christians, reading it five days before Yaldo, can hardly fail to think of another Child.

“for the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea.” (11:9)

This is the reason why the animals are at peace. Harm ends when people truly know God. Habakkuk 2:14 repeats this promise.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses to describe the Spirit who rests on the Messiah (CCC 712; see also 1286) and lists the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety and fear of the Lord (CCC 1831). What was promised to the shoot of Jesse is shared with his members in Baptism and in Holy Mooron, the chrism of our Syriac tradition. The last book of the Bible gives the risen Lord this title: “I am the root and the descendant of David” (Revelation 22:16). He is the shoot from David's line, and he is also the root from which David came.

Hebrews 7:14–17 · A priest by the power of an indestructible life

Setting.

The lectionary reads Hebrews with the Pauline epistles, as the Church has long received it among the letters linked with St Paul, though the letter does not name its author. Chapter 7 builds an argument on one verse, Psalm 110:4, and on the mysterious figure of Melchizedek, who brought out bread and wine to Abraham (Genesis 14:18).

Under the Law, priesthood came by descent from Levi and Aaron. So there is a problem, which the writer states openly: Jesus was of the wrong tribe. Today's genealogies prove that Jesus is a son of Judah and David; they cannot make him a priest.

Structure.

  • v. 14: the fact: our Lord came from Judah, not Levi.
  • v. 15: therefore a different kind of priest has arisen.
  • v. 16: the basis of his priesthood: not descent, but life.
  • v. 17: the proof from Scripture (Psalm 110:4).

Key words.

  • anatetalken, was descended (literally “has sprung up”): The verb anatellō is used of a shoot sprouting or of the sun rising. The Greek Old Testament uses the related noun anatolē for the “Branch” of Jeremiah 23:5, one of today's readings. So the writer's word for Jesus' descent may carry a hint of that promise.
  • taxis, order: Not a religious order as we use the word, but a rank or kind. Jesus' priesthood is of Melchizedek's kind, without a family list.
  • akatalytos, indestructible: “That cannot be dissolved”. Levitical priests died and had to be replaced (7:23). Jesus' priesthood rests on a life that death has not been able to break.

Verse by verse.

“For it is evident that our Lord was descended from Judah,” (7:14)

This is one of the few places outside the Gospels where the New Testament refers to Jesus' earthly family line. Everybody, it seems, knew it.

“one who has become a priest, not through a legal requirement concerning physical descent, but through the power of an indestructible life.” (7:16)

The Greek speaks of a “fleshly” requirement, contrasted with “life”. Here is the key to the two kinds of lists. A family tree can pass on a name and a throne. It cannot pass on what Jesus offers. His priesthood rests on who he is, risen and alive for ever.

““You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.”” (7:17)

The word “forever” is what the writer wants us to hear. Only someone who lives forever can be a priest forever.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christian tradition sees Melchizedek as a figure of the priesthood of Christ, the unique “high priest after the order of Melchizedek”, and that all the priesthood of the Old Covenant finds its fulfilment in him (CCC 1544). His sacrifice is made present in the Eucharist, and his priesthood is shared by those who serve at the altar (CCC 1545). St Cyprian already saw Melchizedek's bread and wine as a figure of the Lord's sacrifice (Letter 63, in substance). Whenever a priest of the Malankara Church stands at the altar, he acts in the priesthood of the Son of Judah.

Luke 2:1–5 · Joseph and Mary go up to Bethlehem

Setting.

Luke sets the birth of Jesus on the stage of world history. Augustus ruled from 27 BC to AD 14 and was praised for bringing peace to the Mediterranean world. An inscription from Priene in Asia Minor (9 BC) calls his birthday the beginning of good news (euangelia) for the world, and honours him as a saviour. A few verses later the angel will use the same language for a baby in a manger: “good news”, “a Savior” (2:10–11).

Luke draws a sharp contrast: the man in Rome issues a decree, and the true King travels as one of those who must obey it. Nazareth and Bethlehem are several days' walk apart, and the road to the hill country goes up.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the imperial decree.
  • v. 3: everyone goes to be registered.
  • vv. 4–5: Joseph and Mary, from Nazareth to the city of David.

Key words.

  • apographesthai, to be registered: To be entered on a list, as for tax. On the Sunday of family lists, here is yet another list. The Son of God allows his name to be written down on Caesar's roll.
  • oikos kai patria, house and family: Joseph's descent is what sends him to Bethlehem. The angel has already called Jesus heir to “the throne of his ancestor David” (1:32), and that claim comes through Joseph, the legal father.
  • emnēsteumenē, engaged: The NRSV “engaged” follows the Greek. Some ancient versions and many later manuscripts read “wife”, in one form or another, and the Malayalam POC gives “ഭാര്യ”. Matthew tells us that Joseph had by then taken her into his home (Matthew 1:24).
  • enkyos, expecting a child: The word occurs only here in the New Testament. Luke's language is plain and his tone quiet, even at this great moment.

Verse by verse.

“In those days a decree went out from Emperor Augustus that all the world should be registered.” (2:1)

“All the world” is the Roman way of speaking of the empire. The most powerful man alive signs a paper, and without knowing it he moves one family to the place of the prophecy. God's plan makes use of even Caesar's paperwork.

“This was the first registration and was taken while Quirinius was governor of Syria.” (2:2)

On the historical question see the Questions below.

“Joseph also went from the town of Nazareth in Galilee to Judea, to the city of David called Bethlehem, because he was descended from the house and family of David.” (2:4)

In the Old Testament “the city of David” normally means Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:7). Luke gives the title to the humble village where David was a shepherd. Here he passes over the royal capital. Micah had said: “But you, O Bethlehem of Ephrathah, who are one of the little clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to rule in Israel” (Micah 5:2).

“He went to be registered with Mary, to whom he was engaged and who was expecting a child.” (2:5)

No word of complaint is recorded. Joseph and Mary obey an unwelcome order, and by doing so they obey God without seeing how. This verse is a picture of ordinary faith in hard times.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus was born at Bethlehem at the time of King Herod the Great and the emperor Caesar Augustus (CCC 423), which keeps the faith rooted in real history, and that he was born in humble surroundings, into a poor family (CCC 525). St Gregory the Great, preaching at Christmas, asks why the world was being registered when the Lord was about to be born, and answers: because he was appearing in the flesh who would register his chosen ones in eternity (in substance). He also notes the name Bethlehem, “house of bread”: the Bread of heaven was born there (in substance). The Hebrew is bêt leḥem. The bread of Bethlehem comes to us on the altar in every Holy Qurbono.

Questions people ask

Why do Matthew and Luke give different genealogies? And why trace Jesus through Joseph if Joseph was not his natural father?

Matthew (1:1–17) goes down from Abraham through Solomon; Luke (3:23–38) goes up from Jesus through David's son Nathan all the way to Adam, and they name different fathers for Joseph (Jacob in Matthew 1:16; Heli in Luke 3:23). Early Christians already noticed this. Julius Africanus, for example, proposed an explanation from the Jewish law of levirate marriage. No solution is certain, and it is best to admit this. What matters is that each list has its own purpose: Matthew arranges his in three groups of fourteen, the numerical value of David's name in Hebrew, to show a Son of David; Luke shows a Son of Adam for all humanity. Both evangelists are careful about Joseph. Matthew breaks his own pattern at 1:16, “Mary, of whom Jesus was born”, and Luke writes “as was thought” (3:23). By naming and accepting the child, Joseph, “son of David” (Matthew 1:20), gives him a legal place in David's line (see CCC 437).

Is Luke wrong about the census of Quirinius?

This is a real historical difficulty. Luke himself places the birth of Jesus in the days of Herod the Great (1:5), who died in 4 BC, whereas Josephus records a census under Quirinius as governor of Syria around AD 6. Proposals have been made: an earlier enrolment, an earlier post held by Quirinius, or a reading of the Greek as “before” Quirinius was governor. None has convinced everyone, and evidence is lacking. A Catholic reader need not hide the problem or fear it. Luke's teaching is clear: the birth of Jesus took place under Roman rule, in real time, and God was at work through it.

Will the wolf really live with the lamb?

Isaiah is writing poetry, and Christians have read these verses in more than one way. Some, like St Irenaeus, expected a renewed creation in a literal sense; others have seen in the animals a picture of hostile peoples reconciled in the Church. Both have something true. Already where Christ reigns, enemies sit at one table. And the Church also hopes for a new heaven and a new earth, in which creation itself shares in redemption (Romans 8:21; CCC 1042–1047).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Yaldo is the Syriac word for birth. On this Sixth Sunday of Suboro, the last before Yaldo, the Malankara Church completes the journey of its Annunciation Sundays: from the message to Zechariah to the road to Bethlehem. The Twenty-Five Days' Fast is near its end, and Yaldo falls this year on Friday 25 December. A good way to keep these last days is to read aloud at home the names of Matthew 1, to pray for our own families and ancestors, some of whom were saints and some not, and to prepare for confession. The God who wrote Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and the wife of Uriah into his Son's family tree (Matthew 1:3, 5, 6) can write our family into his plan as well.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah sees life in a stump that looks dead. Where in my family or parish do I need to look for a green shoot?
  • The list of the Spirit's gifts in Isaiah 11:2 is given to us in Baptism. Which of them do I most need to ask for this week?
  • Joseph and Mary obeyed an unwelcome government order, and God used it. What inconvenient duty in my life may be part of God's plan?
  • Which names in my family tree would I like to give thanks for, and which would I like to pray for, before Yaldo?
