Word for the day

“And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Matthew 28:20

Reflection

The risen Lord stands among his frightened disciples: “Peace be with you… Why are you frightened, and why do doubts arise in your hearts? Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself.” He eats before them. Then “he opened their minds to understand the scriptures”: the Messiah had to suffer and rise, and “repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations… You are witnesses of these things.”

In the evening Gospel the risen Christ gives the great commission on a mountain in Galilee: “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything that I have commanded you. And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” The Church exists to evangelise.

Peter discovers in the house of Cornelius that “God shows no partiality”: the Gospel is for every nation. St Paul calls every Christian an ambassador: “if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation… So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us.” This is why the Church honours the laity today: the mission is not only for priests and religious, but for every baptised person in family, work and society.

Job's fortunes were restored when he prayed for his friends; Solomon received wisdom when he asked for it. Let us ask the risen Lord for the wisdom and courage to be his witnesses, knowing that he is with us always.

Homily outline

Theme: “I am with you always.”

The Risen Lord shows the disciples his hands and feet, opens their minds to the Scriptures, and sends them to all nations. The Gospel of this Sunday is the Church's charter of mission.

1. “Look at my hands”: a real, risen Lord

  • The disciples are frightened and think they see a ghost.
  • Jesus shows his wounds: the Risen One is the Crucified One, truly alive in his body.
  • God never forgets us: our names are inscribed on the palms of his hands.
“Can a woman forget her nursing child, or show no compassion for the child of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands.” Isaiah 49:15–16
“He said to them, “Why are you frightened, and why do doubts arise in your hearts? Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself.”” Luke 24:38–39

Apply: In moments of fear this week, look at a crucifix and remember: he has not forgotten you.

2. Forgiveness for all nations

  • God revealed himself to Moses as merciful and gracious.
  • Repentance and forgiveness are to be proclaimed in Christ's name to all nations.
  • Peter learns that God shows no partiality: every nation is welcome.
“The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness.” Exodus 34:6
“Then Peter began to speak to them: “I truly understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him.”” Acts 10:34–35

Apply: Reject every prejudice against people of other castes, languages or religions.

3. Go and make disciples

  • God made his Servant a covenant for the people.
  • All authority is given to Jesus; he sends us to baptise and teach.
  • He does not send us alone: “I am with you always, to the end of the age.”

“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything that I have commanded you. And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Matthew 28:19–20

Apply: Support the missions with prayer and an offering; teach the faith to the children in your family.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the disciples are gathered, frightened and confused, when the risen Jesus stands among them: “Peace be with you. Why are you frightened? Look at my hands and my feet; it is I myself.” He even eats a piece of fish in front of them. The Resurrection is not a ghost story; the Lord is truly alive, body and soul.

Then he opens their minds to understand the Scriptures, and gives them a task: repentance and forgiveness of sins must be proclaimed in his name to all nations. “You are witnesses of these things.” In Matthew he says it on a mountain: “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations… And remember, I am with you always.”

This command is not only for priests and sisters. St Paul says every Christian is “an ambassador for Christ”. Today our Church honours the laity, because most of the world will never hear a sermon from a priest; they will only see Christ in you: in your honesty at work, your patience at home, your kindness to the poor, your courage to say “I am a Christian.”

Job was restored when he prayed for his friends. Solomon received wisdom when he asked. Let us ask the risen Lord for wisdom and courage, and go out from this Qurbono as his witnesses. He has promised: “I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Amen.

For children

Children, before Jesus went back to heaven, he gave his friends a big job: “Go and tell everyone about me!”

And he made a promise: “I will be with you always.” Jesus is with us every day, at school, at home, everywhere.

You can tell others about Jesus by being kind and honest. Let's say: “Jesus, I will be your helper!”

Prayer

Risen Lord Jesus, you opened the minds of your disciples and sent them to all nations. Open our minds to the Scriptures, make us your ambassadors in our families and work, and keep us faithful to your promise: I am with you always. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Fourth Sunday after Qyomtho every reading looks outward, from the empty tomb to the ends of the earth. The evening Gospel (Matthew 28:16–20) gives the great commission on the mountain in Galilee: “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations.” Exodus 34:10–17 shows God renewing the covenant after the golden calf. Job 42:7–17 tells how the Lord restored Job when he prayed for the friends who had wronged him. In 1 Kings 3:4–15 the young Solomon asks for a listening heart and receives wisdom. In Acts 10:34–43 Peter learns in the house of Cornelius that “God shows no partiality”, and he says of the risen Lord, “We are witnesses.”

We study three readings more closely. Isaiah 49 promises a time of favour, when God's servant becomes a covenant for the people and the scattered come home from every direction. Paul tells the Corinthians that in Christ there is a new creation, and that God now makes his appeal through his ambassadors. In Luke the risen Lord shows his hands and feet, eats before his disciples, opens the Scriptures to them, and sends them to all nations. The key words are favour, reconciliation and witness.

Isaiah 49:8–13 · In a time of favour I have answered you

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah are usually dated by scholars to the last years of the Babylonian exile, around 550–539 BC, when Cyrus of Persia was rising and a return home began to seem possible. They are often called Second Isaiah. The book itself opens this part with the word “Comfort, O comfort my people” (40:1), and today's reading closes on the same note.

Just before our passage stands the second of the four Servant Songs (49:1–6). There the Lord says to his servant: “I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth” (49:6). Verses 8–13 answer that song. The servant will lead a new exodus, and the road home will be easy because God himself prepares it.

Structure.

  • v. 8: God answers and helps his servant and gives him as a covenant to the people.
  • v. 9a: the servant's word to the prisoners: come out into the light.
  • vv. 9b–11: the journey home: pasture, water, mountains turned into roads.
  • v. 12: the exiles return from every direction.
  • v. 13: heaven, earth and mountains are called to sing.

Key words.

  • ‘ēt rāṣôn, time of favour: A time when God is pleased to act. Paul quotes this verse in the Greek, and adds his own comment: “See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” (2 Corinthians 6:2). The time of favour is not only a date in the past. It is today.
  • bᵉrît ‘ām, covenant to the people: A bold phrase. The servant is not only the messenger of the covenant; he himself becomes the covenant, the bond between God and the people. The same words are used of the servant in 42:6. Christians hear in them the one whose blood is “the new covenant” (Luke 22:20).
  • niḥam, has comforted: The verb of 40:1, “Comfort, O comfort my people”. In v. 13 the comfort is no longer only promised. It has been given, and creation is invited to sing because of it.

Verse by verse.

“In a time of favor I have answered you, on a day of salvation I have helped you;” (49:8)

The verbs speak as if it were already done. God's promise is so sure that the prophet can describe it in the past tense.

“saying to the prisoners, “Come out,” to those who are in darkness, “Show yourselves.”” (49:9)

The exiles were not in chains, but they were captives of a foreign empire and of their own despair. The command is to come out into the light. In Luke the risen Lord opens minds that had been closed by grief and fear (Luke 24:45).

“Lo, these shall come from far away, and lo, these from the north and from the west, and these from the land of Syene.” (49:12)

Syene is Aswan in the far south of Egypt. Here the NRSV follows the great Isaiah scroll found at Qumran; the traditional Hebrew text has “Sinim”, a place no one has identified with certainty. Either way, the scattered come from every side.

“For the Lord has comforted his people, and will have compassion on his suffering ones.” (49:13)

The word for compassion is related to the Hebrew for a mother's womb. The next verses make the picture plain: “Can a woman forget her nursing child?” (49:15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the Servant Songs in the light of Christ. Simeon sees in the child Jesus “a light for revelation to the Gentiles” (Luke 2:32), and Paul and Barnabas take up Isaiah 49:6 as a command to themselves (Acts 13:47). Today's verses show the servant's work as a homecoming: prisoners brought out, the hungry fed, every road opened. That is the work the risen Lord gives to his witnesses in the Gospel, “to all nations”. The Catechism says that the Church's mission comes from the love of the Father, who wills that all be saved (CCC 849–851).

2 Corinthians 5:17–21 · A new creation; ambassadors for Christ

Setting.

Paul wrote 2 Corinthians from Macedonia, probably around AD 55–57, after a painful break with the church he had founded in Corinth. Some there doubted him and preferred more impressive preachers. In chapters 2–7 he defends his ministry, and he does it by speaking of what God has done in Christ.

Just before today's verses Paul says that “the love of Christ urges us on”, because “one has died for all” (5:14). Since Christ died and was raised for all, no one can be seen any longer by worldly standards (5:16). Today's reading draws the conclusion.

Structure.

  • v. 17: in Christ, a new creation.
  • vv. 18–19: all is from God, who reconciles the world to himself and gives the ministry of reconciliation.
  • v. 20: we are ambassadors; God makes his appeal through us.
  • v. 21: the exchange: the sinless one made sin, so that we might become God's righteousness.
  • ¶ The appeal goes on at once in 6:1–2, where Paul quotes Isaiah 49:8, today's Old Testament reading.

Key words.

  • kainē ktisis, new creation: The Greek has no verb: “if anyone in Christ: new creation!” The phrase looks back to the first creation and forward to the promise of “new heavens and a new earth” (Isaiah 65:17). The new creation began in the risen body of Christ, and it reaches the believer through Baptism.
  • katallagē, reconciliation: The word for making peace between two parties who had been estranged. Paul is careful: God is never the one who needs to be won over. It is God who reconciles us to himself, and the whole initiative is his. In Christ's sacrifice it is God himself who takes the initiative.
  • presbeuomen, we are ambassadors: An ambassador speaks with the authority of the one who sends him, not his own. Paul's ministry is not his own message. It is God's appeal made through a human voice.

Verse by verse.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (5:17)

The word “see” calls us to look. In Revelation the one seated on the throne says, “See, I am making all things new” (Revelation 21:5).

“All this is from God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ, and has given us the ministry of reconciliation;” (5:18)

Two gifts in one sentence: the reconciliation itself, and the service of bringing it to others. The Church has the second only because she has first received the first.

“So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us; we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” (5:20)

Paul writes this to people who are already baptized. Reconciliation is not a single moment. The baptized also need to hear again, “be reconciled to God”.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (5:21)

One of the boldest sentences Paul wrote. Christ did not become a sinner. He took on himself the whole weight and result of our sin. Some Fathers, among them St Augustine, read “sin” here as “sacrifice for sin”, since the Old Testament could call a sin offering by the word for sin (in substance).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes 2 Corinthians 5:21 when it teaches that Christ died for our sins: Jesus was never a sinner himself, but in his redeeming love he took us upon himself in the state of our sin (CCC 602–603). It also takes the name of the sacrament of Reconciliation from verse 20, “Be reconciled to God” (CCC 1424), and teaches that Christ entrusted this ministry of reconciliation to his apostles and their successors (CCC 1442). Every Christian shares in the appeal. A baptized person who makes peace, forgives, and speaks of Christ is already an ambassador.

Luke 24:36–49 · You are witnesses of these things

Setting.

This is the last scene of Luke's Gospel before the Ascension. On Easter evening the two disciples have come back from Emmaus to tell how the Lord “had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread” (24:35). The eleven and their companions have their own news: “The Lord has risen indeed, and he has appeared to Simon!” (24:34). Our reading begins while they are still talking.

Luke wrote for readers who had never seen Jesus. He takes care to show that the risen Lord is no ghost and no mere memory, and that the mission of the Church flows from his own word. The same author continues the story in Acts, where the promise of verse 49 is fulfilled at Pentecost.

Structure.

  • vv. 36–37: Jesus stands among them; their terror.
  • vv. 38–40: hands and feet: “it is I myself”.
  • vv. 41–43: he eats before them.
  • vv. 44–47: he opens their minds to the Scriptures: suffering, rising, preaching to all nations.
  • vv. 48–49: “You are witnesses”; the promise of the Father; wait in the city.

Key words.

  • pneuma, ghost, spirit: The disciples think they are seeing a disembodied spirit. Jesus answers that “a ghost does not have flesh and bones”. The risen Lord is not less than a human body, but more.
  • martyres, witnesses: A witness speaks of what he has seen and heard. The word will come to mean those who give their lives for that testimony. Peter uses it in today's reading from Acts: “We are witnesses to all that he did” (Acts 10:39).
  • dynamis ex hypsous, power from on high: The Holy Spirit, “what my Father promised”. Luke opens Acts with the same promise (Acts 1:4–8). The witnesses are not to go out in their own strength.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus himself stood among them and said to them, “Peace be with you.”” (24:36)

The greeting is the ordinary Hebrew shalom, but on Easter evening it is a gift. Those who had abandoned him are greeted first with peace, not with reproach. A few ancient manuscripts leave out the greeting, but most have it, and John records the same words (John 20:19).

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself.” (24:39)

The wounds are the proof of identity. The one who stands before them is the same one who was crucified, not another.

“Have you anything here to eat?” (24:41)

A homely question. Their joy is so great that they cannot believe it, and he helps their faith with the plainest sign of a living body. Peter will later say they “ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead” (Acts 10:41).

“Then he opened their minds to understand the scriptures,” (24:45)

The same Lord who opened the Scriptures on the road to Emmaus (24:27, 32) now opens the minds of the whole group. Understanding the Scriptures is his gift, given in his presence.

“and that repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem.” (24:47)

The plan of Acts in one line: from Jerusalem to Judea, Samaria and the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). Isaiah's servant was to be a light to the nations; the Lord now sends his disciples to carry that light.

“You are witnesses of these things.” (24:48)

Not only of the empty tomb, but of all of it: the suffering, the rising, the Scriptures fulfilled, the forgiveness offered.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this passage to teach that the Resurrection was a real event and the risen body a real body: Jesus invites the disciples to see that he is not a ghost, and that his body is the same that was crucified, yet now with new properties of a glorious body (CCC 645). It also notes that the disciples were not credulous; their faith was born of an encounter they found hard to believe (CCC 643–644). Verse 47 is the root of the Church's preaching of repentance and forgiveness, and verse 49 of her waiting for the Spirit.

Questions people ask

Why did the risen Jesus keep his wounds?

The Gospels show that the wounds prove he is the same Jesus who was crucified. Christian tradition has also seen in them the signs of his love and of his victory over death. The Resurrection did not erase the Cross. It transformed it.

Does “all nations” mean the Gospel is against the old covenant with Israel?

No. Jesus says the preaching begins “from Jerusalem”, and he explains it from “the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms”. The mission to the nations fulfils God's promises to Israel, as Isaiah 49 shows. The Church confesses that God's gifts and call to the Jewish people are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; CCC 839).

Is every Christian really an “ambassador”?

Yes, in his or her own way. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the laity share in the mission of the whole Church through Baptism and Confirmation, and carry Christ into family, work and public life (Lumen Gentium 33; CCC 900). Priests have a particular ministry of reconciliation in the sacrament, but the appeal “be reconciled to God” is made through the life of every believer.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Qyomtho is the Syriac word for resurrection, and our Church counts the Sundays of this season from Easter. Today is also the feast of St Mark the Evangelist, whose Gospel ends with the same sending to the whole creation. In the 2025–26 lectionary this Sunday was kept as Syro-Malankara Catholic Laity Day, and the readings still carry that note: the commission of Matthew 28, the witnesses of Luke 24 and the ambassadors of 2 Corinthians 5 are not only for clergy and religious. At the end of every Holy Qurbono the priest blesses the people and sends them out in peace. That blessing is a small Pentecost. We leave the altar as witnesses.

For study and teaching

  • Where in my life does the risen Lord need to show me his hands and his feet, so that my doubts give way to joy?
  • Paul says that God makes his appeal through us. Whom do I know who most needs to hear “be reconciled to God”, and how might I say it?
  • Isaiah calls the servant “a covenant to the people”. How does the Holy Qurbono make that covenant present to us each Sunday?
  • Job was restored when he prayed for the friends who had wronged him. Whom should I pray for this week?
