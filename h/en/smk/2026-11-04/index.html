Word for the day

“Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven;” Luke 6:37

Reflection

The Corinthians were proud of their favourite preachers and of their own gifts. Paul brings them down to earth with one question: “What do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if it were not a gift?” Our talents, our faith, even our good works are gifts. The apostles, by contrast, live as “fools for the sake of Christ”, hungry and homeless, blessing when reviled.

Jesus draws the conclusion for daily life: do not judge, do not condemn, forgive, give. The measure we use for others will be used for us. And he adds the famous picture of the speck and the log: we see the small fault in our brother's eye and do not notice the beam in our own. Humility before God makes us gentle with others.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Corinthians were proud of their favourite preachers and of their own gifts. Paul brings them down to earth with one question: what do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if it were not a gift (1 Corinthians 4:7)? Everything is gift.

Jesus says the same truth another way: do not judge, and you will not be judged; forgive, and you will be forgiven. Why do you see the speck in your neighbour's eye but do not notice the log in your own (Luke 6:37, 41)?

Those who receive everything as gift find it hard to judge others. A practice for today: when you are tempted to criticise someone, first think for a moment about the log in your own eye, then say a short prayer for that person. Amen.

For children

Children, it is easy to find faults in others. Today say only good things about someone!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, everything I have is your gift. Take away my pride, keep me from judging others, and help me to see first the log in my own eye. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about pride and judgment. Paul asks the Corinthians, who were proud of their gifts and of their favourite teachers, “What do you have that you did not receive?” In the Gospel, Jesus warns against judging others while blind to our own faults. Humility before God becomes mercy toward our neighbour.

1 Corinthians 4:6–14 · What do you have that you did not receive?

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, around AD 54–55. Chapters 1–4 deal with the quarrels in the church at Corinth, where groups were forming around different preachers (1:12). Some Corinthians thought of themselves as already wise, rich and spiritually complete. Paul answers with irony, and then with a father's tenderness.

Structure.

  • v. 6: Paul has applied all this to himself and Apollos, so that no one may be “puffed up” for one against another.
  • v. 7: three questions that undo all boasting.
  • vv. 8–10: irony: “Already you have become rich!” set against the apostles' weakness.
  • vv. 11–13: the real life of an apostle: hunger, blows, manual work, blessing in return for insults.
  • v. 14: “not to make you ashamed, but to admonish you as my beloved children”.

Key words.

  • physioō, to be puffed up: A word Paul uses almost only in this letter (4:6, 18, 19; 5:2; 8:1; 13:4). Knowledge “puffs up”, but love builds up (8:1).
  • theatron, spectacle: Paul pictures the apostles as condemned prisoners brought out last in the arena, watched by the world, by angels and by people.
  • peripsēma, dregs: What is scraped off and thrown away. Paul accepts being treated as the world's rubbish for the sake of Christ.

Verse by verse.

“For who sees anything different in you? What do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if it were not a gift?” (4:7)

Every gift, of nature or of grace, is received. Boasting forgets the Giver.

“we have become a spectacle to the world, to angels and to mortals.” (4:9)
“When reviled, we bless; when persecuted, we endure;” (4:12)

Paul lives what Jesus taught: “bless those who curse you” (Luke 6:28). The apostle's authority shows itself in this kind of meekness.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine said that 1 Corinthians 4:7 convinced him that even the beginning of faith is God's gift, and he quoted it again and again against the idea that we can earn grace (in substance; On the Predestination of the Saints). The Catechism teaches that before God we have no strict right to any merit: our merits are themselves God's gifts (CCC 2007–2011).

Luke 6:37–42 · Do not judge

Setting.

These verses belong to Luke's Sermon on the Plain (6:20–49), his shorter parallel to the Sermon on the Mount. Just before, Jesus has said: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (6:36). Today's words show what that mercy means between people.

Structure.

  • vv. 37–38: four commands with four promises: do not judge, do not condemn, forgive, give; the good measure.
  • vv. 39–40: the blind guide; the disciple and the teacher.
  • vv. 41–42: the speck and the log.

Key words.

  • krinō / katadikazō, judge / condemn: Jesus does not forbid all moral discernment (compare 6:43–45 on knowing a tree by its fruit). He forbids passing sentence on people, which belongs to God.
  • karphos / dokos, speck / log: A speck is a tiny bit of straw or chaff; a dokos is a roof beam. The comic exaggeration is meant to make us laugh, and then to see ourselves.
  • kolpos, lap: The fold of a long garment, pulled up and used as a pocket for grain. The measure of wheat is pressed down, shaken and overflowing: the generosity of God.

Verse by verse.

“Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven;” (6:37)

The passive verbs (“will be judged”, “will be forgiven”) are a reverent way of speaking of what God will do.

“for the measure you give will be the measure you get back.” (6:38)
“Why do you see the speck in your neighbor’s eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?” (6:41)

The one who judges harshly is often blind to his own faults. Correction of a brother is still possible, but only after we have dealt with ourselves (v. 42).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that although we can judge that an act is in itself a grave offence, we must entrust the judgment of persons to the justice and mercy of God (CCC 1861). It also warns against rash judgment and asks us to interpret our neighbour's words and deeds in a favourable way as far as possible (CCC 2477–2478).

Questions people ask

Does “Do not judge” mean we may never call anything wrong?

No. Jesus himself calls sin by its name, and the Church must teach right and wrong. What is forbidden is condemning persons, presuming to know their hearts, and correcting others while ignoring our own sins (CCC 1861, 2478).

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks, “What do you have that you did not receive?” Name three gifts I received and have sometimes treated as my own achievement.
  • Whom do I find it easiest to judge? What “log” in my own eye might the Lord be showing me?
