Word for the day

““What do you think of the Messiah? Whose son is he?” They said to him, “The son of David.”” Matthew 22:42

Reflection

Three days after Christmas the Church remembers David, the shepherd boy of Bethlehem who became king, poet and prophet. God promised him a house and a throne forever. Jesus, born in David's town, asks the Pharisees: “What do you think of the Messiah? Whose son is he?” They answer, “The son of David.” Then he quotes Psalm 110, where David calls the Messiah “my Lord”. How can he be both David's son and David's Lord? Only if he is the eternal Son of God who became David's descendant. The mystery of Christmas is hidden in that question.

Hebrews lists the heroes of faith, among them David, “who through faith conquered kingdoms, administered justice, obtained promises, shut the mouths of lions.” Others suffered mockery, chains and death. “Yet all these, though they were commended for their faith, did not receive what was promised”: they were waiting for Christ, and for us. The child of Bethlehem fulfils all their hopes.

Full homily

Dear friends, today our Church remembers David, king and prophet. The Letter to the Hebrews lists him among the heroes of faith: Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David and Samuel and the prophets, who through faith conquered kingdoms, administered justice, obtained promises (Hebrews 11:32–33).

Hebrews also remembers those who suffered: they were stoned, sawn in two, killed by the sword; they went about in skins of sheep and goats, destitute, persecuted, tormented, of whom the world was not worthy (Hebrews 11:37–38). Faith does not always win in this world; but it always wins with God.

David was a great sinner and a great penitent. He committed adultery and murder, yet when the prophet Nathan confronted him, he repented with all his heart. His psalm of repentance, Psalm 51, is prayed by the Church every day. God promised that the Messiah would come from his line.

In the Gospel Jesus asks the Pharisees: what do you think of the Messiah? Whose son is he? They answer: the son of David. Jesus asks: how is it then that David by the Spirit calls him Lord? (Matthew 22:42–43). The Messiah is David's son, but also David's Lord: truly man and truly God.

The Child of Yaldo is born in the city of David as son of David, yet he is David's Lord and ours. In him the promises to David are fulfilled.

Let us learn from David both his faith and his repentance. When we fall, let us not hide, but return to God with a contrite heart: have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love (Psalm 51:1). Amen.

For children

Children, David was a shepherd boy from Bethlehem who became a king. Jesus, born in Bethlehem too, is our King and Shepherd!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Son of David and David's Lord, you fulfil every promise made to the fathers. Give us David's faith and his love for prayer, and keep us faithful until we receive all you have promised. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The day's title names David, King and Prophet, and both readings speak of him. Hebrews places David in the long line of those who lived by faith and did not yet receive what was promised. In Matthew, Jesus asks how the Messiah can be both David's son and David's Lord. Three days after Yaldo, the question points straight to the Child of Bethlehem, born in David's town.

Hebrews 11:32–40 · Through faith they conquered kingdoms

Setting.

Hebrews 11 is the great roll of faith, from Abel onward. The preacher has reached the judges and kings, and sees that he cannot go through them one by one: “time would fail me”. So he lists a few names and then a string of deeds, and those who know the Scriptures can fill in the stories. The chapter prepares for the climax in 12:1–2, where these witnesses surround us and Jesus is “the pioneer and perfecter of our faith”.

Structure.

  • v. 32: the names: Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, the prophets.
  • vv. 33–35a: faith that won visible victories.
  • vv. 35b–38: faith that seemed to lose: torture, chains, death, homelessness.
  • vv. 39–40: none of them received the promise yet; God kept something better.

Key words.

  • martyrēthentes, commended: Literally “having been witnessed to”. God himself bears witness to them. The same family of words gives us “martyr” and “witnesses” in 12:1.
  • kreitton, something better: One of the favourite words of Hebrews: a better hope, a better covenant, better promises (7:19, 22; 8:6). What is better is what God has given in Christ.
  • teleiōthōsin, be made perfect: To be brought to the goal. The saints of Israel reach it together with us, through Christ, and not apart from us.

Verse by verse.

“And what more should I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets” (11:32)

The list includes people with grave faults, and Scripture does not hide David's sin (2 Samuel 11–12). The faith that the list honours is trust in God, kept or found again.

“who through faith conquered kingdoms, administered justice, obtained promises, shut the mouths of lions,” (11:33)

David fits every phrase: he subdued kingdoms (2 Samuel 8), he did justice for his people (2 Samuel 8:15), and he received the promise of a lasting house (2 Samuel 7). The lions recall Daniel in the den (Daniel 6:22), and also David and Samson.

“Others were tortured, refusing to accept release, in order to obtain a better resurrection.” (11:35)

Probably the martyrs of the Maccabean persecution, Eleazar and the seven brothers (2 Maccabees 6–7). “Sawn in two” in v. 37 recalls a Jewish tradition about the death of Isaiah. A note on the text: in v. 37 some manuscripts have another word in the list, “they were tested”, which the POC includes in its own wording and the NRSV omits.

“Yet all these, though they were commended for their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better so that they would not, apart from us, be made perfect.” (11:39–40)

The Church before Christ and the Church after him are one people of faith, moving toward one goal.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the Old Testament is rich in witnesses to faith, and that the “something better” God foresaw is the grace of believing in his Son (CCC 147). It tells us to look to this cloud of witnesses (CCC 165), and it calls David the king “after God's own heart”, the shepherd who prays for his people and in their name (CCC 2579).

Matthew 22:41–46 · David's son and David's Lord

Setting.

We are in Jerusalem, in the last days before the Passion. Jesus has been questioned in the Temple about the tax to Caesar, the resurrection and the greatest commandment (22:15–40). Now he asks the question himself. Many in Israel hoped for a king from David's line, on the strength of God's promise in 2 Samuel 7:12–16 and of texts like Isaiah 11:1, and the Pharisees give the expected answer. Jesus does not deny it. He shows that it is not the whole answer.

Structure.

  • vv. 41–42: Jesus' question, and the answer: “The son of David.”
  • vv. 43–45: Psalm 110:1, and the riddle it raises.
  • v. 46: silence, and the end of the questions.

Key words.

  • ne’um YHWH la’dōnî, the Lord said to my Lord: In Hebrew the two words differ: the first is the Name of God, the second means “my lord”, a title for a king or master. The Greek Bible has kyrios for both, and so the Christian ear hears the Father speaking to the Son.
  • en pneumati, by the Spirit: Jesus treats the psalm as a word given by the Holy Spirit. Acts calls David a prophet for the same reason (Acts 2:30), which fits today's title.
  • Christos, Messiah: The anointed one. Kings of Israel were anointed; David was anointed by Samuel at Bethlehem (1 Samuel 16:13).

Verse by verse.

“What do you think of the Messiah? Whose son is he?” (22:42)

This is the question of the whole Gospel, and it stands in front of each reader.

“The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet”” (22:44)

Psalm 110 is the Old Testament text most often quoted or echoed in the New. In a culture where a father does not call his son “lord”, the question has real force.

“If David thus calls him Lord, how can he be his son?” (22:45)

The answer is not given in words. The reader of Matthew already knows it: he is “the son of David” (1:1) and also “God is with us” (1:23). He is David's son by birth and David's Lord as Son of God. Compare Romans 1:3–4.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus ascribes the title “Lord” to himself in a veiled way when he disputes with the Pharisees about the meaning of Psalm 110 (CCC 447). It also teaches that Christ's sitting at the right hand of the Father is the start of the Messiah's kingdom (CCC 663–664). The Fathers, and the Council of Chalcedon after them, taught the mystery this text points to: one person, true God and true man.

Questions people ask

Did David really write Psalm 110?

The heading says “Of David”, and in Jesus' day this was the common understanding. Jesus' argument starts from that shared ground. Many scholars today think of the Psalter as growing over time around David's name. Whatever the history of the text, the Church reads Psalm 110 as inspired and fulfilled in Christ, which is the point Jesus is making.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

David's voice is never far away in the Malankara Church, since the Psalms run through its daily prayer and its liturgy. To remember him in this week of Yaldo is to remember the promise that brought the Child to Bethlehem.

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews 11 honours people who “did not receive what was promised”. What am I still waiting for from God, and how can I keep faith while I wait?
  • Jesus asks, “What do you think of the Messiah?” How would I answer him today, in my own words?
