Word for the day

“Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.” Mark 9:35

Reflection

In the Gospel the crowd is hungry and the day is ending. The disciples want to send them away. Jesus says: “You give them something to eat.” They have only five loaves and two fish. Jesus “taking the five loaves and the two fish, looked up to heaven, and blessed and broke them, and gave them to the disciples to set before the crowd. And all ate and were filled.” What little we bring, Christ blesses and multiplies; but he gives it through the hands of his disciples. This is the priesthood: hands that receive from Christ and give to the people.

In the evening Gospel the disciples argue about who is the greatest. Jesus sits down, calls the Twelve and says: “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.” Then he takes a little child in his arms: “Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me.” The Church does not need ambitious men but servants.

In Acts the Lord sends Ananias to Saul, the persecutor: “Go, for he is an instrument whom I have chosen to bring my name before Gentiles and kings and before the people of Israel.” Ananias lays hands on him, and Saul is filled with the Holy Spirit. God calls unexpected people, and he uses other people's hands to form them. Seminaries exist for this: so that those whom God chooses may be formed to serve.

Isaiah encourages the weary: “He gives power to the faint… those who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength, they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary.” Priests and seminarians, catechists and parents grow tired. The Spirit renews those who wait on the Lord.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the day is ending, the crowd is hungry, and the disciples want to send them away. Jesus says: “You give them something to eat.” They have only five loaves and two fish. Jesus takes them, looks up to heaven, blesses, breaks, and gives them to the disciples to give to the people. All eat and are satisfied, and twelve baskets are left over.

Notice: Jesus does not drop bread from the sky. He feeds the crowd through the hands of his disciples. That is the mystery of the priesthood. At every Holy Qurbono a man takes bread, and Christ through his hands feeds his people. Today we pray for those hands: our priests, and the young men in our seminaries.

But the disciples also had to learn. In the evening Gospel they argued about who was the greatest. Jesus took a child in his arms and said: “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.” A priest, a parent, a leader is great only by serving.

God chooses surprising people. He chose Saul, the persecutor, and sent Ananias to lay hands on him. He may be choosing a boy or girl in your family. Do not say “not my son, not my daughter.” Offer your five loaves. And to all who are weary in service, Isaiah says: “Those who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles.” Amen.

For children

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you took the five loaves, blessed and broke them, and gave them to your disciples for the crowd. Bless our seminarians and priests; make them servants of all; renew the strength of all who are weary in your service, and send labourers into your harvest. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost, in the Apostles' Fast, the readings speak of what God gives to the weary and how he gives it through those he calls. The evening Gospel (Mark 9:30–37) finds the disciples arguing about who is the greatest, and Jesus answers: “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.” Exodus 23:14–19 commands Israel to keep three pilgrim feasts a year and to bring the first fruits to the house of the Lord. In Zechariah 6:1–8 the prophet sees four chariots going out to patrol the earth. In Hosea 14:5–9 the Lord promises, “I will be like the dew to Israel.” In Acts 9:10–19 the Lord sends Ananias to Saul, “an instrument whom I have chosen”, and Saul is filled with the Holy Spirit and baptized.

We study three readings more closely. In Isaiah, God, who never grows weary, gives strength to those who wait for him. Paul tells the Corinthians that they are “the temple of the living God” and must live as God's own people. In Luke, Jesus feeds five thousand in a deserted place, but he does it through the hands of the Twelve: “You give them something to eat.” The key words are strength, temple and bread.

Isaiah 40:27–31 · Those who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength

Setting.

Chapter 40 opens a new part of the book of Isaiah with the words “Comfort, O comfort my people” (40:1). Many scholars think chapters 40 to 55 come from a prophet of the time of the Babylonian exile, in the sixth century before Christ, who carried on the message of Isaiah of Jerusalem. The people had lost their land, their king and their Temple. Many felt that God had forgotten them.

Chapter 40 answers that fear by pointing to the greatness of the Creator. He measures the waters in the hollow of his hand (40:12); the nations are like a drop from a bucket (40:15); he calls the stars by name (40:26). Our verses draw the conclusion for a tired people: the God who made everything has not grown tired of them.

Structure.

  • v. 27: Israel's complaint: “My way is hidden from the Lord.”
  • v. 28: the answer: the everlasting Creator does not faint or grow weary.
  • vv. 29–30: he gives power to the faint; even the young grow tired.
  • v. 31: those who wait for the Lord: wings like eagles, running, walking.

Key words.

  • qôwê YHWH, those who wait for the Lord: The verb means to wait with hope, to look eagerly towards someone. It is not passive waiting but trust that holds on. The Greek Septuagint renders it “those who wait for God”.
  • yaḥălîpû kōaḥ, shall renew their strength: Literally “shall exchange strength”. The image is of putting on new strength, as one changes a worn-out garment. God does not only add to our strength; he gives his own in exchange for our weakness.
  • mišpāṭ, right, cause: In verse 27 it means one's case or right before a judge. The exiles felt that their cause had been dismissed. The prophet answers that the Lord still holds it.

Verse by verse.

“Why do you say, O Jacob, and speak, O Israel, “My way is hidden from the Lord, and my right is disregarded by my God”?” (40:27)

The prophet does not scold the complaint. He takes it seriously and answers it. Scripture is full of such honest prayers (Psalm 13:1: “How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?”).

“He does not faint or grow weary; his understanding is unsearchable.” (40:28)

Human strength runs out; God's does not. His ways may be hidden from us, but we are never hidden from him.

“He gives power to the faint, and strengthens the powerless.” (40:29)

The gift goes to those who have no strength left. This is the pattern of grace through all Scripture: “power is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9).

“they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary, they shall walk and not faint.” (40:31)

The order is surprising: flying, then running, then walking. The last may be the hardest: the long, ordinary road of each day, walked without giving up.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these verses the virtue of hope, by which we trust in Christ's promises and rely not on our own strength but on the help of the grace of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1817). Hope keeps us from discouragement and sustains us in times of abandonment (CCC 1818). In today's Gospel the crowd that followed Jesus into the deserted place is tired and hungry, and he does not send them away. The God of Isaiah who gives power to the faint has come close in his Son, and he feeds us still in the Holy Qurbono, the bread for the journey.

2 Corinthians 6:14–18 · We are the temple of the living God

Setting.

Paul wrote 2 Corinthians from Macedonia, probably around AD 55–57, to a church he loved but which had hurt him. Just before our passage he pleads: “open wide your hearts also” (6:13), and the same plea returns in 7:2. Our verses stand between them. Many scholars think this short section (6:14 to 7:1) may have been placed here from another letter of Paul, because its style is different, and the name “Beliar” occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Others see it as Paul's own sudden turn. Either way it belongs to the inspired letter as the Church received it.

Corinth was a great port city, full of temples. Christians were few, and much of social life, from trade guilds to family feasts, took place around the worship of idols. Paul had already said that believers do not need to leave the world (1 Corinthians 5:10), and that a Christian married to an unbeliever should not leave the marriage (1 Corinthians 7:12–14). Here he warns against partnerships that would draw believers back into idolatry.

Structure.

  • v. 14a: the command: do not be mismatched with unbelievers.
  • vv. 14b–16a: five questions: righteousness and lawlessness, light and darkness, Christ and Beliar, believer and unbeliever, God's temple and idols.
  • v. 16b: the reason: we are the temple of the living God.
  • vv. 17–18: a chain of Old Testament promises: come out, be separate, and I will be your father.

Key words.

  • heterozygountes, mismatched, unequally yoked: Literally “yoked with a different kind”. It recalls the Law's rule not to plough with an ox and a donkey together (Deuteronomy 22:10). The two pull differently, and the furrow goes crooked.
  • Beliar, Beliar: A form of the Hebrew belîya‘al, “worthlessness”, used in Jewish writings of the time as a name for Satan. Paul sets Christ and Beliar as two masters who cannot share one servant.
  • naos, temple: The inner sanctuary, the dwelling place of God. Paul uses it of the whole community (1 Corinthians 3:16) and of each believer's body (1 Corinthians 6:19).

Verse by verse.

“Do not be mismatched with unbelievers.” (6:14)

Paul is not telling the Corinthians to despise their neighbours. He has just spoken of his own patience and kindness towards all (6:6). He warns against bonds that would make them share in idol worship.

“For we are the temple of the living God;” (6:16)

In Jerusalem, God's presence was in the Temple. Now God dwells in a people. The quotation that follows joins Leviticus 26:11–12 and Ezekiel 37:27: “I will live in them and walk among them.”

“Therefore come out from them, and be separate from them, says the Lord, and touch nothing unclean;” (6:17)

Isaiah 52:11 called the exiles to leave Babylon and come home pure. Paul applies it to the Christian's leaving behind of idolatry: a new exodus.

“and I will be your father, and you shall be my sons and daughters, says the Lord Almighty.” (6:18)

The words come from God's promise to David's son in 2 Samuel 7:14. Paul adds “and daughters” (compare Isaiah 43:6). The promise once made to the king is now made to every believer, man and woman.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Baptism makes the believer a temple of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1265), and the Second Vatican Council speaks of the Church as the temple built of living stones, in which God dwells (Lumen Gentium 6). The Church applies Paul's warning with care. She asks her children not to put their faith at risk, for example in marriage, and so she gives special attention to marriages between a Catholic and a person of another Christian Church or of another religion (CCC 1633–1637). At the same time she rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions and asks us to treat all people with respect (Nostra Aetate 2).

Luke 9:10–17 · You give them something to eat

Setting.

At the start of chapter 9 Jesus sent out the Twelve to proclaim the kingdom and to heal (9:1–6). Herod heard of it and asked, “who is this about whom I hear such things?” (9:9). Our passage begins with the return of the apostles, and soon after it Peter will answer Herod's question: “The Messiah of God” (9:20). The feeding of the five thousand stands between the question and the answer.

This is the only miracle of Jesus told in all four Gospels (Matthew 14:13–21; Mark 6:30–44; John 6:1–15). Luke places it near Bethsaida, a town at the north end of the Sea of Galilee. Readers who knew the Scriptures would remember the manna in the wilderness (Exodus 16) and the prophet Elisha, who fed a hundred men with twenty loaves, and some was left (2 Kings 4:42–44).

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: the apostles return; the crowds follow; Jesus welcomes them, teaches and heals.
  • v. 12: evening; the Twelve want to send the crowd away.
  • vv. 13–15: “You give them something to eat”; five loaves and two fish; the people seated in groups of fifty.
  • vv. 16–17: he blesses, breaks and gives; all are filled; twelve baskets left over.

Key words.

  • apostoloi, apostles: Literally “those who are sent”. Luke uses the title here, on their return from their first mission. The ones who were sent out now learn that they must also feed.
  • eulogēsen, he blessed: The Jewish blessing at meals praises God for the food: “Blessed are you, Lord our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth.” Jesus looks up to heaven and blesses God over the bread.
  • edidou, he gave: The Greek tense suggests action that goes on: he kept on giving. The bread did not run out while it passed from his hands through theirs.

Verse by verse.

“and he welcomed them, and spoke to them about the kingdom of God, and healed those who needed to be cured.” (9:11)

Jesus had gone away to rest with the apostles, yet he welcomes the crowd. His first gift is the word, then healing, then bread.

“But he said to them, “You give them something to eat.”” (9:13)

The “you” is emphatic in Greek. Jesus will do the miracle, but he wants the apostles to see the need as their own responsibility, and to bring what little they have.

“And taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven, and blessed and broke them, and gave them to the disciples to set before the crowd.” (9:16)

Took, blessed, broke, gave: the same four actions return at the Last Supper (22:19, where Luke has “given thanks”) and at Emmaus (24:30). Luke's readers could not hear them without thinking of the Eucharist.

“And all ate and were filled. What was left over was gathered up, twelve baskets of broken pieces.” (9:17)

God's gift is not measured out sparingly. Twelve baskets, one for each apostle, and perhaps a sign of the twelve tribes: there is enough for all Israel and more.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the multiplication of the loaves, when the Lord says the blessing, breaks and distributes the loaves through his disciples to feed the multitude, prefigures the superabundance of the unique bread of his Eucharist (CCC 1335). The Fathers saw in the apostles who carry the bread to the people a picture of the ministry of the Church. In the Holy Qurbono the priest does what the disciples did on the hillside: he receives from Christ's hands the bread that Christ has blessed, and gives it to the people. The miracle also speaks of charity: the Church cannot say to the hungry “send them away”.

Questions people ask

Was the feeding of the five thousand really a miracle, or did the crowd simply share the food they had hidden?

All four Gospels present it as a miracle worked by Jesus, a sign of who he is, and that is how the Church has always read it (CCC 547–549, 1335). The idea that the people merely shared their packed lunches is a modern guess with no support in the text. Sharing is a fine lesson, and it is in the story: the disciples give what they have. But the heart of the story is that Christ multiplies what is placed in his hands.

Does Paul's “do not be mismatched with unbelievers” forbid friendship or marriage with people of another faith?

Paul is warning against bonds that draw a believer into idol worship. He does not forbid ordinary friendship and work with others; he says Christians cannot leave the world (1 Corinthians 5:10). On marriage he says that a believer already married to an unbeliever should stay, because the unbelieving partner is made holy through the believer (1 Corinthians 7:12–14). The Church today allows marriages between a Catholic and a person of another Church or religion with the proper permission or dispensation and pastoral care, so that the faith of the Catholic partner and of the children is protected (CCC 1633–1637).

If God gives strength to those who wait for him, why do good people still grow tired?

Isaiah does not promise that we will never be tired. He says that “even youths will faint and be weary”. The promise is that God gives strength in our weakness, often enough for the next step rather than for the whole road at once. Jesus himself was tired and slept in the boat (Luke 8:23). Rest, prayer and the sacraments are the ways in which God renews our strength.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

This Sunday falls in the Apostles' Fast, which ends with the feast of Sts Peter and Paul on 29 June. The fast keeps the Church with the Shleehe, the apostles whom the Lord sent out to preach and to feed his people. Today's Gospel shows them learning that work: bringing their five loaves to the Lord and carrying his blessed bread to the crowd. In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost unfold the life of the Church that the Spirit has gathered, and this Sunday's readings, with Saul's calling in Acts and the servant-greatness of the evening Gospel, invite us to pray for those whom God calls to serve at his altar, and to give them our support.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah says those who wait for the Lord shall “walk and not faint”. What is the ordinary road I find hardest to keep walking, and how can I wait on the Lord in it?
  • Paul calls the community “the temple of the living God”. What in my home or parish honours that dwelling, and what dishonours it?
  • Jesus said to the apostles, “You give them something to eat.” What are my “five loaves”, and to whom is Christ asking me to give them?
  • How does the pattern “took, blessed, broke, gave” help me to understand what happens in the Holy Qurbono, and what it asks of me afterwards?
