Word for the day

“Just so, I tell you, there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance.” Luke 15:7

Reflection

Jesus tells of a shepherd with a hundred sheep who loses one. He leaves the ninety-nine and goes after the lost one “until he finds it. When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices.” And he calls his friends: “Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep that was lost.” God is not a cold judge counting sins; he is a shepherd who searches and carries. “There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents.”

St Paul tells the Corinthians that the Church is a body: “For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ… If one member suffers, all suffer together with it; if one member is honoured, all rejoice together with it.” The family is the first place where we learn this: every member matters, the weakest most of all.

In the evening Gospel Jesus says hard things about forgiveness: “If the same person sins against you seven times a day, and turns back to you seven times and says, ‘I repent,’ you must forgive.” The apostles cry out: “Increase our faith!” Forgiving again and again needs faith that God is greater than our hurt. And Jesus adds: when you have done everything, say, “We are worthless slaves; we have done only what we ought to have done.” No pride, only humble service.

At the Areopagus Paul tells the philosophers of Athens about the unknown God: “he is not far from each one of us. For ‘In him we live and move and have our being.’” God is near every person, searching for them like the shepherd. Our homes should be places where the lost are welcomed back and where God's nearness is felt.

Homily outline

Theme: “The Shepherd who searches, the body that belongs together.”

Jesus tells of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one lost sheep. Paul reminds us that we are many members of one body. The apostles pray: “Increase our faith.”

1. The Shepherd searches for the lost

  • Through Ezekiel God promised to seek the lost himself.
  • Jesus fulfils it: he goes after the one sheep until he finds it and carries it home on his shoulders.
  • Heaven rejoices more over one sinner who repents; the Sacrament of Reconciliation is that joy today.
“I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak.” Ezekiel 34:16
“Which one of you, having a hundred sheep and losing one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness and go after the one that is lost until he finds it? When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices.” Luke 15:4–5

Apply: Pray for one person who has drifted from the Church, and reach out to them gently.

2. Many members, one body

  • Paul: the body is one though its members are many; no member can say “I have no need of you.”
  • The weaker members are indispensable; when one suffers, all suffer.
  • Family and parish are where this unity is lived first.
“How very good and pleasant it is when kindred live together in unity!” Psalm 133:1
“Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common.” Acts 4:32

Apply: Thank a family member or parishioner whose quiet service is often unnoticed.

3. “Increase our faith”

  • God's plans stand firm; nothing can overturn them.
  • Faith the size of a mustard seed is enough, because its power is God's.
  • Having done all, we say: “We are worthless slaves; we have done only what we ought to have done.” Humility guards faith.
“The LORD of hosts has sworn: As I have designed, so shall it be; and as I have planned, so shall it come to pass.” Isaiah 14:24
“The apostles said to the Lord, “Increase our faith!” The Lord replied, “If you had faith the size of a mustard seed, you could say to this mulberry tree, ‘Be uprooted and planted in the sea,’ and it would obey you.”” Luke 17:5–6

Apply: Each day this week pray the apostles' prayer: “Lord, increase my faith.”

The Shepherd seeks us, the body holds us, and faith keeps us humble and strong.

Let us return to him, belong to one another, and serve without seeking reward.

Take-home resolution: Make a good confession before the feast of St Thomas.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Jesus tells of a shepherd who has a hundred sheep and loses one. Ninety-nine are safe; why bother about one? But he leaves them and searches until he finds it. Then he lays it on his shoulders, joyful, and calls his friends: “Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep that was lost.” That is how God looks at each of us. Not as a number, but as the one he is searching for.

St Paul says the Church is a body. “If one member suffers, all suffer together with it; if one member is honoured, all rejoice.” Our families are the first place we learn this. In a good family no one is left behind, not the sick grandparent, not the child who struggles in school, not the son or daughter who has wandered away.

In the evening Gospel Jesus asks something very hard: forgive your brother seven times in a day if he repents. The apostles cry out: “Increase our faith!” We cannot forgive like that by our own strength. We need faith that God is bigger than our hurt. And when we have done all this, Jesus says, we should say humbly: “We have done only what we ought to have done.”

Paul tells the Athenians: God “is not far from each one of us; in him we live and move and have our being.” Who is the lost sheep in your family, your street, your parish? Call them this week. Pray for them. Light the lamp for them in the evening. And let heaven rejoice. Amen.

For children

Children, a shepherd had a hundred sheep. One got lost! Did he say, “Never mind, I still have ninety-nine”? No! He searched and searched until he found it.

Then he carried it home on his shoulders and had a party! Jesus loves each of us like that.

Is there someone who feels left out in your class or family? How can you help them feel loved?

Prayer

Good Shepherd, you leave the ninety-nine to seek the one who is lost. Seek us when we stray and carry us home on your shoulders. Make our families one body in you, increase our faith to forgive again and again, and let heaven rejoice over every sinner who returns. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sixth Sunday after Pentecost the readings speak of a God who gathers. In the evening Gospel (Luke 17:1–10) Jesus warns against causing others to stumble, commands forgiveness “seven times a day”, and answers the apostles' plea “Increase our faith!” with the mustard seed and the servant who says, “We are worthless slaves; we have done only what we ought to have done!” In Exodus 30:34–38 the Lord gives Moses the recipe for the holy incense, “pure and holy”, to be placed before the covenant in the tent of meeting. Psalm 20 prays for the king: “Some take pride in chariots, and some in horses, but our pride is in the name of the Lord our God.” In Isaiah 14:22–27 the Lord of hosts swears that his plan against Babylon and Assyria shall stand: “His hand is stretched out, and who will turn it back?” In Acts 17:22–34 Paul preaches at the Areopagus in Athens about the unknown God, in whom “we live and move and have our being”.

We study three readings more closely. In 2 Samuel, Abner, the commander of Saul's house and David's old enemy, comes to Hebron to bring all Israel to David; David makes a feast for him and sends him away in peace. Paul tells the Corinthians that they are one body with many members, where the weaker parts are indispensable and every member suffers and rejoices with the others. In Luke, Jesus answers those who grumble because he eats with sinners with the parable of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one lost sheep. The key words are gathering, body and joy.

2 Samuel 3:17–21 · Abner makes peace with David

Setting.

After the death of Saul, David was anointed king over Judah at Hebron (2 Samuel 2:4), while Abner, Saul's army commander, made Saul's son Ishbaal (called Ish-bosheth in many translations) king over the northern tribes. “There was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David” (3:1). Abner was the real power in the north, but after a bitter quarrel with Ishbaal (3:6–11) he decided to change sides and offered David his help “to bring all Israel over to you” (3:12).

Our verses tell how Abner prepares the elders of Israel and the tribe of Benjamin, Saul's own tribe, and then comes to David. The story does not end happily: on his way back Abner is murdered by Joab in revenge for his brother (3:27). David mourns him publicly, and “all Israel understood that day that the king had no part in the killing” (3:37). The unity of the kingdom will be reached only in chapter 5, when all the tribes come to Hebron and anoint David.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–18: Abner tells the elders of Israel to make David king, recalling the Lord's promise.
  • v. 19: he speaks to Benjamin and goes to David at Hebron.
  • v. 20: David makes a feast for Abner and his twenty men.
  • v. 21: Abner promises to rally all Israel to a covenant; David sends him away in peace.
  • ¶ The phrase “in peace” is repeated three times in verses 21–23, as if to underline how fragile that peace was.

Key words.

  • mᵉbaqšîm, seeking: The elders “have been seeking David as king”. The people already sensed where God's choice lay. Abner only brings to the surface a longing that was there.
  • bᵉyad dāwid ‘abdî, through my servant David: Literally “by the hand of David my servant”. No oracle in exactly these words is recorded earlier in Samuel, but the title “my servant David” becomes central in the prophets (Ezekiel 34:23) and points ahead to the Messiah.
  • šālôm, peace: More than the absence of war: wholeness, right relationship. The meal and the peaceful farewell show reconciliation between former enemies, a peace that sin will soon wound.

Verse by verse.

“For some time past you have been seeking David as king over you.” (3:17)

God's purpose ripens slowly. David does not seize the throne of Israel by force; he waits until the people come to him.

“Through my servant David I will save my people Israel from the hand of the Philistines, and from all their enemies.” (3:18)

The deliverer is called a servant. Kingship in Israel is service of God and of his people, a pattern fulfilled in Christ, who came “not to be served but to serve” (Mark 10:45).

“David made a feast for Abner and the men who were with him.” (3:20)

Reconciliation is sealed at a table. A shared meal was a sign of peace and covenant in the ancient world.

“So David dismissed Abner, and he went away in peace.” (3:21)

David receives his former enemy without revenge. The violence that follows comes from Joab, not from the king.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers saw in David the shepherd-king a figure of Christ, the Son of David, who gathers into one the scattered children of God (John 11:52). The Catechism calls David “par excellence the king after God's own heart”, the shepherd who prays for his people (CCC 2579). Today's passage teaches that God's kingdom grows through reconciliation: a former enemy is received, a feast is shared, peace is offered. It also warns, through Joab's crime, how easily the old spirit of revenge can destroy what grace has begun.

1 Corinthians 12:12–26 · Many members, one body

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, probably around AD 53–55, to a gifted but divided community. Some boasted of their spiritual gifts, especially speaking in tongues, and looked down on others (chapters 12–14). Paul answers by recalling that there are varieties of gifts but the same Spirit (12:4), and then uses the image of the body.

The comparison of a city or a people with a body was well known in the ancient world. The Roman historian Livy tells how Menenius Agrippa persuaded the common people of Rome to end a revolt with a fable about the limbs that refused to feed the stomach (Livy 2.32). Paul goes further: the Church is not only like a body; it is the body of Christ (12:27).

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: one body, many members: all baptized in one Spirit.
  • vv. 14–20: no member may say “I do not belong”.
  • vv. 21–24: no member may say “I have no need of you”; the weaker are indispensable.
  • vv. 25–26: one care for all: suffering and rejoicing together.

Key words.

  • sōma, body: The word Paul also uses of the Eucharist: “The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ? Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body” (10:16–17). The body of Christ received at the altar builds the body of Christ which is the Church.
  • ebaptisthēmen… epotisthēmen, we were baptized… we were made to drink: Two images of the one Spirit: plunged into him in Baptism, given him to drink as water. Many interpreters hear in the second an echo of the Spirit as living water (John 7:37–39).
  • sympaschei, suffers together: A single Greek word: to suffer with. Paul's own compassion for all the churches (2 Corinthians 11:28–29) is this word made flesh.

Verse by verse.

“For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ.” (12:12)

Paul does not say “so it is with the Church”, but “so it is with Christ”. Christ and his members form one body, the whole Christ, as St Augustine loved to say.

“For in the one Spirit we were all baptized into one body” (12:13)

The verse goes on: “Jews or Greeks, slaves or free”. Paul names the deepest divisions of his world, of race and of status, and declares them overcome in Baptism. No caste or class divides the body of Christ.

“On the contrary, the members of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable,” (12:22)

The word “seem” matters. The sick, the old, the poor and the little ones only seem weaker. In God's eyes they are necessary to the body.

“If one member suffers, all suffer together with it; if one member is honored, all rejoice together with it.” (12:26)

The test of a living parish: the sorrow of one family is felt by all, and the joy of one is shared by all.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council takes up this chapter to describe the Church as the body of Christ, in which the Spirit gives a diversity of gifts, and from which it follows that if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it (Lumen Gentium 7; CCC 791). Pope Pius XII wrote a whole encyclical on this theme, Mystici Corporis (1943). St Augustine told the newly baptized at Easter that if they are the body of Christ and his members, it is their own mystery that is placed on the Lord's table, and they receive their own mystery (Sermon 272, in substance).

Luke 15:1–7 · The lost sheep

Setting.

Luke 15 is the heart of Luke's Gospel of mercy. It contains three parables of the lost: the sheep, the coin and the two sons. All three answer the same complaint, given in verses 1–2: tax collectors and sinners come to hear Jesus, and some of the Pharisees and scribes grumble that he welcomes them and eats with them.

The Pharisees were devout men who wanted all Israel to live in holiness, and they took great care about table fellowship, because eating together meant belonging together. Luke also tells of Pharisees who invited Jesus to dine (7:36; 14:1). The parable is not an attack on them but an invitation: come and share the shepherd's joy. Matthew gives a similar parable in a different setting, about the care of the little ones in the community (Matthew 18:12–14).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: sinners draw near; the righteous grumble.
  • v. 3: Jesus answers with a parable.
  • v. 4: the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine and seeks the one until he finds it.
  • vv. 5–6: he carries it home on his shoulders and calls his friends to rejoice.
  • v. 7: more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents.

Key words.

  • diegongyzon, were grumbling: The word recalls Israel murmuring against Moses and the Lord in the wilderness (Exodus 16:2–8 in the Greek Old Testament). Grumbling is the sound of a heart that does not understand God's mercy.
  • prosdechetai, welcomes: To receive someone, as a host receives a guest. The complaint is true: Jesus does welcome sinners. What his critics see as a scandal is the Gospel itself.
  • apolōlos, lost: The same word returns at the end of the chapter: the son “was lost and has been found” (15:24, 32). In Luke, Jesus says that “the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost” (19:10).

Verse by verse.

“This fellow welcomes sinners and eats with them.” (15:2)

Meant as an accusation, it is a perfect summary of the ministry of Jesus. It is also a description of the Holy Qurbono, where the Lord still welcomes sinners and feeds them at his table.

“does not leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness and go after the one that is lost until he finds it?” (15:4)

“Until he finds it”: the shepherd does not give up. God's search for us is patient and stubborn.

“When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices.” (15:5)

No scolding, no punishment, only joy. The shepherd carries the weight himself. One of the earliest images of Christ in Christian art, found in the catacombs of Rome, is the young shepherd with a sheep on his shoulders.

“there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance.” (15:7)

The “ninety-nine righteous” are a gentle irony. No one really needs no repentance. The joy of heaven is offered to all who are willing to be found.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to Jesus receiving sinners at his table as a gesture that expresses in an astonishing way both God's forgiveness and the return to the bosom of the People of God (CCC 1443), and it teaches that the Gospel is the revelation in Jesus Christ of God's mercy to sinners, as the parables of Luke 15 show (CCC 1846). St Gregory the Great, preaching on this parable, says that the shepherd laid the sheep on his shoulders because Christ took our human nature and bore our sins (Homilies on the Gospels 34, in substance). The parable stands behind every confessional: the sacrament of Reconciliation is the shepherd carrying the sheep home.

Questions people ask

Is it fair to leave ninety-nine sheep in the wilderness to look for one?

The parable is not a lesson in sheep farming. It shows the heart of God, for whom no one is a mere number. The question in verse 4, “Which one of you…?”, invites the hearers to admit that they too would search for something precious. If we do that for a sheep, how much more does God do it for a person? The ninety-nine are not abandoned; the whole flock is safer for having a shepherd who never gives up on anyone.

Paul says the members are many but the body is one. Does that mean everyone in the Church must be the same?

No. Unity is not sameness. The eye and the hand are different and must remain different, or the body cannot live. The Church holds together many rites, languages and gifts, and this variety is a richness, not a threat, as long as all are joined in the one faith, the sacraments and communion with the successors of the apostles (CCC 814–815).

Abner was a former enemy with mixed motives. Why does David receive him?

David trusts that God's plan for Israel can work even through imperfect people. He does not demand that Abner first prove pure intentions; he offers a feast and peace. Christ goes further still: he receives sinners not because they have earned it but so that they may be changed. Reconciliation always involves the risk of trusting someone who once wronged us.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

We keep this Sunday within the Apostles' Fast, which ends with the feast of Sts Peter and Paul on Tuesday. The readings suit the season: the apostles ask for faith in the evening Gospel, Paul preaches at Athens, and the parable of the lost sheep shows what kind of shepherds the apostles were sent to be. In the West Syriac tradition incense surrounds the prayers of the Holy Qurbono and of the hours, especially the Sedro, the long prayer that is said while the incense rises. Exodus 30 gives the background: the incense is holy, offered before the Lord, a sign of prayer rising to God (Psalm 141:2). When the censer swings in our churches, it reminds us that the whole Church, many members and one body, rises together before God, and that the Shepherd who found us carries us there.

For study and teaching

  • David receives his former enemy with a feast and sends him away in peace. Is there someone with whom I need to share a meal of reconciliation?
  • Paul says no member can say “I have no need of you”. Whom in my parish or family have I treated as unnecessary, and how can I honour them?
  • The shepherd carries the lost sheep home on his shoulders. When did I last let Christ carry me home in the sacrament of Reconciliation?
  • Jesus' critics grumbled because he ate with sinners. When I see someone return to the Church, do I grumble or do I rejoice?
