Word for the day

“Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” John 7:24

Reflection

On this Friday of the Apostles' Fast, the Letter to the Hebrews takes us to the mountain where Abraham's faith was tried to the utmost: “By faith Abraham, when put to the test, offered up Isaac. He who had received the promises was ready to offer up his only son, of whom he had been told, ‘It is through Isaac that descendants shall be named for you.’” Everything God had promised seemed to hang on this one boy, and God asked for him. Abraham did not understand, but he trusted. The letter tells us what held him: “He considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead.” Abraham, whom Jews and Christians alike honour as our father in faith, believed that the God who gives life could give it back. The Fathers of the Church saw in Isaac, climbing the mountain with the wood on his shoulders, a figure of Christ carrying his cross; Origen told his hearers that Isaac bearing the wood for his own sacrifice shows us the Lord who carried the wood of the cross (Homilies on Genesis 8, in substance). On a Friday we remember that the Father did not withhold his only Son, and that the Son was truly raised from the dead.

The reading continues with three deathbeds and three blessings. “By faith Isaac invoked blessings for the future on Jacob and Esau. By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, ‘bowing in worship over the top of his staff.’ By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave instructions about his burial.” These old men could not see the fulfilment of the promise, but they handed it on. Faith looks forward; it blesses those who will live after us. In our homes the elders still make the sign of the cross on the foreheads of children before a journey, an examination or the first day of school. This month, as the schools reopen, that small gesture is repeated in thousands of houses. It is not a superstition; it is the faith of Isaac and Jacob, a blessing given by those who believe that God will keep his promises to the next generation even when they are no longer here to see it.

In the Gospel Jesus teaches in the temple at the feast of Tabernacles, and his hearers are astonished: “How does this man have such learning, when he has never been taught?” Jesus does not boast of his learning. He points away from himself: “My teaching is not mine but his who sent me. Anyone who resolves to do the will of God will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own.” Here is a key to the life of faith: understanding comes to those who obey. St Augustine, commenting on these words, told his people that if they did not yet understand, they should believe and do the will of God, for understanding is the reward of faith (Tractates on John 29, in substance). When the evangelist writes “the Jews” here, he means the religious authorities in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus, not a whole people; the Church teaches that the Jewish people as such may not be blamed for the death of Christ (Catechism 597). Jesus then defends his healing of a man on the sabbath and ends with a word for every age: “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” The fast is a time to cleanse our judgements too: to stop judging others by their clothes, their caste, their accent or their past, and to look, as God does, at the heart.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Letter to the Hebrews tells us: By faith Abraham, when put to the test, offered up Isaac (Hebrews 11:17). It was the hardest test of his life. Isaac was the son of the promise. But Abraham trusted, because he considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead (Hebrews 11:19). On this Friday we think of the Father who gave his own Son for us, and raised him from the dead.

Then the letter speaks of old men blessing their children: by faith Isaac invoked blessings for the future on Jacob and Esau (Hebrews 11:20), and Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph (Hebrews 11:21). They did not see the promise fulfilled, but they passed it on. Our elders still bless children with the sign of the cross before school and before journeys. That is the faith of the patriarchs living in our homes.

In the Gospel, people are amazed at the teaching of Jesus. He says: My teaching is not mine but his who sent me. Anyone who resolves to do the will of God will know whether the teaching is from God (John 7:16–17). If we want to understand our faith, we must begin by living it. Then Jesus adds: Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment (John 7:24).

So on this Friday of the fast, let us ask for a faith that trusts in hard times, a faith that blesses the next generation, and a heart that does not judge by appearances. Let us bless our children, and let us look at every person as God looks at them. Amen.

For children

Children, does your grandmother or your mother make a little cross on your forehead before you go to school? That is a blessing! Long ago, Isaac and Jacob, who were very old, also blessed their children and grandchildren and asked God to take care of them. And Jesus tells us something important: do not judge people by how they look. A new boy or girl in your class may look different, but God loves them very much. Be kind to them!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on this Friday we remember that the Father did not withhold you, his only Son, but gave you up for us all, and raised you from the dead. Give us the faith of Abraham, who trusted when he did not understand, and the faith of Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, who blessed the generations to come. Teach us to do the will of the Father, so that we may know that your teaching is from God. Cleanse our hearts of rash and proud judgements, and let us look on others as you look on them. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask where true teaching and true faith come from. Hebrews looks back at the patriarchs, who trusted God's promise even when it seemed to lead to death: Abraham on the mountain with Isaac, Isaac and Jacob blessing sons they would not see grow old, Joseph speaking of an exodus that lay centuries ahead. In John, Jesus teaches in the temple at the feast of Booths and says that his teaching is not his own but the Father's, and that whoever is ready to do God's will shall recognise it. The key words are faith and right judgment.

Hebrews 11:17–22 · By faith Abraham offered up Isaac

Setting.

Hebrews 11 is the great roll of honour of faith. It opens with a definition: “faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1), and then walks through the story of Israel, each example beginning with the same Greek word, pistei, “by faith”. Our verses come after the call of Abraham (11:8–16) and turn to the testing of his faith and to the blessings handed on by Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, all taken from Genesis 22 to 50.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–19: Abraham offers Isaac, trusting that God can raise the dead.
  • v. 20: Isaac blesses Jacob and Esau.
  • v. 21: the dying Jacob blesses the sons of Joseph.
  • v. 22: the dying Joseph speaks of the exodus and gives orders about his bones.

Key words.

  • pistei, by faith: The word that begins each example of the chapter like a drum beat. Faith here is not a vague feeling. It is trust in God's word that shows itself in acts: leaving, offering, blessing, giving orders for a future one will not see.
  • monogenēs, only son: The same word John uses of Christ, “the only Son” (John 3:16). Abraham had another son, Ishmael, but Isaac was the one son of the promise. The word prepares the Christian reader to see in Isaac a figure of the Father's only Son.
  • en parabolē, figuratively speaking: Literally “in a parable” or “as a figure”. Isaac did not die, but Abraham received him back as if from the dead, and this return was itself a sign pointing ahead.

Verse by verse.

“By faith Abraham, when put to the test, offered up Isaac.” (11:17)

The test of Genesis 22 seemed to set God's command against God's promise, since the promise was to come through Isaac. Abraham held on to both, trusting that God would not contradict himself.

“He considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead” (11:19)

Hebrews reads the story of Moriah in the light of Easter. Abraham's faith reached beyond death long before the resurrection was revealed.

“By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, “bowing in worship over the top of his staff.”” (11:21)

The phrase about the staff comes from the Greek Old Testament of Genesis 47:31. The Hebrew text has “the head of his bed”. The two words differ only in their vowels, which were not written in ancient Hebrew.

“By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave instructions about his burial.” (11:22)

Joseph asked that his bones be carried up from Egypt (Genesis 50:24–25). Moses did so at the exodus (Exodus 13:19), and they were buried at Shechem (Joshua 24:32). A dying man's faith looked some four hundred years ahead.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses these verses when it speaks of Abraham's prayer and faith: asked to sacrifice the son God had given him, Abraham did not waver, for he considered that God was able to raise men even from the dead, and so the father of believers was conformed to the likeness of the Father who would not spare his own Son (CCC 2572). From the earliest centuries Christians have seen in Isaac, carrying the wood up the mountain, a figure of Christ carrying his cross, and in the ram offered in his place a sign of the Lamb whom God himself provides.

John 7:14–24 · My teaching is not mine but his who sent me

Setting.

Booths, or Tabernacles, was the joyful autumn pilgrimage feast, when people lived in shelters of branches to remember the wandering in the wilderness. Jesus had gone up to Jerusalem quietly, “not publicly but as it were in secret” (7:10). Now, half way through the week of the feast, he teaches openly in the temple.

The argument looks back to John 5, where Jesus healed a man at the pool of Bethesda on a sabbath and some of the authorities began to seek his death (5:16–18). In this Gospel “the Jews” often means the religious authorities in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus, not the Jewish people as a whole; Jesus, his mother and his disciples were all Jews, and “the crowd” in verse 20 does not know of any plot.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: Jesus teaches; the hearers wonder at his learning.
  • vv. 16–18: his teaching comes from the one who sent him.
  • vv. 19–20: the law of Moses and the plot to kill him.
  • vv. 21–24: circumcision on the sabbath and the healing of a whole man: judge with right judgment.

Key words.

  • grammata, learning: Literally “letters”. The question is how Jesus can teach the Scriptures so deeply when he has not studied under a recognised teacher. His answer points to the true source of his teaching: the Father.
  • didachē, teaching: Jesus does not deny that he teaches. He denies that the teaching is his own invention. The Son speaks what he receives from the Father, as a messenger speaks for the one who sends him.
  • dikaia krisis, right judgment: A just verdict that looks at the truth of a matter and not at its surface. Isaiah says of the coming king that “he shall not judge by what his eyes see” (Isaiah 11:3).

Verse by verse.

“My teaching is not mine but his who sent me.” (7:16)

St Augustine draws out the paradox: the teaching of the Son is the Word of the Father, and the Son is that Word; so nothing is more his own, and yet it is wholly the Father's (Tractates on John 29, in substance).

“Anyone who resolves to do the will of God will know whether the teaching is from God” (7:17)

Knowledge of God is not reached only by argument. The one who is ready to obey comes to understand. Augustine puts it this way: understanding is the reward of faith (Tractates on John 29, in substance).

“Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” (7:24)

Jesus argues as the teachers of his day argued: if circumcision, which touches one part of the body, may be done on the sabbath, how much more may a whole man be healed? Later rabbis used a similar argument to allow saving a life on the sabbath.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that Jesus Christ, the Word made flesh, sent as a man to men, speaks the words of God and completes the work of salvation that the Father gave him to do (Dei Verbum 4). The Church therefore does not treat the Gospel as one opinion among many but receives it as the teaching of the Father given in the Son. Verse 17 is also a word of encouragement for every searcher: the honest will to do what God asks is already a door to faith.

Questions people ask

Did God really want Abraham to kill his son?

The story itself says that God was testing Abraham (Genesis 22:1), and God stops his hand. In a world where some peoples offered children to their gods, the story teaches the opposite: the God of Israel does not want human sacrifice, and he himself provides the offering. For Christians it points to the one sacrifice God did not withhold, the gift of his own Son, which no human father was asked to make.

For study and teaching

  • The patriarchs blessed a future they would not see. What blessing am I handing on to the next generation in my family and parish?
  • Jesus says that whoever is ready to do God's will shall know his teaching. Is there something God is asking of me that I am delaying, and could that be why I find faith hard?
