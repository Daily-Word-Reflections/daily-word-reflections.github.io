Word for the day

“Share in suffering like a good soldier of Christ Jesus.” 2 Timothy 2:3

Reflection

There is hardly a Christian village in Kerala without a Geevarghese, a Varghese or a George. The name of today's saint has passed from Syriac (Giwargis) into Malayalam homes, and his image, a young soldier on a white horse with his lance aimed at the dragon, hangs in churches of every tradition among us. Today our Church keeps his feast. What history can say with confidence is short and strong. George was a Christian soldier who gave his life for Christ in the great persecution of the early fourth century, most likely under the emperor Diocletian, and his tomb at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine was a place of pilgrimage within a few generations. The longer stories of his trials grew up later; the Church has always honoured him above all as a witness. Our tradition calls him Sahada, the Syriac word for martyr, which means witness. A decree attributed to Pope Gelasius I placed him among those whose names are rightly revered among people but whose deeds are known fully only to God (in substance). That is enough. We do not need every detail of his story to know what he did: when the empire demanded that he deny Christ, he chose Christ.

The readings show why a soldier became the image of every Christian. St Paul writes to Timothy: “You then, my child, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus... Share in suffering like a good soldier of Christ Jesus. No one serving in the army gets entangled in everyday affairs; the soldier's aim is to please the enlisting officer.” Paul adds the athlete, who is not crowned “without competing according to the rules,” and the farmer “who does the work.” All three are pictures of steady, faithful labour, not of sudden heroics. George's martyrdom did not come from nowhere; it grew out of a soldier's daily faithfulness to the one who had enlisted him in Baptism. St Ignatius of Antioch, himself on the way to martyrdom, wrote to Polycarp: please the one under whom you serve as soldiers; let none of you be found a deserter; let your Baptism be your shield, your faith your helmet, your love your spear, your endurance your armour (Letter to Polycarp 6, in substance). In the Gospel Jesus sends his disciples out with no illusions: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” They will be handed over and “dragged before governors and kings because of me, as a testimony to them and the Gentiles.” And he adds the promise that every martyr has leaned on: “do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say... for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” Finally: “the one who endures to the end will be saved.”

Why does a martyr's feast belong in the season of Qyomtho? Because martyrs are the strongest proof that Christians really believe in the Resurrection. No one dies for a story he suspects to be false. St Augustine taught that it is not the suffering but the cause that makes a martyr (Letter 89, in substance): George is honoured not because he was tortured, but because he would not betray the risen Lord. The famous dragon is a later legend, best read as a picture: the dragon is the old enemy, and every Christian who refuses evil for Christ's sake strikes at it. In our own time Christians are still dragged before councils, in many lands, for the name of Jesus. Pray for them today. And ask yourself where your own small martyrdom lies: in refusing a bribe at the office, in telling the truth at home when a lie would be easier, in keeping Sunday when the world pulls the other way. Take part in the Holy Qurbono for the feast if you can, and if you bear his name, thank God for it and ask for his courage.

Theme: “Share in suffering like a good soldier of Christ Jesus.”

1. A good soldier of Christ Jesus

  • Strong in grace.
  • Not entangled in everyday affairs.
  • Pleasing the one who enlisted us.
“I hereby command you: Be strong and courageous; do not be frightened or dismayed, for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go.” Joshua 1:9
“Finally, be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his power. Put on the whole armor of God, so that you may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil.” Ephesians 6:10–11

Apply: Each morning this week, before work or school, make the sign of the Cross slowly and offer the day to Christ as his soldier.

2. Sheep in the midst of wolves

  • Wise as serpents, innocent as doves.
  • Dragged before governors and kings.
  • The Spirit speaks through them.
“Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I fear no evil; for you are with me;” Psalm 23:4
“Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life.” Revelation 2:10

Apply: Pray by name for one country where Christians suffer for their faith, and learn one fact about their situation.

3. Sahada: a witness of the risen Lord

  • No one dies for a lie.
  • The cause makes the martyr.
  • The dragon and our daily refusals.

Apply: Choose one wrong thing you will refuse this week for Christ's sake, quietly and firmly.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today our Church honours Mar Geevarghese Sahada, St George, a Christian soldier who gave his life for Christ in the great persecution of the early fourth century. Sahada means martyr, and martyr means witness. His tomb at Lydda drew pilgrims from very early times, and his name lives in countless Kerala families.

St Paul writes: share in suffering like a good soldier of Christ Jesus. No one serving in the army gets entangled in everyday affairs; the soldier's aim is to please the enlisting officer (2 Timothy 2:3–4). George served an earthly emperor, but his first loyalty was to Christ, who enlisted him in Baptism.

Jesus did not hide the cost: see, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves (Matthew 10:16). His witnesses would be dragged before governors and kings. But he promised: it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you, and the one who endures to the end will be saved (Matthew 10:20, 22).

The martyrs belong to Qyomtho, because no one dies for a lie: they died for the risen Lord whom they knew. The dragon of the legend is evil itself. Each time we refuse evil for Christ, we strike it. Let us pray for persecuted Christians today, and ask Mar Geevarghese for the courage to be faithful soldiers in small things. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Today is the feast of Mar Geevarghese, St George. Do you know anyone called Varghese or George? They are named after him! He was a soldier who loved Jesus so much that he would not stop being a Christian, even when the emperor ordered him. There is a famous story of him fighting a dragon. The dragon means everything bad. When you say no to lying or hitting or cheating because you love Jesus, you are as brave as St George!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you gave Mar Geevarghese the courage of a good soldier and the crown of a martyr. You sent your disciples as sheep among wolves and promised that your Spirit would speak through them. By his prayers make us strong in your grace; let us not be entangled in what is passing, but seek only to please you who enlisted us in Baptism. Strengthen all Christians who suffer for your name, and give us endurance to the end, so that with all your witnesses we may share in your Resurrection. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today our Church keeps the feast of Mar Geevarghese Sahada, St George the Martyr, a Christian soldier who gave his life for Christ in the persecution of the early fourth century and whose tomb at Lydda in Palestine was soon a place of pilgrimage. The weekday reading of Hebrews gives way to two readings chosen for a martyr.

In the Epistle, Paul, near the end of his own life, gives Timothy three pictures of the Christian: the soldier who pleases the one who enlisted him, the athlete who competes by the rules, and the farmer who works. In the Gospel, Jesus sends his disciples out like sheep among wolves, warns them that they will be handed over, and promises that the Spirit of the Father will speak through them. Read in the season of Qyomtho, both readings say the same thing: a martyr is a witness to the risen Lord. The key words are soldier, testimony and endurance.

2 Timothy 2:1–6 · A good soldier of Christ Jesus

Setting.

The Second Letter to Timothy presents itself as Paul's last letter, written from prison in Rome near the end of his life (1:8, 17; 4:6–8). Timothy, his younger co-worker, has been left in charge of the Church at Ephesus (1 Timothy 1:3). Many scholars today think that the Pastoral Letters were written by a disciple in Paul's name after his death; others, with the long tradition of the Church, hold that they come from Paul himself, perhaps with the help of a secretary. Either way the Church receives them as inspired Scripture, and their picture of Paul's last charge is precious.

Paul often uses the images of soldier and athlete. He calls Epaphroditus his “fellow soldier” (Philippians 2:25), describes the armour of God (Ephesians 6:10–17), and compares himself to a runner and a boxer in training (1 Corinthians 9:24–27). In the same chapter of 1 Corinthians he asks who serves as a soldier at his own expense, or plants a vineyard and does not eat its fruit (9:7).

Structure.

  • v. 1: be strong in grace.
  • v. 2: hand on what you have received.
  • vv. 3–4: the soldier.
  • v. 5: the athlete.
  • v. 6: the farmer.
  • ¶ The next verse adds: “Think over what I say, for the Lord will give you understanding in all things” (2:7). The three pictures are meant to be pondered, not just admired.

Key words.

  • synkakopathēson, share in suffering: One Greek word: “suffer hardship together with”. Timothy's sufferings are joined to Paul's, and both are joined to Christ's (compare 1:8).
  • stratologēsanti, the enlisting officer: The one who enlisted the soldier. A soldier does not choose his commander; he is chosen. The Christian is enlisted by Christ in Baptism.
  • nomimōs, according to the rules: At the games an athlete had to keep the rules of training and of the contest to win the crown. The crown, stephanos, was a wreath; the Church speaks of the martyr's crown, and the first martyr's own name, Stephen, means “crown”.

Verse by verse.

“You then, my child, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus;” (2:1)

The verb is passive in form: “be strengthened”. Strength comes first from grace, not from will power.

“and what you have heard from me through many witnesses entrust to faithful people who will be able to teach others as well.” (2:2)

Four links in one chain: Paul, Timothy, faithful people, others. The verb is the one used for placing something in safekeeping, as with “the good treasure entrusted to you” (1:14). This is what Tradition means: handing on.

“Share in suffering like a good soldier of Christ Jesus.” (2:3)

Paul does not promise Timothy an easy life. He invites him to share the hardship that Paul himself is bearing in chains.

“No one serving in the army gets entangled in everyday affairs; the soldier’s aim is to please the enlisting officer.” (2:4)

Not a rejection of ordinary life, but a matter of first loyalty. A soldier does not let other concerns decide where he stands.

“It is the farmer who does the work who ought to have the first share of the crops.” (2:6)

The last picture is the quietest: patient labour, and a harvest that comes later.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers loved this picture. St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom, wrote to St Polycarp that Christians should please the one under whom they serve as soldiers, and let none be found a deserter; let Baptism be their weapons, faith their helmet, love their spear, endurance their armour (Letter to Polycarp 6, in substance). The Church has many soldier saints, and Mar Geevarghese is the best loved of them in the East.

The Second Vatican Council says that the Church considers martyrdom an exceptional gift and the highest proof of love, by which the disciple is made like the Master (Lumen Gentium 42). Verse 2 is also read as a witness to the apostolic succession: the apostles handed on to their successors what they had received, so that the Gospel would be kept whole and alive in the Church (CCC 77).

Matthew 10:16–25 · Sheep in the midst of wolves

Setting.

Matthew 10 is the mission discourse. Jesus names the Twelve and sends them first “to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:5–6). In our passage the horizon widens: the disciples will stand “before governors and kings” and bear witness “to them and the Gentiles” (10:18). Mark places similar words in Jesus' teaching about the end (Mark 13:9–13). The warnings reach beyond the first mission to the whole life of the Church.

When Jesus speaks of councils and “their synagogues”, he is speaking of the world his first disciples knew. They were Jews preaching to fellow Jews, and the first conflicts were quarrels within one family of faith. The passage does not accuse a people. Persecution comes from many sides: from governors and kings, who were Gentile rulers, and even from one's own household (10:21).

Structure.

  • v. 16: sheep among wolves; wise and innocent.
  • vv. 17–20: handed over, and the Spirit who speaks.
  • vv. 21–22: division in families, and endurance to the end.
  • v. 23: flee to the next town.
  • vv. 24–25: the disciple will be like the teacher.

Key words.

  • phronimoi … akeraioi, wise … innocent: Prudent like a serpent, which knows danger, and without guile like a dove, literally “unmixed”, with nothing false in it. The disciple needs both: neither naive nor cunning.
  • eis martyrion, as a testimony: From martys, witness, the word that became “martyr”. The Syriac word sahdo, in our Malayalam sahada, means the same: a witness. The martyrs are those whose testimony was sealed with their blood.
  • hypomeinas, the one who endures: From hypomonē, steady staying power. It is the same word family that Hebrews uses for the endurance of Christ (Hebrews 12:2).

Verse by verse.

“See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” (10:16)

The Shepherd sends sheep, not wolves. Their strength is not in their own claws.

“and you will be dragged before governors and kings because of me, as a testimony to them and the Gentiles.” (10:18)

Even arrest becomes a pulpit. Many of the martyrs' acts record a confession of faith made before a judge.

“for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” (10:20)

This is the only place in Matthew where the Spirit is called “the Spirit of your Father”. The disciples are not alone in court; the Father speaks for his children.

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” (10:22)

Salvation is not promised to the fearless but to those who remain. Endurance is itself a gift of grace.

“A disciple is not above the teacher, nor a slave above the master;” (10:24)

What happened to Jesus will happen to his own. If they called the master of the house Beelzebul, a name for the prince of demons (12:24), they will not spare his household.

How the Church reads it.

St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, says that as long as we are sheep we conquer, even when surrounded by many wolves; but if we become wolves we are beaten, because the help of the Shepherd leaves us (Homilies on Matthew 33, in substance). Tertullian wrote that the blood of Christians is seed (Apology 50, in substance): the Church grew where the martyrs died.

The Catechism teaches that martyrdom is the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith: the martyr bears witness to Christ who died and rose, to whom he is united by charity (CCC 2473). It adds that the Church has gathered with great care the records of those who persevered to the end (CCC 2474). The martyr's feast belongs in the season of the Resurrection, because no one gives his life for a story he thinks is untrue.

Questions people ask

How much do we really know about St George?

The core is firm: he was a Christian soldier martyred in the great persecution of the early fourth century, most likely under the emperor Diocletian, and his tomb at Lydda (Diospolis), where St Peter once healed Aeneas (Acts 9:32–35), was honoured very early. The long stories of his tortures, and the dragon, came later; the dragon is best read as a picture of the old enemy whom every faithful Christian strikes down. The Roman calendar too keeps his memorial on this day. We honour him for what is certain: he chose Christ.

Does Jesus tell us to seek martyrdom?

No. In v. 23 he says, “When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next.” The Church has never praised those who rashly hand themselves over. The account of St Polycarp's martyrdom tells of a man named Quintus who gave himself up, and then lost courage, and it says that the Gospel does not teach this (Martyrdom of Polycarp 4, in substance). Martyrdom is a gift received when there is no faithful way out, not a goal to be pursued.

Can a Christian serve as a soldier?

Yes. The Catechism says that those who serve their country in the armed forces are servants of the security and freedom of nations, and that if they carry out their duty honourably they truly contribute to the common good and the maintenance of peace (CCC 2310). St George's witness was not that he left the army but that, as a soldier, he would not deny Christ.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the Syriac tradition St George is Mar Giwargis, and the title sahdo, Sahada in our usage, is given to the martyrs: it simply means witness. The Panchangam keeps his feast today, and across Kerala churches of several traditions are dedicated to him and countless families carry his name. In the Holy Qurbono the Church remembers the saints and martyrs before the altar, and the feast of a martyr in Qyomtho is itself an Easter feast: we honour Mar Geevarghese because Christ is risen, and pray for those who suffer for the name of Jesus in many lands today.

For study and teaching

  • Paul gives three pictures: the soldier, the athlete, the farmer. Which one describes what Christ is asking of me at this time of my life?
  • Who in my life has handed on the faith to me, as Paul to Timothy? To whom am I handing it on?
  • Jesus asks us to be wise as serpents and innocent as doves. Where do I tend to be naive, and where do I tend to be cunning?
  • If I bear the name Geevarghese, Varghese or George, or love this saint, what small act of courage for Christ can I offer today?
