Word for the day

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” Matthew 24:13

Reflection

Jesus is sitting on the Mount of Olives when the disciples come to him privately, saying: “Tell us, when will this be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?” He does not give them a timetable. He gives them a way to live: “Beware that no one leads you astray. For many will come in my name, saying, ‘I am the Messiah!’ and they will lead many astray. And you will hear of wars and rumors of wars; see that you are not alarmed; for this must take place, but the end is not yet.” Wars, famines and earthquakes are “but the beginning of the birth pangs”, which means that they are pains that lead to birth, not to death. Then comes the harder word about the disciples themselves: they will be hated, many will fall away, false prophets will arise, and “because of the increase of lawlessness, the love of many will grow cold.” In the floods that have come upon Kerala in recent years, we have seen both: fear, and also the warm love of neighbour for neighbour, the fishermen with their boats and the people who opened their churches and homes. Jesus says: “But the one who endures to the end will be saved. And this good news of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the world, as a testimony to all the nations; and then the end will come.” The Church has never turned these words into a calendar. She hears in them a call to watchfulness, to endurance, and to mission.

Today the Panchangam remembers Ezekiel, a prophet for hard times. He was a priest, “Ezekiel son of Buzi,” who was carried into exile with the leaders of Judah, and in the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin, as he was “among the exiles by the river Chebar,” he writes, “the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God.” He saw the glory of the Lord, not in the Temple in Jerusalem, but beside a canal in Babylon: the Lord had not been left behind in the ruined city, and he came to his people in exile. Ezekiel was told to eat a scroll, and he says: “Then I ate it; and in my mouth it was as sweet as honey.” The word of God is sweet even when it announces hard things. And he was made a sentinel: “Mortal, I have made you a sentinel for the house of Israel; whenever you hear a word from my mouth, you shall give them warning from me.” The watchman on the wall is the picture of today's Gospel: beware, do not be led astray, stay awake. A watchman does not frighten people for the sake of it. He cries out so that they may live.

But Ezekiel is also the prophet of the new heart. To the exiles who thought that all was over, the Lord promised: “A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you; and I will remove from your body the heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. I will put my spirit within you, and make you follow my statutes and be careful to observe my ordinances.” In the vision of the dry bones, the Lord asks: “Mortal, can these bones live?” and he commands the prophet to speak: “I will cause breath to enter you, and you shall live.” The Church has long heard in this vision the promise of resurrection and of the gift of the Holy Spirit, which we remember in this season after Pentecost. At the end of the book, Ezekiel sees water flowing from below the threshold of the temple toward the east, growing into a river, so that “everything will live where the river goes.” In July in Kerala, when our rivers are full and the land is green, we know what a river can do for a dry land. The Fathers saw in this river baptism and the water that flowed from the side of Christ, and Saint John saw in the Book of Revelation “the river of the water of life, bright as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb.”

Today's Epistle gives the ground of our endurance: “giving thanks to the Father, who has enabled you to share in the inheritance of the saints in the light. He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins.” The exiles in Babylon were transferred back to their land; we have been transferred into the kingdom of the Son by baptism. This is why we can endure to the end: our endurance does not rest on our own strength, but on what has already been done for us, the rescue, the transfer, the redemption. The Lord who spoke to the exiles by the river Chebar, who gave them a new heart and spoke of a river that heals, is the same Lord who tells us today: do not be alarmed. Let us ask him for the heart of flesh, for the watchfulness of the sentinel, and for the love that does not grow cold.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel the disciples ask Jesus on the Mount of Olives: when will this be, and what will be the sign of your coming? Jesus gives no timetable. He says: Beware that no one leads you astray. You will hear of wars and rumors of wars; see that you are not alarmed (Matthew 24:4, 6). All this is but the beginning of the birth pangs, the pains that lead to a birth. Many will fall away, and because of the increase of lawlessness, the love of many will grow cold. But the one who endures to the end will be saved (Matthew 24:12–13).

Today the Church remembers Ezekiel, a prophet for hard times. He was a priest among the exiles by the river Chebar in Babylon, and there the heavens were opened and he saw visions of God (Ezekiel 1:1). The Lord was not left behind in Jerusalem; he came to his people in exile. God made him a sentinel for the house of Israel (Ezekiel 3:17), and promised: A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you (Ezekiel 36:26). At the end of the book Ezekiel sees a river flowing from the temple, and everything will live where the river goes (Ezekiel 47:9).

St Paul tells us why we can endure: God has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins (Colossians 1:13–14). Our endurance rests on what Christ has done for us. And the good news of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the world, as a testimony to all the nations (Matthew 24:14).

Let us ask for the heart of flesh, for the watchfulness of the sentinel, and for the love that does not grow cold. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what a watchman does? He stands high on a wall and looks out, and if he sees danger coming, he shouts so that everyone is safe. God made Ezekiel a watchman for his people. Jesus also asks us to stay awake and not to be afraid when we hear scary news. And God promised a new heart, a soft one that can love. You can ask Jesus every day: Please give me a soft and loving heart.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sat on the Mount of Olives and taught your disciples to watch and to endure. We thank you for the prophet Ezekiel, who saw your glory among the exiles, who ate your word as sweet as honey, who was a sentinel for your people, and who promised a new heart and a river of life. Keep us from being led astray, and from alarm. Give us a heart of flesh, and let your love never grow cold in us. You have rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of your beloved Son; give us strength to endure to the end and to proclaim the good news to all the nations. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers Ezekiel the Prophet, the watchman among the exiles, who warned his people and also promised them a new heart and a new spirit. Both readings speak to a people in a hard time. Paul thanks the Father who has rescued us from the power of darkness and brought us into the kingdom of his Son. In the Gospel Jesus warns the disciples of wars, false teachers, hatred and cold love, and promises: “the one who endures to the end will be saved.” The key words are rescued, watch and endure.

Colossians 1:12–14 · Rescued from darkness into the kingdom

Setting.

After the greeting, Paul tells the Colossians that he thanks God for their faith and love (1:3–8) and prays that they may be filled with the knowledge of God's will (1:9–11). Our verses continue that prayer in the form of thanksgiving, and they lead straight into the great hymn to Christ, the image of the invisible God (1:15–20).

Paul's words are rich with the memory of Israel. The “inheritance” recalls the land given to Israel as its share, and the “rescue” is the vocabulary of the Exodus. What God did for Israel in bringing it out of Egypt, he has now done in Christ for those who believe, whether Jew or Gentile.

Structure.

  • v. 12: thanksgiving to the Father, who has made us fit to share the inheritance of the saints in light.
  • v. 13: he rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son.
  • v. 14: in the Son we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins.

Key words.

  • klēros, inheritance, share: The word for a portion assigned by lot, the share of the land that each tribe received. Here the share is “in the light”, a place in God's own life, which is given and not earned.
  • methestēsen, transferred: The word was used for moving a whole people from one land to another. We have been moved out of one kingdom into another, as the people of Israel passed from Egypt to the land of promise.
  • apolytrōsis, redemption: Literally a release by the payment of a ransom. In the Old Testament it recalls the release of slaves and Israel's rescue from Egypt. Paul identifies it with the forgiveness of sins.

Verse by verse.

“giving thanks to the Father, who has enabled you to share in the inheritance of the saints in the light.” (1:12)

Paul begins with thanks. Our place in the light is something we have been enabled to receive. It is the Father's gift, and the right response to a gift is gratitude.

“He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son,” (1:13)

Paul uses the past tense: it has happened. “His beloved Son” is literally “the Son of his love”, so the kingdom is the kingdom of the one whom the Father loves, and we are brought into that love.

“in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins.” (1:14)

Some later manuscripts add “through his blood” here, from Ephesians 1:7; it is not in the oldest ones. The truth of the added words is clear from other texts, such as Colossians 1:20.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in these verses the meaning of Baptism, in which we are delivered from sin and from the power of the evil one and brought into the family of God. The rite of Baptism includes exorcisms that express this liberation (CCC 1237), and the Catechism teaches that by Baptism all sins are forgiven (CCC 1263). The Syriac Fathers loved to speak of the font as a womb from which the baptized are born again, clothed in a robe of glory (Mar Aphrem, in substance). Giving thanks, as Paul does, is also the meaning of the Eucharist, which in Greek means “thanksgiving”, the Church's prayer of gratitude for being rescued.

Matthew 24:3–14 · The one who endures to the end

Setting.

Jesus has just left the Temple and told his disciples that not one stone will be left on another (24:1–2). On the Mount of Olives, facing the Temple, they ask him privately: when will this be, and what will be the sign of his coming and of the end of the age? This is the beginning of the long discourse of Matthew 24–25, often called the Olivet Discourse. Many interpreters see in it both the coming destruction of the Temple and the end of the age, spoken of together, as the prophets often did.

Our verses are the first part. Jesus does not give dates or maps. He gives the disciples a way to live in the time between his departure and his return: do not be led astray, do not be alarmed, stay faithful, and preach the Gospel to all nations.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–4: the disciples' question and the first warning: do not be led astray.
  • vv. 5–8: false messiahs, wars and rumours, famines and earthquakes: only the beginning of the birth pangs.
  • vv. 9–12: persecution, falling away, betrayal, false prophets, and the cooling of love.
  • vv. 13–14: endurance leads to salvation; the Gospel is preached to all the nations, and then the end.

Key words.

  • ōdin, birth pangs: The pains of a woman in labour. In the Old Testament the pains of childbirth are an image for the trials that come before God acts (Isaiah 26:17–19; 66:7–9). Pains that lead to a birth are not meaningless.
  • hypomonē, endurance: Not passive waiting but staying under a load and holding on. It is the great virtue of the disciple in a hard time, and the same word family appears in Paul's prayer just before our verses, that the Colossians may be prepared to endure everything with patience (Colossians 1:11).
  • martyrion, testimony: The Gospel will be proclaimed “as a testimony to all the nations”. The word is the root of martyr: the witness of disciples, sometimes at the price of their lives, is how the good news reaches the world.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus answered them, “Beware that no one leads you astray.” (24:4)

The first danger Jesus names is not persecution but deception. The disciples need clear eyes more than fearful hearts.

“all this is but the beginning of the birth pangs.” (24:8)

Wars and disasters are not the end. They show a world still in labour. Jesus tells his disciples not to read every shock as the final sign, and not to panic.

“And because of the increase of lawlessness, the love of many will grow cold.” (24:12)

The saddest line of the passage. The danger within the community is not only the enemy outside but the loss of love inside. The remedy is to keep love warm.

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” (24:13)

Salvation is promised to those who hold on in faith, and it is God's grace that makes holding on possible. The disciple does not endure by his own strength alone.

“And this good news of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the world, as a testimony to all the nations; and then the end will come.” (24:14)

The Church's mission is not a side matter. The Gospel goes out to all peoples, and this is part of how history moves to its end. The same promise stands at the end of the Gospel: “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations” (28:19).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that since the Ascension, Christ's coming in glory is imminent, yet no one knows the day or the hour, and the times belong to the Father (CCC 673). Before his coming the Church will pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers (CCC 675). The Church reads Jesus' words not as a timetable, but as a call to watchfulness, mission and faithfulness. Hope, the Catechism says, means that each of us should hope, with the grace of God, to persevere to the end (CCC 1821). Today many Christians in many lands suffer for their faith, and others suffer for other reasons; we pray for all who suffer, and for those who wrong them, that their hearts may be changed.

Questions people ask

Does this passage tell us when the world will end?

No. Jesus warns against exactly that kind of calculation. A few verses later he says that about “that day and hour” no one knows, “neither the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father” (24:36). The Church has always rejected attempts to fix dates. What the passage gives is a way of life: watch, do not be afraid, keep love warm, hold on, and proclaim the Gospel.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says the Father has “rescued us from the power of darkness”. What has God rescued me from, and have I given thanks for it?
  • Jesus says the love of many will grow cold. What helps me to keep my love warm: in my family, in my parish, towards people I find difficult?
