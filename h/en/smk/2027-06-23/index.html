Readings

Word for the day

“He said, “Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him.” John 9:38

Reflection

Today we hear the last lines of St Paul's Letter to the Ephesians, and they are not about doctrine but about a friend. “So that you also may know how I am and what I am doing, Tychicus will tell you everything. He is a dear brother and a faithful minister in the Lord. I am sending him to you for this very purpose, to let you know how we are, and to encourage your hearts.” Tychicus is one of those names we pass over quickly. He carried Paul's letters over sea and road, he brought news from the prison, and he sat with the believers to strengthen them. He was not one of the Twelve, and no great church is named after him, yet the Church would not have grown without people like him. The Apostles' Fast honours the Apostles, and with them all the quiet helpers of the gospel: the catechist who teaches on Sunday mornings, the sacristan who opens the church in the rain, the friend who visits the sick. Paul's blessing reaches all of them: “Peace be to the whole community, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Grace be with all who have an undying love for our Lord Jesus Christ.”

The Gospel brings us to the end of the long story of the man born blind. Jesus had made mud, anointed his eyes and sent him to wash in the pool of Siloam, and he came back seeing. Instead of rejoicing, his questioners argued, his parents grew afraid, and in the end he was driven out. Then comes a beautiful line: “Jesus heard that they had driven him out, and when he found him, he said, ‘Do you believe in the Son of Man?’” The one whom others pushed out, Jesus goes looking for. The man answers with an open heart: “And who is he, sir? Tell me, so that I may believe in him.” Jesus said to him, “You have seen him, and the one speaking with you is he.” And the man's eyes, which had first seen the light of day, now see the Lord: “Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him. Through the chapter his faith has grown step by step: he first spoke of “the man called Jesus”, then called him a prophet, then said he must be from God, and now he kneels. St Augustine saw in this man a picture of the catechumen: anointed, sent to the water, washed, and given sight, so that he could worship (Tractates on John 44, in substance). The early Church called Baptism illumination, as St Justin Martyr records (First Apology 61), and every baptized Christian is that man, given eyes to see the Lord.

The last verses turn the light on another kind of blindness. “I came into this world for judgment so that those who do not see may see, and those who do see may become blind.” Some of the Pharisees near him heard this and said to him, “Surely we are not blind, are we?” Jesus said to them, “If you were blind, you would not have sin. But now that you say, ‘We see,’ your sin remains.” This is not a judgement on a whole people; the evangelist is showing a danger that lives in every religious heart, including ours. The person who knows he is blind can be healed; the person who insists that he sees everything clearly shuts the door on the doctor. A fast is a good time to admit what we cannot see: our own faults, the needs of others at our own table, the hand of God in a hard year. Tomorrow we keep the birth of John the Baptist, who would one day say of Jesus that he must increase. To say “Lord, I believe”, and to kneel, is the beginning of that decrease, and the beginning of true sight.

Theme: “Lord, I believe.”

On Wednesday of the fifth week after Pentecost, in the Apostles' Fast: Tychicus the faithful minister, the man born blind who comes to worship, and the blindness that says “We see”.

“So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” Romans 10:17

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul ends his Letter to the Ephesians by sending a friend: Tychicus, a dear brother and a faithful minister in the Lord (Ephesians 6:21). Paul sends him to let you know how we are, and to encourage your hearts (Ephesians 6:22). The Church grows through the great Apostles, and also through quiet helpers like Tychicus. Every parish has them, and we thank God for them.

In the Gospel, the man born blind has been healed, but instead of rejoicing with him, people argue and throw him out. Then Jesus heard that they had driven him out, and when he found him, he said, Do you believe in the Son of Man? (John 9:35). The man answers: Tell me, so that I may believe in him (John 9:36). And when Jesus shows himself, he said, Lord, I believe. And he worshiped him (John 9:38).

Then Jesus speaks of another blindness. Some said: Surely we are not blind, are we? (John 9:40). And Jesus answers that if they admitted their blindness, they could be healed; but now that you say, We see, your sin remains (John 9:41). The most dangerous blindness is the one that refuses to admit it is blind.

In this Apostles' Fast, let us ask the Lord to show us what we do not see, and let us go to confession. Let us look for those who are left out, as Jesus did. And let us say with our whole heart, and with our knees: Lord, I believe. Amen.

For children

Children, close your eyes for a moment. Now open them. What do you see? The man in today's Gospel could not see anything from the day he was born. Jesus healed him, and then Jesus came to find him again. When the man understood who Jesus was, he said: Lord, I believe! and he knelt down. When you come into the church and see the tabernacle, you can kneel and whisper the same words: Jesus, I believe in you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, light of the world, you went looking for the man whom others had driven out, and you opened the eyes of his heart to know you. Find us when we are pushed aside, and lead us from faith to worship. Take from us the pride that says “We see” and keeps us blind. Bless all the quiet ministers of your gospel, like Tychicus, who carry your word and encourage hearts. Grant us an undying love for you, and peace to our whole community. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The epistle is the quiet ending of a great letter: Paul sends Tychicus to bring news and comfort, and closes with peace, love, faith and grace. The Gospel is the ending of a great sign: the man born blind, now healed and driven out, meets Jesus again and says, “Lord, I believe.” Some who claim to see remain blind. Both readings end in faith and love for the Lord. The key words are encourage, believe and see.

Ephesians 6:21–24 · To encourage your hearts

Setting.

Ephesians presents itself as written by Paul from prison (3:1; 4:1; 6:20). Many scholars think it was written after his death by a disciple who wrote in his name, because its style and some of its ideas differ from the letters that all agree are his; others hold that Paul wrote it himself, perhaps with the help of a secretary. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture in either case. The words “in Ephesus” in 1:1 are missing from some of the oldest manuscripts, and the letter has few personal greetings, so it may have been meant for several churches of the province of Asia.

Our verses are almost word for word the same as Colossians 4:7–8, where Tychicus is also sent. Tychicus came from Asia (Acts 20:4) and was one of those who travelled with Paul on his last journey towards Jerusalem; later letters show Paul sending him on other errands (2 Timothy 4:12; Titus 3:12).

Structure.

  • vv. 21–22: Tychicus is sent to bring news and to encourage.
  • v. 23: the blessing of peace, and love with faith, from the Father and the Son.
  • v. 24: grace to all who love the Lord Jesus Christ.

Key words.

  • diakonos, minister, servant: Tychicus is a “faithful minister in the Lord”. The word does not yet mean the ordained deacon; it describes a trusted servant of the Gospel.
  • parakalesē, encourage: The verb can mean to comfort, to exhort and to strengthen. It is related to the name of the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete (John 14:16).
  • aphtharsia, incorruption: The last word of the letter. The NRSV takes it with love: “an undying love”. It can also be read with grace, as a promise of immortal life. The POC follows that reading: “grace and eternal life”.

Verse by verse.

“Tychicus will tell you everything. He is a dear brother and a faithful minister in the Lord.” (6:21)

The letter has spoken of the vast plan of God to unite all things in Christ (1:10). It ends with a man on the road carrying news. God's great plan moves forward through faithful messengers.

“I am sending him to you for this very purpose, to let you know how we are, and to encourage your hearts.” (6:22)

Paul is in chains, yet his concern is for their hearts, not his own comfort.

“Peace be to the whole community, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.” (6:23)

The Greek says “to the brothers”, which the NRSV renders “the whole community”; the POC keeps “brothers”. Peace, love and faith come from the Father and the Son together.

“Grace be with all who have an undying love for our Lord Jesus Christ.” (6:24)

The letter ends where Christian life begins and ends: love for the Lord Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The greetings of Paul's letters passed into the Church's worship. In the Holy Qurbono the celebrant again and again turns to the people with the blessing of peace, and the people answer, so that the liturgy itself becomes a letter of encouragement from Christ to his Church. Tychicus also reminds us that the Church has always needed faithful messengers: those who carry news, comfort the anxious and bind communities together. Every catechist and every visitor of the sick does the work of Tychicus.

John 9:35–41 · Lord, I believe

Setting.

Chapter 9 tells how Jesus healed a man blind from birth. He made mud, spread it on the man's eyes and sent him to wash in the pool of Siloam, a name John explains as “Sent” (9:7). Then the healed man is questioned by his neighbours, by some of the Pharisees and by his parents, and he is finally driven out (9:34). Throughout the chapter he sees more and more clearly: first “the man called Jesus” (9:11), then “a prophet” (9:17), then one who must be “from God” (9:33). In our verses he comes to full faith.

When John speaks of the man's opponents, he means some of the religious leaders of that time, not the Jewish people as a whole; even the Pharisees were divided about Jesus (9:16). Many scholars think that John's account also reflects later conflicts between the first Christians and the synagogue. The Church firmly rejects any use of such passages to blame the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4).

Structure.

  • v. 35: Jesus seeks out the man who was driven out.
  • vv. 36–38: the dialogue, ending in faith and worship.
  • v. 39: Jesus' saying about judgment: the blind see, the seeing become blind.
  • vv. 40–41: the question of some Pharisees, and Jesus' reply.

Key words.

  • huios tou anthrōpou, Son of Man: The oldest manuscripts read “Son of Man”; later ones have “Son of God”. Jesus uses the title of himself, as the one who comes from heaven and will judge (5:27).
  • prosekynēsen, he worshiped: To bow down before someone. In John this verb is used for the worship of God (4:20–24). The man born blind does not only thank his healer. He worships him.
  • krima, judgment: The coming of the light divides. Jesus did not come to condemn (3:17), but his presence shows what is in each heart.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus heard that they had driven him out, and when he found him, he said, “Do you believe in the Son of Man?”” (9:35)

The man was put out of the community, and Jesus goes looking for him. The Good Shepherd, of whom the next chapter speaks, seeks the sheep that has been cast out.

“He said, “Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him.” (9:38)

“Sir” in verse 36 becomes “Lord” here: the same Greek word, kyrie, now said with full faith. The healing of his eyes is complete when he sees who Jesus is.

“I came into this world for judgment so that those who do not see may see, and those who do see may become blind.” (9:39)

Jesus speaks of spiritual sight. Those who know their blindness can be healed; those who think they see everything close themselves to the light.

“If you were blind, you would not have sin. But now that you say, ‘We see,’ your sin remains.” (9:41)

Not knowing can excuse; refusing to see cannot. The danger is not weakness but the pride that will not admit any need of healing.

How the Church reads it.

From early times the Church read this chapter in the preparation of those about to be baptized. St Justin Martyr already calls Baptism “enlightenment”, because those who receive it are enlightened in mind (CCC 1216). St Augustine saw in the man born blind the whole human race, blind from its first parents, anointed by Christ and washed in the waters of the One who was “Sent” (Tractates on the Gospel of John 44, in substance). The confession “Lord, I believe” is the answer that every baptized person is called to make, again and again.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus mean that the Pharisees were worse sinners than everyone else?

No. Jesus speaks to some Pharisees who were present and who refused to see. The warning is for every believer and every religious person, including Christians: the moment we say “We see” and admit no need of the light, our sin remains. Elsewhere John tells of Pharisees who came to faith, such as Nicodemus (3:1; 19:39).

For study and teaching

  • Paul sent Tychicus “to encourage your hearts”. Whom could I encourage this week by a visit, a call or a message?
  • The man born blind came to faith step by step. Where am I on that road, and what would it mean for me to say today, “Lord, I believe”?
