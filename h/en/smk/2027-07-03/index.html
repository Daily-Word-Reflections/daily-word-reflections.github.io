Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” John 20:28

Reflection

On the evening of Easter Thomas was not with the others. When they told him, “We have seen the Lord,” he said: “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” A week later Jesus came again and said to him: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” Thomas answered: “My Lord and my God!” It is the fullest confession of faith in the Gospels: the risen Jesus is Lord and God.

Jesus answered: “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” That blessing is for us. The faith of India was not built on seeing but on the word of the apostle who saw, touched and believed. According to the ancient tradition of our Church, Thomas came to Kerala around AD 52, preached, founded communities, and gave his life as a martyr at Mylapore around AD 72. We are Thomas Christians, Mar Thoma Nasranikal.

At the Last Supper it was Thomas who asked: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” And Jesus gave the great answer: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” The questioner became the witness; the one who wanted proof travelled the furthest of all the apostles to give it.

Abraham left his country for a land God would show him; Thomas, according to tradition, left Galilee for India. Isaiah sings: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, who brings good news.” Paul writes: “In Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” Thomas is our father in faith. Today we thank God for him and ask for his courage to confess Jesus as Lord and God in our own land.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today we celebrate our father in faith, St Thomas. We often call him “doubting Thomas,” but the Gospel shows more. When Jesus decided to go to Judea, where they wanted to stone him, it was Thomas who said: “Let us also go, that we may die with him.” He was brave and honest.

On Easter evening he was absent. He would not simply accept the others' word: “Unless I see the mark of the nails… I will not believe.” A week later Jesus came and said: “Put your finger here and see my hands… Do not doubt but believe.” And Thomas fell down: “My Lord and my God!” No one in the Gospels says it more clearly: Jesus is not only teacher or prophet; he is Lord and God.

Jesus said: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” That is us. Our ancestors in Kerala did not see Jesus, but they believed the word of Thomas, who had seen and touched. And according to our tradition, Thomas sealed that word with his blood at Mylapore.

So today let us thank God for the faith we received. Let us not be ashamed of it in our schools, offices and on social media. And when doubt comes, do not run away from Jesus; bring it to him, as Thomas did. At this Qurbono, when the Holy Mysteries are raised, say in your heart with Thomas: “My Lord and my God.” Amen.

Lord Jesus, you showed your wounds to Thomas and led him from doubt to faith. Through his prayers keep us faithful to the faith he brought to India; make us witnesses in our land, and let our lives say with him: “My Lord and my God.” Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church remembers St Thomas, the apostle who, according to tradition, brought the gospel to India. The Panchangam lists eight readings. The Evening Gospel (John 14:1–6) holds Thomas's own question: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?”, and the answer, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.” Four Old Testament readings walk the same road. In Genesis 12:1–9 Abram sets out for “the land that I will show you”. Proverbs 4:10–18 says that the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn. In Job 23:1–7 the sufferer longs to find God. In Isaiah 52 a messenger comes over the mountains with good news. The Catholic Epistle (1 Peter 2:15–17) asks believers to live as servants of God and honour everyone.

We study three readings more closely. Isaiah 52 gives the missionary's picture: the feet of the messenger who announces peace, and “all the ends of the earth” seeing the salvation of God. In 1 Corinthians 4 Paul says, “in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel”, the words of an apostle who is also a father in faith. In John 20 the doubter becomes the one who makes the Gospel's fullest confession of faith: “My Lord and my God!” The key words are the way, sent and believe.

Isaiah 52:1–15 · How beautiful are the feet of the messenger

Setting.

These verses come from the second part of the Book of Isaiah (chapters 40–55), addressed to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC. Many scholars attribute these chapters to a prophet of the exile, whom they call Second Isaiah; the Church receives the whole book as the word of God. The tone is comfort: the exile will end, and the Lord himself will bring his people home.

In today's reading Zion, Jerusalem, is told to wake and put on her beautiful clothes (v. 1). The Lord reminds his people that they were sold “for nothing” and will be redeemed (v. 3). Then comes the cry of the herald on the mountains (v. 7), the watchmen who sing (vv. 8–9), and the call to leave Babylon in procession, a new exodus (vv. 11–12). The reading ends with the opening of the Servant's song (vv. 13–15), which continues in chapter 53.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–6: wake, Zion; sold for nothing, redeemed without money; the Lord will make his name known.
  • vv. 7–10: the messenger on the mountains, the watchmen's song, the Lord bares his arm for all the nations to see.
  • vv. 11–12: leave Babylon; the Lord goes before you and guards your rear.
  • vv. 13–15: the opening of the Servant's song: he will be lifted up, though his appearance was marred; nations will see what they had not been told.
  • ¶ The movement is from captivity to release, from news to a procession, and from Israel to all the nations.

Key words.

  • mĕbaśśēr, herald of good news: From the verb meaning to bring news, above all good news. The Greek Old Testament renders it with the verb euangelizomai, which gives us the word “gospel”. Paul quotes this verse of the apostles' preaching in Romans 10:15.
  • mālak ’ĕlōhāyik, your God reigns: The herald's whole message is these words. The Lord is king; the powers that held his people are not. It is the heart of the later preaching of “the kingdom of God”.
  • gā’al, to redeem: A word from the family: the kinsman who buys back a relative who has been sold. God acts as the close relative of Israel and pays the price himself (compare Isaiah 43:1).

Verse by verse.

“You were sold for nothing, and you shall be redeemed without money.” (52:3)

The people sold themselves into exile by their sins, and no price was received. The Lord will buy them back, and he will ask no payment. Redemption is his gift.

“How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace,” (52:7)

The herald runs over the hills with the good news, and the prophet looks at his feet. It is the news that is beautiful, and so the one who carries it. This verse became the apostles' own picture of themselves.

“all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God.” (52:10)

The salvation of Zion is not hers alone. The ends of the earth will see it. In the New Testament this is the world to which the apostles are sent (Acts 1:8).

“the Lord will go before you,” (52:12)

This is the new exodus. At the first exodus the Lord went before his people in a pillar of cloud and fire (Exodus 13:21). The one who sends his messengers also goes ahead of them.

“my servant shall prosper; he shall be exalted and lifted up,” (52:13)

With these words the Servant's song begins. The Church has always seen here Christ, whose lifting up on the cross is his exaltation (John 12:32). Many Jewish readers have understood the Servant as Israel, and the New Testament itself reads the song in more than one way; Christians read it in the light of Jesus.

“that which had not been told them they shall see,” (52:15)

Those who have not heard will see. Paul quotes this verse when he says that he wants to preach Christ where he has not yet been named (Romans 15:21).

How the Church reads it.

Paul quotes verse 7 in Romans 10:15 when he asks, “And how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent?” The Church takes the verse as the picture of every missionary, and her mission to all nations is part of her very being: the Council teaches that the pilgrim Church is missionary by her very nature (Ad Gentes 2; compare CCC 849). In the Servant's song that opens at verse 13, the Catechism sees the divine plan fulfilled in the death of Christ, the righteous Servant whose death is a ransom for the many (CCC 601; Isaiah 53:11). On Dukrono the Church reads this passage for the apostle sent to the ends of the earth.

1 Corinthians 4:15–21 · I became your father through the gospel

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus around AD 54–55 to the Church he had founded in Corinth. The first four chapters deal with divisions: groups were boasting of their favourite teachers, saying “I belong to Paul” or “I belong to Apollos” (1:12). In chapter 4 he answers with irony, setting the proud Corinthians beside the apostles who are “fools for the sake of Christ” (4:8–13).

Today's verses are the end of that appeal, and the tone changes. Paul no longer speaks as a teacher in debate but as a father to his children. He does not wish to shame them; he wants to warn them as his beloved children (4:14).

Structure.

  • v. 15: many guardians, but one father: Paul begot them in Christ through the gospel.
  • vv. 16–17: be imitators of me; Timothy is sent to remind them of his ways.
  • vv. 18–19: some are arrogant, thinking he will not come; he will come and test not their talk but their power.
  • vv. 20–21: the kingdom of God is power, not talk; will he come with a stick or with love and gentleness?

Key words.

  • paidagōgoi, guardians: In a Greek household the paidagogos was a trusted slave who led the child to school and watched over his conduct. He could correct but was not the father. Paul says the Corinthians have had many such guides, but only one father in faith.
  • egennēsa, I became father: Literally “I begot”. Paul uses the strongest image of his work: preaching the gospel is a kind of giving birth to new life in Christ. He says it of Onesimus too (Philemon 10).
  • dynamis, power: Not noise or clever speech but the power of God at work. For Paul the sign of the kingdom is a changed life and works of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:4).

Verse by verse.

“in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” (4:15)

A father gives life. Paul's fatherhood does not rival God's; it is the way the gospel, through his preaching, gave the Corinthians new life in Christ.

“I appeal to you, then, be imitators of me.” (4:16)

This is not pride. Elsewhere Paul explains: “Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ” (11:1). A father in faith shows the way by walking it.

“my beloved and faithful child in the Lord,” (4:17)

Timothy is Paul's own convert and companion. Faith is passed on through persons who love one another, not only through books and doctrines.

“the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” (4:20)

Words are cheap, and a community that boasts of its speakers can easily be empty. God's kingdom shows itself in what he does in people.

“Am I to come to you with a stick, or with love in a spirit of gentleness?” (4:21)

The father offers a choice, and everything in him hopes for the second. A true apostle corrects, but he wants to come in love.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours the apostles as the foundation on which she is built, and Paul's words show what apostolic fatherhood is: to bring people to birth in Christ through the gospel, and to guide them with gentleness. The Catechism teaches that the Lord's own life is the model: in all his life Jesus presents himself as our model, and invites us to follow him (CCC 520, in substance). Paul asks to be imitated only as he imitates Christ. On this feast the Church thanks God for St Thomas as her father in faith, the one through whom, according to tradition, the gospel gave life to the first Christians of India.

John 20:19–29 · My Lord and my God!

Setting.

The scene is the evening of Easter Sunday and then “a week later”, on the eighth day, the next Sunday. The disciples are gathered in a house in Jerusalem with the doors locked “for fear of the Jews”. In John “the Jews” in such places means the authorities in Jerusalem who had handed Jesus over; the disciples themselves were Jews, as was Jesus.

This is the end of John's story of the Resurrection, and in a sense of his whole Gospel: the next two verses (20:30–31) state the purpose of the book, “so that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God”. Thomas has spoken twice before: when he says “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (11:16); when he asks the way (14:5); and now, when he refuses to believe unless he sees.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–23: Jesus appears to the disciples: peace, the showing of the wounds, the sending, the breath of the Spirit, the forgiveness of sins.
  • vv. 24–25: Thomas was not there; he will not believe unless he sees and touches.
  • vv. 26–27: eight days later Jesus comes again and invites Thomas to touch his wounds.
  • vv. 28–29: Thomas's confession; a blessing for all who believe without seeing.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: In Hebrew, shalom: wholeness and the fullness of God's gifts. It is the usual greeting, but on Easter evening it is also the gift of the risen Lord, spoken three times in this passage (vv. 19, 21, 26).
  • enephysēsen, he breathed on: This rare verb is the same one used in the Greek Old Testament for what God did in Genesis 2:7, when he “breathed” the breath of life into the first man. The risen Christ now breathes the Holy Spirit on the apostles, as a new creation.
  • ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, my Lord and my God: Thomas speaks to Jesus with the divine title Lord and the word God. It is the highest confession in the four Gospels, and it comes from the one who had doubted most.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus came and stood among them” (20:19)

The locked door does not stop him. The risen body is real and yet no longer subject to the limits of this world.

“As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” (20:21)

The mission of the apostles is a share in the Son's mission from the Father. The word “apostle” itself means one who is sent.

“he breathed on them and said to them,” (20:22)

The Spirit is given in the Church's first sending out. The next verse links the Spirit with the forgiveness of sins.

“If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them;” (20:23)

Jesus gives the apostles his own power to forgive. This is why the Church has always taught that sins are forgiven through the ministry of those whom Christ sent.

“Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands” (20:25)

Thomas does not ask for more than the others had been given: they had seen the hands and the side (v. 20). He wants to be sure that the risen one is the crucified one.

“Do not doubt but believe.” (20:27)

Literally, “do not become unbelieving, but believing”. Jesus does not reject the question; he answers it with his own wounds and calls Thomas out of doubt.

“My Lord and my God!” (20:28)

John does not say that Thomas touched the wounds; seeing the Lord is enough, and faith breaks out. The Gospel opened with “the Word was God” (1:1); here the disciple who was slowest to believe is the one who says it aloud of Jesus.

“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

This blessing is for every later generation, including ours. It does not belittle Thomas; his witness is the very thing by which we who have not seen come to believe.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the risen Jesus allowed himself to be touched and to be seen with his wounds, and that even before the Risen One the disciples doubted (CCC 644, in substance). It sees the Easter evening as the moment when Christ gave the apostles the power to forgive sins: “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them” (CCC 1485; compare CCC 976). The Church also reads verse 21 as the root of apostolic mission: Jesus sent the apostles as the Father sent him (CCC 858). Thomas's cry has become a prayer of adoration for the whole Church.

Questions people ask

Did St Thomas really come to India?

The Church in India has held this for nearly two thousand years, and it has always been a tradition and not a document that proves it. According to tradition he reached the coast of Kerala in AD 52, founded Christian communities, and was martyred at Mylapore near Chennai about AD 72. The third-century Acts of Thomas tells of his mission to India, though it is legendary in many of its details. Scholars debate how much can be proved. The Church does not need to settle every detail to honour the faith that Indian Christians have kept, and Benedict XVI, in a general audience on 27 September 2006, spoke of Thomas's journey to India as part of the tradition of the Church.

Was it wrong of Thomas to doubt?

Thomas did not believe what the others told him, and Jesus calls him to believe. But Jesus does not shame him. He comes back after eight days, shows his wounds, and Thomas answers with the greatest confession of faith in the Gospels. His honest question is also what leads to our knowing that the risen Christ is the same one who was crucified. Doubt that is brought to Jesus can become faith; doubt that stays away from him cannot.

Why does the risen Jesus still have the marks of the nails?

The Church teaches that the risen body is the same body that was crucified, now glorified, and that the marks of the Passion remain (CCC 645, in substance). They are the sign that the cross was not undone but transformed: Christ keeps his wounds as the mark of his love, and they are the means by which Thomas recognises him.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam calls today the Dukrono of St Thomas the Apostle, Father in Faith of the Church in India, and gives it a Holy Qurbono for the people. “Dukrono” is the Syriac word for remembrance. According to tradition Thomas landed at Muziris, near Kodungallur, in AD 52, founded communities along the Kerala coast, and was martyred at Mylapore. The Malankara faithful, whose Church traces its beginnings to his preaching, keep this day as a feast of their whole heritage. The Syriac liturgy honours him as Mar Thoma Shleeha, Apostle. Today is also the name day of Most Rev. Joseph Mar Thomas, whom we remember in prayer.

For study and teaching

  • Thomas asked, “How can we know the way?” and Jesus answered, “I am the way.” Which question do I most need to bring to Jesus today?
  • The messenger's feet are called “beautiful” on the mountains. Who brought the good news of Christ to me, and how can I thank them?
  • Paul calls the Corinthians his children in faith. Who has been a father or mother in faith to me, and to whom am I one?
  • Thomas moved from doubt to adoration in a single sentence. What would it mean for me to say “My Lord and my God” from the heart at the Holy Qurbono this week?
