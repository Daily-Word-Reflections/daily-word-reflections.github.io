Word for the day

“While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” Luke 24:51

Reflection

Forty days after his Resurrection, Jesus leads his disciples out to Bethany, “and, lifting up his hands, he blessed them. While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” The last earthly sight of Jesus is of hands raised in blessing. He goes, but he does not stop blessing his Church.

The Ascension is not Jesus leaving us; it is our humanity entering heaven. The Son of God, who took our flesh, now takes it to the right hand of the Father. St Mark says: “he was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” Where the Head has gone, the body hopes to follow. Our true home is with him.

The angels ask the disciples: “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven? This Jesus… will come in the same way as you saw him go.” The Ascension is not a time for gazing at the sky but for mission: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation… And they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them.”

As Elijah was taken up and Elisha received his mantle and spirit, so Christ ascends and pours out his Spirit on his Church. Jeremiah's promise will be fulfilled at Pentecost: “I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts.” Like the disciples, who “returned to Jerusalem with great joy and were continually in the temple blessing God”, we now enter ten days of prayer for the Spirit.

Homily outline

Theme: “He was carried up into heaven, blessing them.”

Forty days after his Resurrection the Lord ascends. It is not a farewell of absence but of glory: our human nature, in Christ, enters heaven, and the Church is sent to the ends of the earth.

1. He ascends while blessing

  • Elijah was taken up in a whirlwind; Elisha received his spirit.
  • Jesus lifts up his hands and blesses the disciples; while blessing them he is carried up.
  • His last gesture on earth is blessing: his hands remain raised over the Church.
“As they continued walking and talking, a chariot of fire and horses of fire separated the two of them, and Elijah ascended in a whirlwind into heaven.” 2 Kings 2:11
“Then he led them out as far as Bethany, and, lifting up his hands, he blessed them. While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” Luke 24:50–51

Apply: Receive the final blessing at every Qurbono as Christ's own blessing.

2. Seated at the right hand of the Father

  • The Psalm foretold: “Sit at my right hand.”
  • Christ, true God and true man, takes our humanity into the glory of the Father.
  • There he intercedes for us as our High Priest; where the Head has gone, the members hope to follow (CCC 661–667).
“The LORD says to my lord, “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies your footstool.”” Psalm 110:1
“So then the Lord Jesus, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” Mark 16:19

Apply: Lift your heart to heaven each day: “Lord, draw me to where you are.”

3. Why stand looking up? Be witnesses

  • The prophets saw that the spirit of Elijah rested on Elisha.
  • The angels ask: “Why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” He will come again.
  • Meanwhile, with the power of the Spirit, we are witnesses to the ends of the earth.
“When the company of prophets who were at Jericho saw him at a distance, they declared, “The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.”” 2 Kings 2:15
“But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.” Acts 1:8

Apply: Begin the novena to the Holy Spirit with Mary, from tomorrow until Pentecost.

The Ascension is the feast of our hope: our humanity is in heaven with Christ.

Let us return like the apostles “with great joy”, praying with Mary for the Spirit.

Take-home resolution: Pray the novena to the Holy Spirit from the Ascension to Pentecost.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, forty days after Easter Jesus leads his disciples out to Bethany. He lifts up his hands and blesses them, and while he is blessing them he is carried up into heaven. The last picture the disciples have of Jesus is of hands raised in blessing. He has never lowered them.

The Ascension does not mean that Jesus has gone far away. It means that our human nature, which he took from Mary, now sits at the right hand of the Father. Where he, our Head, has gone, we, his body, hope to follow. Heaven is our home.

But the angels say: “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” The Ascension is not a time for gazing at the sky; it is a time for mission. Jesus said: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news.” And they went out and preached everywhere, and the Lord worked with them.

Elijah was taken up, and Elisha received his mantle and his spirit. Jesus ascends, and he will send his Spirit upon us at Pentecost. Like the apostles with Mary, let us spend these ten days in prayer: Come, Holy Spirit. And at every Qurbono, when we hear “Lift up your hearts,” let us truly lift them up to him. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, on this feast of Suloko Saint Paul helps us to see the Ascension in a new way. He quotes an old psalm of victory: when he ascended on high he made captivity itself a captive; he gave gifts to his people (Ephesians 4:8; Psalm 68:18). The picture is of a king returning from battle, leading his defeated enemies behind him and sharing the spoils with his people.

Christ has fought the great battle. On the Cross and in the Resurrection he defeated sin and death. Today he goes up to the Father as victor. But he does not keep the victory for himself. He gives gifts. Paul names them: the gifts he gave were that some would be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, some pastors and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ (Ephesians 4:11–12).

In the book of Exodus, when the tabernacle was to be built, God said of the craftsman Bezalel: I have filled him with divine spirit, with ability, intelligence, and knowledge in every kind of craft (Exodus 31:3). God gave skills so that his dwelling could be built. So too the ascended Christ gives gifts so that his Church may be built: bishops and priests, catechists and singers, parents and teachers, those who care for the sick and those who pray in hidden ways.

What is the purpose of all these gifts? That we may grow. Paul says we must no longer be children, tossed to and fro and blown about by every wind of doctrine, but speaking the truth in love, we must grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ (Ephesians 4:14–15). Hosea shows God teaching Israel to walk, holding them by the arms (Hosea 11:3). The ascended Lord still holds us and teaches us to walk.

In these ten days before Pentecost, let us ask: what gift has the Lord given me, and am I using it for his body? Let us offer it to our parish. Let us learn our faith more deeply, so that we are not tossed about. And at every Holy Qurbono let us lift up our hearts to Christ our Head, who has gone up to give gifts to his people. Amen.

The ascended Christ is our High Priest in heaven, interceding for us always.

From there he sends the Spirit to write his law on our hearts.

Take-home resolution: In these days before Pentecost I will pray each day for those I love, trusting that Christ intercedes for them, and ask the Spirit to write his law in my heart.

Dear friends, the Ascension can sound like a farewell. But the Letter to the Hebrews shows us that it is an entrance. Once a year the high priest of Israel passed through the curtain into the Holy of Holies with the blood of the offering (Leviticus 16:15). Today Christ, our High Priest, enters a greater sanctuary: Christ did not enter a sanctuary made by human hands, a mere copy of the true one, but he entered into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf (Hebrews 9:24).

On our behalf. That is the heart of today's feast. Our Lord takes our human nature, the body he received from Mary, the body that bore the wounds of the Cross, into the presence of the Father. Where our Head has gone, we his members hope to follow.

And what does he do there? When Aaron went into the holy place, he carried on his breast the names of the twelve tribes: so Aaron shall bear the names of the sons of Israel on his heart (Exodus 28:29). Christ carries our names on his heart. He always lives to make intercession for them (Hebrews 7:25). When we pray, we never pray alone. The ascended Lord is already praying for us.

One of today's readings tells how Moses placed the tablets of the law in the ark (Deuteronomy 10:5). Another gives God's promise through Jeremiah: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). From heaven the ascended Christ will send his Spirit to do exactly this. Saint Paul says that we are a letter written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts (2 Corinthians 3:3).

So in these ten days of waiting, let us pray each day for those we love, trusting that Christ carries their names before the Father. Let us ask: Holy Spirit, write God's law on my heart. And at every Holy Qurbono, when we are called to lift up our minds and hearts on high, let us lift them to where our High Priest is. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Suloko, the feast of the Ascension, gathers many readings around one movement: the Lord goes up, and his gifts come down. In the evening Gospel (Mark 16:14–20) the risen Lord says, “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” Deuteronomy 10:1–5 tells how Moses placed the new tablets of the covenant in the ark, and Jeremiah 31:31–34 promises a new covenant: “I will write it on their hearts.” In Isaiah 49:1–7 the servant is given “as a light to the nations”. In Acts 1:6–11 a cloud takes Jesus from the apostles' sight, and the angels say that “this Jesus” will come again “in the same way as you saw him go into heaven.”

We study three readings more closely. In 2 Kings 2 Elijah is taken up in a whirlwind, and Elisha, who sees him go, receives a double share of his spirit. In Ephesians 4 the Christ who ascended “far above all the heavens” gives gifts to his Church so that it may grow into his full stature. Luke ends his Gospel with Jesus lifting his hands in blessing and being carried up into heaven, while the disciples return to Jerusalem with great joy. The key words are taken up, gifts and blessing.

2 Kings 2:9–15 · Elijah taken up; Elisha takes his mantle

Setting.

Elijah, the great prophet of the ninth century BC, had stood alone for the Lord against the worship of Baal (1 Kings 18). His work was coming to an end, and he had been told to anoint Elisha as prophet in his place (1 Kings 19:16). Elisha had followed him ever since. At the start of chapter 2 we learn that “the Lord was about to take Elijah up to heaven by a whirlwind” (2:1), and Elisha refuses three times to leave his master's side.

Just before our verses Elijah strikes the Jordan with his mantle, the waters part, and the two cross on dry ground (2:8), as Israel once crossed the Jordan under Joshua (Joshua 3).

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: “Ask what I may do for you”: Elisha asks for a double share of Elijah's spirit, on condition that he sees him taken.
  • vv. 11–12: the chariot of fire; Elijah goes up in a whirlwind; Elisha cries out and tears his clothes.
  • vv. 13–14: Elisha takes up the mantle and parts the Jordan.
  • v. 15: the prophets of Jericho: “The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.”

Key words.

  • pî-shᵉnayim, a double share: The portion of the firstborn son, who received twice what the other sons received (Deuteronomy 21:17). Elisha does not ask to be greater than Elijah. He asks to be his heir, the son who carries on his father's work.
  • ’adderet, mantle: The prophet's cloak, the sign of his office. Elijah had thrown it over Elisha when he first called him from the plough (1 Kings 19:19). Now it falls to him for good.
  • sᵉ‘ārâ, whirlwind: A storm wind, the kind in which God speaks and acts (Job 38:1). Elijah does not die in the ordinary way; God takes him.

Verse by verse.

“Please let me inherit a double share of your spirit.” (2:9)

Elisha could have asked for anything. He asks for the one gift he needs to continue the mission. It is a model of prayer.

“if you see me as I am being taken from you, it will be granted you; if not, it will not.” (2:10)

Seeing is the condition. The disciples too must be witnesses of the Lord's going up, “as they were watching” (Acts 1:9), before they receive the Spirit.

“Father, father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!” (2:12)

Elisha calls Elijah his father and Israel's true defence, worth more than an army. The same words will be spoken over Elisha at his own death (13:14).

“The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.” (2:15)

The master is gone, but his spirit remains in the disciple. The mission does not end when the prophet is taken up.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers saw in Elijah's ascent a figure of Christ's Ascension, and also noted the difference. St Gregory the Great says that Elijah was taken up in a chariot, as one who needed help, while the Redeemer ascended by his own power (in substance). Elijah leaves his mantle and a double share of his spirit; Christ sends the Holy Spirit himself. Jesus tells his disciples to stay in the city “until you have been clothed with power from on high” (Luke 24:49), and in Acts 1:8 he promises, “you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you.” The Church is Elisha standing on the bank of the Jordan, holding the mantle and asking, “Where is the Lord?”, and finding that he is still at work.

Ephesians 4:7–16 · He ascended and gave gifts

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter about the Church as the body of Christ. It speaks in Paul's name, from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think it was written by a disciple of Paul after his death, while others hold that Paul wrote it; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

Chapter 4 begins with a call to unity: “one body and one Spirit… one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (4:4–5). Today's verses show that unity is not sameness. The one Lord gives different gifts to each, for the building up of the one body.

Structure.

  • v. 7: grace is given to each according to the measure of Christ's gift.
  • vv. 8–10: a psalm explained: he ascended, and he who ascended first descended.
  • vv. 11–12: the gifts: apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers, to equip the saints.
  • vv. 13–16: growing up into Christ the head, speaking the truth in love.

Key words.

  • anabas eis hypsos, when he ascended on high: From Psalm 68:18, where God goes up to his holy mountain in triumph. The Hebrew says that he received gifts; Ephesians reads that he gave gifts, a reading also found in an ancient Aramaic paraphrase of the psalm. The risen Christ does not take tribute; he gives.
  • katartismos, to equip: A word used for setting a broken bone or mending nets (Mark 1:19). The ministers of the Church exist to make all the faithful fit for their own service.
  • alētheuontes en agapē, speaking the truth in love: Literally “truthing in love”: living and speaking the truth. Truth without love wounds; love without truth deceives. The body grows only when both are held together.

Verse by verse.

“But each of us was given grace according to the measure of Christ’s gift.” (4:7)

No one is left out. Every baptized person has received a gift for the good of the whole body.

“He who descended is the same one who ascended far above all the heavens, so that he might fill all things.” (4:10)

The Ascension is not Christ's absence from the world. He goes up in order to fill all things with his presence and his power.

“to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ,” (4:12)

“The saints” are all the baptized. The pastors equip; all the faithful do the work of ministry, each in his or her place.

“But speaking the truth in love, we must grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ,” (4:15)

The goal is maturity: not children tossed about by every wind of teaching, but grown men and women who reflect Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Ephesians 4:9–10 when it teaches that Christ descended into the realm of the dead before he ascended (CCC 631). Some interpreters read “the lower parts of the earth” as the Incarnation, his coming down to earth; others as his descent among the dead. Both readings agree that the one who went up is the one who first came all the way down. The Catechism also teaches that as Lord, Christ is head of the Church, which is his body, and that, taken up into heaven and glorified, he dwells on earth in his Church (CCC 668–669). The Second Vatican Council uses these verses to teach that the variety of ministries in the Church serves the unity of the one body (Lumen Gentium 7).

Luke 24:50–53 · While he was blessing them, he was carried up into heaven

Setting.

Luke tells the Ascension twice: here, at the end of his Gospel, and again at the start of Acts (Acts 1:6–11). Here it closes the long day of Easter that began at the empty tomb (24:1). Jesus has opened the Scriptures to the disciples and promised them “power from on high” (24:49). Now he leads them out to Bethany, on the Mount of Olives, east of Jerusalem.

Structure.

  • v. 50: he leads them to Bethany and lifts his hands in blessing.
  • v. 51: while blessing them, he withdraws and is carried up into heaven.
  • vv. 52–53: they worship him, return with great joy, and bless God in the Temple.

Key words.

  • eulogeō, bless: The verb comes three times in these four verses: Jesus blesses the disciples twice (vv. 50–51), and the disciples bless God (v. 53). The Gospel ends in an exchange of blessing between heaven and earth.
  • anephereto, was carried up: A passive verb: he was carried up, a way of saying that God the Father took him. The same verb is used in the Greek Old Testament for offerings carried up on the altar.

Verse by verse.

“Then he led them out as far as Bethany, and, lifting up his hands, he blessed them.” (24:50)

Luke's Gospel began with a priest, Zechariah, who came out of the sanctuary and could not speak the blessing (1:22). It ends with Jesus lifting his hands like the high priest Simon in Sirach 50:20, and blessing his people. The great High Priest completes what the old priesthood began.

“While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” (24:51)

He leaves while blessing. The last sight the disciples have of him is of hands raised over them. A few ancient manuscripts lack the words “and was carried up into heaven”, and also “they worshiped him” in v. 52; the NRSV, with most modern editions, keeps them, and they agree with Acts 1:9–11.

“And they worshiped him, and returned to Jerusalem with great joy;” (24:52)

This is the first time in Luke's Gospel that the disciples worship Jesus. The angels' message at his birth was “good news of great joy” (2:10). The Gospel begins and ends in joy.

“and they were continually in the temple blessing God.” (24:53)

Luke's Gospel began in the Temple (1:9) and ends there. The disciples wait in prayer, as Jesus told them, for the gift of the Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ's Ascension marks the definitive entrance of Jesus' humanity into God's heavenly domain, from where he will come again (CCC 665). Jesus Christ, the head of the Church, goes before us into the Father's glorious kingdom so that we, the members of his body, may live in the hope of one day being with him for ever (CCC 666). There, as the one priest of the new and eternal covenant, he intercedes for us without ceasing (CCC 662; Hebrews 7:25). The Ascension is not a sad parting. The disciples return “with great joy”, because the one who is gone from their sight is now with them in a new way, and their own humanity has gone ahead of them into heaven.

Questions people ask

Where did Jesus go? Is heaven up above the clouds?

Heaven is not a place in the sky, reached by travelling upward. The cloud in Acts 1:9 is the sign of God's presence, as it was at Sinai and at the Transfiguration. The Ascension means that the risen humanity of Jesus has entered fully into the glory of God, seated at the right hand of the Father (CCC 659–664). Scripture speaks of “going up” because that is how human beings naturally speak of what is above us in dignity.

If Jesus has gone up into heaven, how is he still with us?

He promised, “I am with you always, to the end of the age” (Matthew 28:20). He is present in his Church, in his word, in the poor, in prayer, and above all in the Holy Qurbono, where he is present truly, really and substantially (CCC 1373–1374). His Ascension is the beginning of a new nearness, through the Holy Spirit he sends.

Was Elijah's going up the same as Christ's Ascension?

No. Elijah was taken up by God, and Scripture does not say that he sits at God's right hand or that he sends the Spirit. Christ ascends as Lord and Son, in the humanity he took from the Virgin Mary, and he opens the way to heaven for all who are united to him (CCC 661). Elijah's story points ahead; Christ's fulfils it.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Suloko is the Syriac word for “going up”. Our Church keeps the feast on Thursday, as the ancient Churches of both East and West have long done; our Latin neighbours in India keep it this year on the Sunday, 9 May. In the Syriac tradition we pray facing the east, the direction of the rising sun, waiting for the Lord who, the angels said, “will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven” (Acts 1:11). The days from Suloko to Pentecost are days of prayer with Mary and the apostles in the upper room (Acts 1:14), asking for the Spirit, as Elisha asked for a double share.
