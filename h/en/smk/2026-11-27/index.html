Word for the day

“Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate.” Mark 10:9

Reflection

Some Thessalonians, expecting the Lord's return at any moment, had stopped working and were living as busybodies. Paul reminds them that he and his companions worked “night and day” so as not to burden anyone, and gives a blunt rule: anyone unwilling to work should not eat. Then a word for all who are tired of doing good: “Brothers and sisters, do not be weary in doing what is right.” Faith makes us responsible, not idle.

When the Pharisees ask about divorce, Jesus takes them back beyond Moses' concession, which was given “because of your hardness of heart”, to the beginning of creation: God made them male and female, and the two become one flesh. “Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate.” Christian marriage is a covenant God himself seals; in the Syriac tradition the crowning of bride and groom shows that their union is holy and royal.

Full homily

Dear friends, some Thessalonians, expecting the Lord's return at any moment, had stopped working and were living as busybodies. Paul reminds them that he and his companions worked night and day so as not to burden anyone: anyone unwilling to work should not eat. Brothers and sisters, do not be weary in doing what is right (2 Thessalonians 3:8–13).

In the Gospel, when the Pharisees ask about divorce, Jesus goes back to the beginning: from the beginning of creation God made them male and female; the two shall become one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate (Mark 10:6–9).

Faithful work and faithful love: both are good preparation for the Lord's coming. Today do your work with joy; let husbands and wives say a word of thanks to each other; and let us pray for couples who are struggling in their marriage. Amen.

For children

Children, helping with small jobs at home is part of loving Jesus. Help your mother or father with one job today!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you blessed marriage from the beginning. Strengthen husbands and wives in faithful love, soften hard hearts, and keep us from growing weary in doing what is right. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about faithfulness in ordinary life. Paul tells the Thessalonians to work quietly and not grow weary in doing good. Jesus, asked about divorce, leads his questioners back “to the beginning of creation”, where God made man and woman to become one flesh. Daily work and faithful marriage are two places where the Kingdom is lived, quietly and over many years.

2 Thessalonians 3:6–15 · Do not be weary in doing what is right

Setting.

In Thessalonica some believers had stopped working. The letter does not say exactly why, but chapter 2 shows that some were unsettled by claims that “the day of the Lord is already here” (2:2). Others perhaps simply preferred to live on the generosity of the community. Paul answers with his own example and a firm rule.

Structure.

  • v. 6: the command: keep away from believers who live in idleness.
  • vv. 7–9: Paul's example: he worked night and day so as not to be a burden.
  • v. 10: the rule: anyone unwilling to work should not eat.
  • vv. 11–12: the problem named, and the remedy: work quietly and earn your own living.
  • vv. 13–15: do not grow weary in doing good; correct the disobedient as brothers, not enemies.

Key words.

  • ataktōs, in idleness, disorderly: A military word for a soldier out of rank. The idle are not only lazy; they have stepped out of the ordered life of the community.
  • periergazomenous, busybodies: A play on words in Greek: not ergazomenous (working) but periergazomenous (working around, meddling). Those who do no work of their own easily busy themselves with others' affairs.
  • paradosis, tradition: What Paul handed on, by word and example (v. 6; compare 2:15). Christian tradition includes a way of living, not only doctrines.

Verse by verse.

“and we did not eat anyone’s bread without paying for it; but with toil and labor we worked night and day, so that we might not burden any of you.” (3:8)

Paul was a tentmaker (Acts 18:3). He had the right to be supported (v. 9), but he gave it up so that the Gospel would cost his hearers nothing.

“For even when we were with you, we gave you this command: Anyone unwilling to work should not eat.” (3:10)

Note the word “unwilling”. The rule is about those who refuse to work, not those who cannot. The Church has always cared for the sick, the old and the unemployed.

“Do not regard them as enemies, but warn them as believers.” (3:15)

Even correction is an act of love. The goal is to win back a brother or sister, not to drive them away.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes 3:10 in its teaching on human work: work is a duty, and through it a person cooperates with the Creator and shares in Christ's redemptive work (CCC 2427). The Didache, an early Christian manual, gives the same rule for a travelling believer who wants to settle in a community: if he has a trade, let him work for his bread; Christ is not to be made a business (in substance, Didache 12).

Mark 10:1–12 · What God has joined together

Setting.

Jesus has left Galilee and is on the road to Jerusalem, in Judea and beyond the Jordan. The land beyond the Jordan (Perea) was ruled by Herod Antipas, who had imprisoned John the Baptist for criticising his marriage (Mark 6:17–18). The question about divorce is a test. Deuteronomy 24:1–4 allowed a husband to give a certificate of dismissal, and the rabbinic schools debated how serious the reason had to be.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the crowds; the Pharisees' testing question.
  • vv. 3–5: Jesus' counter-question about Moses, and his explanation: “because of your hardness of heart”.
  • vv. 6–9: back to the beginning: Genesis 1:27 and 2:24; “what God has joined together, let no one separate”.
  • vv. 10–12: in the house, the teaching for the disciples: remarriage after divorce is adultery, for both husband and wife.

Key words.

  • sklērokardia, hardness of heart: A word from the Greek Old Testament for a heart closed to God (compare Deuteronomy 10:16). The Law of Moses limited the damage of sin; it did not express God's original plan.
  • mia sarx, one flesh: Not only physical union but one life, one shared existence, a new family.
  • synezeuxen, has joined together: Literally “yoked together”, like two oxen under one yoke. God himself is the one who joins.

Verse by verse.

“But Jesus said to them, “Because of your hardness of heart he wrote this commandment for you.” (10:5)
“But from the beginning of creation, ‘God made them male and female.’” (10:6)

Jesus answers a legal question by going behind the Law to creation. The question is not “what is permitted?” but “what did God intend?”

“Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate.”” (10:9)

“No one”: not the husband, not the court, not the state. The bond is God's work.

“and if she divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery.”” (10:12)

Only Mark has this verse. Jewish law generally did not allow a wife to divorce, but Roman law did, and Mark was probably writing for Christians who lived under Roman law. Jesus' teaching applies equally to both.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that Jesus restored the original meaning of marriage, and that the permission given by Moses was a concession to hardness of heart (CCC 1614). A valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power (CCC 1640, 2382). At the same time, separation may be permitted in certain cases while the bond remains (CCC 2383), and the Church is called to accompany with care those whose marriages have broken.

Questions people ask

Does “anyone unwilling to work should not eat” mean the poor should not be helped?

No. It is about those who refuse to work while able to, and who live off others. Care for the poor, the sick and the unemployed is a constant command of Scripture and of the Church (CCC 2443–2447).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “Do not be weary in doing what is right.” In which good work am I most tempted to give up?
  • Jesus takes his hearers back to “the beginning”. How can families in our parish be helped to live God's original plan for marriage?
