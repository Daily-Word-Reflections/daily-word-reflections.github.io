Word for the day

“Let us hold fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who has promised is faithful.” Hebrews 10:23

Reflection

Christ is risen! Yesterday we walked with the two disciples to Emmaus and saw them run back to Jerusalem to find the others. Today the Letter to the Hebrews tells us what the risen Lord has opened for us: “we have confidence to enter the sanctuary by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain (that is, through his flesh).” In the temple at Jerusalem a curtain separated the Holy of Holies, and only the high priest passed through it, once a year. When Jesus died, “the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom” (Matthew 27:51). His own flesh, given on the Cross and raised on the third day, has become the doorway into the presence of God. In our churches the curtain of the sanctuary is drawn back as the Holy Qurbono begins, and the faithful see the altar opened before them. We may see in it a sign of what Christ has done: heaven is no longer closed. So the Letter invites us: “let us approach with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water,” words that recall our Baptism.

Then come three simple commands. “Let us hold fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who has promised is faithful.” “Let us consider how to provoke one another to love and good deeds.” And: do not neglect “to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another.” Already in the first generation some Christians were drifting away from the Sunday assembly. The Gospel shows the heart of God for those who drift. Tax collectors and sinners are “coming near to listen” to Jesus, and some Pharisees and scribes grumble: “This fellow welcomes sinners and eats with them.” They were devout people, and their grumbling is a temptation that every religious person knows. Jesus answers with a story: a shepherd with a hundred sheep loses one, leaves the ninety-nine, goes “after the one that is lost until he finds it,” lays it on his shoulders, and calls his neighbours to rejoice. St Ambrose saw in the shepherd's shoulders the arms of the Cross, on which Christ carried our sins home (in substance).

Put the two readings together and a task appears. The new and living way is open; the shepherd is searching; and we are asked not to neglect meeting together, but to encourage one another. Who is missing from our Sunday Qurbono? Perhaps a young person who moved to a city for studies, a family hurt by some quarrel in the parish, an elderly person no longer able to walk to church, a neighbour who simply lost the habit. This week, be the shepherd's hands. Call one of them, visit, offer a ride, carry the Holy Qurbono's joy to those who cannot come by asking the priest to bring Holy Communion to the sick. And look into your own heart: if you are the one who has wandered, the Lord is not waiting at a distance to scold you. He is already on the road looking for you, and there is joy in heaven when you are found.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews says that Jesus opened for us a new and living way through the curtain, that is, through his flesh (Hebrews 10:20). At his death the curtain of the temple was torn. Heaven is open.

So we are told: let us hold fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who has promised is faithful (Hebrews 10:23). Let us encourage one another, and not neglect to meet together, as is the habit of some.

In the Gospel the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine and goes after the one that is lost until he finds it. He does not scold; he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices. St Ambrose saw in those shoulders the arms of the Cross (in substance).

Yesterday we heard how the Emmaus disciples ran back to the community. Today let us look for those who have not come back. Call one of them; visit; bring them with you. And if you are the lost one, know that the Shepherd is already on the road looking for you. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever lost something you loved and searched everywhere for it? Jesus told a story about a shepherd who lost one sheep. He searched and searched until he found it, then carried it home on his shoulders and had a party! Jesus loves every one of us like that. If a friend stops coming to Sunday school, can you tell them: we miss you?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen Shepherd, you opened for us a new and living way through the curtain of your flesh, and you go after every sheep that is lost until you find it. Give us a true heart and full assurance of faith. Keep us faithful to the confession of our hope, and make us eager to meet together, to provoke one another to love and good deeds, and to go out to those who have wandered. Carry home on your shoulders all who are lost, and fill heaven with joy. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the week after the Emmaus Sunday the Church reads on in the Letter to the Hebrews. Monday opens its great call to come near: by the blood of Jesus the way into God's presence stands open, so let us approach, hold fast and keep meeting together. Luke tells the same truth as a story. A shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to go after one sheep and carries it home rejoicing. One reading shows the door that has been opened, the other the shepherd who goes looking for those still outside it.

Hebrews 10:19–25 · A new and living way

Setting.

Hebrews does not name its author. The Church has always received it with the letters of St Paul, though since the early centuries many have thought that another hand, perhaps one of Paul's companions, wrote it. It is addressed to Christians who were growing tired and were tempted to drift away. Chapters 7 to 10 have shown at length that Christ is the high priest who entered the true sanctuary once for all with his own blood (9:11–12). Today's passage turns that teaching into encouragement. It begins with “Therefore”: everything argued so far now becomes a reason to act.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–21: what we have: confidence to enter, a new and living way, a great priest.
  • v. 22: let us approach.
  • v. 23: let us hold fast.
  • vv. 24–25: let us consider one another, and keep meeting together.
  • ¶ The three calls follow the order of faith (v. 22), hope (v. 23) and love (v. 24).

Key words.

  • parrēsia, confidence: Openness, boldness. In a Greek city it was the right of a citizen to speak openly in the assembly. Here it is the right of children to enter their Father's house without fear. The word returns in 10:35.
  • katapetasma, curtain: The veil that closed off the Holy of Holies, which only the high priest entered, once a year on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:7). The writer calls Christ's flesh the curtain: his humanity, given up on the Cross, is the way through. The Gospels record that at his death the curtain of the temple was torn in two (Mark 15:38).
  • episynagōgē, meeting together: Assembling, gathering; the word is related to “synagogue”. The writer means the gathering of Christians for worship, which some among them had already stopped attending.

Verse by verse.

“by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain (that is, through his flesh),” (10:20)

The way is “new”, freshly made, and “living”, because it is not a road of stones but a person. Jesus said of himself, “I am the way” (John 14:6).

“let us approach with a true heart in full assurance of faith,” (10:22)

The rest of the verse speaks of hearts sprinkled clean and bodies washed with pure water. The sprinkling recalls the consecration of priests (Exodus 29:21), and the washing most likely recalls Baptism. Those who have been washed may come near.

“Let us hold fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who has promised is faithful.” (10:23)

Our steadiness rests on God's faithfulness, not on our feelings.

“not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day approaching.” (10:25)

“The Day” is the day of the Lord's coming. The nearer it draws, the more the assembly matters, not the less.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it explains why Christians gather on Sunday: the practice of the Christian assembly goes back to the apostolic age, and Hebrews reminds the faithful not to neglect to meet together (CCC 2178). The Church sees in the opened curtain the meaning of her worship. In the Holy Qurbono the faithful do not stand outside; they are led by their high priest into the presence of the Father, and they receive the Body and Blood through which the way was opened.

Luke 15:1–7 · Until he finds it

Setting.

Luke 15 holds three parables of things lost and found: a sheep, a coin and a son. They are told in answer to a complaint. Tax collectors, who gathered taxes for Rome or for local rulers and were widely thought dishonest, and others known as sinners, came to listen to Jesus, and he shared their table. The Pharisees and scribes were serious teachers of the Law, and to them sharing a meal meant approval. Jesus does not deny that these people are lost. He says that God goes looking for them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: who comes near, and who grumbles.
  • vv. 3–6: the parable: the search, the shoulders, the celebration.
  • v. 7: the meaning: joy in heaven over one who repents.

Key words.

  • diegongyzon, were grumbling: The word the Greek Bible uses for Israel's muttering in the wilderness (Exodus 16:2). Grumbling against God's generosity is an old temptation.
  • prosdechetai, welcomes: To receive someone as a guest. The complaint is true, and it is the heart of the Gospel.
  • chara, joy: Joy returns at the end of each of the three parables (15:7, 10, 32). The chapter is about the joy of God.

Verse by verse.

“go after the one that is lost until he finds it?” (15:4)

“Until he finds it.” The shepherd does not search for a while and then give up. Ezekiel had promised that God himself would seek out the lost (Ezekiel 34:11–16).

“When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices.” (15:5)

There is no scolding. St Gregory the Great says that the shepherd laid the sheep on his shoulders because Christ, taking up our human nature, carried our sins himself (Homilies on the Gospels 34, in substance). Some of the earliest Christian paintings, in the Roman catacombs, show this very picture.

“there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance.” (15:7)

“Heaven” is a reverent way of saying God. Many read the words about the ninety-nine as gentle irony, since all need repentance. The point is how much one returning person matters to God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that Jesus invites sinners to the table of the Kingdom (CCC 545), and that by receiving them at his table he showed that forgiveness brings a person back into the community of God's people (CCC 1443). The Church reads the parable as the pattern of the Sacrament of Reconciliation and of all pastoral care: the shepherd's work is to go out, and the welcome home is a feast, not a trial.

Questions people ask

Does God care less for the ninety-nine?

No. The shepherd leaves them in the wilderness because he is sure of them, and he will bring the found sheep back to the same flock. The parable does not measure God's love; it shows its direction. Matthew's form of it ends: it is not the will of the Father that one of these little ones should be lost (Matthew 18:14).

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews says that some had made a habit of staying away from the assembly. Who from my parish or family has stopped coming, and what could I do this week to encourage them?
  • Do I grumble, even quietly, when God's mercy reaches someone I think does not deserve it?
