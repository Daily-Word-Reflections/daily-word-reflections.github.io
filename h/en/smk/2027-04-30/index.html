Word for the day

“For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” Mark 10:45

Reflection

Today our Church keeps the feast of Mar Yaqob Shleeha, St James the son of Zebedee, the brother of St John. (He is not to be confused with the other apostle James, the son of Alphaeus.) The Byzantine Churches also honour him on this day; in the Latin Church his feast falls in July. James was a fisherman on the Sea of Galilee who left his boat, his nets and his father to follow Jesus. With Peter and John he formed the inner circle of the Twelve: he saw the daughter of Jairus raised, he saw the Lord transfigured on the mountain, and he was taken close to him in the agony of Gethsemane. Jesus gave the two brothers a nickname, “Boanerges, that is, Sons of Thunder,” perhaps for their fiery temper. And he was the first of the apostles to die for Christ: the Acts of the Apostles records, in a single line, that King Herod “had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword.” Our Church calls him Shleeha, the Syriac word for apostle, one who is sent.

The Gospel shows James before he understood. He and John come to Jesus with a bold request: “Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we ask of you... Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory.” They are thinking of thrones. Jesus answers gently: “You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, or be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with?” They say at once, “We are able.” And Jesus says, “The cup that I drink you will drink.” St John Chrysostom notes that the Lord did not scold them, but led them gently from the thought of honour to the thought of the cup, from the throne to the share in his sufferings (Homilies on Matthew 65, in substance). The other ten are angry, and Jesus gathers them all: among the nations, rulers “lord it over them,” “But it is not so among you; but whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wishes to be first among you must be slave of all. For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” James learned the lesson. A little more than ten years after the Resurrection he drank the cup. Clement of Alexandria, as Eusebius records, tells that the man who brought James to trial was so moved by his witness that he confessed himself a Christian; as they were led out together, he asked James to forgive him, and James said, “Peace be with you,” and kissed him; and both were beheaded (Church History II.9, in substance).

St Paul's words to the Galatians show what the cup and the service look like in an ordinary parish. “My friends, if anyone is detected in a transgression, you who have received the Spirit should restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness.” Not gossip, not public shaming, but gentle restoring, as James restored his accuser with a kiss. “Bear one another's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ.” Each of us “must carry their own loads,” and yet we help carry each other's. And Paul adds the farmer's law: “you reap whatever you sow... So let us not grow weary in doing what is right, for we will reap at harvest time, if we do not give up.” In Kerala many parishes and families bear the name of St James, and many pilgrims know the long road to his shrine at Compostela in Spain, where by an old tradition his relics are venerated. The road that matters most, though, is the one from the throne we would like to the cup we are given. Today, serve someone who cannot repay you; gently restore someone who has fallen; and carry one burden that is not your own. That is to drink the Lord's cup and to share his glory.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Today our Church honours Mar Yaqob Shleeha, St James the son of Zebedee, brother of John, one of the three closest disciples of Jesus. He saw the Transfiguration and was near the Lord in Gethsemane. And Acts tells us that King Herod had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword (Acts 12:2): he was the first of the Twelve to give his life.

But in today's Gospel James has not yet understood. He and John ask: grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory. Jesus answers: you do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink? (Mark 10:37–38). They want thrones; he offers them his cup.

Then Jesus teaches all of us: whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant... For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many (Mark 10:43–45). James learned. An ancient account says he forgave the man who accused him with the words: peace be with you.

St Paul shows us how to live this: restore the fallen in a spirit of gentleness; bear one another's burdens; and let us not grow weary in doing what is right, for we will reap at harvest time, if we do not give up (Galatians 6:1–2, 9). Today let us serve someone who cannot repay us. That is the cup, and that is the glory. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Today is the feast of St James, a fisherman who followed Jesus. Jesus called James and his brother John sons of thunder, because they were so loud and full of energy! Once they asked Jesus for the best seats. Jesus said: the greatest is the one who helps others. Jesus himself came to serve us. This week, be like St James after he learned: help at home without being asked, and you will be great in God's eyes!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you came not to be served but to serve and to give your life as a ransom for many. You called Mar Yaqob from his boat and nets, bore with his ambition, and taught him to drink your cup; he became the first of your apostles to give his life for you. By his prayers deliver us from the desire to sit in the first places, and make us servants of one another. Teach us to restore those who fall with gentleness, to bear one another's burdens, and never to grow weary in doing good. Bring us at last to share your glory. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Yaqob Shleeha, St James the son of Zebedee, the Gospel shows him before he understood: asking with his brother John for the seats of honour, and hearing instead of a cup and a baptism. Jesus promised, “The cup that I drink you will drink”, and James became the first of the Twelve to die for him (Acts 12:2). The Epistle from Galatians shows what greatness looks like in a community formed by that cup: restoring the fallen gently, bearing one another's burdens, and not growing weary in doing good. The key words are cup, serve and restore.

Galatians 6:1–10 · Bear one another's burdens

Setting.

Paul wrote to the churches of Galatia, in the interior of what is now Turkey, when some teachers were urging his Gentile converts to be circumcised and keep the Mosaic law in order to belong fully to God's people. Paul's answer fills the letter: we are made right with God through faith in Christ, and the Spirit has been given to us (3:1–5). The date is uncertain; many place it in the early or middle 50s.

Chapter 5 has set the works of the flesh against the fruit of the Spirit (5:16–26). Today's verses show what life in the Spirit looks like in ordinary church life. Paul is not speaking of a vague goodwill but of concrete care: for the one who falls, for the teacher, for all, and especially for “the family of faith”.

Structure.

  • v. 1: restore the one who has fallen, gently, watching yourself.
  • vv. 2–5: bear one another's burdens; test your own work; carry your own load.
  • v. 6: share good things with those who teach you.
  • vv. 7–8: you reap what you sow: flesh or Spirit.
  • vv. 9–10: do not grow weary; do good to all.

Key words.

  • katartizete, restore: A word used of setting a broken bone or mending nets. Mark uses it of James and John themselves, “in their boat mending the nets” when Jesus called them (Mark 1:19). The fishermen of Galilee became menders of people.
  • baros / phortion, burden / load: Two different words. The baros of v. 2 is a heavy weight that one person cannot carry alone, so others must help. The phortion of v. 5 is one's own pack, the responsibility no one else can take. Paul holds both together.
  • kairos, time, opportunity: Used in vv. 9 and 10: the harvest will come in its own time, and meanwhile “whenever we have an opportunity”, we are to do good. God's time and our chance are the same word.

Verse by verse.

“if anyone is detected in a transgression, you who have received the Spirit should restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness.” (6:1)

The test of a spiritual person is not that he never sees sin in others, but how he handles it: with gentleness, which is part of the fruit of the Spirit (5:23).

“Bear one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ.” (6:2)

Paul has argued against putting Gentiles under the Mosaic law. Yet Christians are not lawless. The law of Christ is the love that he showed, summed up in 5:14: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

“Do not be deceived; God is not mocked, for you reap whatever you sow.” (6:7)

A farmer's proverb. What we give our life to will grow. God's grace does not cancel this; it makes it possible to sow to the Spirit.

“So let us not grow weary in doing what is right, for we will reap at harvest time, if we do not give up.” (6:9)

Good done in secret and without thanks is not lost. The harvest is God's, and it comes in his time.

“let us work for the good of all, and especially for those of the family of faith.” (6:10)

The circle of love reaches everyone, and has a centre: the household of believers. The two are not rivals.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as a rule of fraternal life. The Catechism speaks of fraternal correction as a work of charity (CCC 1829), and the Church's practice of correction is meant to heal and restore, never to humiliate. Bearing one another's burdens is the root of the works of mercy (CCC 2447). Verse 8 does not teach that we earn eternal life by our own power. We sow to the Spirit by grace, and the life we reap is God's gift; the Council of Trent teaches that eternal life is both a grace and a reward promised by God to good works done in Christ (CCC 2006–2011 in substance).

Mark 10:35–45 · Are you able to drink the cup that I drink?

Setting.

Jesus is on the road to Jerusalem, walking ahead of his frightened disciples (10:32). He has just foretold his Passion for the third time, in the fullest detail: he will be handed over, condemned, mocked, flogged and killed, and after three days he will rise (10:33–34). At once James and John ask for the places of honour. Mark sets the two scenes side by side so that we feel the distance between the Lord's mind and theirs.

James and John were among the first four disciples called (1:16–20). With Peter they saw the raising of Jairus' daughter (5:37), the Transfiguration (9:2) and the agony in Gethsemane (14:33). Jesus named them “Boanerges, that is, Sons of Thunder” (3:17). Matthew tells the same scene but has their mother make the request (Matthew 20:20–21).

Structure.

  • vv. 35–37: the request for the right and the left.
  • vv. 38–40: the cup and the baptism; the places are not Jesus' to grant.
  • v. 41: the anger of the ten.
  • vv. 42–44: not so among you: the great must serve.
  • v. 45: the Son of Man came to serve and to give his life a ransom for many.

Key words.

  • potērion, cup: In the Old Testament the cup can mean the portion God gives, of blessing (Psalm 23:5; 116:13) or of judgment and suffering (Isaiah 51:17). Here it is the cup of the Passion. In Gethsemane Jesus will pray, “remove this cup from me” (14:36).
  • baptisma, baptism: From the verb for plunging or immersing. Jesus speaks of being plunged into suffering and death. Luke records a similar saying: “I have a baptism with which to be baptized” (Luke 12:50).
  • lytron, ransom: The price paid to set a captive or slave at liberty. Jesus does not explain to whom the price is paid; he says what it costs and what it achieves. The phrase “for many” echoes the Servant of Isaiah 53:11–12, who bears the sins of many.

Verse by verse.

“Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory.” (10:37)

They believe he will reign, and that is to their credit. What they do not yet see is the throne he goes to. Mark will later show who are at his right and his left in that hour: two bandits on crosses (15:27).

“You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, or be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with?” (10:38)

Jesus does not rebuke them harshly. He leads them from the thought of honour to the thought of sharing his suffering.

“The cup that I drink you will drink;” (10:39)

A prophecy fulfilled for James in Jerusalem around AD 44, when Herod Agrippa I “had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword” (Acts 12:2). John drank the cup by long witness and suffering for Christ.

“But it is not so among you; but whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant,” (10:43)

Jesus does not forbid authority in his community. He changes its meaning: authority becomes service.

“For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” (10:45)

The key to the whole Gospel of Mark. The one who will come on the clouds with power (Daniel 7:13–14) first comes to serve and to die.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus willingly offered his life as “a ransom for many”, and that “many” is not restrictive: it sets the whole of humanity over against the one Redeemer who gives himself (CCC 605; see 622). It also says that ministry in the Church is by its nature service, after the pattern of Christ who made himself the servant of all (CCC 876). St John Chrysostom notes that the Lord did not scold the brothers, but led them gently from the request for honour to the cup (in substance). James learned the lesson, and the Church honours him as the first apostle to give his life for Christ.

Questions people ask

Which James is this?

James the son of Zebedee, brother of John, sometimes called James the Greater. He is not the same as James the son of Alphaeus, another of the Twelve (Mark 3:18), nor the same as “James the brother of the Lord”, who still led the church in Jerusalem after the son of Zebedee had been put to death (Galatians 1:19; Acts 12:2; 15:13).

Was the request of James and John simply ambition?

Partly, and Jesus corrects it. But it was also faith: they believed he would come into his glory when others saw only danger ahead. Jesus does not reject them. He promises them a share in his cup, and he keeps that promise.

Does Galatians 6:5 contradict 6:2?

No. Paul uses two different words. Some burdens are too heavy for one person, and the community must carry them together. Others belong to each person, like conscience and our own account before God. A healthy Christian community does both: it helps, and it does not take away each person's responsibility.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church keeps today the feast of Mar Yaqob Shleeha. Shleeha is the Syriac word for apostle, one who is sent. The Byzantine Churches also honour St James the son of Zebedee on this day, while the Latin Church keeps his feast on 25 July. He is the only apostle whose martyrdom is recorded in the New Testament. In the Holy Qurbono we drink from the cup that Jesus spoke of in this Gospel, and we are reminded that sharing his cup means sharing his life of service. As we receive the Holy Mysteries today, we ask Mar Yaqob to pray that we may be able to drink it as he did.

For study and teaching

  • If Jesus asked me, “What is it you want me to do for you?”, what would I honestly answer?
  • What “cup” has the Lord given me to drink at this time in my life? How can I drink it with him?
  • Whom in my family or parish might I restore “in a spirit of gentleness” rather than judge?
  • Where am I growing weary in doing good? What would help me not to give up?
