Word for the day

“for you are all children of light and children of the day; we are not of the night or of darkness.” 1 Thessalonians 5:5

Reflection

Paul begins this passage by saying that he has no need to write about “the times and the seasons.” The Thessalonians were anxious about when the Lord would return, and some seem to have been neglecting their daily work (compare 2 Thessalonians 3:11). Paul does not give a date. He gives a picture that every household knows: “you yourselves know very well that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.” A thief sends no notice; no one who is ready for him is troubled when he comes. The people who are caught are those who say, “There is peace and security,” and have settled down comfortably on their own foundations, as though nothing could shake the house. We have seen in Kerala how fast a calm monsoon morning can turn into a flood. Security that depends on our own arrangements is thin. The security of the believer is of another kind, and Paul states it as a fact about who we are: “But you, beloved, are not in darkness, for that day to surprise you like a thief; for you are all children of light and children of the day; we are not of the night or of darkness.”

From that identity Paul draws a single instruction: “So then let us not fall asleep as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober.” To keep awake is not to live in fear or to be forever scanning the sky. It means to be alert in the Lord, the opposite of the sleep of routine, in which days pass one after another and nothing is asked and nothing is given. To be sober is to keep a clear head, not dulled by the noise of news, by money or by pleasure. Paul goes on a few verses further to give the reason for the confidence: God “has destined us not for wrath but for obtaining salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us, so that whether we are awake or asleep we may live with him.” The Day is not a trap but a meeting with the One who died for us. The early morning, with the first bell of the church, the first fishing boats going out and the first tea stalls opening, is the most natural picture of the Christian life: we live as people who are up before the light because we are expecting someone.

In the Gospel the first one awake is the Lord himself. The evening before, the whole town of Capernaum had crowded around the door with its sick; and now, “at daybreak he departed and went into a deserted place.” The Gospel of Mark tells us what he did there: he prayed (Mark 1:35). When the crowds find him and “wanted to prevent him from leaving them,” he answers, “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other cities also; for I was sent for this purpose.” It would have been easy to stay where he was loved and needed. But he lives by his mission, not by popularity, and his mission comes from prayer: before he speaks to the people he speaks to the Father. Christians who keep awake are those who begin the day as he did and then go where they are sent, to the next town, the next ward, the next neighbour. “So he continued proclaiming the message in the synagogues of Judea.” The kingdom is not kept in one village; it goes on.

Homily outline

Theme: “You are all children of light and children of the day.”

On Friday of the tenth week after Pentecost: the thief in the night, the call to keep awake and sober, and the Lord who rises at daybreak to pray.

1. Children of light

  • We are not of the night or of darkness.
  • The day of the Lord comes like a thief.
  • A thief surprises the sleeping, not the watchful.
“Arise, shine; for your light has come, and the glory of the Lord has risen upon you.” Isaiah 60:1
“For once you were darkness, but now in the Lord you are light.” Ephesians 5:8

Apply: Each night this week say: Lord, I am a child of your day; keep me awake in you.

2. Awake and sober

  • Not fear, but alertness in the Lord.
  • A clear head in a noisy world.
  • Destined for salvation, awake or asleep.
“O Lord, in the morning you hear my voice; in the morning I plead my case to you, and watch.” Psalm 5:3
“And what I say to you I say to all: Keep awake.” Mark 13:37

Apply: Give the first ten minutes of the morning to prayer, before the phone and the news.

3. The Lord at daybreak

  • He rises early and prays in a deserted place.
  • The crowds want to keep him.
  • He goes on, for he was sent.

Apply: Ask the Lord today where you are sent, and go there.

The Lord himself rises at daybreak to pray, and then goes where he is sent; we follow him.

Take-home resolution: Keep awake and sober, begin the day in prayer, and go where you are sent.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells the Thessalonians that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night (1 Thessalonians 5:2). But he does not frighten them. You are not in darkness; you are all children of light and children of the day (1 Thessalonians 5:4–5). A thief surprises the sleeping, not those who are awake.

So the instruction is simple: let us not fall asleep as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober (1 Thessalonians 5:6). To keep awake is not to live in fear. It is to be alert in the Lord, the opposite of the sleep of routine, and to keep a clear head. And the reason for our confidence is that God has destined us not for wrath but for salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us, so that whether we are awake or asleep we may live with him (1 Thessalonians 5:9–10).

In the Gospel, the first one awake is Jesus himself. At daybreak he went to a deserted place to pray. The crowds wanted to keep him, but he said, I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other cities also; for I was sent for this purpose (Luke 4:43). His mission comes out of prayer.

So let us rise a little earlier, give the first minutes to the Lord, and then go where we are sent. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know who is awake in the early morning? The farmers, the fishermen, the bakers, the milk sellers, and the birds! Jesus also got up very early in the morning, before everyone else, and went to a quiet place to talk to his Father. Then he went to the next town to tell the people about God. St Paul says that we are children of the light and of the day. So tomorrow morning, when you wake up, say hello to Jesus before you say hello to anyone else. Say, “Good morning, Lord!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you rose at daybreak and went to a deserted place to pray, and you went on to the other cities because you were sent. Keep us awake and sober, children of light and of the day. Let no thief of the night find us asleep, and send us where you wish, so that whether we are awake or asleep we may live with you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about wakefulness and about the work that cannot wait. Paul tells the Thessalonians that the day of the Lord comes without warning, and that those who belong to the light are to stay awake and sober. In Luke, Jesus rises before dawn to pray alone, and then refuses to stay where he is welcomed because he was sent to proclaim the kingdom to other towns as well. The key words are keep awake and I must proclaim.

1 Thessalonians 5:1–6 · Children of light and of the day

Setting.

First Thessalonians was written by Paul from Corinth, probably around AD 50–51, and is one of the earliest writings of the New Testament. The young church at Thessalonica was anxious about believers who had died (4:13–18) and about when the Lord would return. Paul has comforted them about the first and now turns to the second.

He does not give a timetable. He says that they know very well that the day of the Lord will come “like a thief in the night”, and then tells them how to live in the meantime. Many scholars hear in “peace and security” (v. 3) an echo of the slogans of the Roman Empire; whether or not this is so, the words describe the false calm that comes before a sudden crisis.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: no need to write about times and seasons: the day comes like a thief in the night.
  • v. 3: those who say “peace and security” will be taken by sudden destruction, like labour pains.
  • vv. 4–5: but you are not in darkness; you are children of light and of the day.
  • v. 6: so let us not sleep but keep awake and be sober.

Key words.

  • kleptēs, thief: The image comes from Jesus’ own teaching (Matthew 24:43; Luke 12:39). It is not the picture of a threat to believers but of a surprise for those who do not expect the Lord.
  • grēgoreō, to keep awake: To be spiritually alert. In the Gospels Jesus repeats this call at Gethsemane and in his teaching on the end (Mark 13:35–37).
  • nēphō, to be sober: Literally not drunk. A clear mind, clear of the intoxication of the world’s false promises, is the mark of those who live in the light.

Verse by verse.

“the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night.” (5:2)

No one knows the day. The right response is not to calculate but to live ready.

“you are all children of light and children of the day” (5:5)

Paul reminds them who they are before telling them what to do. Their identity is given by God in Baptism, and their behaviour follows from it.

“let us keep awake and be sober” (5:6)

Paul says “let us”, and includes himself. Wakefulness is a task for the whole Church, together.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always taught that no one knows the time of the end. The Catechism says that we know neither the moment of the consummation of the earth and of humanity nor the way in which the universe will be transformed (CCC 1048). The right reaction to this is the one Paul asks of the Thessalonians: faith, hope and love (5:8), and prayer. The Church’s whole liturgical year is a school of watchfulness, and the call “watch” returns constantly, with the parable of the ten virgins in Matthew 25:13.

Luke 4:42–44 · I must proclaim the good news

Setting.

The passage closes Luke’s first day of ministry at Capernaum: Jesus has taught in the synagogue, driven out a demon and healed Simon’s mother-in-law and many others (4:31–41). The crowds are now at his door. In the morning he goes alone to a deserted place, as he does at other key moments in Luke (5:16; 6:12; 9:18, 28).

The crowds find him and want to keep him. Jesus answers that his mission is larger than one town. A textual note: many manuscripts read “synagogues of Galilee” in verse 44 (as in Mark 1:39), but the NRSV text has “Judea”, and Luke sometimes uses “Judea” for the land of the Jews as a whole.

Structure.

  • v. 42: at daybreak Jesus goes to a deserted place; the crowds seek him and try to keep him.
  • v. 43: “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other cities also”.
  • v. 44: he continues preaching in the synagogues.

Key words.

  • erēmos topos, deserted place: A place of prayer, silence and meeting with God, as in the story of Moses and Elijah. Jesus’ own source of strength is in this solitude.
  • dei, it is necessary, I must: A word Luke uses of God’s plan. Jesus does not follow popularity but the Father’s will.
  • apestalēn, I was sent: Jesus knows that he is sent. The mission precedes the crowds’ desires.

Verse by verse.

“At daybreak he departed and went into a deserted place.” (4:42)

After a day of healing, Jesus goes to pray. His public work comes out of a hidden life with the Father.

“the crowds were looking for him” (4:42)

The crowds are not wrong to want him; they are right to seek him. But they would keep him for themselves.

“I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other cities also; for I was sent for this purpose.” (4:43)

Jesus’ mission is for everyone. He cannot be kept in one place, however much he is loved there.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to the prayer of Jesus before decisive moments in his ministry, as Luke’s Gospel stresses (CCC 2602), and to his proclamation of the kingdom as the centre of his mission (CCC 541–542). The Church sees in this passage the rhythm of her own life: prayer first, then mission. A parish that wishes to proclaim the Gospel to the other “cities” must also keep its deserted places of prayer. The sentence “I was sent” is repeated in the Church’s own sending: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you” (John 20:21).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac tradition the Church keeps vigil in the night hours of prayer, the Lilyo, as well as the morning hours of the Sapro. Paul’s words about being awake and sober are lived each time the faithful rise to pray while others sleep, and Jesus’ early morning prayer in the deserted place is the model of these hours.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that we are children of light and of the day. What is one way in which I can live as one who is awake this week, and not as one who sleeps?
  • Jesus withdrew to pray before going on to other towns. Where is my own “deserted place”, and what mission might come out of it?
