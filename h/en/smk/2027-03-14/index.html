Word for the day

“One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see.” John 9:25

Reflection

On the last Sunday before Holy Week the Church reads two healings of the blind. In John, Jesus meets a man blind from birth. The disciples ask whose sin caused it. Jesus answers: “Neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God's works might be revealed in him… As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” He makes mud, anoints the man's eyes, and sends him: “Go, wash in the pool of Siloam (which means Sent).”

The healed man is questioned again and again by the Pharisees. He does not know all the theology, but he knows one thing: “Though I was blind, now I see.” Step by step his sight grows: first “the man called Jesus”, then “a prophet”, then, kneeling before him, “Lord, I believe.” Meanwhile those who claim to see become blind. Jesus says: “I came into this world for judgment so that those who do not see may see, and those who do see may become blind.”

In Mark, blind Bartimaeus sits by the road at Jericho and cries out: “Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!” People tell him to be quiet; he cries out louder. Jesus asks: “What do you want me to do for you?” “My teacher, let me see again.” “Go; your faith has made you well.” And Bartimaeus “followed him on the way”, the way to Jerusalem and the Cross.

Job, after all his suffering, says: “I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.” This is the goal of the Fast: not only to hear about God, but to see him with the eyes of faith. In Baptism, called “enlightenment” by the Fathers, we were washed and given sight. As Holy Week begins, let us ask with Bartimaeus: “Lord, let me see,” and then follow Jesus on the way.

Homily outline

Theme: “I was blind, now I see.”

The Gospel of the man born blind is a journey from darkness to light, from not seeing to believing and worshipping. It is also our story: Baptism is called “illumination”, and Lent prepares us to see Christ more clearly.

1. Suffering is not a punishment

  • The disciples ask: who sinned, this man or his parents?
  • Jesus refuses that easy answer: God's works will be revealed in him.
  • Isaiah promised a day when the eyes of the blind would be opened: Christ brings that day.
“Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped.” Isaiah 35:5
“Jesus answered, “Neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God's works might be revealed in him.”” John 9:3

Apply: Never judge the suffering of others as God's punishment; offer help instead.

2. Go and wash: the light of Baptism

  • Jesus makes mud, anoints the man's eyes, and sends him to the pool of Siloam, “Sent”.
  • He obeys, washes and comes back seeing: an image of Baptism, the sacrament of enlightenment.
  • Solomon prayed for an understanding mind; true sight is to see as God sees.
“Give your servant therefore an understanding mind to govern your people, able to discern between good and evil.” 1 Kings 3:9
““Go, wash in the pool of Siloam” (which means Sent). Then he went and washed and came back able to see.” John 9:7

Apply: Renew your baptismal faith: pray the Creed slowly each day this week.

3. From sight to faith and worship

  • Questioned and expelled, the man holds to what he knows: “I was blind, now I see.”
  • Meeting Jesus again, he believes and worships: “Lord, I believe.”
  • Like Job, he moves from hearing about God to seeing him; like Bartimaeus, he follows Jesus on the way.
“I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.” Job 42:5
“One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see. … He said, “Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him.” John 9:25, 38

Apply: Share your simple testimony with someone: what Christ has done for you.

Christ is the light of the world. The real blindness is to refuse to see him.

Let us wash, believe and worship, and walk as children of light toward Easter.

Take-home resolution: Spend fifteen minutes before the Blessed Sacrament this week, asking: “Lord, let me see.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on the last Sunday before Holy Week we hear of two blind men who receive their sight. In John's Gospel a man blind from birth; in Mark's, Bartimaeus, a beggar sitting by the road at Jericho.

Jesus makes clay, anoints the blind man's eyes and sends him to wash in the pool of Siloam, which means “Sent”. He comes back seeing. Then come the questions, the arguments, the threats. He does not know how to answer the scholars, but he knows one thing: “I was blind; now I see.” And his faith grows until he kneels and says: “Lord, I believe.” Meanwhile those who are sure they see refuse the light.

Bartimaeus cannot see, but he can shout. “Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!” People tell him to be quiet. He shouts louder. Jesus stops and asks: “What do you want me to do for you?” “Teacher, let me see again.” He receives his sight and follows Jesus on the road, the road to Jerusalem.

Next Sunday is Hosanna. Holy Week begins. Let us ask Jesus to heal our blindness: to the poor beside us, to our own sins, to God's love. Let us pray Bartimaeus' prayer all through this week. And having received our sight, let us follow Jesus on the way, to his Cross and his Resurrection. Amen.

For children

Children, close your eyes for a moment. Now open them! It's wonderful to see, isn't it? Bartimaeus was blind. He heard Jesus was passing and shouted: “Jesus, help me!”

People said, “Be quiet!” But he shouted even louder. Jesus asked: “What do you want?” “I want to see!” And Jesus gave him his sight!

Next week we will wave palms for Jesus. Let's say: “Jesus, open my eyes to see you!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, light of the world, you opened the eyes of the man born blind and of Bartimaeus. Have mercy on us: heal our blindness, let us see you with the eyes of faith, and give us grace to follow you on the way to Jerusalem, to your Cross and your Resurrection. Amen.
