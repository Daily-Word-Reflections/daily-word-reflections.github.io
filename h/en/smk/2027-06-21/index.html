Readings

Word for the day

“Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes not me but the one who sent me.” Mark 9:37

Reflection

Yesterday, on the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost, the evening Gospel already brought us to this house in Capernaum; today the Church lets us stay there a little longer. Jesus has just told the disciples a second time that he will be betrayed, killed and raised. On the road behind him they have been talking about something else. “And when he was in the house he asked them, ‘What were you arguing about on the way?’ But they were silent, for on the way they had argued with one another who was the greatest.” Their silence says everything: they knew the argument would not bear the light of his question. Jesus does not scold them at length. “He sat down, called the twelve, and said to them, ‘Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.’” Then he does something unforgettable. “He took a little child and put it among them; and taking it in his arms, he said to them, ‘Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes not me but the one who sent me.’” In that world a child had no rank, no voice and no claim. Jesus puts the one who counts least in the centre and says: here you will find me, and in me the Father. St Bede, commenting on this scene, says that the Lord set a child before them so that they might learn humility from its simplicity, a child who does not cling to anger or envy (Commentary on Mark, in substance).

St Paul asks the Romans the question that lies under the disciples' argument: whom do you serve? “Do you not know that if you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness?” There is no neutral ground. The disciples who wanted to be greatest were, without knowing it, serving pride. Every human life is given to something: money, pleasure, reputation, a career, a political party, or God. Paul says: “just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to greater and greater iniquity, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness for sanctification.” Eyes, hands, tongue, time: all can be given either way. And he names the end of each road: “For the wages of sin is death,” but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord. Wages are earned; a gift is received. The service of sin pays exactly what it promises, and it promises death. The service of God gives far more than we could earn. The Apostles' Fast, now in its second week, is a time to look at our members, our eyes and our hands and our evenings, and ask honestly whom they are serving.

Today we remember St Aloysius Gonzaga, a young man who answered that question early. He was born in 1568 in northern Italy, the eldest son of a marquis, trained from childhood for power and war and the life of princely courts. He received his First Holy Communion from St Charles Borromeo. In the courts he saw ambition, vanity and cruelty, and he chose another master. Against his father's long resistance he gave up his inheritance to his younger brother and entered the Society of Jesus in Rome. There he studied, prayed, and accepted the humblest work. In 1591, when plague and famine struck Rome, he went out with his brother Jesuits to care for the sick, carried a dying man on his shoulders to the hospital, caught the disease, and died on 21 June 1591, aged twenty-three. The heir who could have been first made himself last of all and servant of all. Before he died he wrote to his mother asking her not to grieve for him as for one dead, since he was going to live with God and would help her more from there (Letter to his mother, in substance). The Church has made him a patron of young people and students. As the new school and college year begins in Kerala, let us pray for our students, that they may learn not only to be first in examinations, but to serve.

Homily outline

Theme: “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.”

On Monday of the fifth week after Pentecost, in the Apostles' Fast, with St Aloysius Gonzaga: the argument on the way, the question of whom we serve, and the young saint who chose to be last.

1. Whose slave are you?

  • No neutral ground.
  • Members given to sin or to righteousness.
  • Wages and gift.
“choose this day whom you will serve” Joshua 24:15
“No one can serve two masters; for a slave will either hate the one and love the other, or be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and wealth.” Matthew 6:24

Apply: Write down honestly where your time went yesterday, and offer tomorrow's hours to God in advance.

2. Last of all and servant of all

  • The argument on the road.
  • The silence of the disciples.
  • The child in the centre.
“The reward for humility and fear of the Lord is riches and honor and life.” Proverbs 22:4
“Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility regard others as better than yourselves.” Philippians 2:3

Apply: Take on one humble task this week that usually falls to others, and do it without telling anyone.

3. St Aloysius: a young heart given whole

  • The heir who chose another Master.
  • Carrying the sick in plague-stricken Rome.
  • Patron of students and the young.
“Remember your creator in the days of your youth” Ecclesiastes 12:1
“Let no one despise your youth, but set the believers an example in speech and conduct, in love, in faith, in purity.” 1 Timothy 4:12

Apply: Pray for one young person you know who is starting a new year of study, and encourage him or her in person.

Every life serves someone; only the service of God ends in eternal life.

Jesus, and St Aloysius after him, show that the greatest is the one who serves.

Take-home resolution: Stop arguing about who is greatest: welcome the least, and give your members to the service of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel Jesus asks his disciples: what were you arguing about on the way? (Mark 9:33). They were silent, because they had argued about who was the greatest (Mark 9:34). Jesus had just told them that he would suffer and die, and they were thinking about their own importance.

Jesus sat down and said: whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all (Mark 9:35). Then he took a little child in his arms and said: whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me (Mark 9:37). A child had no importance in that world. Jesus puts the least in the centre, and says that we find him there.

St Paul asks: whom are you serving? If you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness (Romans 6:16). Every life serves something. In this Apostles' Fast, let us look at our eyes, our tongue, our hands and our time, and give them to God.

Today we remember St Aloysius Gonzaga. He was the heir of a noble family, but he left everything to serve God. In 1591 he carried the sick of Rome on his shoulders, caught the plague and died at twenty-three. He chose to be last and servant of all. As schools and colleges reopen, let us pray for our students, that they may learn not only to be first, but to serve. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever argued about who is the best, who is the fastest or who gets the first turn? The disciples of Jesus did that too! Jesus told them: the most important person is the one who helps everyone. Then he hugged a little child and said: when you are kind to a child, you are kind to me. Today we remember St Aloysius, who was rich and could have been a prince, but he chose to help sick people. As school starts again, try to be the one who helps, not the one who wants to be first.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you came not to be served but to serve, and you placed a little child in the midst of your disciples. Forgive our arguments about who is greatest. Deliver us from the service of sin, which leads to death, and make us slaves of righteousness, that we may receive the gift of eternal life. Through the prayers of St Aloysius Gonzaga, who left the honours of the world to serve you and the sick, give our young people pure hearts and generous hands, and bless all students and teachers in this new school year. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
