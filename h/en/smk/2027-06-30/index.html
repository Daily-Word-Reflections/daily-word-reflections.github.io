Word for the day

“And he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message,” Mark 3:14

Reflection

Yesterday the Church celebrated the two great pillars, Peter and Paul, and the Apostles' Fast came to an end. Today, as if gathering the whole family together after the feast, she remembers all the Twelve. St Mark tells how it began: “He went up the mountain and called to him those whom he wanted, and they came to him.” St Luke adds that before choosing them Jesus “spent the night in prayer to God” (Luke 6:12). The call of the Apostles was born in the prayer of the Son to the Father, and it came from his choice, not theirs. The mountain recalls Sinai, where God formed Israel as his people, and the number twelve recalls the twelve tribes: in choosing the Twelve, Jesus was gathering a renewed people of God, the Church. The Book of Revelation sees the end of the story in the heavenly city: “And the wall of the city has twelve foundations, and on them are the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb” (Revelation 21:14). Every one of us who believes stands on those foundations.

Mark tells us why they were chosen: “And he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message, and to have authority to cast out demons.” There are two movements here, and their order matters. First, to be with him; then, to be sent. No one can be sent who has not first been with Jesus, at table, on the road, in prayer, at the foot of the cross. And whoever has truly been with him cannot keep him to himself. Then Mark gives the names, and what a mixture they are: Simon, to whom Jesus gave the name Peter; James and John, the fiery brothers he called Sons of Thunder; Andrew and Philip; Bartholomew, whom our calendar remembered as Mar Barthulmai on 11 June; Matthew, the tax collector; Thomas, who brought the gospel to our land and whose Dukhrono we keep on Saturday; James son of Alphaeus; Thaddaeus, remembered as Mar Yuda Thaddai on 19 June; Simon the Cananaean; and Judas Iscariot, “who betrayed him.” Fishermen and a tax collector, quiet men and hot-tempered ones: Jesus did not choose them because they were alike, but made them one around himself. The sad last name reminds us that even one who has been close to the Lord can fall away, and the Church later chose Matthias to take his place among the Twelve.

In the Epistle, St Paul, who was not one of the Twelve but was called by the risen Lord, describes the apostolic life from the inside. “As we work together with him, we urge you also not to accept the grace of God in vain. For he says, ‘At an acceptable time I have listened to you, and on a day of salvation I have helped you.’ See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” The fast is over, but the acceptable time is not; it is always now. Then Paul lists what the work of an apostle cost him: “through great endurance, in afflictions, hardships, calamities, beatings, imprisonments, riots, labors, sleepless nights, hunger.” Tradition tells that almost all of the Twelve gave their lives for Christ. Yet Paul ends with a song of paradoxes: “as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing everything.” St John Chrysostom, who loved Paul dearly, said that the apostles had no house of their own, yet every house of the faithful was open to them, and that this is what it means to have nothing and possess everything (Homilies on Second Corinthians, in substance).

The Church that the Twelve founded is still apostolic. The bishops, gathered around the successor of Peter, are the successors of the Apostles, and the faith we profess is the faith they preached. Our own Church treasures the apostolic faith brought to India by Mar Thoma Shleeha, and lives it in full communion with the Bishop of Rome. But to be apostolic is not only a matter of history and of bishops. Every baptized Christian is called, like the Twelve, to be with Jesus and to be sent. To be with him in prayer and in the Holy Qurbono; to be sent to the office, the field, the classroom and the hospital ward, carrying the message. The fast has ended; the mission has not. Today, at the turning of the month, let us thank God for the Twelve, ask their prayers, and offer ourselves again to be with the Lord and to go where he sends us.

Full homily

Dear friends, yesterday we celebrated Sts Peter and Paul, and today we remember all twelve Apostles together. St Mark tells us: He went up the mountain and called to him those whom he wanted, and they came to him (Mark 3:13). St Luke says that Jesus first spent the night in prayer (Luke 6:12). The Apostles were born from the prayer of Jesus.

Why did he choose them? Mark answers: he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message (Mark 3:14). First, to be with him. Then, to be sent. We cannot give Jesus to others if we have not spent time with him. And if we really have been with him, we cannot keep him to ourselves.

The Twelve were very different: fishermen, a tax collector, fiery brothers, quiet men. Among them was Thomas, who came to our land and whose Dukhrono we keep on Saturday. St Paul tells us what the apostolic life costs and what it gives: as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing everything (2 Corinthians 6:10). And he reminds us: now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation (2 Corinthians 6:2).

The Apostles' Fast is over, but the mission continues. Our bishops, with the successor of Peter, carry on the work of the Twelve, and every one of us is called in our own way. So let us be with Jesus each day in prayer and in the Holy Qurbono, and let us go where he sends us. Holy Apostles of the Lord, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus once went up a mountain and called twelve friends to be his Apostles. Can you count them on your fingers? Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James, Thaddaeus, Simon and Judas. They were all different, but Jesus loved each one. First he wanted them to be with him, and then he sent them to tell others about God. Jesus wants you to be with him too, when you pray, and then to go and be kind to others in his name.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, after a night of prayer you called to yourself those whom you wanted, and you appointed the Twelve to be with you and to be sent out to proclaim your message. We thank you for your holy Apostles, the foundations of your Church, and especially for Mar Thoma Shleeha, who brought your gospel to our land. Through their prayers keep us in the apostolic faith and in communion with the successor of Peter. Draw us to be with you in prayer and in the Holy Qurbono, and send us out as your witnesses. When the work is hard, let us be sorrowful yet always rejoicing, poor yet making many rich, having nothing and yet possessing everything in you. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The day after the feast of Peter and Paul, the Church honours all twelve apostles together. In Mark, Jesus goes up the mountain, calls those whom he wants, and appoints twelve “to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message”. In 2 Corinthians, Paul shows what that sending looked like in real life: hardships, beatings, sleepless nights, and through it all a strange wealth, “as having nothing, and yet possessing everything.” The key words are with him and sent.

2 Corinthians 6:1–10 · As having nothing, and yet possessing everything

Setting.

In 2 Corinthians Paul defends his ministry before a community where some questioned whether he was a true apostle. Just before our passage he has said that God has given him “the ministry of reconciliation” and that “we are ambassadors for Christ” (5:18–20). Now he urges the Corinthians not to let God's grace go to waste, and describes the life of a servant of God.

The list of sufferings is a form known in the ancient world, where philosophers listed the trials they had endured to show their steadiness. Paul uses it in his own way: his endurance proves not his own strength but the power of God at work in weakness (compare 4:7–12; 11:23–29).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: do not accept God's grace in vain: now is the day of salvation.
  • v. 3: no obstacle in anyone's way.
  • vv. 4–5: endurance in afflictions, beatings, labours, hunger.
  • vv. 6–7: purity, knowledge, kindness, the Holy Spirit, genuine love, the power of God.
  • vv. 8–10: a chain of paradoxes: dying, and see, we are alive.

Key words.

  • kairos euprosdektos, acceptable time: Paul quotes Isaiah 49:8 and then declares its fulfilment: “See, now is the acceptable time.” Kairos is not clock time but the decisive moment, God's moment, which must be seized.
  • diakonoi theou, servants of God: The apostles are not masters of the faithful but servants of God for them. The word diakonos means one who serves at table or carries out another's errand.
  • hōs… kai, as… and yet: The pattern of verses 8–10: what the world sees, and what God sees. The apostle seems unknown, dying, sorrowful, poor; in truth he is known, alive, rejoicing and rich.

Verse by verse.

“As we work together with him, we urge you also not to accept the grace of God in vain.” (6:1)

Grace is never forced on anyone. It can be received in vain, like seed that falls on rocky ground. Paul calls himself God's co-worker in bringing it to fruit.

“See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” (6:2)

The Catechism uses this verse when it teaches that the message of the Last Judgment calls us to conversion while God still gives us the acceptable time (CCC 1041).

“as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing everything.” (6:10)

The apostles left everything to follow Christ (Mark 10:28), and in him they possessed all things. Their poverty enriched the whole world with the Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses Paul's words to describe the apostles and their successors: they are ministers of the new covenant, servants of God, ambassadors for Christ, stewards of the mysteries of God (CCC 859). Their ministry belongs to Christ, who acts through them. These verses fit ordinations of priests and bishops and the feasts of apostles, as a mirror held up to every minister: endurance, purity, kindness, genuine love, and joy in the midst of trial.

Mark 3:13–19 · He appointed twelve

Setting.

Up to this point in Mark, Jesus has called a few disciples one by one by the Sea of Galilee (1:16–20; 2:14), healed many, and met growing opposition (3:6). Now, on a mountain, he forms the group of the Twelve. The mountain recalls Sinai, where God formed his people Israel, and the number twelve recalls the twelve tribes. Jesus is gathering the renewed people of God.

The list of the Twelve appears four times in the New Testament (Matthew 10:2–4; Mark 3:16–19; Luke 6:14–16; Acts 1:13). Peter is always first and Judas Iscariot, where he appears, always last. Some names differ: Mark's Thaddaeus seems to be the same as Luke's “Judas son of James”.

Structure.

  • v. 13: on the mountain he calls those whom he wants; they come.
  • vv. 14–15: he appoints twelve: to be with him, to be sent to preach, to have authority over demons.
  • vv. 16–19: the names, with Simon renamed Peter and the sons of Zebedee named Boanerges.

Key words.

  • epoiēsen dōdeka, he appointed twelve: Literally “he made twelve”. The Greek verb is the one used in Genesis for God's creating. Jesus does not find the Twelve ready made; he makes them into what they are to be.
  • hina ōsin met’ autou, to be with him: The first purpose of the call, before any work. An apostle is first a companion of Jesus. Only those who have been with him can be sent to speak of him.
  • apostellō, to send: The root of the word “apostle”: one who is sent with the authority of the sender. In verse 14 Mark adds that Jesus “also named” them apostles. The Syriac word shliha, used in our liturgy for an apostle, also means “one who is sent”.

Verse by verse.

“He went up the mountain and called to him those whom he wanted, and they came to him.” (3:13)

The call depends on his will, not on their qualifications. Their part is simply to come.

“And he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message,” (3:14)

Being with and being sent belong together. Prayer without mission becomes closed; mission without prayer becomes empty. Some manuscripts lack the words “whom he also named apostles”, but the NRSV keeps them.

“and Thomas,” (3:18)

The apostle whom our ancient tradition in India honours as the one who first brought the Gospel to our land. Every list of the Twelve names him.

“and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him.” (3:19)

Even the list of the chosen ends in sorrow. The call of Christ is real, but so is human freedom.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that from the beginning of his public life Jesus chose certain men, twelve in number, to be with him and to share in his mission, and gave them a share in his authority (CCC 551). Representing the twelve tribes of Israel, they are the foundation stones of the new Jerusalem (CCC 765; Revelation 21:14). The bishops, as successors of the apostles, continue their mission until the end of time (Lumen Gentium 20).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus choose Judas, if he knew he would betray him?

The Gospels do not fully explain this mystery. They show that Jesus called Judas truly, gave him the same share in the mission as the others, and kept offering him friendship even at the Supper (John 13:26). Judas was not created to betray; he freely turned away. His story warns every disciple that being chosen does not remove the need to stay faithful, and it shows that Christ's love reaches even to the one who will betray him.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Church, like some other Eastern Churches, keeps the memory of the Twelve Apostles together on the day after the feast of Peter and Paul. It is a feast of the whole apostolic foundation of the Church, the faith we confess when we say in the Creed that the Church is “apostolic”. For the Christians of India it has a special warmth: our forefathers in faith, according to our ancient tradition, received the Gospel through Mar Thoma, the apostle Thomas, who is named among the Twelve in today's Gospel. Today we thank God for the apostles' witness, and pray for our bishops, their successors, that they may be “with him” in prayer and faithful in being sent.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus called the Twelve first “to be with him”. How much time do I give simply to being with the Lord, before I try to do things for him?
  • Paul describes himself “as having nothing, and yet possessing everything”. What would it mean for me to be rich in Christ even when I lack what the world values?
