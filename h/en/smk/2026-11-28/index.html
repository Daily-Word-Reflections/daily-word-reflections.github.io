Word for the day

“It is like a mustard seed, which, when sown upon the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth; yet when it is sown it grows up and becomes the greatest of all shrubs, and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.” Mark 4:31–32

Reflection

Paul writes as a father, not a schoolmaster: “though you might have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers. Indeed, in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” He sends Timothy to remind them of his ways, and warns those who are puffed up: “the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” Spiritual fatherhood and motherhood are a great gift in the Church: parents, godparents, priests and catechists who give birth to faith in others.

The kingdom, Jesus says, is like a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, which becomes a great shrub where birds nest in its shade. Ezekiel had seen God plant a tender shoot that became a noble cedar. The Church began with twelve men and a few women; in India it began with one apostle on the coast of Kerala. On the last Saturday before the new liturgical season of fasting, we trust that small beginnings in God's hands bear great fruit.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul writes as a father, not a schoolmaster: though you might have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers. Indeed, in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel. I appeal to you, then, be imitators of me (1 Corinthians 4:15–16). The kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.

In the Gospel Jesus compares the Kingdom to a mustard seed: when sown upon the ground it is the smallest of all seeds, yet it grows up and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade (Mark 4:30–32).

Today the Malankara Church remembers St Catherine. Our Church too began like a mustard seed, and after the reunion of 1930 it grew to give shade to many. Let us thank the spiritual fathers and mothers who handed on the faith to us, and let us become a father or mother in faith to someone. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are about small beginnings and patient growth. Paul reminds the Corinthians that he became their father in Christ through the Gospel, and that the Kingdom depends “not on talk but on power”. Jesus compares the Kingdom to a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, which becomes a shelter for the birds. The Church honours St Catherine of Alexandria, remembered as a young martyr whose wisdom and courage bore fruit far beyond her short life.

1 Corinthians 4:15–21 · A father in Christ through the gospel

Setting.

The church at Corinth was divided into groups, each attached to a favourite teacher: “I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos” (1:12). In chapters 1–4 Paul shows that the preachers are only servants, and that the wisdom of the Cross is not the wisdom of the world. He ends this section with a personal appeal, as a father to his children.

Structure.

  • v. 15: many guardians, but one father.
  • vv. 16–17: be imitators of me; Timothy is sent to remind you of my ways.
  • vv. 18–20: some are arrogant, but Paul will come and test their power, not their talk.
  • v. 21: the choice: a stick, or love and gentleness.

Key words.

  • paidagōgos, guardian: In a Greek household, the slave who took a child to school and watched over his behaviour. Necessary, but not a parent.
  • egennēsa, I became your father, I begot you: Paul uses the language of birth. Through his preaching the Corinthians were born into new life in Christ.
  • mimētai, imitators: Paul does not ask them to copy his personality but his “ways in Christ Jesus” (v. 17). He imitates Christ, and they are to imitate him (11:1).

Verse by verse.

“For though you might have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers. Indeed, in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” (4:15)

“Ten thousand” is a deliberate exaggeration. Teachers can be many; the one through whom you came to faith has a unique bond with you.

“For the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” (4:20)

Words alone, however clever, do not make the Kingdom present. The power of God shows itself in changed lives.

“What would you prefer? Am I to come to you with a stick, or with love in a spirit of gentleness?” (4:21)

A father can correct firmly, but he would rather come in gentleness. The choice is left to the children.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen in this verse the root of spiritual fatherhood: bishops and priests are fathers because they give life in Christ through the word and the sacraments. This is why Christians in Kerala call their priest “Achen”, father. Such fatherhood is a service and a share in the fatherhood of God, “from whom every family in heaven and on earth takes its name” (Ephesians 3:15).

Mark 4:30–34 · The mustard seed

Setting.

Mark 4 gathers the parables of seeds: the sower (4:1–20), the seed growing secretly (4:26–29) and the mustard seed. They speak to disciples who may be discouraged: Jesus' ministry seems small, and many reject it. The parables promise that what God has sown will grow.

Structure.

  • v. 30: a double question: to what shall we compare the Kingdom?
  • vv. 31–32: the mustard seed: smallest when sown, greatest when grown, a shelter for birds.
  • vv. 33–34: a summary: Jesus spoke in parables “as they were able to hear”, and explained everything privately to the disciples.

Key words.

  • kokkos sinapeōs, mustard seed: In Jewish speech the mustard seed was proverbial for the smallest thing. The mustard plant of Galilee can grow to two or three metres in a season.
  • kataskēnoun, make nests: Literally “pitch their tents”. The image of birds nesting in a great tree comes from the prophets (Ezekiel 17:23; Daniel 4:12), where it pictures a kingdom that shelters the nations.
  • kat’ idian, in private: The disciples receive more because they stay with Jesus. The same pattern is in Mark 4:10.

Verse by verse.

“It is like a mustard seed, which, when sown upon the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth;” (4:31)
“yet when it is sown it grows up and becomes the greatest of all shrubs, and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.”” (4:32)

Ezekiel's great tree is a noble cedar (Ezekiel 17:23); Jesus chooses a garden shrub. The Kingdom grows great, but it never loses its humble beginning.

“With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it;” (4:33)

Jesus adapts his teaching to his hearers' capacity. Good catechesis does the same.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church is on earth the seed and the beginning of the Kingdom (Lumen Gentium 5; CCC 764). The parable gives hope to small communities and to every humble beginning made in faith. St Catherine of Alexandria, a young woman of the fourth century, is a fitting example: tradition remembers her as a wise defender of the faith whose witness grew into one of the most loved cults of East and West.

Questions people ask

Jesus said, “call no one your father on earth” (Matthew 23:9). Why do we call priests “father”?

Jesus forbids putting anyone in God's place, or seeking titles for pride. He does not forbid the word itself: Paul calls himself the Corinthians' father in Christ (4:15). Spiritual fathers are true fathers only when they lead people to the one Father in heaven.

  • What small seed of faith or service could I plant this week, trusting God to make it grow?
