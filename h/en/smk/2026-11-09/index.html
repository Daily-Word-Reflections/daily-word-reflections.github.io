Word for the day

“So it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” Matthew 18:14

Reflection

Hebrews reads Psalm 95 as a call for today: “O that today you would listen to his voice! Do not harden your hearts.” Israel in the desert did not enter God's rest because of disobedience. But the promise still stands: “a sabbath rest still remains for the people of God.” Every Sunday Qurbono is a foretaste of that rest, and every day is the “today” in which we can listen.

In the Gospel Jesus defends the little ones: children, the weak, the easily forgotten. Their angels always behold the Father's face. The shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to look for the one that strayed, and rejoices more over it than over the rest. The Father does not want even one of them to be lost. A parish is judged by how it seeks the one who is missing.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this Monday after Hudos Eetho Sunday, Hebrews reads Psalm 95 as a call for today: today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts. Israel in the desert did not enter God's rest because of disobedience; but the promise is still open (Hebrews 4:7–9).

In the Gospel Jesus says: take care that you do not despise one of these little ones; for in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father. Like a shepherd who goes after one lost sheep, it is not the will of the Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost (Matthew 18:10–14).

Today is the day to listen. Do today the conversion, the reconciliation or the prayer you have put off till tomorrow. And let us protect children and the weak, as the angels of God protect them. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The week after Hudos Eetho keeps God's people on the way. Hebrews speaks of a rest that still lies ahead, and of the “today” in which we must not harden our hearts. In Matthew the Father does not want even one little one to be lost, and the shepherd goes after the one that strayed. Entering God's rest and being brought back by the shepherd are one grace seen from two sides.

Hebrews 4:6–11 · A sabbath rest still remains

Setting.

Hebrews 3–4 is a sermon on Psalm 95, the psalm that warns, “O that today you would listen to his voice!” The generation of the desert heard the good news of the promised land but did not enter, because of disobedience. The author draws the lesson for Christians: the promise is still open, and the word “today” is spoken to us.

Structure.

  • vv. 6–7: the promise remains open, and God sets a new day, “today”.
  • v. 8: Joshua did not give the final rest.
  • vv. 9–10: a sabbath rest remains for God's people.
  • v. 11: make every effort to enter.

Key words.

  • katapausis, rest: The word Psalm 95 uses for the promised land as God's rest. Hebrews reads it as more than land: it is a share in God's own rest.
  • sabbatismos, sabbath rest: A rare word, found nowhere else in the New Testament. It means keeping sabbath: a rest that is also feast and worship, like God's rest on the seventh day (Genesis 2:2).
  • sēmeron, today: The key word of the passage. God's offer is always in the present tense, and every day of the Church's life is that “today”.

Verse by verse.

“For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not speak later about another day.” (4:8)

In Greek, Joshua's name is Iēsous, the same as Jesus. The first Joshua led Israel into the land; the second leads his people into God's own rest.

“So then, a sabbath rest still remains for the people of God;” (4:9)
“Let us therefore make every effort to enter that rest, so that no one may fall through such disobedience as theirs.” (4:11)

Rest is a gift, yet we must strive to enter it. Grace does not make us idle; it makes effort possible.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Sunday, the day of the Resurrection, fulfils the spiritual truth of the sabbath and announces our eternal rest in God (CCC 2174–2175). St Augustine ends The City of God with the great sabbath that has no evening, where we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise (in substance).

Matthew 18:10–14 · The one that went astray

Setting.

Matthew 18 is the fourth of Jesus' five great discourses, often called the discourse on life in the Church. It begins with the disciples asking who is the greatest, and Jesus puts a child among them. “These little ones” are the humble and weak members of the community. Luke tells the parable of the lost sheep about sinners and the joy of finding them (Luke 15:3–7); Matthew tells it about the care the Church owes to a brother or sister who strays.

Structure.

  • v. 10: do not despise the little ones; their angels see the Father's face.
  • vv. 12–13: the shepherd, the hundred sheep, and the one that went astray.
  • v. 14: the Father's will: not one should be lost.
  • ¶ The NRSV goes from v. 10 to v. 12. Verse 11, a saying that the Son of Man came to save the lost, is missing from the oldest manuscripts and was probably added from Luke 19:10. The POC numbers the verses differently and divides v. 10 into two.

Key words.

  • mikroi, little ones: Not only children, but disciples who are small and vulnerable in the community.
  • planaō, go astray: To wander. Matthew uses the same verb for those who lead people astray (24:4–5). The sheep is not called wicked, but lost on the hills.

Verse by verse.

“Take care that you do not despise one of these little ones; for, I tell you, in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father in heaven.” (18:10)

The little ones have friends in the highest court. This verse is a chief scriptural foundation for the Church's teaching on guardian angels.

“So it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” (18:14)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites v. 10 when it teaches that from infancy to death human life is surrounded by the watchful care and intercession of angels (CCC 336), and v. 14 to show that God's love excludes no one (CCC 605). Many Fathers, among them St Gregory the Great, saw in the shepherd Christ himself, who left heaven to seek the lost human race (in substance).

Questions people ask

Does each person have a guardian angel?

Yes. The Catechism quotes St Basil: beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd, leading him to life (CCC 336).

For study and teaching

  • Where is “today” in my life, the moment when I must not harden my heart?
  • Who are the “little ones” in my parish or family whom I may be overlooking?
