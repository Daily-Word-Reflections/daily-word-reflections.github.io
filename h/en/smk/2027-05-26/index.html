Word for the day

“Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” John 6:68

Reflection

The Gospel today is only three verses long, but it comes at the hinge of St John's sixth chapter. On Sunday we heard Jesus speak of the true bread from heaven. In the verses that follow he says plainly that the bread is his flesh, given for the life of the world, and that whoever eats his flesh and drinks his blood has eternal life. Many disciples find this too hard and walk away. Then Jesus turns to the Twelve: “Do you also wish to go away?” It is one of the loneliest questions in the Gospel, and Jesus does not soften what he has said in order to keep them. Simon Peter answers for them all: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God.” Peter does not claim to understand everything. He has simply looked at every other road and found that none of them leads to life. St John Chrysostom notes that Peter's words are not those of a man with nowhere else to go but of a man who loves: who else, he means, will teach us such things (Homilies on John 47, in substance). St Augustine observed that Peter says “we have come to believe and know” and not the other way round: we believe in order to understand (Tractates on John 27, in substance). That is the faith we bring to the Holy Qurbono every Sunday. We do not see how bread becomes his Body; we believe his word, and the believing opens our eyes.

St Paul gives thanks for the same kind of faith in the Thessalonians: “We also constantly give thanks to God for this, that when you received the word of God that you heard from us, you accepted it not as a human word but as what it really is, God's word, which is also at work in you believers.” The word of God is not only read; it works. It changes those who receive it. The Catechism takes up this very verse to say that the Church welcomes Sacred Scripture not as a human word but as the word of God, and finds in it her nourishment and her strength (CCC 104). The verses that follow speak with pain of opponents in Judea who had resisted the apostles. Paul, himself a son of Israel who loved his people deeply (Romans 9:1-5), is describing particular adversaries of his own day. The Church teaches firmly that what happened in the Passion of Christ cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today, and that the Jewish people must never be presented as rejected or accursed by God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). We read such passages with that teaching, and with humility, because every one of us has also resisted the word at times.

Today the Panchangam remembers a saint in whom that word worked with joy: St Philip Neri. Born in Florence in 1515, he came to Rome as a young man and lived for years as a layman, teaching, visiting the sick in the hospitals, and praying at night in the catacombs. The tradition of his life records that on the eve of Pentecost 1544, while he prayed in the catacomb of San Sebastiano, he felt the fire of the Holy Spirit enter his heart, and from then on his heart burned with a love that people could almost feel. He founded a confraternity to care for poor pilgrims, was ordained a priest in 1551, and began to gather people in a simple room for prayer, the reading of Scripture, the lives of the saints and music. From those meetings came the Congregation of the Oratory, approved by Pope Gregory XIII in 1575. He heard confessions for hours each day, laughed easily, played jokes on himself to keep from pride, and was loved by cardinals and street boys alike. People called him the Apostle of Rome. He died in the early hours of 26 May 1595, after celebrating Corpus Christi, and was canonised in 1622. This week, in the season of Pentecost, he reminds us that the Holy Spirit does not make Christians gloomy. Where the Word is welcomed and the Eucharist is loved, joy follows.

Full homily

Dear friends, in the Gospel today many disciples have left Jesus because his teaching about the bread of life seemed too hard. Jesus does not run after them and change his words. He turns to the Twelve and asks: do you also wish to go away? (John 6:67).

Peter answers: Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God (John 6:68–69). Peter does not understand everything. But he knows that no other road leads to life. That is our faith when we come to the Holy Qurbono.

St Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians received the gospel not as a human word but as what it really is, God's word, which is also at work in you believers (1 Thessalonians 2:13). The word of God works. It changed the Thessalonians, and it changed a young man in Rome named Philip Neri, whom we remember today. On the eve of Pentecost 1544, his life tells us, the fire of the Spirit filled his heart. He became a priest who heard confessions for hours, who loved the Eucharist, and who made a whole city smile.

So when following Jesus is hard, let us not walk away. Let us say with Peter: to whom can we go? And let us ask St Philip Neri to pray for us, that our faith may be joyful, humble and full of the Holy Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, once Jesus said something that was hard to understand, and many people went away. Jesus asked his friends: are you going too? Peter said: no, Lord, we will stay with you! Today we also remember St Philip Neri. He was a priest who loved Jesus so much that he was always happy. He laughed and played with children and told them: be good, if you can! When you love Jesus, you can be joyful too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Holy One of God, you have the words of eternal life, and you give us your flesh as the bread of life. When others turn away, and when we ourselves are tempted to leave, keep us with you. Let us receive your word not as a human word but as the word of God, at work in us. Through the prayers of St Philip Neri, whose heart you set on fire with your Spirit, fill us with joy in your service, make us humble and cheerful, and draw many to you through our love for the Holy Qurbono. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are about how the word of God is received. Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians accepted his preaching “not as a human word but as what it really is, God's word”, and he knows what it cost them. In the Gospel many disciples have turned away after the teaching on the bread of life, and Peter answers for the Twelve: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” The key words are receive and believe.

1 Thessalonians 2:13–16 · Not as a human word but as God's word

Setting.

1 Thessalonians is very probably the oldest writing in the New Testament, sent by Paul from Corinth around AD 50–51. Acts tells how Paul preached in the synagogue of Thessalonica, how some Jews and many Greeks believed, and how opposition forced him to leave by night (Acts 17:1–10). The young church had suffered from the start.

Verses 14–16 contain the sharpest words Paul ever wrote about some of his own people. They must be read with care. Paul was himself a Jew, proud of his people and grieving for them (Romans 9:1–5; 11:1). Here he speaks of particular opponents in Judea who had persecuted the churches there and tried to stop the preaching to the Gentiles, just as the Thessalonians suffered from “your own compatriots”. A few scholars have thought these verses were added later, but most accept them as Paul's. Either way, the Church reads them in the light of his mature teaching in Romans 9–11.

Structure.

  • v. 13: thanksgiving: the word received as God's word, at work in believers.
  • v. 14: the Thessalonians share the sufferings of the churches in Judea.
  • vv. 15–16: Paul's complaint against those who opposed the Gospel and hindered his mission.

Key words.

  • paralabontes / edexasthe, received / accepted: Two verbs. The first is the word for receiving a tradition handed on, used of the Lord's Supper and of the Resurrection (1 Corinthians 11:23; 15:3). The second means to welcome a guest. The Thessalonians did both: they took in the teaching and made it at home in their hearts.
  • energeitai, is at work: From this verb comes our word “energy”. God's word is not a dead letter. It goes on working in those who believe.
  • mimētai, imitators: The Thessalonians did not set out to imitate anyone's sufferings. They found themselves walking the same road as the first churches, and so they became, in Paul's eyes, part of one family.

Verse by verse.

“you accepted it not as a human word but as what it really is, God’s word, which is also at work in you believers.” (2:13)

Paul does not deny that he spoke with a human voice. He says that through his human words God himself was speaking. This is how the Church understands all preaching that is faithful to the apostles.

“For you, brothers and sisters, became imitators of the churches of God in Christ Jesus that are in Judea,” (2:14)

The first Christians in Judea were themselves Jews. Paul's comparison is between persecuted believers in two places, not between two peoples.

“by hindering us from speaking to the Gentiles so that they may be saved.” (2:16)

This is the heart of Paul's complaint: not who these opponents were, but that they tried to block the Gospel. Scholars do not agree what event Paul means by the “wrath” at the end of the verse.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes up verse 13 to describe how the Church receives Scripture: she welcomes it not as a human word but as what it really is, the word of God, and finds in it her nourishment and her strength (CCC 104). On verses 14–16 the Church is clear. The Second Vatican Council teaches that what happened in Christ's passion cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today, and that the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). Paul himself writes later that “God has not rejected his people” (Romans 11:1–2) and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29).

John 6:67–69 · Lord, to whom can we go?

Setting.

These verses close the long sixth chapter of John. Jesus has fed the five thousand and then, in the synagogue at Capernaum (6:59), has spoken of himself as the bread of life and of eating his flesh and drinking his blood. Many disciples say, “This teaching is difficult” (6:60), and many turn back (6:66). This is the first time John names “the twelve”. John has no scene at Caesarea Philippi; this is his form of Peter's confession.

Structure.

  • v. 67: Jesus' question to the Twelve.
  • v. 68: Peter's answer: there is no one else to go to.
  • v. 69: Peter's confession of faith.

Key words.

  • rhēmata zōēs aiōniou, words of eternal life: Peter repeats what Jesus has just said: “The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life” (6:63). The words that drove others away are the very words Peter cannot leave.
  • pepisteukamen kai egnōkamen, we have come to believe and know: Both verbs are in a tense that speaks of a settled state: we have believed and we still believe; we have come to know and we still know. Faith comes first, and knowledge grows from it.
  • ho hagios tou theou, the Holy One of God: A rare title. In the other Gospels it is spoken by an unclean spirit (Mark 1:24; Luke 4:34). Here it is spoken by a disciple in faith. Jesus is the one whom the Father has set apart and sent (10:36).

Verse by verse.

“Do you also wish to go away?” (6:67)

Jesus does not soften his teaching to keep the Twelve. He respects their freedom, and the question is a real one.

“Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” (6:68)

Peter does not say that he understands everything. He has looked at every other road and found none that leads to life.

“We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God.” (6:69)

Some later manuscripts read “the Christ, the Son of the living God”, the wording of Matthew 16:16. The best manuscripts have the shorter and more unusual title, which the NRSV follows.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the first announcement of the Eucharist divided the disciples, just as the announcement of the Passion scandalized them, and that the question “Do you also wish to go away?” goes on through the ages, inviting us to discover that only the Lord has the words of eternal life (CCC 1336). St Augustine noted that Peter says “we have come to believe and know”, not the other way round: we believe in order to understand (Tractates on John 27, in substance). That is the faith we bring to the Holy Qurbono: we do not see how the bread becomes his Body, but we believe his word.

Questions people ask

Does Paul blame the Jewish people for the death of Jesus?

No. Paul, a Jew himself, complains of particular opponents of his day who persecuted believers and hindered his mission. The Church teaches that the passion of Christ cannot be charged against all Jews of that time or of today, and that all sinners were the authors of his sufferings (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598). Paul's own last word on his people is hope: “all Israel will be saved” (Romans 11:26).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Philip Neri (1515–1595), a priest of Rome born in Florence. He founded the Congregation of the Oratory, where people met to pray, to hear the word of God explained simply, and to sing. He was known for his joy and his humour, for long hours in the confessional, and for his love of the Holy Eucharist. He shows what Peter's answer looks like in a life: a man who had found the words of eternal life and could not stop sharing them.

For study and teaching

  • When a teaching of the Church seems hard to me, do I walk away, or do I say with Peter, “Lord, to whom can we go?”
  • How do I listen to the readings in the Holy Qurbono: as a human word, or as God's word at work in me?
