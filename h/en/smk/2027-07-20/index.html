Word for the day

“Elijah is indeed coming first to restore all things.” Mark 9:12

Reflection

Peter, James and John are coming down the mountain, and their heads are still full of what they have seen: the Lord shining like light, and with him Moses and Elijah, “who were talking with Jesus.” Jesus tells them to say nothing “until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead,” and they keep the matter to themselves, “questioning what this rising from the dead could mean.” Then comes the question that has been burning in them: “Why do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?” The scribes were reading the prophet Malachi: “Lo, I will send you the prophet Elijah before the great and terrible day of the Lord comes. He will turn the hearts of parents to their children and the hearts of children to their parents.” They had just seen Elijah on the mountain. Why was he gone again? Jesus answers in two steps. First: “Elijah is indeed coming first to restore all things.” The promise stands. Then: “But I tell you that Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased, as it is written about him.” Elijah has come in the person of John the Baptist, who, as the angel told Zechariah, would go before the Lord “with the spirit and power of Elijah.” And the way of the forerunner prepares the way of the Lord: they did to John whatever they pleased, and so the Son of Man “is to go through many sufferings and be treated with contempt.”

Who was Elijah, and why does the Church keep his memory in the season of Pentecost? He appears suddenly in the First Book of Kings, “the Tishbite, of Tishbe in Gilead,” announcing a drought in the days of King Ahab. He is fed first by ravens, then by a poor widow of Zarephath, whose jar of meal “was not emptied, neither did the jug of oil fail, according to the word of the Lord.” On Mount Carmel he stands alone against the prophets of Baal and asks the people the question that he asks still: “How long will you go limping with two different opinions? If the Lord is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him.” Fire falls and the people fall on their faces. Then Elijah climbs to the top of Carmel, bows with his face between his knees, and sends his servant to look at the sea, seven times. At the seventh time the servant says: “Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person's hand is rising out of the sea.” In July in Kerala, when the whole land waits for the rain and listens to it on the tiles, we know exactly what that cloud is. St James adds a word for ordinary believers: “Elijah was a human being like us, and he prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not rain on the earth. Then he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain and the earth yielded its harvest.”

The same prophet then sits exhausted under a broom tree and begs to die: “It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life, for I am no better than my ancestors.” The great prophet is a person like us. And God does not answer with a speech. An angel touches him and says: “Get up and eat.” A second time: “Get up and eat, otherwise the journey will be too much for you.” “He got up, and ate and drank; then he went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb the mount of God.” There, God is not in the wind, nor the earthquake, nor the fire, but in “a sound of sheer silence.” The Fathers saw in the cake and the jar of water a sign of the bread that sustains the pilgrim, and the Church gives the Holy Qurbono to the dying as food for the last journey. The Second Book of Kings tells that Elijah went up in a whirlwind, with a chariot of fire, and on the mountain of the Transfiguration he appears again, to listen to the Son.

Today's Epistle gives the key. Paul tells the Colossians not to let anyone judge them in matters of food and drink, festivals, new moons or sabbaths: “These are only a shadow of what is to come, but the substance belongs to Christ.” Paul is not despising the feasts of Israel, which the same Paul calls gifts that belong to Israel (Romans 9:4); he is telling Gentile believers not to let any human group make them anxious about what Christ has already fulfilled. The prophets, too, are a shadow, a light thrown ahead by the one who is coming. The voice at the Transfiguration says of Jesus: “Listen to him!” and then, “they saw no one with them any more, but only Jesus.” We honour Elijah, we pray with him, and we learn courage from him; but we follow the one to whom Moses and Elijah pointed, “the head, from whom the whole body, nourished and held together by its ligaments and sinews, grows with a growth that is from God.” Let us ask the Lord to give us the courage of Elijah to choose, the humility of Elijah to rest and to eat what God provides, and the stillness of Elijah to hear a sound of sheer silence.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel the disciples are coming down the mountain after seeing Jesus shining, with Moses and Elijah beside him. They ask: Why do the scribes say that Elijah must come first? Jesus answers: Elijah is indeed coming first to restore all things. But I tell you that Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased (Mark 9:12–13). He means John the Baptist, who came in the spirit and power of Elijah (Luke 1:17).

Who was Elijah? A prophet who stood alone for the living God. On Mount Carmel he asked the people: How long will you go limping with two different opinions? If the Lord is God, follow him (1 Kings 18:21). He prayed fervently, and after a long drought he saw a little cloud no bigger than a person's hand rising out of the sea (1 Kings 18:44). In these monsoon days we know what that cloud means. St James says that Elijah was a human being like us (James 5:17). He also became tired and sad, and God sent him bread and water with the words: Get up and eat, otherwise the journey will be too much for you (1 Kings 19:7).

St Paul tells the Colossians that festivals, new moons and sabbaths are only a shadow of what is to come, but the substance belongs to Christ (Colossians 2:17). The prophets, too, are lights thrown ahead by the one who is coming. When the disciples looked up on the mountain, they saw no one with them any more, but only Jesus (Mark 9:8). We honour Elijah, but we follow Jesus, the head of the body.

Let us ask the Lord for Elijah's courage to choose, his persistence in prayer and his humility to eat the bread God gives. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what Elijah did when the land was dry and there was no rain? He prayed and prayed, and he sent his servant to look at the sea. Seven times! At last the servant saw a tiny cloud, as small as a hand. And soon there was a big rain, just as in our monsoon! Elijah teaches us to keep praying and not to give up. And when Elijah was tired, an angel gave him bread and said, Get up and eat. God gives us strength for our journey, too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you spoke with Moses and Elijah on the holy mountain, and the Father said of you, Listen to him. We thank you for the prophet Elijah, who stood alone for the living God, who prayed that the rain might come, and whom you strengthened with bread on the way. Give us the courage to choose you without dividing our hearts, the perseverance to pray when the heavens seem closed, and the humility to rise and eat when we are weary. Let us not be led by shadows, but hold fast to you, the head from whom the whole body grows with a growth that is from God. Through the prayers of the prophet Elijah, bless the farmers and the fields in this season of rain. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Panchangam remembers Elijah the Prophet, and the Gospel brings him to the mountain road: the disciples come down from the Transfiguration and ask why the scribes say Elijah must come first. Jesus answers that Elijah has come, and that the Son of Man must suffer. The epistle warns the Colossians not to let anyone judge them over food, festivals and sabbaths, because these were a shadow and the substance is Christ. The key words are shadow, substance and restore.

Colossians 2:16–19 · The shadow and the substance

Setting.

Paul writes to a church he has not visited (2:1), warning against teachers who offered a “philosophy” (2:8) of rules, visions and special practices. We do not know exactly what these teachers taught, only what Paul answers. Many scholars think Colossians was written by Paul himself while in prison; some think it was written by a disciple in his name. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture in either case.

Paul does not say that the feasts and sabbaths of Israel were bad. God gave them, and they pointed ahead. His point is that nothing in them may be set up as a rival to Christ, and that the Colossians should not allow anyone to condemn them for not following a particular set of rules.

Structure.

  • v. 16: let no one condemn you over food, drink, festivals, new moons or sabbaths.
  • v. 17: these are a shadow; the substance belongs to Christ.
  • v. 18: let no one disqualify you by false humility, worship of angels and visions.
  • v. 19: such people are not holding fast to the Head, from whom the whole body grows.

Key words.

  • skia, shadow: A shadow is real and shows the shape of a body, but it is not the body. The same image is used in Hebrews 10:1 for the Law, which has “only a shadow of the good things to come”.
  • sōma, body, substance: The word means a body. In verse 17 the real body that casts the shadow is Christ; in verse 19 the same word names the body that grows from the Head, the Church.
  • kephalē, head: Christ is the head of the body, the Church (1:18). A body that is cut off from the head may keep every outward rule and still not live.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore do not let anyone condemn you in matters of food and drink or of observing festivals, new moons, or sabbaths.” (2:16)

These were the signs of Israel's covenant. Paul does not mock them. He tells Gentile believers that they are not to be judged by someone else's standard of observance.

“These are only a shadow of what is to come, but the substance belongs to Christ.” (2:17)

The Old Testament is not cancelled. It was a true shadow, cast by the Christ who was coming. A shadow gives shape and direction, and we recognise the body because we have first seen the shadow.

“and not holding fast to the head, from whom the whole body, nourished and held together by its ligaments and sinews, grows with a growth that is from God.” (2:19)

Life does not come from the joints and sinews by themselves. It flows from the Head through them. The Church lives by being joined to Christ, not by its own machinery.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Old Testament is an indispensable part of Scripture; its books are divinely inspired and keep their permanent value, because the Old Covenant has never been revoked (CCC 121). The Old Law is a preparation for the Gospel (CCC 1964). Reading Colossians in that light, the Church does not set Israel's worship against Christ's but sees it as pointing to him, and she asks that no one use these verses against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4). The words about angels are balanced by what the Church teaches elsewhere: angels watch over us and pray for us (CCC 336), but adoration belongs to God alone (CCC 2096).

Mark 9:9–13 · Elijah has come

Setting.

Jesus and three disciples are coming down from the mountain of the Transfiguration (9:2–8), where Moses and Elijah appeared with him. He tells them to say nothing “until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead”, a phrase they cannot understand. Their question shows what was in their minds: the scribes taught that Elijah must come before the Messiah, as the prophet Malachi had promised (Malachi 4:5–6; 3:23–24 in the Hebrew).

Elijah had been taken up to heaven in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11), and it was believed that he would return. The Gospels identify the one who came in his spirit and power as John the Baptist (Matthew 17:13; Luke 1:17). Mark has already dressed John like Elijah, in a garment of camel's hair with a leather belt (Mark 1:6; compare 2 Kings 1:8).

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: the command to silence, and the disciples' puzzlement about rising from the dead.
  • v. 11: the question: why do the scribes say Elijah must come first?
  • v. 12: the answer: Elijah comes first to restore all things; and the Son of Man must suffer.
  • v. 13: Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased.

Key words.

  • apokathistanei, restore: To set right again, to bring back to the original order. Malachi's promise was that Elijah would turn the hearts of fathers to their children and children to their fathers, and so heal what was broken.
  • exoudenēthē, treated with contempt: Literally “counted as nothing”. A related word is used in the Greek of Psalm 22:6 for the righteous sufferer “despised by the people”. Jesus joins the glory of the Son of Man to the suffering of the righteous one.
  • gegraptai, it is written: Mark's way of saying that what happens is part of God's plan in Scripture, even the suffering. Nothing is out of control.

Verse by verse.

“So they kept the matter to themselves, questioning what this rising from the dead could mean.” (9:10)

The disciples knew that the righteous would rise at the end of time. What they could not grasp was that this Son of Man would rise before the end, after dying. They will not understand until Easter.

“He said to them, “Elijah is indeed coming first to restore all things.” (9:12)

Jesus accepts the scribes' teaching and shows how it is fulfilled. The restoration of all things is not a restoration by force. It begins with a call to repentance.

“But I tell you that Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased, as it is written about him.”” (9:13)

“They did to him” points to the fate of John, whom Herod seized and put to death (Mark 6:17–29). Some think “as it is written” points back to the story of Elijah himself, hunted by Jezebel (1 Kings 19:2, 10). Both Elijah and John were persecuted by rulers who did not want to hear God's word.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that John the Baptist is “Elijah who must come”, the forerunner of the Lord, in whom the fire of the Holy Spirit dwells (CCC 718). It also recalls Elijah as the prophet of fire who by his prayer brought fire from heaven on Mount Carmel (CCC 696) and as the father of the prophets, who teaches us to seek God's face (CCC 2581–2584). At the Transfiguration, Moses and Elijah, the Law and the Prophets, speak with Jesus about his departure, which he was to accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31). The cross and the glory are one story.

Questions people ask

Is John the Baptist Elijah returned?

No. John himself said he was not Elijah in person (John 1:21). Jesus says he is the Elijah who was promised, because John came, as the angel said, “with the spirit and power of Elijah” (Luke 1:17). He had the same mission and the same zeal, but he was a new prophet. The Church does not teach reincarnation.

For study and teaching

  • The Colossians were told not to let anyone condemn them over rules. Do I ever judge others by the outward rules I keep, and forget the Head from whom all life comes?
  • The disciples did not understand what rising from the dead meant. What part of the Gospel do I hear again and again without really understanding it?
