Holy Saturday · The Lord Rests in the Tomb

Word for the day

“So they went with the guard and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone.” Matthew 27:66

Reflection

Holy Saturday is the day of great silence. The body of the Lord lies in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea. The disciples are hidden in fear; the women wait for the sabbath to pass. His enemies, remembering his words about rising on the third day, ask Pilate for a guard; “so they went with the guard and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone.” They seal the tomb, not knowing that they are preparing witnesses to the Resurrection.

The Creed says: “he descended to the dead.” While his body rested in the tomb, Christ went down to the realm of the dead to bring the good news to the just who had waited for him, from Adam onwards. The Eastern Churches love the image of Christ breaking the gates of Sheol and taking Adam and Eve by the hand. Holy Saturday is not empty: it is the hidden victory.

St Paul explains what this means for us: “we have been buried with him by baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life.” Our Baptism was a burial and a resurrection. Today we let die in us whatever must die, so that tonight we may rise with him.

Mary, his Mother, kept faith on this day when everyone else despaired. Let us keep this day in quiet prayer, with her, waiting in hope for the light of Qyomtho.

Homily outline

Theme: “The great silence of the sealed tomb.”

Holy Saturday is the day of silence. The Lord rests in the tomb; the stone is sealed and guarded. Yet the Church professes that he descended to the dead to set free the just who had gone before him (CCC 632–635). We wait, as Mary waited, in faith.

1. The tomb is sealed, but God is not

  • The authorities seal the stone and post a guard, afraid of the third day.
  • The Psalmist trusted that God would not let his faithful one see corruption.
  • Human seals cannot hold the Lord of life.
“For you do not give me up to Sheol, or let your faithful one see the Pit.” Psalm 16:10
“So they went with the guard and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone.” Matthew 27:66

Apply: Name the “sealed tomb” in your life where you have lost hope, and entrust it to Christ.

2. The sign of Jonah

  • Hosea foretold: on the third day he will raise us up.
  • Jesus himself gave the sign: as Jonah three days in the fish, so the Son of Man in the heart of the earth.
  • His rest in the tomb is not the end; it is the night before the dawn.
“After two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up, that we may live before him.” Hosea 6:2
“For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth.” Matthew 12:40

Apply: Spend some time today in quiet waiting, without noise or screens.

3. Buried with him, to rise with him

  • Ezekiel saw God opening graves and bringing his people home.
  • In Baptism we were buried with Christ so that we might walk in newness of life.
  • The grain of wheat must die to bear fruit; our dying to sin prepares Easter joy.
“I am going to open your graves, and bring you up from your graves, O my people; and I will bring you back to the land of Israel.” Ezekiel 37:12
“Therefore we have been buried with him by baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life.” Romans 6:4

Apply: Prepare to renew your baptismal promises at Easter: renounce sin and profess the faith.

Holy Saturday teaches us to wait in faith when God seems silent.

Tomorrow the stone will be rolled away. Let us keep vigil with Mary, Mother of hope.

Take-home resolution: Pray with Mary tonight: the Rosary or the Sorrowful Mysteries, waiting for the Resurrection.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today is the day of the great silence. The Lord's body lies in the tomb. The disciples hide in fear. The stone is sealed and soldiers stand guard. It seems that death has won.

But the ancient Church sang a different song. While his body rested, Christ descended to the dead, to Adam and Eve, to Abraham and the prophets, to all who had waited for him. An old homily imagines him taking Adam by the hand: “Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead.” Holy Saturday is not empty; it is the hidden victory.

St Paul tells us that in Baptism we were buried with Christ, so that we might rise with him. Today is a good day to bury with him whatever is dead in us: a sin we keep returning to, a grudge, a fear. Bring it to the tomb, and leave it there.

And let us stay with Mary. On this day, when everyone else despaired, she kept faith. Let us keep silence, prepare our hearts and our homes, and wait for the night when the stone will be rolled away. Amen.

For children

Children, today Jesus' body is resting in the tomb. A big stone is in front, and soldiers are guarding it. Everyone is quiet and sad.

But Mary, Jesus' mother, kept trusting God. And tonight something wonderful will happen!

Today let's be quiet and get ready for Easter. Let's say: “Jesus, I'm waiting for you!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you rested in the tomb and went down to those who sat in darkness. Bury with you all that is dead in us, keep us in hope with your Mother, and raise us with you to newness of life. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On Holy Saturday the Church stands before a closed tomb, and each of the three readings speaks out of that stillness. The Old Testament lesson is Amos 6:10–14, where someone in a house of death whispers, “Hush!” Paul tells the baptized that they were buried with Christ so that they might walk in newness of life. Matthew shows a stone being sealed and a guard being posted, by men who remember the words about rising better than the disciples do. The line runs from a silence of judgement to a silence of hope, and its key words are hush, buried and sealed.

Amos 6:10–14 · Hush, in the house of the dead

Setting.

Amos was a herdsman from Tekoa in Judah who was sent to preach in the northern kingdom in the days of King Jeroboam II (1:1), in the eighth century before Christ. It was a time of wealth and of military success, and the prophet looked behind both. Chapter 6 opens against “those who are at ease in Zion” (6:1), the people who lie on ivory beds and drink wine from bowls “but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph” (6:6).

Just before our reading the Lord swears that he will hand the city over, and adds, “If ten people remain in one house, they shall die” (6:9). Our verses begin inside such a house. Verse 10 is hard Hebrew and translators differ on its details, but the picture is clear: a relative comes for the dead, and finds one survivor hiding in the back of the house.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the relative, the survivor, and the whispered “Hush!”
  • v. 11: great house and little house alike are broken.
  • v. 12: two absurd questions, and the charge: justice has been turned into poison.
  • v. 13: the boast over two captured towns.
  • v. 14: the Lord raises up a nation against Israel.
  • ¶ The reason for the disaster stands in the middle. The ruin is not bad luck. It is what follows when a people twists justice and then trusts its own strength.

Key words.

  • hās, hush: A word that is almost a sound, like our “sh”. Amos uses it again in 8:3 (“Be silent!”). Habakkuk 2:20, Zephaniah 1:7 and Zechariah 2:13 use it for the silence of all creatures before the Lord.
  • mishpāṭ, tsedāqāh, justice, righteousness: The pair Amos cares about most: “let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream” (5:24). They mean fair dealing in court and right relations in daily life, above all toward the poor.
  • lō’ dābār, qarnayim, Lo-debar, Karnaim: Two towns east of the Jordan, apparently won back in Jeroboam's wars. Amos plays on their names. Lo-debar sounds like “nothing”, and Karnaim means “two horns”, the horn being a sign of strength. They are boasting of a Nothing and of a strength that will not last.

Verse by verse.

“Hush! We must not mention the name of the Lord.” (6:10)

The survivor has been so struck by the disaster that he is afraid to pray. Most commentators take the meaning to be that speaking the Lord's name might draw his judgement down again. It is a terrible moment: the people who once called on that name now dare not say it.

“See, the Lord commands, and the great house shall be shattered to bits, and the little house to pieces.” (6:11)

No one's house is safe, rich or poor, when a whole society has gone wrong.

“Do horses run on rocks? Does one plow the sea with oxen?” (6:12)

A note on the text. The usual Hebrew reads as “Does one plow with oxen?”, which would not be an absurd question. By dividing one Hebrew word differently, most modern translators get “the sea with oxen”, as the NRSV does, and the Malayalam POC has the same sense. To turn justice into poison, says Amos, is as much against nature as ploughing the sea.

“Have we not by our own strength taken Karnaim for ourselves?” (6:13)

Israel's true sin here is the words “by our own strength”. The God who gave the victories has dropped out of the story.

“Indeed, I am raising up against you a nation, O house of Israel, says the Lord, the God of hosts,” (6:14)

Amos does not name the nation; history gives the name of Assyria, which took Samaria in 722/721 BC (2 Kings 17:6). The map in this verse, from Lebo-hamath to the Arabah, is the very territory that Jeroboam had won back (2 Kings 14:25). What they boasted of is what they will lose.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls the fierce words of Amos against those who buy the poor for silver (Amos 8:6) and says that Jesus does not soften them (CCC 2449). The prophet's first meaning must stand: it is a warning about injustice and false security, and it speaks to every age. Amos also does not end here. His last chapter promises, “On that day I will raise up the booth of David that is fallen” (9:11), and in the Acts of the Apostles James hears that promise at work when the Gentiles are welcomed into the Church (Acts 15:16). On this day of all days, the Christian ear also hears “Hush” differently. An ancient homily for Holy Saturday, quoted by the Catechism, begins: “Today a great silence reigns on earth” (CCC 635). That silence is not fear. The King is asleep, and the name of the Lord will soon be spoken again in joy.

Romans 6:1–11 · Buried with him, alive to God

Setting.

Most scholars think Paul wrote to the Romans from Corinth around AD 57, to a Church he had not founded and hoped to visit. In chapter 5 he has said that “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (5:20). That raises an obvious objection, and he states it himself: then why not go on sinning?

His answer does not begin with a rule but with an event. He reminds them of what happened to them in Baptism. For Paul, the believer's life has a before and an after, and the line between them runs through the death and burial of Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the objection and the reply: “By no means!”
  • vv. 3–4: baptized into his death, buried with him, so as to walk in newness of life.
  • vv. 5–7: united with him in death and resurrection; the old self crucified.
  • vv. 8–10: Christ will never die again; death has no more dominion over him.
  • v. 11: the conclusion: count yourselves dead to sin and alive to God.

Key words.

  • synetaphēmen, we have been buried with: One Greek word built on syn, “with”. Paul uses such “with” words again and again in this chapter: buried with, crucified with, live with. The Christian life is lived in company with Christ.
  • symphytoi, united: In verse 5 the word has the sense of “grown together”, as parts of a living thing grow together. Union with Christ is not only an imitation from outside.
  • ephapax, once for all: Christ's death is never repeated, because it is complete (v. 10). Hebrews uses the same word of his entry into the sanctuary (Hebrews 9:12).

Verse by verse.

“Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?” (6:3)

The Greek word for baptize means to plunge or immerse. The Catechism says that the plunging into the water symbolizes burial into Christ's death (CCC 1214).

“Therefore we have been buried with him by baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in newness of life.” (6:4)

This is the verse for today. Burial is not the end of the sentence, which ends in a way of walking. “Walk” is Paul's ordinary word for daily conduct.

“We know that our old self was crucified with him so that the body of sin might be destroyed,” (6:6)

“Body of sin” does not mean that the human body is evil; our bodies will rise. It means the whole person as held under the power of sin. That self has been put to death with Christ.

“For whoever has died is freed from sin.” (6:7)

The Greek says literally “has been justified from sin”. Sin is pictured as a master whose claim ends at death.

“So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus.” (6:11)

Paul asks for a daily reckoning. What God has done in Baptism must be taken up by grace, in choices made every day.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this passage for Baptism: the believer enters into communion with Christ's death, is buried with him, and rises with him (CCC 1227). It also says that Baptism, whose original and full sign is immersion, signifies the descent into the tomb of the Christian who dies to sin with Christ in order to live a new life (CCC 628), and it uses verse 11 in its teaching on life in Christ (CCC 1694). St Cyril of Jerusalem told the newly baptized that the water of salvation was at once their grave and their mother (Mystagogical Catecheses 2, in substance). This is why Holy Saturday is the day to read this page: the tomb of Christ and the font of the Church belong together.

Matthew 27:62–66 · The stone is sealed

Setting.

Only Matthew tells of the guard at the tomb. The Lord has been buried by Joseph of Arimathea, and Mary Magdalene and the other Mary sit opposite the tomb (27:57–61). Matthew then names the next day in an unusual way, “after the day of Preparation”. The Preparation is Friday, so this is the Sabbath.

Many scholars think that Matthew tells this story partly to answer a claim that was going round in his own day, that the disciples stole the body (28:13–15). He takes up the thread again after the Resurrection, so the two passages should be read together. The people in this scene are particular leaders in a particular moment. Neither Matthew nor the Church lays the events of the Passion on the Jewish people (CCC 597–598; Nostra Aetate 4).

Structure.

  • v. 62: the chief priests and the Pharisees go to Pilate.
  • v. 63: they recall the words “After three days I will rise again.”
  • v. 64: the request, and the fear of a “last deception”.
  • v. 65: Pilate's short answer.
  • v. 66: the tomb is secured, the stone is sealed, and the guard is posted.
  • ¶ This is the end of the Passion in Matthew. Everything human power can do has now been done.

Key words.

  • planos, planē, impostor, deception: From a verb meaning to lead astray. It is the leaders' verdict on Jesus. Matthew lets the reader weigh it against what the angel says at the tomb (28:5–6).
  • koustōdia, guard: A Latin word (custodia) in Greek letters. Pilate's “You have a guard” can also be taken in Greek as “Take a guard”, so scholars discuss whether these are Temple police or Roman soldiers. The mention of the governor in 28:14 inclines many to think they were Roman.
  • sphragisantes, sealing: Probably a cord across the stone, fixed with clay or wax and an official mark, so that any tampering would show. The seal does not make the stone heavier. It makes it a matter of public record.

Verse by verse.

“Sir, we remember what that impostor said while he was still alive, ‘After three days I will rise again.’” (27:63)

The ones who remember are the Lord's opponents. Nowhere on this day does Matthew say that the disciples recalled his promise (16:21; 17:23; 20:19). It is a small and humbling irony.

“and the last deception would be worse than the first.” (27:64)

Without meaning to, they state what is at stake. If he rose, all that he taught stands. Paul will say the same from the other side (1 Corinthians 15:14).

“Pilate said to them, “You have a guard of soldiers; go, make it as secure as you can.”” (27:65)

“As secure as you can”: the phrase hangs in the air. The reader knows that there is a limit to what anyone can make secure.

“So they went with the guard and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone.” (27:66)

The last line of the Passion. By the next chapter's dawn the stone is rolled away, and “the guards shook and became like dead men” (28:4). The living guards lie as if dead, at the tomb of the One who was dead and now lives.

How the Church reads it.

St John Chrysostom, preaching on Matthew, points out that these precautions only strengthen the proof: with a seal and a guard, no one can claim that the body was quietly removed (in substance). The Church's own attention on this day goes to the Lord who lies in the tomb. The Catechism says that his stay there is the real link between his state of suffering and his glorious state after the Resurrection (CCC 625), and it names this day as “God's great sabbath rest” after the fulfilment of man's salvation (CCC 624). The body in the tomb did not see corruption, because the divine person kept hold of both soul and body in death (CCC 627, 630).

Questions people ask

What does “he descended to the dead” mean?

The word in Scripture is Sheol or Hades, the realm of all the dead, who were deprived of the vision of God while they awaited the Redeemer (CCC 632–633). Jesus truly died and went there as Saviour, proclaiming the good news to those who had waited (1 Peter 3:19; 4:6). The Catechism is careful: he did not go down to deliver the damned or to destroy the hell of damnation, but to set the just who had gone before him at liberty (CCC 633). Eastern icons show him breaking the gates and raising Adam and Eve by the hand. So Holy Saturday is not an empty day between two great days. It is the hidden part of the victory.

Why is such a harsh prophecy read on Holy Saturday?

Amos is first of all a warning against injustice, and it should be heard as one. But set beside the closed tomb, its picture of a house of death, of whispered fear, and of a name no one dares to say, helps us feel what a world without the Resurrection would be. The Christian hears it knowing that this silence will be broken.

If Baptism means we have died to sin, why do we still sin?

Baptism truly takes away original sin and all personal sin, but it leaves in us certain consequences of sin, among them an inclination to sin that Tradition calls concupiscence. This is left for us “to wrestle with” by the grace of Christ (CCC 1263–1264). That is why Paul, right after saying that we have died to sin, goes on to say, “do not let sin exercise dominion” (6:12). We become by grace what we already are by Baptism, and the Sacrament of Reconciliation is there for the road.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Antiochene and Syriac tradition loves to look into Sheol on this day. Mar Aprem, in his Nisibene Hymns, gives Death himself a voice: he complains that this one dead man has undone his kingdom, and that those he held have heard a voice calling them out (in substance). The tomb is seen from below, as a defeat for Death. The Church keeps the day in quiet with Mary, the mother of the Lord, and as evening comes it prepares for Qyomtho. This is a good day for families to speak about their own Baptism, to learn its date, and to pray for those they have buried.

For study and teaching

  • Amos accuses people who say “by our own strength”. Where in my life do I speak of God's gifts as my own achievement?
  • Do I know the date of my Baptism? What would it mean this year to “walk in newness of life” in one concrete thing?
  • The opponents in Matthew remembered the Lord's words about rising, and the disciples seemed to have forgotten them. Which of his promises have I let slip out of memory?
  • How does the belief that Christ went down to the dead change the way I pray for my own departed?
