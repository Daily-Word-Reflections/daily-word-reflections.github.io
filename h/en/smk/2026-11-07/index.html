Word for the day

“They came to Philip, who was from Bethsaida in Galilee, and said to him, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.”” John 12:21

Reflection

The Church in Corinth was splitting into parties: “I belong to Paul,” “I belong to Apollos,” “I belong to Cephas,” “I belong to Christ.” Paul's answer is sharp: “Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you?” Every preacher is only a servant. Paul was sent “to proclaim the gospel, and not with eloquent wisdom, so that the cross of Christ might not be emptied of its power.”

In the Gospel some Greeks come to Philip with a request that every parish should hear: “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” People do not come to church to see us, our quarrels or our cleverness, but to see him. A united community that points away from itself to Christ is the best answer to that request. The martyrs we remember today gave the same answer with their lives.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Church in Corinth was splitting into parties: I belong to Paul, I belong to Apollos, I belong to Cephas, I belong to Christ. Paul's answer is sharp: has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you (1 Corinthians 1:12–13)?

In the Gospel some Greeks who came up for the feast ask Philip: sir, we wish to see Jesus (John 12:21). This is what the world wants from us: not our parties, but to see Jesus.

Today the Church remembers the 33 martyrs of Melitene, who, tradition says, gave their lives together for the one Christ. This week take one step to heal a division in the parish or in the family; then others will see Jesus in us. Amen.

For children

Children, some people said: we wish to see Jesus. Let others see Jesus through your kindness!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you are not divided. Heal the divisions in our families and parishes, keep us faithful like the martyrs, and let all who wish to see you find you in us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church remembers the 33 Martyrs of Melitene, soldiers honoured in the Eastern calendars, of whom little is known for certain. The readings ask what people see when they look at the Church. Paul pleads with the Corinthians to stop dividing into parties, because Christ is not divided. In the Gospel, some Greeks come with a request every parish should hear: “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.”

1 Corinthians 1:10–17 · Is Christ divided?

Setting.

Right after his opening thanksgiving, Paul goes straight to the first problem at Corinth: quarrels. People from the household of Chloe have told him that the community is splitting into groups around favourite preachers. Paul had founded the church; Apollos, an eloquent teacher from Alexandria, had worked there after him (Acts 18:24–28); some may have known Peter, or claimed his authority.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the appeal: no divisions, the same mind and purpose.
  • vv. 11–12: the report and the four slogans.
  • v. 13: three questions: Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptised in Paul's name?
  • vv. 14–16: Paul is glad he baptised few of them.
  • v. 17: his mission: to preach the gospel, so that the cross is not emptied of its power.

Key words.

  • schismata, divisions: Literally “tears”, as in a torn garment (the same word in Mark 2:21). From it comes our word “schism”.
  • katartizō, to be united, restored: The word used for mending fishing nets (Mark 1:19). Unity is something to be repaired with patience.
  • kenoō, to empty: If the gospel depends on the preacher's cleverness, the cross is “emptied” of its power. The power is in Christ crucified, not in the messenger.

Verse by verse.

“Now I appeal to you, brothers and sisters, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you be in agreement and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same purpose.” (1:10)
“Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” (1:13)

Three questions with one obvious answer. Christians belong to Christ, who was crucified for them, and into whose name they were baptised.

“For Christ did not send me to baptize but to proclaim the gospel, and not with eloquent wisdom, so that the cross of Christ might not be emptied of its power.” (1:17)

Paul is not belittling baptism, which he himself administered (vv. 14–16). He is saying that his particular mission was preaching, and that no minister should become the centre.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that division among Christians openly contradicts the will of Christ, scandalises the world and damages the preaching of the Gospel (Unitatis Redintegratio 1). The Catechism speaks of the wounds to the unity of the Church and of the call to pray and work for the unity of all Christians (CCC 817–822).

John 12:20–22 · Sir, we wish to see Jesus

Setting.

It is the last Passover of Jesus' ministry. He has just entered Jerusalem to the crowd's acclaim, and the Pharisees say, “Look, the world has gone after him!” (12:19). At once John shows “the world” arriving: some Greeks, probably Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel, come up for the feast. Their coming leads Jesus to say, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified” (12:23).

Structure.

  • v. 20: Greeks among the pilgrims.
  • v. 21: they approach Philip: “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.”
  • v. 22: Philip tells Andrew, and both tell Jesus.

Key words.

  • Hellēnes, Greeks: Not Greek-speaking Jews but Gentiles. In John, their arrival is a sign that Jesus will draw “all people” to himself (12:32).
  • idein, to see: In John, “seeing” Jesus often means coming to faith. Philip himself had once said, “Come and see” (1:46).
  • kyrie, sir, lord: A polite address, but in John the word keeps growing in meaning until Thomas says, “My Lord and my God!” (20:28).

Verse by verse.

“They came to Philip, who was from Bethsaida in Galilee, and said to him, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.”” (12:21)

Philip and Andrew both have Greek names and come from Bethsaida (1:44), a town in a mixed region. Perhaps that is why the Greeks come to them.

“Philip went and told Andrew; then Andrew and Philip went and told Jesus.” (12:22)

The disciples do not keep the seekers for themselves; they bring them to Jesus. Andrew has done this before: he brought his brother Simon (1:41–42) and the boy with the loaves (6:8–9).

How the Church reads it.

The coming of the Greeks shows that the Kingdom is meant for all nations: first announced to Israel, it is open to people of every nation (CCC 543). In some churches these words, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus”, are written inside the pulpit where only the preacher can read them, as a reminder of what the people have come for.

Questions people ask

Why does John not tell us whether the Greeks met Jesus?

Because the answer Jesus gives is about the cross. From now on, the nations will “see” him when he is lifted up (12:32). We too see Jesus by faith, through the word and the Holy Qurbono, and in a Church that points to him.

For study and teaching

  • When people look at my parish, or my family, do they see our divisions or do they see Jesus?
  • Like Philip and Andrew, whom could I bring to Jesus this week?
