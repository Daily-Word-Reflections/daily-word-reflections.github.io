Word for the day

“And blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of what was spoken to her by the Lord.” Luke 1:45

Reflection

After the angel leaves, Mary does not stay at home to enjoy her secret. St Luke says she “set out and went with haste” to the hill country to visit Elizabeth. The first thing the Mother of God does, with the Son of God in her womb, is to serve. Faith that has received Christ goes out.

When Mary greets Elizabeth, the child John leaps in his mother's womb and Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit. John, who will later point to the Lamb of God, recognises him already before birth. Elizabeth cries out: “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb… why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?” With these words the Church has greeted Mary ever since.

Mary answers with the Magnificat: “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior.” Her song is full of the Old Testament, especially Hannah's song: “My heart exults in the LORD… He raises up the poor from the dust” (1 Samuel 2:1, 8). God looks on the lowly, scatters the proud, fills the hungry, and remembers his mercy to Abraham. Mary sings not about herself but about God.

The readings also praise faithful women: Rebekah's kindness at the well (Genesis 24), the capable wife of Proverbs 31, whose strength is the fear of the Lord. Mary is blessed above all because she believed. And St Paul reminds us that God “desires everyone to be saved” (1 Timothy 2:4). The Visitation is the first Christian mission: Christ carried to a home, and a home filled with joy.

Homily outline

Theme: “Carrying Christ to others.”

Mary has just said “yes” to God. Her first act is not to stay home in wonder but to set out in haste to help her elderly cousin. Where Mary goes, Christ goes, and the Spirit fills a home with joy.

  • At her greeting the child leaps in Elizabeth's womb, and Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit.
  • Like Abraham's servant led by God to Rebekah's house, Mary is led to the home that needs her.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, what do we do when we receive great news? We pick up the phone and tell someone. When Mary received the greatest news in history, she did something more: she set out and went with haste to the hill country, to help her elderly cousin Elizabeth.

Imagine the scene. Mary greets Elizabeth, and the baby in Elizabeth's womb leaps for joy. John recognises Jesus before either of them is born. Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, says: “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb.” Every time we pray the Hail Mary, we repeat her words.

Then Mary sings: “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior.” She does not sing about herself. She sings about a God who looks on the lowly, lifts up the poor, fills the hungry with good things, and sends the proud away empty. Like Hannah before her, she knows that God works through the small and the humble.

On Tuesday the Twenty-Five Days Nombu begins. Let us keep it like Mary: not only by giving up food, but by going out. Visit someone who is old, sick or lonely. Carry Christ to them, as Mary did. And after every Holy Qurbono, remember: you leave this church as Mary left Nazareth, carrying the Lord within you. Amen.

For children

Children, Mary had a cousin called Elizabeth who was going to have a baby. Mary walked a long way over the hills to help her.

When Mary said hello, baby John jumped inside Elizabeth's tummy, because Jesus was there inside Mary! John was so happy.

Before Christmas, let's be like Mary: help someone at home, visit Grandma or Grandpa, and bring Jesus' joy. Let's say: “I will bring Jesus to others!”

Prayer

Lord God, Mary carried your Son in haste to Elizabeth and filled her house with joy. As we begin the fast before Yaldo, make us carry Christ to those who need him, and let our souls, with hers, magnify your name. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sunday of the Visitation of Mary, Mother of God, to Elizabeth, the Suboro season moves from Nazareth to the hill country of Judea. The evening Gospel already tells of the meeting (Luke 1:39–45). The Old Testament lessons gather women through whom God worked: Rebekah at the well, who runs to serve a stranger and is chosen to carry the promise (Genesis 24:15–27); Hannah, who sings when God gives her a son (1 Samuel 2:1–11); the capable woman whose “strength and dignity are her clothing” (Proverbs 31:25); and Isaiah's promise that the deaf will hear and “the meek shall obtain fresh joy in the Lord” (Isaiah 29:19). The Catholic Epistle calls the Church “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation” (1 Peter 2:9).

The three readings studied here form one song. Hannah sings of the God who lifts up the poor; Paul proclaims the God who “desires everyone to be saved” through one mediator; Mary, carrying that mediator in her womb, sings Hannah's song again in a new key. Visit, joy and praise are the key words of this Sunday.

1 Samuel 2:1–11 · Hannah's song

Setting.

Hannah was one of the two wives of Elkanah, and she had no children. Year after year at the shrine of Shiloh she wept, and she prayed so intensely that the priest Eli thought she was drunk (1 Samuel 1:12–14). God heard her, and she bore Samuel. When the child was weaned she brought him to Shiloh and gave him to the Lord. Her song is her response.

Many scholars think the song was an older hymn of Israel, placed on Hannah's lips because it fits her story. It ends with a king and an “anointed one” (v. 10), though Israel had no king yet; it looks forward to David, and beyond him to the Messiah.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Hannah's joy: “My heart exults in the Lord”.
  • vv. 2–3: there is no Holy One like the Lord; a warning to the proud.
  • vv. 4–8: the great reversals: the mighty broken, the feeble strengthened; the barren bears seven; the poor raised from the dust.
  • vv. 9–10: the Lord guards his faithful, judges the earth and strengthens his anointed.
  • v. 11: Elkanah goes home; the boy Samuel stays to serve the Lord.

Key words.

  • qeren, horn, strength: In v. 1, “my strength is exalted” is literally “my horn is lifted up”, an image from a wild ox raising its horns. The same word returns in v. 10, “the power of his anointed”. Zechariah's song uses it for Christ, the “horn of salvation” (Luke 1:69, NRSV “a mighty savior”).
  • ṣûr, Rock: A title of God in the Psalms and Deuteronomy: firm, safe, unchanging. The POC renders it “a sure refuge”.
  • māšîaḥ, anointed: The first time in the Bible that a king is called God's anointed. In Greek this is christos. Hannah's song, at the beginning of the books of Samuel, already points to the Messiah.

Verse by verse.

“Hannah prayed and said, “My heart exults in the Lord; my strength is exalted in my God.” (2:1)

Hannah's prayer begins with joy in God himself, not first in the gift. Mary will begin the same way: “my spirit rejoices in God my Savior”.

“He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes and inherit a seat of honor.” (2:8)

The ash heap outside a town was where beggars and lepers sat. God's way is to turn the world's order upside down. Psalm 113:7–8 repeats these words almost exactly.

“The Lord will judge the ends of the earth; he will give strength to his king, and exalt the power of his anointed.” (2:10)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names Hannah, the mother of Samuel, among the holy women of the Old Covenant whose mission prepared for that of Mary (CCC 489). Many Fathers and later commentators have noticed how closely Mary's Magnificat follows Hannah's song: the joy in God, the lowly lifted up, the hungry filled, the proud brought down. In Hannah, a barren woman who gives her long-awaited son to God, the Church sees a figure of Mary who offers her Son, and of the Church who rejoices in the God who lifts up the poor.

1 Timothy 2:4–10 · God desires everyone to be saved

Setting.

In the First Letter to Timothy, Paul instructs his co-worker, who leads the church at Ephesus, on the order of community life. Chapter 2 begins with a call to pray “for everyone, for kings and all who are in high positions” (2:1–2). Today's verses give the reason: the God to whom we pray wants all to be saved. Then Paul speaks of how men and women should pray and present themselves in the assembly.

Structure.

  • v. 4: God's will: everyone saved, everyone knowing the truth.
  • vv. 5–6: a short creed: one God, one mediator, Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all.
  • v. 7: Paul appointed herald, apostle and teacher of the Gentiles.
  • v. 8: men are to pray with holy hands lifted up, without anger.
  • vv. 9–10: women are to adorn themselves with modesty and good works, not with costly display.

Key words.

  • mesitēs, mediator: One who stands between two parties to reconcile them. Christ can be the one mediator because he is both God and “himself human”.
  • antilytron, ransom: The only place this form is found in the New Testament. It echoes Jesus' own words: the Son of Man came “to give his life a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). Here Paul says “for all”.
  • kosmios, modest, well-ordered: Related to kosmos (order, ornament). True adornment is an ordered life, shown in good works.

Verse by verse.

“who desires everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.” (2:4)

This verse is the reason for praying for everyone, even rulers who persecute. No one lies outside God's saving desire.

“For there is one God; there is also one mediator between God and humankind, Christ Jesus, himself human,” (2:5)

“One God” is the faith of Israel (Deuteronomy 6:4). “One mediator” is the faith of the Church. Christ's mediation does not compete with anyone; it makes room for others to share in it.

“I desire, then, that in every place the men should pray, lifting up holy hands without anger or argument;” (2:8)

Standing with lifted hands was the common posture of prayer for Jews and early Christians. Prayer and quarrelling do not go together.

“but with good works, as is proper for women who profess reverence for God.” (2:10)

The point of vv. 9–10 is not a dress code but where beauty is found. Mary, the handmaid of the Lord, is the model of a woman adorned with faith and service.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes 2:4 to show that God wants all to be saved and to know the truth, which is why the Church is missionary (CCC 74, 851). On the one mediator, the Second Vatican Council teaches that Mary's maternal role “in no way obscures or diminishes this unique mediation of Christ, but rather shows its power”; it flows from Christ's merits and depends entirely on him (Lumen Gentium 60; CCC 970). Mary at the Visitation is the clearest picture of this: she brings Christ to Elizabeth, and all the joy comes from him.

Luke 1:39–56 · The Visitation and the Magnificat

Setting.

Right after the Annunciation, Mary goes to visit Elizabeth, whose pregnancy the angel had given her as a sign (1:36). Luke does not name the town; an ancient tradition places it at Ein Karem, a few kilometres west of Jerusalem. From Nazareth that is a journey of several days.

Luke tells the story with echoes of 2 Samuel 6, when King David brought the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem. David asked, “How can the ark of the Lord come into my care?” (2 Samuel 6:9); Elizabeth asks, “why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?” David danced before the ark; John leaps in the womb. The ark stayed in the house of Obed-edom three months (2 Samuel 6:11); Mary stays with Elizabeth about three months. Several Fathers and many Catholic commentators see Mary as the new ark, carrying God's presence.

Structure.

  • vv. 39–40: the journey and the greeting.
  • vv. 41–45: Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, blesses Mary and her child and praises her faith.
  • vv. 46–50: the Magnificat, first part: what God has done for Mary.
  • vv. 51–55: second part: what God does for his people: reversals, mercy, the promise to Abraham.
  • v. 56: Mary stays about three months and returns home.

Key words.

  • meta spoudēs, with haste: Not nervous hurry but eager readiness. The Greek word also carries the sense of zeal. Love does not delay.
  • eskirtēsen, leaped: The same verb is used in the Greek Old Testament for Jacob and Esau struggling in Rebekah's womb (Genesis 25:22). Here the child leaps not in struggle but “for joy” (v. 44). John begins his mission as forerunner before he is born.
  • megalynei, magnifies: To make great, to declare great. Mary cannot make God greater; she makes his greatness known. The Latin first word, Magnificat, gives the song its name.
  • tapeinōsis, lowliness: Humble condition, low estate. God looks on what the world overlooks, as he looked on Hannah's misery (1 Samuel 1:11).

Verse by verse.

“When Elizabeth heard Mary’s greeting, the child leaped in her womb. And Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit” (1:41)

Mary speaks, and the Spirit comes. Where Mary brings Christ, the Holy Spirit fills hearts with joy.

“And why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?” (1:43)

“My Lord” is the title the Old Testament gives to God and to the Messiah king (Psalm 110:1). Elizabeth is the first to call Mary, in effect, Mother of God.

“And blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of what was spoken to her by the Lord.”” (1:45)

Mary's first blessedness is her faith. Jesus himself will say the same: blessed are those who hear the word of God and obey it (11:28).

“for he has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant. Surely, from now on all generations will call me blessed;” (1:48)

A prophecy fulfilled every time the Church sings to Mary or prays the Hail Mary.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that John was “filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother's womb” and that the Visitation was a visit of God to his people (CCC 717). Elizabeth's greeting, joined to Gabriel's, forms the first part of the Hail Mary (CCC 2676). The Magnificat is “the song both of the Mother of God and of the Church” (CCC 2619), and the words “all generations will call me blessed” are the foundation of the Church's devotion to Mary (CCC 971).

St Ambrose, commenting on this Gospel, wished that the soul of Mary might be in each believer to magnify the Lord, and her spirit in each to rejoice in God (in substance). The Magnificat is not only Mary's song but the song of every lowly person whom God has visited.

Questions people ask

If Christ is the “one mediator”, why do we ask Mary to pray for us?

Because her prayer, like the prayers we ask of one another, depends entirely on Christ and leads to him. The Church teaches that Mary's role shows the power of Christ's mediation rather than diminishing it (Lumen Gentium 60; CCC 970). At the Visitation she brings Christ, and Elizabeth is filled with the Spirit.

Did Mary herself compose the Magnificat?

Luke presents it as Mary's song, and the Church prays it as hers. It is woven from the Psalms and Hannah's song, the prayers a devout young woman of Israel would know by heart. Mary prays the Scriptures and makes them her own.

Why does the Magnificat speak of the mighty brought down? Is it a political song?

It speaks of God's way of acting in history, which overturns pride and lifts up the lowly. It is not a call to hatred but a warning to the proud and a hope for the poor, and it calls the Church to share God's care for the poor (CCC 2448).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Malankara faithful have a deep love for St Mary, the Mother of God, shown in many prayers and in the fast of the first eight days of September before the feast of her birth. On this Sunday the Church sees in Mary the model of the one who receives Christ and at once goes out to serve. In the Holy Qurbono we receive the same Lord whom Mary carried to Elizabeth; the Visitation teaches us to carry him from the altar into the homes of the sick, the elderly and the lonely. As the Suboro season moves toward Yaldo, many Malankara families prepare by prayer and fasting, so that their hearts, like Elizabeth's house, may be ready to welcome him.

For study and teaching

  • 1 Timothy 2:4 says God desires everyone to be saved. For whom, outside my circle, should I pray this week?
  • Mary went “with haste” to serve. Who is the Elizabeth near me, waiting for a visit?
  • Which line of the Magnificat do I most need to make my own in this Suboro season, on the way to Yaldo?
