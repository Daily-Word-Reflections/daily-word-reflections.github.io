Word for the day

“I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live” John 11:25

Reflection

The Epistle today is one of the hardest pages St Paul ever wrote, and it is wise to read it whole, in its place. Paul is beginning his letter to the Romans by showing that the whole human race needs the Gospel. The root of all that goes wrong, he says, is not first a bad deed but a false worship: “they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever! Amen.” When God is pushed out of the centre, something else is put there: pleasure, money, power, the self. And three times Paul repeats a frightening phrase: “God gave them up.” God does not need to send a thunderbolt. He respects our choice and lets us have what we have chosen, and the choice bears its own fruit. A heart that will not acknowledge God slowly loses its sense of what is true and good, in the body, in the mind and in every relationship. St Augustine saw this in his own youth: he had looked for beauty and joy in created things instead of in the One who made them, and found only restlessness (Confessions 10.27, in substance).

Among the fruits of that exchange Paul names sexual acts between persons of the same sex. The Church, faithful to the word of God, teaches that such acts are contrary to the Creator's plan and can never be approved, because the gift of sexuality belongs within the faithful marriage of a man and a woman, open to life (Catechism 2357). But we must not stop reading there, as if this page were about other people. Paul's list goes on: “They were filled with every kind of wickedness, evil, covetousness, malice. Full of envy, murder, strife, deceit, craftiness, they are gossips, slanderers, God-haters, insolent, haughty, boastful, inventors of evil, rebellious toward parents, foolish, faithless, heartless, ruthless.” Gossip, envy, boasting, disobedience to parents: there is no one in the church this morning who is not somewhere on this list. And the very next sentence of the letter, which we do not read today, turns on the reader: “Therefore you have no excuse, whoever you are, when you judge others.” The Catechism asks us to receive every person who carries this struggle with respect, compassion and sensitivity, avoiding every sign of unjust discrimination (Catechism 2358), and it calls every one of us, beginning with ourselves, to chastity and conversion (Catechism 2348, 2359). Paul writes this dark chapter only so that the light of chapter 3 may shine: all have sinned, and all may be justified by God's grace through faith in Christ.

The Gospel brings that light into a house of mourning. Lazarus has been in the tomb four days; Bethany is full of neighbours, “many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them about their brother”, a tender picture of a community that shows up for a funeral, as our Kerala villages still do. Martha goes out to meet Jesus with grief and faith mixed together: “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him.” Jesus answers with one of the greatest sentences in the Gospel: “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?” And Martha, standing near the tomb, makes a confession as full as Peter's: “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” Here is the answer to Romans 1. The human heart that exchanged the Creator for creatures ends in the tomb; but the Creator has come to the tomb in person. Whatever has died in us, whatever we have exchanged and lost, the Lord who stands at Lazarus's grave says to us too: even now. In every Holy Qurbono we pray for our departed, and we receive the One who is the resurrection and the life.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul shows us today what happens when people turn away from God: they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator (Romans 1:25). When God is not at the centre, something else takes his place, and life slowly falls into disorder.

Paul's list of sins is long, and it includes all of us: gossips, slanderers, boastful, rebellious toward parents (Romans 1:29–30). We must not read this page and think of other people. Paul says in the next chapter: you have no excuse, whoever you are, when you judge others (Romans 2:1). We all need mercy.

And mercy comes. In the Gospel Jesus stands at the tomb of Lazarus. Martha says: even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him (John 11:22). Jesus answers: I am the resurrection and the life (John 11:25). He asks her, and he asks us: Do you believe this?

Let us answer with Martha: Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God (John 11:27). Whatever has died in us, whatever we have lost by turning away, Jesus can raise. Let us put him back at the centre today, and let us trust him with our departed. Amen.

For children

Children, sometimes people love things more than they love God: toys, phones, money. St Paul says that when we put things in God's place, everything goes wrong. In the Gospel, Jesus' friend Lazarus had died, and his sister Martha was crying. Jesus told her: I am the resurrection and the life. Jesus is stronger than death! Let us always keep Jesus in the first place in our hearts, and he will keep us safe for ever.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the resurrection and the life. Too often we exchange the truth about God for a lie and serve created things rather than the Creator; forgive us, and do not give us up to ourselves. Take from us the sins of the tongue and of the heart, gossip, envy, pride and every disorder of the body, and give us clean hearts. When we stand before the tombs of our hopes and of our loved ones, give us Martha's faith to say: even now. Raise us to new life, and bring our departed into the light of your face. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings set side by side the darkness that follows when God is pushed aside and the light that shines when he is met. Paul describes a humanity that “exchanged the truth about God for a lie” and was handed over to its own desires. At Bethany, near the tomb of Lazarus, Martha meets Jesus and hears, “I am the resurrection and the life.” Paul's picture of death is answered by Christ's word of life. The key words are exchange and believe.

Romans 1:24–32 · They exchanged the truth about God for a lie

Setting.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth, probably around AD 57–58, to a church he had not yet visited. After stating his theme, that the Gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who has faith (1:16–17), he shows that all, Gentiles and Jews alike, need that salvation (1:18–3:20). He begins with the pagan world: God can be known through the things he has made (1:20), but people did not honour him as God (1:21) and exchanged his glory for images (1:23). Today's verses describe what follows.

The thought has a Jewish background, above all Wisdom 13–14, which says that the worship of idols is “the beginning and cause and end of every evil” (Wisdom 14:27). Then, in 2:1, Paul turns on the reader who has been nodding in agreement: “you have no excuse, whoever you are, when you judge others.”

Structure.

  • vv. 24–25: God gave them up to impurity, because they exchanged the truth about God for a lie.
  • vv. 26–27: God gave them up to degrading passions.
  • v. 28: God gave them up to a debased mind.
  • vv. 29–31: a long list of vices.
  • v. 32: knowing God's decree, they not only do these things but applaud others who do them.

Key words.

  • paredōken, he gave them up: Three times (vv. 24, 26, 28). God does not push anyone into sin; he lets people have what they have chosen, and the choice becomes its own punishment. It is the same verb Paul uses of Christ, “handed over to death for our trespasses” (4:25), and of the Father who “gave him up for all of us” (8:32).
  • metēllaxan, they exchanged: The key word of the passage: God's glory exchanged for images (v. 23, a related verb), the truth for a lie (v. 25), the natural for the unnatural (v. 26). Sin is shown as a bad bargain, a trading of the real for the false.
  • para physin, contrary to nature: Against the order the Creator has placed in things. Paul's argument rests on Genesis 1: those who refuse to honour the Creator end by losing sight of the order of his creation.

Verse by verse.

“because they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever! Amen.” (1:25)

In the middle of the darkest passage Paul breaks into a blessing. A devout Jew could hardly name the Creator without blessing him.

“And since they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them up to a debased mind” (1:28)

There is a play on words in the Greek: they did not think it fit (edokimasan) to acknowledge God, so he gave them up to an unfit (adokimon) mind. When God is refused, even the power to judge rightly is damaged.

“They know God’s decree, that those who practice such things deserve to die” (1:32)

The last stage is not weakness but approval: to call evil good. Isaiah had warned: “Ah, you who call evil good and good evil” (Isaiah 5:20).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses as Paul meant them: as the first step of an argument that ends in mercy. “All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God; they are now justified by his grace as a gift” (3:23–24). No reader stands outside the list of verses 29–31, which includes envy, gossip and boasting. From verses 26–27 the Church draws her teaching that homosexual acts are contrary to the natural law; in the same place she teaches that persons with such tendencies must be accepted with respect, compassion and sensitivity, and that every sign of unjust discrimination must be avoided (CCC 2357–2358). All are called to chastity, and all are offered the same mercy.

John 11:17–27 · I am the resurrection and the life

Setting.

Lazarus of Bethany, brother of Martha and Mary, has died. When Jesus heard of his illness he stayed two days longer where he was (11:6), saying that the illness was “for God’s glory” (11:4). Bethany lay on the Mount of Olives, about three kilometres from Jerusalem. The raising of Lazarus is the last and greatest of the signs in John's Gospel, and it leads directly to the decision to put Jesus to death (11:53).

Many had come to console the sisters, as Jewish custom asked of friends during the days of mourning. A later rabbinic tradition held that the soul stayed near the body for three days; whether or not that belief lies behind the “four days” here, John means that Lazarus is beyond all doubt dead.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–19: Jesus arrives; Lazarus four days in the tomb; many come to console.
  • vv. 20–22: Martha goes out to meet him: sorrow and trust.
  • vv. 23–24: “Your brother will rise again”; Martha's faith in the last day.
  • vv. 25–26: “I am the resurrection and the life. Do you believe this?”
  • v. 27: Martha's confession.

Key words.

  • anastasis, resurrection: Martha uses the word of the general resurrection at the end of time, which many Jews of her day believed (Daniel 12:2; compare Acts 23:8). Jesus tells her that the resurrection is not only an event in the future: it is a person, standing before her.
  • egō eimi, I am: One of the great “I am” sayings of John's Gospel: the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the way, the truth and the life. They echo the divine name revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14).
  • pisteueis touto, do you believe this?: The question is put to Martha, and through her to every reader. John says he wrote his Gospel “so that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God” (20:31).

Verse by verse.

“Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” (11:21)

Grief speaks honestly to Jesus. Mary will say the same words (11:32). Jesus does not rebuke the complaint.

“I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live,” (11:25)

Bodily death remains, but it no longer has the last word. The life Jesus gives begins now, in faith and Baptism, and death does not break it.

“Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” (11:27)

Martha's confession is as full as Peter's in Matthew 16:16, and it uses the very titles of John's statement of purpose (20:31). She makes it before she has seen her brother raised.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in this Gospel her faith in the resurrection of the body: Jesus links faith in the resurrection to his own person (CCC 994). St Augustine, preaching on this passage, saw in Lazarus four days dead an image of the soul long bound by the habit of sin, whom the voice of Christ still calls out of the tomb (Tractates on John 49, in substance). Martha, often remembered only for being busy (Luke 10:40), is here the one who makes the great confession of faith.

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus wait two days before going to Bethany?

John gives the reason in Jesus' own words: the illness was “for God’s glory, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it” (11:4). He also says plainly that Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus (11:5). The delay was not indifference; it made room for a greater sign, through which many came to believe (11:45). God's delays in our own lives can also hide a larger mercy.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that sin begins with exchanging the truth about God for a lie. What false “gods” compete for the first place in my life?
  • Jesus asks Martha, “Do you believe this?” How would I answer him at the grave of someone I love?
