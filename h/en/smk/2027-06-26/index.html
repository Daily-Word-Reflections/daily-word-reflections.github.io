Word for the day

“And my God will fully satisfy every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus.” Philippians 4:19

Reflection

St Paul ends his letter to the Philippians with a thank-you. “You Philippians indeed know that in the early days of the gospel, when I left Macedonia, no church shared with me in the matter of giving and receiving, except you alone. For even when I was in Thessalonica, you sent me help for my needs more than once.” The church at Philippi was poor and often persecuted, yet it was the one community that kept on supporting the apostle as he carried the gospel from town to town. Paul is careful about how he thanks them: “Not that I seek the gift, but I seek the profit that accumulates to your account.” What delights him is not the money but what their giving does to them; every gift for the gospel is written in God's book to their credit. Tomorrow the Panchangam keeps Seminary Day, and the offerings of the Holy Qurbono will go to the Major Seminary of our Church, where young men are formed to be priests. In the Apostles' Fast, when we remember the first men Christ sent, it is fitting that we support those who are being prepared to be sent today. The parish that gives to the seminary is doing exactly what the Philippians did for Paul: sharing in the gospel by giving and receiving.

Paul then describes their gift in the language of worship: “I have been paid in full and have more than enough; I am fully satisfied, now that I have received from Epaphroditus the gifts you sent, a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God.” A gift for the poor or for the mission is not only an act of kindness; it rises to God like the incense of the temple. And it never leaves the giver poorer in the end: “And my God will fully satisfy every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus. To our God and Father be glory forever and ever. Amen.” Paul does not promise that the Philippians will become wealthy. He promises that God, who is rich beyond measure, will not let them lack what they truly need. A family that shares even from a small income discovers this again and again, and that discovery is itself a reason to give glory to God.

The Gospel brings us into one of the sharpest disputes in St John's Gospel. Jesus is speaking with listeners who, a few verses earlier, are said to be looking for an opportunity to kill him, and he tells them: “If God were your Father, you would love me, for I came from God and now I am here.” Then he speaks plainly of the devil: “He was a murderer from the beginning and does not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he lies, he speaks according to his own nature, for he is a liar and the father of lies.” These words were spoken to particular opponents at a particular moment; the Church has always taught that they may not be turned against the Jewish people as a whole (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 597). Their warning is for us. From the first lie in the garden, the enemy has worked through falsehood. St Augustine observed that whoever tells a lie speaks from what is his own, while whoever speaks the truth speaks from God, the source of all truth (Tractates on John 42, in substance). In an age of rumours forwarded from phone to phone, the fast of the tongue matters as much as the fast of the stomach: no lie, no gossip, no message passed on without knowing it is true. “Whoever is from God hears the words of God.” Let us belong to God by listening to his word and by speaking only what is true.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul thanks the Philippians for their help: no church shared with me in the matter of giving and receiving, except you alone (Philippians 4:15). They were not a rich church, but they kept supporting the apostle so that the gospel could go further. Tomorrow is Seminary Day, and we will be asked to help the seminary where young men are formed to be priests. That is our way of sharing in the gospel, as the Philippians did.

Paul calls their gift a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God (Philippians 4:18). And he makes a promise: my God will fully satisfy every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus (Philippians 4:19). No one becomes truly poor by giving to God.

In the Gospel, Jesus speaks hard words to those who were opposing him, and he warns about the devil, who is a liar and the father of lies (John 8:44). These words are not a judgement on a people; they are a warning to every heart. And Jesus gives us a sign of those who belong to God: Whoever is from God hears the words of God (John 8:47).

So let us do two things. Let us give generously tomorrow for the seminary and pray for our future priests. And for the rest of this Apostles' Fast, let us keep the fast of the tongue: no lies, no gossip, no rumours passed on. Let us listen to the word of God and speak the truth in love. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago the Christians of a town called Philippi sent presents to St Paul so that he could keep going from place to place telling people about Jesus. Tomorrow our Church will collect money for the seminary, the big school where young men learn to become priests. You can bring a coin too! And Jesus tells us today that God's children always tell the truth. If you have told a lie, say sorry, and try to always speak the truth.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you came from the Father and you speak the truth. Make us children of God who hear your words and love you. Keep us from every lie, from gossip and from rumours that wound our neighbours, and let our speech be true and kind. Bless all who share in the work of the gospel by their gifts, and make their offering a fragrant sacrifice, pleasing to the Father. Bless our Major Seminary, its teachers and its students, and send faithful labourers into your harvest. According to the riches of your glory, supply every need of our families. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings speak of what we receive and from whom. Paul thanks the Philippians for the gifts they sent him, and calls their generosity “a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God”, adding that God will supply every need of theirs. In John, Jesus tells his opponents that the one who is from God hears the words of God. A gift given in love rises to God like incense; a word received with an open heart shows whose children we are. The key words are offering and hearing.

Philippians 4:15–20 · A fragrant offering, pleasing to God

Setting.

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison. Many scholars place the letter in Rome around AD 61–62; others think of an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus. Philippi was the first church he founded in Europe (Acts 16), and it remained especially close to him. The Philippians had sent him help by Epaphroditus, who fell gravely ill while with Paul and then returned home with this letter (2:25–30).

Our verses close the letter's thanksgiving for that gift (4:10–20). Just before them Paul says that he has learned to be content in plenty and in want, and that he can do all things through him who strengthens him (4:11–13). So he thanks them warmly, yet without making his peace depend on their money.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: the Philippians alone shared with him from the beginning, even at Thessalonica.
  • v. 17: not the gift but the fruit that grows to their account.
  • v. 18: the gift as a fragrant sacrifice to God.
  • vv. 19–20: God will supply their every need; a doxology.

Key words.

  • eis logon doseōs kai lēmpseōs, in the matter of giving and receiving: The language of business accounts. Paul uses a merchant's words to describe a partnership in the Gospel: they gave material help, he gave them the word of God, and both shared in one work.
  • apechō, I have been paid in full: The word written on ancient receipts, meaning “received in full”. With a smile Paul gives the Philippians a receipt for their gift.
  • osmē euōdias, a fragrant offering: The Greek Old Testament uses these words for sacrifices that rise as a pleasing smell before the Lord (Genesis 8:21; Leviticus 1:9). Ephesians 5:2 uses them of Christ's own self-offering. Gifts to the poor and to the Church's mission share in that worship.

Verse by verse.

“Not that I seek the gift, but I seek the profit that accumulates to your account.” (4:17)

The giver gains more than the one who receives. Paul is glad, not for his own comfort, but because their love is bearing fruit before God.

“a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God.” (4:18)

A gift of money sent to a prisoner is called a sacrifice. Christian worship is not shut up in the sanctuary; it flows from the altar into acts of love, which in turn are offered back to God.

“And my God will fully satisfy every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus.” (4:19)

Paul cannot repay them, but his God can. The same verb was used in verse 18: “I am fully satisfied”. As God has filled Paul through them, he will fill them.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that the faithful are obliged to help with the material needs of the Church, each according to his means (CCC 2043). Philippians shows the spirit of that duty: not a tax but a share in the Gospel, a sacrifice pleasing to God. Gifts given for priests, missionaries, catechists and the poor are part of the offering that the faithful bring to the Holy Qurbono, where all our gifts are joined to the one sacrifice of Christ.

John 8:42–47 · Whoever is from God hears the words of God

Setting.

John 8 records a long and sharp dispute during the feast of Booths. The part that leads to our verses begins with Jesus speaking to “the Jews who had believed in him” (8:31–32): “If you continue in my word, you are truly my disciples; and you will know the truth, and the truth will make you free.” His hearers protest that they are children of Abraham and have never been slaves (8:33). The conversation turns on the question of fatherhood: who is your real father, shown by what you do?

These words must be read with great care. They were spoken in a particular quarrel to particular opponents who, the text says, were seeking to kill Jesus (8:40). They are not a judgment on the Jewish people. The Church teaches that what happened in the Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews then living nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

Structure.

  • v. 42: if God were your Father, you would love me, for I came from God.
  • v. 43: why you cannot understand: you cannot hear my word.
  • v. 44: your deeds show another father: the devil, murderer and liar.
  • vv. 45–47: I tell the truth; who can convict me of sin? Whoever is from God hears God's words.

Key words.

  • ek tou theou, from God: In John to be “from” someone is to have one's origin and nature from him. Jesus came from God; those who are born of God (1:13) recognise the voice of their Father in him.
  • anthrōpoktonos, murderer: Literally “man-killer”. The book of Wisdom says that “through the devil's envy death entered the world” (Wisdom 2:24). The devil was a murderer from the beginning because he led humanity into sin and death.
  • pseustēs, liar: The opposite of truth, which in John is not just correct information but the reality of God revealed in Jesus (14:6). The devil's first word in Scripture was a lie about God (Genesis 3:4–5).

Verse by verse.

“If God were your Father, you would love me, for I came from God and now I am here.” (8:42)

Love is the proof of kinship. Those who belong to the Father love the Son whom he has sent.

“Why do you not understand what I say? It is because you cannot accept my word.” (8:43)

Literally “you cannot hear my word”. Not understanding is a matter of the heart before it is a matter of the mind.

“Whoever is from God hears the words of God.” (8:47)

A test that applies to every reader of the Gospel, not only to the hearers that day. Each of us shows whose child we are by how we receive the word of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 44 when it speaks of the fallen angel: Scripture witnesses to the disastrous influence of the one Jesus calls “a murderer from the beginning” (CCC 394). It quotes it again when it teaches that lying is a direct offence against the truth: the Lord denounces lying as the work of the devil (CCC 2482). The Church reads this hard passage as a warning to every believer, including Christians: the children of God are known by love of the Son and by truthfulness, not by descent or name alone.

Questions people ask

Did Jesus call the Jewish people children of the devil?

No. Jesus, his mother and his apostles were Jews, and he loved his people. In John 8 he speaks to opponents in a heated dispute and judges deeds, not birth: those who seek to kill an innocent man and reject the truth are doing the work of the murderer and liar. The Church firmly rejects any use of these words against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). The warning is meant for every reader, Christian or not, who closes his ears to God's word.

For study and teaching

  • Paul calls a gift of money “a fragrant offering”. How might my giving to the parish, the missions and the poor become an act of worship and not only a duty?
  • Jesus says that whoever is from God hears his words. When I listen to the Gospel at the Holy Qurbono, what helps me really to hear, and what blocks my ears?
