Word for the day

“For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are sanctified.” Hebrews 10:14

Reflection

Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews sets two pictures side by side. In the first, “every priest stands day after day at his service, offering again and again the same sacrifices that can never take away sins.” In the second, Christ “offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins” and “sat down at the right hand of God.” The priests of the old covenant stand, because their work is never finished. Christ sits, because his work is done: he died once, rose, and is enthroned with the Father. “For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are sanctified.” What, then, do we do at every Holy Qurbono? We do not add a new sacrifice to the Cross, and we do not repeat it as if it were incomplete. The one sacrifice of Calvary is made present on our altar, and the risen Lord who offered it once offers himself now through the ministry of the priest (Catechism 1366–1367). St John Chrysostom, preaching on this chapter, put it simply: we do not offer one lamb today and another tomorrow, but always the same; the sacrifice is one (in substance).

The Letter then calls the Holy Spirit as witness, quoting the prophet Jeremiah: “I will put my laws in their hearts, and I will write them on their minds,” and “I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more.” This is the new covenant: not a law carved on stone outside us, but the Spirit writing God's will inside us, and a forgiveness so complete that God himself chooses not to remember. The Gospel then opens a door to the feast. At a dinner someone exclaims, “Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!” Jesus answers with a parable. A man gave a great dinner and sent his servant to say, “Come; for everything is ready now.” But they all began to make excuses: a field, five yoke of oxen, a new marriage. None of these is a sin. They are good things put in the place of the invitation. The parable is not aimed at one people; the first disciples who accepted the invitation were themselves sons and daughters of Israel. It is a warning to every invited guest, and so to us. St Gregory the Great observed that the pleasures of the body, once possessed, soon weary us, but spiritual delights, once tasted, make us hungry for more (in substance).

The master then sends his servant into the streets and lanes for “the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame,” and again to the roads and lanes: “compel people to come in, so that my house may be filled.” The Church reads this “compel” as the urgency of love, never as force, for the act of faith must be given willingly (Catechism 160; Dignitatis Humanae 10). Tonight, as Saturday evening opens the Lord's Day, the invitation comes to us again: “Come; for everything is ready now.” Everything is ready at the Holy Qurbono tomorrow: the Word, the Body and Blood of the Lord, the peace of the risen Christ. What excuse is waiting in my week? A tuition class, a function, a site visit, tiredness? Prepare tonight. Make peace with someone, go to confession if you need to, plan to go as a family, and bring along one person who has stopped coming. The house is not yet full; there is still room.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! The Letter to the Hebrews contrasts the priests who stand day after day offering the same sacrifices with Christ, who offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins and sat down at the right hand of God (Hebrews 10:12).

He sits because his work is finished. The Holy Qurbono does not add to the Cross; it makes the one sacrifice present, and the risen Lord gives himself to us. And in this new covenant God says: I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more.

In the Gospel a man gives a great dinner and says: Come; for everything is ready now (Luke 14:17). But the guests make excuses: a field, oxen, a marriage. Good things, put in the place of the invitation. So the master calls the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame, and still there is room.

Tonight Sunday begins. Everything is ready for us tomorrow: the Word, the Body and Blood of Christ, his peace. Let us lay aside our excuses, prepare our hearts, and bring someone with us. Amen.

For children

Children, imagine someone made a huge feast with all your favourite food, and invited you, and you said: “Sorry, I'm busy.” In Jesus' story people said exactly that! So the man invited poor and sick people, and the house was filled. Every Sunday Jesus invites us to his feast, the Holy Qurbono. Will you say “Yes, I am coming!” tomorrow?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, by one offering you have perfected for all time those who are sanctified, and now you sit at the right hand of the Father. Write your law on our hearts and remember our sins no more. You have prepared a great dinner and say to us, “Come; for everything is ready now.” Deliver us from every excuse that keeps us from your table, and send us into the streets and lanes to bring others with us, until your house is filled. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hebrews sets two pictures side by side: priests who stand day after day offering sacrifices again and again, and Christ who, after one offering, has sat down at God's right hand. Luke tells of a great dinner where everything is ready and the invited guests make excuses, so the host fills his house with the poor and with strangers from the roads. The sacrifice is complete, the table is set, and the invitation is still going out. This evening the Third Sunday after Qyomtho begins. The key words are single offering, ready and come.

Hebrews 10:11–18 · By a single offering

Setting.

This passage closes the long central section of Hebrews on Christ's priesthood and sacrifice (chapters 7–10). In 10:1–10 the writer has said that the law's sacrifices, repeated year after year, could not take away sin, and that Christ came to do the Father's will, offering his own body “once for all” (10:10).

Now the writer returns to the promise of Jeremiah 31 he quoted in chapter 8, and lets the Holy Spirit's own words close the argument.

Structure.

  • v. 11: every priest stands, offering again and again.
  • vv. 12–14: Christ, after a single sacrifice, sat down.
  • vv. 15–17: the witness of the Spirit in Jeremiah's promise.
  • v. 18: where there is forgiveness, there is no longer an offering for sin.

Key words.

  • mia prosphora, a single offering: The offering of Christ on the Cross is one and is never repeated. Its effect reaches all times.
  • teteleiōken, he has perfected: The Greek perfect tense describes an action complete in the past whose result lasts. What Christ did once remains effective.
  • tous hagiazomenous, those who are sanctified: The participle is present: “those who are being made holy”. The one sacrifice is finished; our sanctification goes on as we receive it.

Verse by verse.

“And every priest stands day after day at his service,” (10:11)

In the sanctuary of the old worship there was no seat for the priest. His work was never finished.

“But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, “he sat down at the right hand of God,”” (10:12)

Sitting means the work is done, and it means royal authority (Psalm 110:1). The risen Christ reigns and intercedes.

“For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are sanctified.” (10:14)

The whole of salvation is contained in this one sentence: one offering, all time, all who are being made holy.

“Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin.” (10:18)

No new sacrifice can be added to Christ's. Every Christian offering is a sharing in his.

How the Church reads it.

The Catholic Church teaches that the Eucharist does not repeat or add to the sacrifice of the Cross, but makes that one sacrifice present. “The sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice”, with the same victim and the same offerer, now offering through the ministry of priests (CCC 1367, following the Council of Trent). So when the Holy Qurbono is offered, the one sacrifice of Hebrews 10 is made present among us, and its fruits are given to us.

Luke 14:15–24 · Come, for everything is ready now

Setting.

The setting is still the sabbath meal at the house of a leader of the Pharisees (14:1). One guest, hearing Jesus speak of the resurrection of the righteous, exclaims how blessed are those who will eat bread in the kingdom. Jesus answers with a parable that asks whether we will come when we are called.

In that world, a host sent a first invitation and then, when the meal was ready, a servant to call the guests. To refuse at the last moment was a public insult.

Structure.

  • v. 15: a guest's exclamation.
  • vv. 16–17: the dinner and the call: “Come.”
  • vv. 18–20: three excuses.
  • vv. 21–23: the poor and the strangers brought in.
  • v. 24: the closing word.

Key words.

  • deipnon mega, great dinner: The main evening meal, here a feast. It is an image of the banquet of the kingdom that the prophets foretold (Isaiah 25:6).
  • paraiteisthai, to make excuses: To ask to be let off. All three excuses are polite, and all three put something good, land, oxen and marriage, before the host.
  • anankason, compel: A strong word, but in the customs of the East it describes pressing an invitation on someone who is too humble to accept. The disciples at Emmaus “urged him strongly” in the same spirit (Luke 24:29).

Verse by verse.

“Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!” (14:15)

A pious wish about the future. Jesus turns it into a question about the present: you are invited now.

“Come; for everything is ready now.” (14:17)

Everything has been prepared by the host. Nothing is asked of the guests except to come.

“Go out at once into the streets and lanes of the town and bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame.” (14:21)

The same four groups Jesus told his host to invite (14:13). God does what he asks us to do.

“and compel people to come in, so that my house may be filled.” (14:23)

The host wants a full house. The urgency is the urgency of love.

How the Church reads it.

The parable is a warning to every hearer, and first of all to the baptized, not to let good things keep us from God's table. It must never be read as God rejecting a whole people. Some in past centuries used “compel them to come in” to justify forcing people into the Church. The Church today teaches clearly that no one may be forced to embrace the faith against his will, because the act of faith is by its very nature voluntary (Dignitatis Humanae 10; CCC 160). The only compulsion the Gospel knows is the pressure of a warm invitation.

Questions people ask

If Christ's sacrifice is complete, why do we offer the Holy Qurbono every day?

Not to add to the Cross, but to receive it. The one sacrifice of Christ is made present in the Holy Qurbono, so that each generation can share in it and receive its fruit (CCC 1366–1367). Hebrews says there is no other offering for sin; the Holy Qurbono is not another one but the same, offered in the way the Lord commanded at the Supper.

For study and teaching

  • Christ “sat down” because his work was finished. Where am I still trying to earn what he has already given?
  • Which of the three excuses sounds most like mine? What is keeping me from the Lord's table?
