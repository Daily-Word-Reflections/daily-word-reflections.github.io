Word for the day

“Therefore, I tell you, her sins, which were many, have been forgiven; hence she has shown great love.” Luke 7:47

Reflection

Paul tells Timothy what to say to the rich: not to be haughty, not to hope in uncertain riches but in God, who richly provides everything for our enjoyment; to be rich in good works, generous and ready to share. Money is a good servant and a cruel master. Shared, it becomes “a good foundation for the future”.

At Simon's table a woman known as a sinner bathes Jesus' feet with her tears, dries them with her hair and anoints them. Simon sees only her past; Jesus sees her love. The one who is forgiven much loves much. On Friday, the day of the Cross, we stand with her at his feet: “Your faith has saved you; go in peace.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells Timothy what to say to the rich: not to be haughty, not to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment; to do good, to be rich in good works, generous and ready to share (1 Timothy 6:17–18).

In the Gospel, at a Pharisee's house, a sinful woman washes Jesus' feet with her tears, dries them with her hair and anoints them with ointment. Jesus says: her sins, which were many, have been forgiven; hence she has shown great love (Luke 7:47). Her true treasure was her broken heart.

Those who are forgiven much love much. Today is the First Friday; this week experience the joy of forgiveness in a good confession, and let that love flow out in a generous gift. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings set two kinds of wealth side by side. Paul tells Timothy to warn the rich not to trust in uncertain riches but to be “rich in good works”. In the Gospel, a woman known as a sinner pours out costly ointment and tears on the feet of Jesus, and is called rich in love. The true treasure is a heart that has received much and gives much.

1 Timothy 6:17–19 · Rich in good works

Setting.

1 Timothy is one of the three Pastoral Letters, addressed to Timothy, Paul's companion, who was caring for the church at Ephesus. Earlier in this chapter the letter warns that “the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil” (6:10). Now, near the end, it turns to those in the community who are already rich, and does not condemn them but gives them a task.

Structure.

  • v. 17: two warnings (do not be haughty; do not hope in riches) and the right hope: God, who richly provides.
  • v. 18: four positive commands: do good, be rich in good works, be generous, share.
  • v. 19: the result: a good foundation for the future, and “the life that really is life”.

Key words.

  • plousios, rich: The letter speaks of the rich “in the present age”: their riches belong to a world that is passing.
  • koinōnikos, ready to share: From koinōnia, communion. Sharing goods is a form of the fellowship of the Church (compare Acts 2:44–45).
  • tēs ontōs zōēs, the life that really is life: Literally “the real life”. Wealth gives a kind of life; God gives the life that is truly life.

Verse by verse.

“As for those who in the present age are rich, command them not to be haughty, or to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but rather on God who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment.” (6:17)

Notice the last words: God gives us things “for our enjoyment”. The letter does not despise created goods; it puts them in their place.

“They are to do good, to be rich in good works, generous, and ready to share,” (6:18)
“so that they may take hold of the life that really is life.” (6:19)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the owner of goods is a steward of Providence, who should make them bear fruit and share the benefits with others, first of all his family (CCC 2404). It quotes St John Chrysostom: “Not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life” (CCC 2446).

Luke 7:36–50 · The woman who loved much

Setting.

This scene is found only in Luke. Jesus has just been called “a friend of tax collectors and sinners” (7:34), and now we see what that means. A Pharisee named Simon invites him to a meal. Luke shows Jesus at table with Pharisees several times (11:37; 14:1), and the meal becomes a lesson for the host.

Structure.

  • vv. 36–38: the woman's act at Jesus' feet.
  • v. 39: Simon's silent judgment.
  • vv. 40–43: the parable of two debtors.
  • vv. 44–46: three contrasts: water, kiss, oil.
  • v. 47: her many sins forgiven; little forgiveness, little love.
  • vv. 48–50: “Your sins are forgiven … Your faith has saved you; go in peace.”

Key words.

  • hamartōlos, sinner: Luke does not tell us her sin, and he does not give her a name. She is known by her love.
  • dēnarion, denarius: A labourer's wage for one day (Matthew 20:2). The two debts are about a year and a half's wages, and less than two months'.
  • hoti ēgapēsen poly, for she loved much: The little word hoti can give the reason (“because”) or the evidence (“which shows that”). The NRSV reads “hence she has shown great love”, following the parable, where love comes from forgiveness; the Malayalam POC reads “because she loved much”. Catholic interpreters hold both: love is the fruit of forgiveness received, and love opens the heart to receive it.

Verse by verse.

“She stood behind him at his feet, weeping, and began to bathe his feet with her tears and to dry them with her hair.” (7:38)

Guests reclined on low couches with their feet stretched out behind them, so she could reach his feet without coming to the table. Every gesture is a gesture of love: tears, hair, kisses, ointment.

“Do you see this woman?” (7:44)

Simon has seen only “a sinner” (v. 39). Jesus asks him to see a person.

“Therefore, I tell you, her sins, which were many, have been forgiven; hence she has shown great love. But the one to whom little is forgiven, loves little.” (7:47)
“Your faith has saved you; go in peace.” (7:50)

The same words Jesus speaks to the woman healed of bleeding (8:48). Faith receives; peace is the gift.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only God forgives sins, and that Jesus, as Son of God, exercises this divine power and gives it to his Church (CCC 1441–1442). The guests' question, “Who is this who even forgives sins?” (v. 49), is the right question. Pope St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, saw in the woman's tears the model of a penitent who turns all that once served sin into the service of God (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was this woman Mary Magdalene?

Luke does not say so. He introduces Mary Magdalene separately in the next chapter (8:2). In the West, from the time of St Gregory the Great, the two were often identified; the Eastern tradition has generally kept them distinct. The Church does not require either view; the Gospel's point is her love and her forgiveness.

For study and teaching

  • Paul tells the rich to be “rich in good works”. What could I share this week, of money, time or skill?
  • Simon saw “a sinner”; Jesus saw a woman who loved. Whom do I need to see with the eyes of Jesus?
