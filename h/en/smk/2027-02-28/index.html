Word for the day

“Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” Matthew 15:28

Reflection

A Canaanite woman, a foreigner and a pagan, comes shouting after Jesus: “Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David; my daughter is tormented by a demon.” At first Jesus does not answer her. The disciples want her sent away. Jesus says he was sent to the lost sheep of Israel. But she kneels and says: “Lord, help me.” And when Jesus speaks of the children's bread, she replies: “Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table.”

This is one of the most moving prayers in the Gospel. She does not argue about her rights; she does not give up; she accepts being called small, and still trusts. Jesus is amazed: “Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” Her daughter is healed from that hour. Jesus' silence was not a refusal; it was a school of persevering faith.

Isaiah had prophesied that foreigners who hold fast to the covenant would be brought to God's holy mountain, “for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.” The Canaanite mother is the first fruit of that promise. The bread meant for the children is enough for all; in the desert God “rained bread from heaven” for the whole people (Exodus 16:4).

St Paul cries out in Romans: “Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” The mother's cry and Paul's cry are the same: we cannot save ourselves, but Christ can. Halfway through the Fast, the Church sets up the Cross in the middle of the church: the answer to every cry for help.

Homily outline

Theme: “Woman, great is your faith!”

A foreign mother cries out for her daughter. Jesus is silent; the disciples want her sent away; she is answered with a hard word. Yet she persists, and Jesus praises her great faith. Her story teaches us how to pray when God seems silent.

1. A mother's cry

  • She is a Canaanite, an outsider, yet she calls Jesus “Lord, Son of David”.
  • She brings her daughter's suffering as her own: “Have mercy on me.”
  • Isaiah's prayer is hers and ours: “O LORD, be gracious to us; we wait for you.”

  • “He did not answer her at all.” God's silence is not his absence.
  • The Psalmist also cried: “How long, O LORD?”

Apply: Do not stop praying for the intention you have carried longest.

  • She accepts the hard word humbly and turns it into hope: even the dogs eat the crumbs.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today a mother comes to Jesus. She is not one of his people; she is a Canaanite, a foreigner. Her daughter is suffering terribly. She cries out: “Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David!” And Jesus does not answer her a word.

Have you ever felt that? You pray and pray, and heaven seems silent. The disciples even want her sent away. Jesus says he was sent first to Israel. But she does not give up. She kneels and says three simple words: “Lord, help me.” And when Jesus speaks of the children's bread, she answers with amazing humility: “Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table.”

Jesus is amazed: “Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” His silence was not a refusal. It was a school of faith, and she passed with honours. Isaiah had said that God's house would be a house of prayer for all peoples. This foreign mother shows us that no one is outside God's mercy.

This Wednesday we reach the middle of the Fast, and the Cross is set up in our church. Look at it when you feel God is silent. St Paul cries: “Who will rescue me? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” Mothers and fathers, keep praying for your children. Don't give up. Say with her: “Lord, help me.” Amen.

For children

Children, a mother came to Jesus because her daughter was very sick. She asked and asked: “Jesus, please help!” At first Jesus did not answer.

But she kept trusting and asking. Jesus said: “Your faith is great!” And her daughter was healed.

When you pray and nothing seems to happen, don't stop! Jesus is listening. Let's say: “Jesus, help me!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on us. Give us the humble, persevering faith of the Canaanite mother; hear our prayers for our children and families; and make your Church a house of prayer for all peoples. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Knanoitho Sunday, the fourth of the Great Fast, is named after the Canaanite woman who would not stop asking. The Sunday's many readings surround her. The Evening Gospel (Mark 7:24–30) tells the same meeting in Mark's words. Exodus 16 gives bread from heaven to a complaining people. In 1 Samuel 7, Samuel sets up a stone and says, “Thus far the Lord has helped us.” Sirach 10 warns about the beginning of pride. In Acts 20, Paul entrusts the elders at Ephesus to God. We study here the three that speak most directly to the Gospel.

Isaiah promises that God's house will be a house of prayer for all peoples. Paul cries out, “Who will rescue me from this body of death?”, and thanks God through Christ. In Matthew, a foreign mother cries out and will not go away, and Jesus calls her faith great. The key words are foreigner, rescue and crumbs. Not merit but need brings us to the Lord, and his mercy is enough.

Isaiah 56:1–7 · A house of prayer for all peoples

Setting.

Most scholars treat Isaiah 56–66 as the last part of the book, from the time after the return from exile, when the people were rebuilding and were asking who truly belonged to them. There were laws that kept some out: Deuteronomy 23:1–8 excluded the castrated and certain foreign peoples from the assembly. There were real reasons for these rules, above all the fear of losing Israel's faith and identity.

This prophet speaks a word of God that opens the door. Foreigners and eunuchs who hold on to the covenant will not be cut off. The condition is the covenant itself, which has not been loosened. What has changed is who may enter it.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: justice and sabbath: the opening call.
  • v. 3: the two who feel shut out speak.
  • vv. 4–5: the promise to eunuchs.
  • vv. 6–7: the promise to foreigners, ending with the house of prayer.

Key words.

  • ben-hannēkār, foreigner: Literally “son of the foreign land”, a person outside Israel by birth. Here he is one who joins himself to the Lord.
  • sārîs, eunuch: A castrated man, often a court official. He could have no children, which is why he calls himself “a dry tree”. In that world, to have no descendants was to lose one's name.
  • yād wāshēm, a monument and a name: Yād is “hand”, and also a memorial. God himself gives the childless man what he could not have by himself. Israel's memorial to the victims of the Shoah in Jerusalem takes its name from this verse.

Verse by verse.

“Do not let the foreigner joined to the Lord say, “The Lord will surely separate me from his people”;” (56:3)

God answers the fear before it is even spoken aloud to him. In today's Gospel the disciples speak as if that fear were true: “Send her away”.

“a monument and a name better than sons and daughters;” (56:5)

God does not merely tolerate these people. He gives them more than they thought they had lost.

“these I will bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer;” (56:7)

Joy, and not merely admission. They are guests of honour.

“for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.” (56:7)

Jesus quotes this line when he clears the Temple (Mark 11:17). Mark alone keeps the words “for all the nations”.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees this promise reaching its fullness in Christ, and it hears it in the story of the Ethiopian official, a eunuch and a foreigner, whom Philip baptises as he travels home reading Isaiah (Acts 8:26–39). The Catechism says that everyone is called to enter the kingdom, which was first announced to the children of Israel and is intended to accept men of all nations (CCC 543). Lumen Gentium 13 teaches that all people are called to belong to the new People of God. The Church makes this claim without denying Israel's calling: the gifts and the call of God are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; Nostra Aetate 4).

Romans 7:13–25 · Who will rescue me?

Setting.

Paul has just said some hard things about the Law, and he is defending it from a misunderstanding. If the Law brings about the knowledge of sin, is it evil? “By no means!” The Law is “holy” (7:12). The fault lies with sin, which uses even a good commandment for its own ends.

He writes in the first person, in the present tense, one of the most personal-sounding passages in his letters. The reading leads up to the opening of chapter 8: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.”

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: not the Law, but sin.
  • vv. 15–20: I do not do what I want.
  • vv. 21–23: two laws at war.
  • vv. 24–25: the cry, and the thanks.

Key words.

  • sarkinos, of the flesh: For Paul, “flesh” does not mean the body as bad. It means humanity on its own, weak and turned away from God. The good creation is not the problem.
  • nomos, law: Here Paul plays on the word: God's Law, and the “law” in the sense of a power that keeps working in me. The two must be told apart.
  • talaipōros, wretched: Miserable, worn out by hardship. The word cries out from the depth of the need.

Verse by verse.

“I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate.” (7:15)

Anyone who has tried to change a habit in the Fast knows this sentence. Paul's point is that knowing what is right does not give the strength to do it.

“For I delight in the law of God in my inmost self,” (7:22)

This speaks well of the Law, and of the human being. The desire for good is real, and it has not been destroyed.

“Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?” (7:24)

The question is “who”, and not “what”. Paul is not looking for a better technique. He needs a person.

“Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (7:25)

The answer comes before the argument is finished. The struggle is still real in the same verse, but it has already been won by another.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches, following Paul, that the Law is holy, spiritual and good, yet still imperfect: it shows what must be done, but does not of itself give the strength, the grace of the Spirit, to fulfil it (CCC 1963). Baptism takes away sin, but an inclination to sin called concupiscence remains, as a struggle for us to engage in with the help of the grace of Christ (CCC 1264). So the Christian who still struggles need not think that Baptism has failed.

Matthew 15:21–28 · Woman, great is your faith

Setting.

Jesus has just answered a dispute on what makes a person unclean (15:1–20). Now he goes to the district of Tyre and Sidon, on the Phoenician coast, outside Jewish territory. The passage stands between two feedings in the wilderness (14:13–21; 15:32–39), which is why talk of bread and crumbs carries such weight.

Mark calls the woman “a Gentile, of Syrophoenician origin” (Mark 7:26). Matthew alone uses the old biblical name “Canaanite”, which brings back the long history of Israel and the peoples of the land. Earlier, Jesus sent the Twelve only “to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:6). After the Resurrection he will send them to all nations (28:19). This woman meets him before that second sending, and she anticipates it.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–22: the journey and the cry.
  • v. 23: silence; the disciples want her sent away.
  • v. 24: the first answer: sent only to Israel.
  • vv. 25–26: she kneels; the second answer: the children's food.
  • vv. 27–28: her reply, and his praise; the healing.

Key words.

  • Chananaia, Canaanite: The word occurs only here in the New Testament. For Matthew's first readers it would be striking to hear the Son of David addressed by a Canaanite, using a title that many in Israel did not yet use for him.
  • eleēson, have mercy: Eleēson me, Kyrie. These are the words that have become Kyrie eleison, in our tradition Kurielaison. She uses the language of the Psalms.
  • kynaria, dogs: A diminutive form, which may mean house dogs, or small dogs under the table, and not the street dogs of the wider world. Scholars warn against softening it too much, since the word still stings. Yet her reply makes use of just that picture of a household.
  • megalē sou hē pistis, great is your faith: In this Gospel, Jesus says “you of little faith” to his own disciples (14:31; 16:8). He praises great faith in two foreigners: this woman, and the centurion (8:10).

Verse by verse.

“Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David; my daughter is tormented by a demon.” (15:22)

She says “on me”: the mother has made her daughter's pain her own. This is the beginning of intercession.

“But he did not answer her at all.” (15:23)

This silence is one of the hardest verses in the Gospels, and many who pray know it from within. St John Chrysostom says that the delay was not to drive her away but to show her faith to all (Homilies on Matthew 52, in substance).

“I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” (15:24)

This is not an insult to anyone. It is the order of God's plan, which begins with Israel. The covenant with Israel is kept, and it is through this that the nations will be blessed (Genesis 12:3).

“But she came and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, help me.”” (15:25)

The shortest prayer in the passage. The argument seems lost. She now asks once again, on her knees.

“Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters’ table.” (15:27)

She agrees, and then turns the picture around. She does not deny Israel's place at the table; she knows the table is big enough to have crumbs. St Augustine sees in this a great humility, and he says that it is what made her fit to receive (Sermon 77, in substance).

“Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” (15:28)

She came asking for a healing and goes home with this praise as well. It is a healing at a distance, like that of the centurion's servant (8:13).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names the Canaanite woman among those whose prayer of faith, expressed in words, Jesus heard (CCC 2616). Jesus teaches his disciples to pray with lively and persevering faith and with filial boldness (CCC 2610). The Church has always loved this woman because she represents all of us who had no claim to the table: the nations who have been brought in. In the Holy Qurbono we come to the table with her humility and her confidence.

Questions people ask

Did Jesus really call her a dog? Was that not cruel?

Jesus does use the word, and we should not pretend that it is gentle. Scholars read the exchange in different ways. Some note the diminutive and hear in it the household pet. Others think that Jesus is voicing the view his disciples held, to bring it into the open and then overturn it. Chrysostom saw it as a test meant to display her faith. What is beyond dispute is how the scene ends: the woman is praised and heard, and no one who reads it can think that Jesus despises her. Nor may anyone use this verse to look down on another person.

Why does Jesus say that he was sent only to Israel?

God's plan is to bless all nations through Abraham's family (Genesis 12:3). Jesus begins his earthly mission with Israel, God's covenant people, and this is not a rejection of others but the order of the promise. Paul says it in the words, “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” (Romans 1:16). This woman's faith gives a preview of the mission to all nations that Jesus will entrust to his disciples (Matthew 28:19).

Who is the “I” in Romans 7? Is it Paul, or the Christian?

This has been debated since the Fathers. St Augustine first thought that it described the person under the Law before grace. Later he came to apply it also to the baptised believer, who still struggles. Many modern scholars take the “I” as a way of speaking: the person under the Law without the Spirit, as Paul now sees it in the light of Christ. The next chapter stresses victory by the Spirit. Both readings agree on what matters: we are not saved by our own strength, and our help is Christ.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

Our Great Fast names its Sundays from Cana, from the Lord's healings and from his entry into Jerusalem: Kothine (Cana), Garbo (the leper), M'shario (the paralytic), Knanoitho (the Canaanite woman), K'piphtho (the bent woman), Samio (the blind) and Hosanna. Today's name means “the Canaanite woman”. The people repeat her cry in the Holy Qurbono: Kurielaison, Lord, have mercy. We are nearing the middle of the Fast, and on Wednesday 3 March the Cross is set up in the church. The woman's persistence is a good companion for the second half of the road.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah promises a place to those who felt shut out. Who feels shut out from my parish or my family, and what can I do about it?
  • Romans 7:15 is honest about failure. Where do I find this in my own Fast, and do I turn it into thanks to Christ or into despair?
  • The woman kept asking through silence and refusal. What have I stopped asking God for? Should I start again?
  • How would I explain “the crumbs” to a child, in a way that shows both the woman's humility and the size of God's table?
