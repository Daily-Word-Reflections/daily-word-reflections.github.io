Word for the day

“Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.” Matthew 1:20

Reflection

Luke told us the story through Mary; Matthew tells it through Joseph. Joseph discovers that Mary, his betrothed, is with child, and he does not yet know how. St Matthew describes him in one word: he was “a righteous man”. His righteousness is shown in mercy: “unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, he planned to dismiss her quietly.”

Then God speaks to him in a dream: “Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit. She will bear a son, and you are to name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.” Joseph is given two tasks: to receive Mary and to name the child. By naming him, Joseph gives Jesus his legal place in the house of David.

Joseph never speaks a word in the Gospels. But he acts: “When Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took her as his wife.” His obedience is immediate and complete. Like the young Samuel he says, in deeds if not in words, “Speak, for your servant is listening.” The patriarch Joseph, the dreamer of Genesis, saved his family by going down to Egypt; this Joseph will save the Holy Family by taking them to Egypt.

Matthew adds: “All this took place to fulfill what had been spoken by the Lord through the prophet: ‘Look, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall name him Emmanuel,’ which means, ‘God is with us.’” Through Joseph's quiet faith, God-with-us receives a home, a family name and a father's protection.

Homily outline

Theme: “Joseph: the just man who listened and obeyed.”

Joseph never speaks a word in the Gospels, yet his silence is full of faith. Faced with a crisis he did not understand, he chose mercy, listened to God in the night, and acted at once.

1. A just man chooses mercy

  • Joseph discovers that Mary is with child and does not understand.
  • Being righteous, he will not expose her to disgrace: his justice is clothed in mercy.
  • Micah sums up God's will: do justice, love kindness, walk humbly.
“He has told you, O mortal, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?” Micah 6:8
“Her husband Joseph, being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, planned to dismiss her quietly.” Matthew 1:19

Apply: In a family conflict this week, choose the way that protects the other's dignity.

2. God speaks in the night

  • While Joseph is still deciding, the angel speaks in a dream.
  • Like the boy Samuel, Joseph has a listening heart.
  • God still speaks in silence, in Scripture, in conscience and through the Church; we must make room to listen.
“Now the LORD came and stood there, calling as before, “Samuel! Samuel!” And Samuel said, “Speak, for your servant is listening.”” 1 Samuel 3:10
“An angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream and said, “Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.”” Matthew 1:20

Apply: Before sleeping, spend five minutes in silence with the Lord, without your phone.

3. Obedience in action

  • Joseph wakes and does exactly as the angel commanded.
  • He takes Mary into his home and gives the child the name Jesus, “God saves”.
  • Fathers of families are called to the same quiet courage: to protect, provide and lead their homes to God.
“Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth! For I am God, and there is no other.” Isaiah 45:22
“When Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took her as his wife.” Matthew 1:24

Apply: Fathers: pray with your family this week and bless your children.

St Joseph teaches us that holiness is often silent: justice with mercy, listening in the night, obedience in the morning.

As Christmas draws near, let us ask him to guard our families as he guarded the Holy Family.

Take-home resolution: Entrust your family to St Joseph each day this week with an Our Father.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, this Sunday the Gospel turns from Mary to Joseph. Joseph has made his plans: he is betrothed to Mary, he is preparing a home. Then he discovers that she is with child, and he does not understand.

What does he do? St Matthew says that Joseph, being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, decided to send her away quietly. Notice: his righteousness is mercy. He does not want to shame her. How different from our world, where people love to expose others on social media and in gossip!

Then God speaks to him in a dream: “Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife.” And Joseph, who never speaks a word in the Gospels, simply obeys: he woke up and did as the angel commanded. No delay, no argument. Like young Samuel he could say: “Speak, Lord, your servant is listening.”

Joseph gave Jesus a home, a family name and a father's protection. Dear fathers, your quiet work, your sacrifices, your faithfulness are part of God's plan. And all of us: let us give God some silence each night, so that, like Joseph, we may hear him and obey. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph was a carpenter who loved Mary. One night an angel came to him in a dream and said: “Don't be afraid! Take care of Mary and her baby Jesus.”

When Joseph woke up, he did exactly what God said, straight away. He became Jesus' father on earth. He kept Jesus safe and taught him to work.

Let's thank God for our fathers. And let's be like Joseph: when Mummy or Daddy asks, do it straight away! Let's say: “Like Joseph, I will listen and obey!”

Prayer

Lord God, you entrusted your Son and the Virgin Mary to the faithful care of Joseph, the righteous man. Give us his listening heart, his merciful justice and his prompt obedience, and bless all fathers with his tender strength. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Suboro Sunday the good news comes to a carpenter, in his sleep. Matthew tells of the birth of Jesus from Joseph's side. The evening Gospel and the Gospel of the Holy Qurbono are the same passage (Matthew 1:18–25), and the other readings gather around it. Sirach 7 teaches humility and fidelity. In 1 Samuel 3 the boy Samuel says, “Speak, for your servant is listening”, and receives a hard word without hiding it. In Isaiah 45 God calls all the ends of the earth to turn to him and be saved. In 1 Peter 2 believers are told to live honourably among the nations.

This study takes three of them. In Genesis 37 another Joseph dreams, and the dream costs him everything before it saves his family. In Galatians 1 Paul tells how God called him through grace and revealed his Son to him. In Matthew 1 the just man wakes up and does what he was told. All three are about a call from God that comes unasked and that overturns a person's plans. The key words are dream, call and obedience.

Genesis 37:5–11 · The dreams of Joseph, son of Jacob

Setting.

The long story of Joseph (Genesis 37–50) explains how the family of Jacob came to Egypt. It opens with a family already in trouble. Joseph is seventeen, the son of his father's old age, loved more than his brothers, and he has brought their father a bad report about them (37:2–4). His brothers cannot speak peaceably to him. Into this tension come two dreams.

In the ancient Near East dreams were commonly taken as a way God might make his will known. In Genesis, God does not speak directly to Joseph as he spoke to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. He guides events through dreams and through the hidden course of the story, as Joseph himself will say at the end (45:5; 50:20).

Structure.

  • vv. 5–8: the first dream: the sheaves bow; the brothers hate him more.
  • vv. 9–10: the second dream: sun, moon and eleven stars bow; his father rebukes him.
  • v. 11: the two responses: the brothers' jealousy, and the father's keeping the matter.
  • ¶ The sheaves look forward to the famine years, when grain in Egypt will bring these same brothers to bow before Joseph (42:6). The dream, which starts all the trouble, is also what God fulfils.

Key words.

  • ḥălôm, dream: The word is repeated through the passage. Joseph's story is told in three pairs of dreams: his own two, those of the butler and baker, and those of Pharaoh.
  • hištaḥăwāh, to bow down: The word for homage to a king and for worship of God. It appears in both dreams, and it is the very thing the brothers cannot bear.
  • šāmar, kept: The basic meaning is to guard or to keep. In v. 11 Jacob “kept the matter in mind”. He has rebuked his son, and yet he does not dismiss the dream.

Verse by verse.

“Once Joseph had a dream, and when he told it to his brothers, they hated him even more.” (37:5)

The text does not call Joseph blameless. A favourite son telling such dreams is not being wise. God works through a young man who still has much to learn in a pit and a prison.

““What kind of dream is this that you have had? Shall we indeed come, I and your mother and your brothers, and bow to the ground before you?”” (37:10)

Rachel's death has already been told (35:19). The story here does not stop over this, and interpreters explain it in different ways: perhaps “your mother” means Leah or whoever held the mother's place in the household, or the narrative is not arranged strictly in time. The moon in the dream stands for the mother in any case.

“So his brothers were jealous of him, but his father kept the matter in mind.” (37:11)

Readers of Luke will recall Mary, who “treasured all these words” (Luke 2:19, 51). There are those who hate what God is doing, and those who hold on to it without yet understanding.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites the story of Joseph to show how God, in his providence, can bring good even out of evil: “It was not you who sent me here, but God” (CCC 312). The Fathers saw in Joseph a figure of Christ: beloved of his father, sold by his own, and saving those who had rejected him; St Ambrose wrote a whole work on him. St Bernard compares the two Josephs: the first stored grain for Egypt, and the second kept safe the Bread from heaven for the whole world (in substance).

Galatians 1:11–24 · Called through his grace

Setting.

Paul is under attack in Galatia. Some have been saying that his gospel is a second-hand and watered-down version of what he learned from the leaders in Jerusalem. He answers with his own story. It is one of the fullest pieces of autobiography in his letters, and it gives us precious dates and places for the years after his call.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: the thesis: my gospel came by revelation of Jesus Christ.
  • vv. 13–14: my earlier life: a persecutor and a zealous Jew.
  • vv. 15–17: God's call, then Arabia and Damascus.
  • vv. 18–20: after three years, fifteen days with Cephas in Jerusalem.
  • vv. 21–24: Syria and Cilicia; the churches of Judea glorify God.

Key words.

  • apokalypsis, revelation: An unveiling. In v. 12 it may mean a revelation from Jesus Christ or about him, and v. 16 suggests both: God “was pleased to reveal his Son to me”. The Greek en emoi there is literally “in me”, so the revelation is both to him and within him.
  • aphorizō, set apart: Paul's words about being set apart before birth echo the call of Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:5) and of the Servant (Isaiah 49:1). He sees his call as a prophet's call, and not as a change of God.
  • Ioudaismos, Judaism: In the New Testament this word occurs only here (vv. 13–14). It means the Jewish way of life, zealously practised. Paul does not speak of leaving his God. The God who called him is the God of his ancestors.
  • historēsai, to visit: The verb in v. 18, used only here in the New Testament. It suggests a visit made to get to know someone and to learn from him, as a visitor comes to see a person of importance.

Verse by verse.

“I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it.” (1:13)

Paul never hides this (1 Corinthians 15:9). The worst thing about him becomes the proof of grace.

“But when God, who had set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace, was pleased to reveal his Son to me, so that I might proclaim him among the Gentiles,” (1:15–16)

Every word places the initiative with God: he set apart, he called, he was pleased, he revealed. And the call has a purpose: it is for others.

“Then after three years I did go up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and stayed with him fifteen days;” (1:18)

Paul stresses his independence, and yet he goes to Peter, using his Aramaic name, “Cephas”. Revelation does not make him a private Christian. He will go up again later and lay his gospel before the leaders “in order to make sure that I was not running, or had not run, in vain” (2:2).

“And they glorified God because of me.” (1:24)

The persecutor's life has become a reason for others to praise God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage when it teaches that faith in Jesus as Son of God is at the centre of Paul's preaching: from the start, his call was the revealing of the Son (CCC 442). Paul also says that he handed on what he had received (1 Corinthians 15:3). The Church holds these together: the gospel comes from God and not from men, and it is kept and handed on in the communion of the apostles (CCC 75–77). It belongs among the Sunday's readings because of the word “called”: like Joseph, Paul has his plans overturned by God.

Matthew 1:18–25 · Joseph did as the angel commanded

Setting.

Matthew has just given the genealogy of Jesus, which ends strangely: “Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born” (1:16). He has not said “Joseph the father of Jesus”. Today's passage explains why. The question is how Jesus, who has no human father, can be the son of David. The answer lies with Joseph's obedience.

A Jewish betrothal of that time was far more binding than an engagement today. The couple were legally husband and wife, and only a divorce could end it, though they did not yet live together. So Matthew calls Joseph “her husband” even at this stage. A woman found with child in this situation faced public shame, and the law spoke of severe penalties (Deuteronomy 22:20–27).

Structure.

  • v. 18: the situation: found with child, from the Holy Spirit.
  • v. 19: Joseph's decision.
  • vv. 20–21: the angel's word in a dream: take her, and name him.
  • vv. 22–23: the fulfilment of Isaiah 7:14.
  • vv. 24–25: Joseph does exactly what he was told.
  • ¶ Joseph does not say a word, here or anywhere in the Gospels. Matthew's account is built around what he does: he plans, he wakes, he takes, he names. Matthew's account of the childhood will repeat this pattern (2:13–15, 19–23).

Key words.

  • dikaios, righteous: A just man in the Bible is one who lives by God's law. Joseph's justice appears in a surprising way: he will not expose her. In him justice and mercy are not in conflict.
  • Yēšûa‘, Jesus: The Hebrew name means “the Lord saves” (CCC 430, 452). The angel gives the explanation himself: “he will save his people from their sins”.
  • huios Dauid, son of David: A title that Matthew otherwise keeps for Jesus. By naming the child, Joseph accepts him as his own in law, and through Joseph Jesus enters the line of David (CCC 437).
  • Emmanouēl, God is with us: From Isaiah 7:14. The Hebrew has ‘almāh, young woman; the Greek Old Testament, which Matthew quotes, has parthenos, virgin. Matthew's Gospel begins with “God is with us” and ends with Jesus' own promise, “I am with you always” (28:20).

Verse by verse.

“Her husband Joseph, being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, planned to dismiss her quietly.” (1:19)

St John Chrysostom admires the gentleness of Joseph, who wished to hide the matter and not to punish (in substance). This is the moment before the angel: a good man doing the best he can see, in the dark.

““Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.” (1:20)

“Do not be afraid” is the word of this whole season. God does not remove Joseph from the difficulty. He gives him a new part in it.

“She will bear a son, and you are to name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.”” (1:21)

Naming was the father's right and task. God gives this legal fatherhood to a man who did not beget the child. It is real fatherhood, in care and in law.

“When Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took her as his wife,” (1:24)

No delay and no conditions. St John Paul II says that what Joseph did is the clearest “obedience of faith”, and that in this he was joined to the faith of Mary (Redemptoris Custos 4).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Joseph was called by God to take Mary as his wife, so that Jesus should be born of Joseph's spouse into the messianic lineage of David (CCC 437). It reads the angel's words as the heart of the Gospel: the revelation of God's mercy to sinners (CCC 1846). The Gospel understands the virginal conception as a divine work, beyond human understanding and possibility (CCC 497). St John Paul II calls Joseph the guardian of the Redeemer (Redemptoris Custos, 1989), and Pope Francis speaks of him as a father in the shadows (Patris Corde, 2020).

Questions people ask

Did Joseph suspect Mary of sin?

Matthew does not say what he thought. Many readers, ancient and modern, take it that he knew only that she was with child, and did not know how, and that he chose not to expose her. Others in the tradition suggest that Joseph, sensing that something holy was happening, felt unworthy and meant to withdraw out of reverence. The text allows both. It tells us plainly only that he was just and that he did not want to shame her.

Does “until she had borne a son” mean that they lived as a married couple afterwards?

No. The Greek heōs, until, states what happened up to a point, and says nothing about what came after. St Jerome argued this at length against Helvidius, citing many biblical examples (in substance). Matthew's concern here is that Joseph was not the child's natural father. The Church confesses Mary as ever virgin (CCC 499–501).

Paul speaks of “James the Lord's brother”. Did Jesus have brothers?

Semitic languages had no separate common word for a cousin or other near relative, and the Bible often uses “brother” in a wide sense (see Genesis 13:8). The Church does not read these passages as speaking of other children of Mary (CCC 500). Many Eastern Fathers took them as children of Joseph by an earlier marriage, while St Jerome took them as cousins.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Sunday of the Apparition to St Joseph is one of the Suboro Sundays, which walk us step by step toward Yaldo: the announcement to Zechariah, the announcement to Mary, the visit to Elizabeth, the birth of John, and now the angel in Joseph's dream. Only with today's reading has the good news reached everyone who will share the home at Nazareth. The Malankara faithful, who are used to the long patience of the Syriac office, may find Joseph a friend in prayer: he has no recorded words, only a listening heart. At the Holy Qurbono we receive Christ under the humble appearances of bread and wine, and we are asked for Joseph's kind of faith, which takes home what God gives without needing to understand all of it first.

For study and teaching

  • Joseph had already made a decision when God spoke. When have I had to let God change a good plan of mine?
  • Jacob “kept the matter in mind” and Joseph “did as the angel commanded”. Which do I need more at present: patience with what I do not understand, or prompt obedience to what I do?
  • Paul says that God “set me apart before I was born”. How does it change my view of my life to think this is true of me too?
  • Where in my family or parish is the “quiet” justice of Joseph needed, a justice that refuses to expose or shame anyone?
