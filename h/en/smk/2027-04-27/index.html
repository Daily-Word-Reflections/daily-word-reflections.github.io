Word for the day

“Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us give thanks, by which we offer to God an acceptable worship with reverence and awe” Hebrews 12:28

Reflection

Kerala knows what it is for the ground to shake. Many of us remember the floods of 2018, when houses built over a lifetime were gone in a night and families learned how little of what they owned could really be kept. The Letter to the Hebrews speaks of a greater shaking. God's voice once “shook the earth” at Sinai, “but now he has promised, ‘Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also the heaven.’” This phrase, Hebrews explains, “indicates the removal of what is shaken... so that what cannot be shaken may remain.” Everything made will one day be tested. And then comes the good news: “Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us give thanks, by which we offer to God an acceptable worship with reverence and awe; for indeed our God is a consuming fire.” The kingdom of the risen Christ is the one thing that will not be washed away. The fitting response is thanksgiving and worship, which is exactly what the Holy Qurbono is: the great thanksgiving of the Church. But Hebrews also warns: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking.” God still speaks, from heaven, through his Son.

The Gospel shows a man who heard that voice and could not let go of what shakes. “A certain ruler asked him, ‘Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?’” He has kept the commandments since his youth, and he is sincere. Then Jesus says: “There is still one thing lacking. Sell all that you own and distribute the money to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me.” Luke records simply: “he became sad; for he was very rich.” He was holding on to the house that the flood would take. Jesus looks at him, not with anger but with sorrow, and says: “How hard it is for those who have wealth to enter the kingdom of God!” The listeners ask, “Then who can be saved?” and he answers, “What is impossible for mortals is possible for God.” Then Peter speaks for the Twelve: “Look, we have left our homes and followed you.” Jesus' reply is one of the most generous promises in the Gospel: “there is no one who has left house or wife or brothers or parents or children, for the sake of the kingdom of God, who will not get back very much more in this age, and in the age to come eternal life.” What we let go of for Christ is not lost; it is placed in the one kingdom that cannot be shaken.

Today the Panchangam remembers St Cletus, one of the first Bishops of Rome after St Peter. St Irenaeus of Lyons, writing in the second century, lists the succession at Rome: after the apostles Peter and Paul came Linus, then Anencletus (Cletus), then Clement (Against Heresies III.3, in substance). Later tradition counts Cletus among the martyrs. We know very little more about him, but what we know is precious: he received what Peter had handed on and passed it on faithfully. He belongs to the unshakable kingdom because he built on the rock. Our own Church, since the reunion of 1930, lives in full communion with the Successor of Peter, and so we honour the early Bishops of Rome as fathers in the faith. Today, pray for the Pope and for our Major Archbishop-Catholicos and our bishops. And look at your own life after the readings: what are you holding on to that could be shaken away in a night? Let go of one thing this week, give something to the poor, and offer thanks at the Qurbono for the kingdom you are already receiving.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! Hebrews tells us that God will shake not only the earth but also the heaven, so that what cannot be shaken may remain (Hebrews 12:26–27). Many of us remember the floods of 2018. We learned then how quickly things can be swept away.

But there is something that will never be swept away: since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us give thanks, by which we offer to God an acceptable worship with reverence and awe (Hebrews 12:28). That thanksgiving is the Holy Qurbono.

The rich ruler asked Jesus about eternal life, but when Jesus said, sell all that you own and distribute the money to the poor... then come, follow me, he became sad; for he was very rich (Luke 18:22–23). Peter said, look, we have left our homes and followed you, and Jesus promised: no one who has left house or family for the kingdom of God will fail to get back very much more in this age, and in the age to come eternal life (Luke 18:28–30).

Today we remember St Cletus, one of the first Bishops of Rome after St Peter, who handed on faithfully what he had received. He built on the rock. Let us pray for the Pope and our bishops, let go of one thing that holds us back, and give thanks at the altar for the kingdom that cannot be shaken. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! Have you ever built a sandcastle, and then a wave came and washed it away? Many things in this world are like that sandcastle. But Jesus gives us something that no wave can wash away: God's kingdom and his love. A rich man was sad because he did not want to share his things. But Peter left everything to follow Jesus, and Jesus gave him much more. This week, can you share one toy or book with someone who needs it?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you give us a kingdom that cannot be shaken. When the earth and the heavens are shaken, you remain. Deliver us from clinging to what passes, as the rich ruler clung to his wealth, and give us the courage of Peter and the apostles who left everything to follow you. By the prayers of St Cletus, who received the faith from your apostle Peter and handed it on faithfully, keep your Church firm on the rock, and bless our Holy Father the Pope and the bishops of our Church. Let us offer you, with reverence and awe, the worship of thanksgiving. You live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings ask what will remain when everything is shaken. Hebrews promises “a kingdom that cannot be shaken” and calls us to thank God for it. In Luke, a sincere ruler asks the way to eternal life, and finds that his wealth holds him more tightly than he knew. Peter's reply, “we have left our homes and followed you”, receives a promise. The key words are unshaken and follow.

Hebrews 12:25–29 · A kingdom that cannot be shaken

Setting.

These verses close the comparison between Sinai and Zion that began in 12:18. At Sinai God spoke on earth and the earth shook (Exodus 19:18). Now he speaks from heaven through his Son. Hebrews quotes the prophet Haggai, who promised after the exile that God would shake heaven and earth and fill his house with glory (Haggai 2:6–7).

Structure.

  • v. 25: do not refuse the one who speaks from heaven.
  • vv. 26–27: the final shaking, which removes what is made and leaves what cannot be shaken.
  • vv. 28–29: so give thanks and worship with reverence, for God is a consuming fire.

Key words.

  • asaleutos, unshakable: Used of the kingdom we are “receiving”. The present tense matters: the kingdom is being given now, and we are receiving it.
  • echōmen charin, let us give thanks: Literally “let us have grace” or “gratitude”. Thanksgiving is the right answer to a gift. The Greek word for thanksgiving, eucharistia, became the name of the Church's great act of worship.

Verse by verse.

“See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking;” (12:25)

God is still speaking. The danger is not that he is silent but that we turn away.

“This phrase, “Yet once more,” indicates the removal of what is shaken … so that what cannot be shaken may remain.” (12:27)

Everything made will be tested. What remains is what belongs to God's kingdom.

“for indeed our God is a consuming fire.” (12:29)

Words from Deuteronomy 4:24. The fire that frightened Israel at Sinai is the same holy love that purifies and does not destroy what is true.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in verse 28 a description of the Holy Qurbono, the thanksgiving in which we receive the kingdom and offer “acceptable worship with reverence and awe”. The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is above all thanksgiving to the Father (CCC 1359–1360). The fire of verse 29 is not something to run from. In the Syriac tradition the Holy Mysteries themselves are often called fire, which those who come in faith and reverence receive without harm.

Luke 18:18–30 · One thing lacking

Setting.

Luke places this meeting on Jesus' long journey to Jerusalem. Just before it, Jesus has blessed the little children and said that the kingdom must be received like a child (18:15–17). Only Luke calls the questioner a “ruler”, perhaps a leader in a synagogue or a man of standing. Matthew says he was young (Matthew 19:20). Mark adds that Jesus looked at him and loved him (Mark 10:21).

Structure.

  • vv. 18–21: the question and the commandments.
  • vv. 22–23: “one thing lacking”; the ruler's sorrow.
  • vv. 24–27: the camel and the needle; what is possible for God.
  • vv. 28–30: Peter's word and Jesus' promise.

Key words.

  • archōn, ruler: A man with position as well as money. What he must leave is not only property but status.
  • kamēlos, camel: The largest animal in the land and the smallest opening anyone could picture. The saying is a deliberate exaggeration. There is no good evidence for the popular story of a small gate in Jerusalem called “the needle's eye”.
  • ta idia, our own things: Peter's words, translated “our homes” in the NRSV. The disciples had left their own boats, nets and families.

Verse by verse.

“Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?” (18:18)

A good question asked sincerely. Jesus' reply, “No one is good but God alone”, is not a denial of his own goodness. It directs the man to the source of all goodness, the God who now stands before him.

“There is still one thing lacking. Sell all that you own and distribute the money to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me.” (18:22)

The point of the command is the last words: “follow me”. The poor gain, and he gains treasure, but the real gift offered is Jesus himself.

“What is impossible for mortals is possible for God.” (18:27)

Salvation is never a human achievement. Even letting go of what we cling to is a grace.

“Then Peter said, “Look, we have left our homes and followed you.”” (18:28)

Peter is not boasting; he speaks for all who have left something for Jesus. The Lord's answer promises much more in this age and eternal life in the next.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses this meeting to teach that the commandments are the first step on the way to eternal life, and that following Jesus is their fulfilment (CCC 2052–2053). The Church has never read the passage as a condemnation of all property. It calls every Christian to detachment, so that riches serve love of God and neighbour (CCC 2544–2547). Clement of Alexandria wrote a whole treatise, Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?, to show that what must be given up is the soul's attachment to wealth (in substance).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today St Cletus, also called Anacletus, Bishop of Rome. St Irenaeus names him as the successor of Linus, to whom the apostles Peter and Paul had entrusted the Church of Rome (Against Heresies III.3.3). Very little else is known of him. His memory reminds us that the faith reaches us through an unbroken line of shepherds, and that the Church of Rome, with which our Church is in full communion since 1930, has kept the apostles' teaching from the first generation.

For study and teaching

  • What in my life do I hold so tightly that I would be sad if Jesus asked me to let it go?
  • Hebrews calls us to give thanks for an unshakable kingdom. For what will I thank God at the next Holy Qurbono?
