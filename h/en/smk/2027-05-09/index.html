Word for the day

“It is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you.” John 16:7

Reflection

Between the Ascension and Pentecost the Church lives in the upper room. The apostles, “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus”, are “constantly devoting themselves to prayer.” Jesus has gone to the Father; the Spirit has not yet come. It is a time of waiting, but not of emptiness: it is the Church at prayer, gathered around Mary.

Jesus had told them: “It is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you.” His physical presence was limited to one place; the Spirit will make him present everywhere, in every heart and every altar. The Spirit “will prove the world wrong about sin and righteousness and judgment”: he convicts, converts and gives courage.

In the evening Gospel we hear the beginning of Jesus' great priestly prayer: “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you… And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” The ascended Christ continues this prayer for us at the Father's right hand.

God says to Joshua as he takes up Moses' task: “Be strong and courageous; do not be frightened or dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go.” St Paul prays that “with the eyes of your heart enlightened, you may know what is the hope to which he has called you.” Let us spend this week like the apostles: in prayer, with Mary, waiting for the Spirit.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, we are living the days between the Ascension and Pentecost. Jesus has gone to the Father; the Spirit has not yet come. And what did the apostles do? They went to the upper room and “devoted themselves to prayer, together with Mary the mother of Jesus.”

Jesus had said something surprising: “It is to your advantage that I go away.” How can it be better that Jesus goes? Because when he was on earth he could be in one place at a time. The Spirit makes him present everywhere: in every heart, in every family, on every altar. The Spirit will convict the world of sin, of righteousness and of judgment.

In the evening Gospel Jesus prays: “Father, the hour has come… This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” He is still praying for us at the right hand of the Father.

And God says to us what he said to Joshua: “Be strong and courageous; do not be frightened, for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go.” This week let us do what the apostles did: pray with Mary, every day, “Come, Holy Spirit.” Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Sixth Sunday after Qyomtho falls between Suloko, the Ascension, which our Church kept on Thursday, and Pentecost next Sunday. All the readings speak of a Lord who goes away and yet does not leave his people alone. In the evening Gospel (John 17:1–5) Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven: “Father, the hour has come.” In Exodus 6:1–8 God tells Moses his name and promises to set his people at liberty. Wisdom 12:1–11 praises God's immortal spirit in all things and his patience, correcting sinners little by little. In Isaiah 44:21–28 God has swept away his people's sins like a cloud and calls them to return. In Acts 1:12–14 the apostles return from the Mount of Olives and devote themselves to prayer with Mary, the mother of Jesus.

We study three readings more closely. In Joshua 1:5–9 Moses has died, and God promises his successor: “As I was with Moses, so I will be with you.” Paul prays that the eyes of our hearts may be enlightened to see the power that raised Christ and seated him at the right hand of the Father. In John 16 Jesus tells his sad disciples that it is to their advantage that he goes away, because then the Advocate will come. The key words are with you, enlightened and Advocate.

Joshua 1:5–9 · As I was with Moses, so I will be with you

Setting.

The book of Deuteronomy ends with the death of Moses on Mount Nebo, in sight of the promised land which he would not enter (Deuteronomy 34). The book of Joshua opens at that moment of loss: “My servant Moses is dead” (1:2). The people stand on the east bank of the Jordan. Their great leader is gone, and the land before them is not yet theirs.

Joshua had been Moses' assistant from his youth (Numbers 11:28) and was appointed his successor with the laying on of hands (Numbers 27:18–23; Deuteronomy 34:9). Many scholars think the book of Joshua took its final form much later, as part of a long history running from Deuteronomy to Kings, written to call Israel to faithfulness.

Structure.

  • v. 5: the promise of God's presence: as with Moses, so with you.
  • v. 6: be strong and courageous: you will lead the people into the land.
  • vv. 7–8: the law of Moses: do not turn aside; meditate on it day and night.
  • v. 9: the command repeated: do not fear, for the Lord is with you.

Key words.

  • ḥăzaq we’ĕmāṣ, be strong and courageous: The words come three times in these few verses (vv. 6, 7, 9), and Moses had said them to Joshua before (Deuteronomy 31:7). The courage asked of Joshua does not come from his own strength but from the promise that God is with him.
  • hāgâ, meditate: The word means to murmur, to speak in a low voice. In the ancient world people read aloud, even alone. To meditate on the law day and night is to keep repeating God's word until it fills the mind, as in Psalm 1:2.
  • Yᵉhôšua‘, Joshua: The name means “the Lord saves”. In Greek it is written Iēsous, the same as the name of Jesus. Moses had changed his name from Hoshea to Joshua (Numbers 13:16).

Verse by verse.

“As I was with Moses, so I will be with you; I will not fail you or forsake you.” (1:5)

The leader changes; the Lord does not. The letter to the Hebrews takes up this promise for all believers: “I will never leave you or forsake you” (Hebrews 13:5).

“do not turn from it to the right hand or to the left,” (1:7)

The way of God is pictured as a road. One can fall off on either side, by too little or by too much. Faithfulness walks straight on.

“you shall meditate on it day and night,” (1:8)

Joshua is a soldier and a leader, yet his first task is to keep God's word in his mouth. Action without the word becomes blind.

“do not be frightened or dismayed, for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go.” (1:9)

The promise ends with the same truth with which it began. Fear is not answered by an argument but by a presence.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers saw in Joshua a figure of Jesus. St Justin Martyr points out that the one who led the people into the land bore the same name as Jesus, who leads us into our true inheritance (in substance), and Origen preached a whole series of homilies on Joshua in this light. The Catechism explains that the name Jesus means “God saves” (CCC 430). The Church also reads Joshua's story at this season for its pattern of departure and presence: Moses goes, and the Lord remains with his people; Jesus ascends, and the Spirit comes. What God said to Joshua, the risen Lord says to his Church: “I am with you always” (Matthew 28:20).

Ephesians 1:15–23 · With the eyes of your heart enlightened

Setting.

Ephesians presents itself as a letter of Paul written from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think it was written after his lifetime by a disciple who gathered up his teaching, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way. It may have been meant for several churches of the province of Asia, since some early manuscripts lack the words “in Ephesus” in 1:1.

The letter opens with a long hymn of blessing (1:3–14) for all that God has done in Christ. In today's verses the hymn turns into a prayer. Paul thanks God for the faith and love of his readers and asks that they may see what they have been given. The prayer ends where this season is: with Christ raised and seated at the right hand of the Father.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: thanksgiving for the readers' faith and love.
  • v. 17: prayer for a spirit of wisdom and revelation.
  • vv. 18–19: that they may know three things: the hope, the inheritance, the power.
  • vv. 20–21: that power at work in raising and enthroning Christ.
  • vv. 22–23: Christ, head over all things for the Church, his body.

Key words.

  • ophthalmoi tēs kardias, eyes of the heart: For the Bible the heart is the centre of the person, the place of thought and decision, not only of feeling. Paul prays for an inner sight, a seeing with the whole self, which only God can give.
  • kephalē, head: Christ is head both over all things and of the Church. As head he rules, and as head he gives life to the body, as the head does to its members (4:15–16).
  • plērōma, fullness: The Church is called “the fullness of him who fills all in all”. The phrase is difficult; it may mean that the Church is filled by Christ, or that in some way she completes him as a body completes its head. Either way, the Church is not an organisation beside Christ but his body, filled with his life.

Verse by verse.

“may give you a spirit of wisdom and revelation as you come to know him,” (1:17)

Paul does not pray first that they may do more, but that they may know God better. The Christian life grows from knowing him.

“so that, with the eyes of your heart enlightened, you may know what is the hope to which he has called you,” (1:18)

In the early Church Baptism was often called enlightenment (compare Hebrews 6:4). The baptized have received a new light, and Paul prays that it may grow.

“God put this power to work in Christ when he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places,” (1:20)

The same power is “for us who believe” (v. 19). The power that raised Christ is at work in his people.

“has made him the head over all things for the church, which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all.” (1:22–23)

The Ascension does not take Christ away from the Church. He reigns over all things for the sake of his body.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ's sitting at the right hand of the Father means the beginning of the reign of the Messiah (CCC 663–664), and that Christ, raised and exalted, is the head of the Church, which is his body (CCC 669; 792). The Second Vatican Council develops the image of the Church as Christ's body, in which he pours out his life on the believers through the sacraments (Lumen Gentium 7). The prayer of these verses is a good prayer for every parish between the Ascension and Pentecost: that we may see the hope, the inheritance and the power we have been given.

John 16:5–11 · It is to your advantage that I go away

Setting.

Jesus speaks these words at the Last Supper, in the long farewell discourse of John 13–17. He has washed his disciples' feet, given them the new commandment, and warned them that the world will hate them as it hated him (15:18–25). Now he tells them plainly that he is going, and they are filled with sorrow.

In this discourse Jesus speaks five times of the Holy Spirit as the Paraclete, the Advocate (14:16–17; 14:26; 15:26; 16:7–11; 16:13–15). Today's passage is the fourth. It shows the Spirit not only as the comforter of the disciples but as the one who brings the truth about Jesus before the world.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–6: Jesus is going to the Father; the disciples' sorrow.
  • v. 7: it is to your advantage: the Advocate will come.
  • v. 8: he will prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment.
  • vv. 9–11: the three explained: unbelief, Jesus' going to the Father, the condemnation of the ruler of this world.

Key words.

  • paraklētos, Advocate: One called to stand beside another, especially in a court: a defender, helper, counsellor. Jesus has been their Paraclete; now he promises “another Advocate” (14:16).
  • sympherei, it is to your advantage: The same verb Caiaphas used when he said that it was better for one man to die for the people (11:50). Caiaphas spoke more truly than he knew. The death and departure of Jesus are for the good of all.
  • elenchein, to prove wrong, convict: A word from the law court: to bring out the truth, to show someone their error so that they may see it. The Spirit will reopen the trial of Jesus, in which the world thought it judged him, and show where the truth lay.

Verse by verse.

“yet none of you asks me, ‘Where are you going?’” (16:5)

Peter and Thomas had asked something like this earlier (13:36; 14:5). Now the disciples are too filled with sorrow to ask. Their grief looks only at what they are losing, not at where he is going.

“it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you;” (16:7)

The Spirit is the fruit of the whole Paschal mystery. Only when Jesus has been glorified will the Spirit be given (7:39). His going is not loss but gift.

“And when he comes, he will prove the world wrong about sin and righteousness and judgment:” (16:8)

Three words from a trial. The world judged Jesus a sinner, condemned him, and thought itself righteous. The Spirit reverses that verdict.

“about judgment, because the ruler of this world has been condemned.” (16:11)

At the Cross it is not Jesus who is condemned, but the power of evil. Jesus had said, “now the ruler of this world will be driven out” (12:31).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Spirit, the other Paraclete, is promised when the hour of Jesus' glorification has come, and that he will prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment (CCC 729). It explains that conversion begins when the Spirit convinces us of sin, and at the same time gives the grace of repentance (CCC 1433). St John Paul II's encyclical on the Holy Spirit, Dominum et Vivificantem (1986), gives a long reflection on these verses, teaching that the Spirit convinces of sin in order to save, never to crush. The Spirit's work is like a doctor's: he shows the wound so that it can be healed.

Questions people ask

How can Jesus' going away be good for us?

While Jesus walked in Galilee he could be in one place at a time. Glorified at the right hand of the Father, he sends the Spirit, who makes him present to every believer, in every place, above all in the sacraments. The Catechism says the Spirit makes present the mystery of Christ (CCC 1092). We do not have less of Christ since the Ascension, but more.

Does “the world” here mean that everyone outside the Church is condemned?

No. In John, “the world” can mean the creation God loves (3:16) or the human order that closes itself against God. The Spirit convicts the world in order to bring it to truth and life. It is the ruler of this world who is condemned, not the people God wants to save.

Why do some Catholics in India keep the Ascension today?

The Ascension falls on a Thursday, and our Church kept Suloko on that day. In the Latin Church the bishops of a country may, with the approval of the Holy See, move the feast to the following Sunday so that more people can take part. The Latin Church in India does so, and keeps it today. The mystery is the same; only the day differs.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The days between Suloko and Pentecost are days of prayer with Mary and the apostles in the upper room, as today's reading from Acts shows. In every Holy Qurbono the priest calls down the Holy Spirit on the offerings and on the people, and the whole Church becomes, in a small way, the upper room waiting for the Spirit. Our Latin brothers and sisters in India celebrate the Ascension today. Next Sunday our Church keeps Pentecost, the fiftieth day. This week is a good time for families to pray each day for the gifts of the Spirit, so that the eyes of our hearts may be enlightened.
