Word for the day

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” Luke 10:2

Reflection

On the Monday after the Seventh Sunday after Pentecost the Panchangam turns to a group whose names we hardly know, the Seventy-two Heralds. St Luke writes: “After this the Lord appointed seventy others and sent them on ahead of him in pairs to every town and place where he himself intended to go.” The word “others” tells us that these are not the Twelve, whom Luke has just shown being sent out in the previous chapter. As for the number, the manuscripts of the Gospel are divided between seventy and seventy-two, and the Malayalam Bible that we use has seventy-two, as does the Panchangam. Both numbers carry a meaning. The Twelve recall the twelve tribes of Israel; the seventy or seventy-two recall the nations of the earth, which the book of Genesis lists as seventy in its Hebrew text and seventy-two in the old Greek version, and also the seventy elders on whom Moses saw the spirit rest in the wilderness (Numbers 11:24–26). Many Christian writers, ancient and modern, therefore see in this second sending a sign that the Gospel is meant for all peoples. They are called heralds, announcers, because they go ahead: they prepare the towns for the Lord who is coming after them.

Their instructions begin with a surprise. Before saying “Go,” the Lord says “Pray”: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” The mission starts on our knees, and the Lord is the one who sends. Then comes a warning that is plain and without decoration: “See, I am sending you out like lambs into the midst of wolves.” They are to be defenceless and dependent: “Carry no purse, no bag, no sandals; and greet no one on the road.” The last words are not rudeness. On eastern roads a greeting could take hours of ceremony; the heralds have no time to lose. And they go in pairs. St Gregory the Great noticed that the Lord sends his disciples two by two because there are two commandments of love, of God and of neighbour, and love needs at least two (Homilies on the Gospels 17, in substance). Two people carry the message more truthfully than one, they hold each other up on the road, and their agreement is itself a proof.

Then the Lord describes how the Gospel spreads, and it is through homes. “Whatever house you enter, first say, ‘Peace to this house!’ And if anyone is there who shares in peace, your peace will rest on that person; but if not, it will return to you.” The first word of the herald is a blessing. “Remain in the same house, eating and drinking whatever they provide, for the laborer deserves to be paid. Do not move about from house to house.” They are to stay, to eat what is set before them and to learn the family's life; they are not to go about looking for a more comfortable house. It is a Kerala picture as much as a Galilean one: the family prayer in the evening, the priest's visit to bless the house, the neighbours who take a stranger in. And the message comes with healing: “cure the sick who are there, and say to them, ‘The kingdom of God has come near to you.’” Care and word go together. Even if a town refuses them, the message stands: “Yet know this: the kingdom of God has come near.” The refusal of some does not cancel the nearness of the kingdom.

The Epistle tells us what kind of weapons such messengers carry. St Paul has been ridiculed in Corinth: they say “His letters are weighty and strong, but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible.” He answers: “Indeed, we live as human beings, but we do not wage war according to human standards; for the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power to destroy strongholds.” The heralds' weapons are the word, peace, prayer, patience and the care of the sick, and their aim is “to take every thought captive to obey Christ.” Their authority is “for building you up and not for tearing you down.” Our own Syriac tradition remembers heralds of this kind. Eusebius tells that after the Ascension Thomas sent Thaddaeus, whom he counts “one of the seventy,” to Edessa, where he healed and preached; the Syriac Churches honour him as Mar Addai (Church History 1.13, in substance). And on Saturday we kept the Dukrono of Mar Thoma Shleeha, the Apostle whom tradition says brought the Gospel to India. Every baptized person is also a herald today: Pope Francis wrote that we are no longer to say that we are disciples and missionaries, but always “missionary disciples.” Let us ask the Lord of the harvest to send laborers, and let us begin by saying “Peace to this house” at our own door.

On Monday of the seventh week after Pentecost, the commemoration of the Seventy-two Heralds: sent in pairs, sent with peace, and armed with weapons that are not merely human.

“As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” John 20:21

Apply: Ask the Lord where he is sending you ahead of him this week, and find one companion to go with you.

2. Pray first

  • The harvest is plentiful and the laborers are few.
  • The Lord of the harvest is the one who sends.
  • The mission begins on our knees.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” Matthew 9:37-38

Apply: Pray every day this week for laborers for the harvest, and ask whether the Lord is calling you.

3. Peace to this house

  • The first word of the herald is a blessing.
  • Eat what is set before you and care for the sick.
  • The kingdom of God has come near.

Apply: Greet your own house and one neighbour's house this week with a blessing of peace.

  • Authority for building up, not tearing down.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Panchangam remembers the Seventy-two Heralds. St Luke tells that the Lord appointed others, seventy or seventy-two in number, and sent them on ahead of him in pairs to every town and place where he himself intended to go (Luke 10:1). They are not the Twelve. They stand for all who are sent to announce that the Lord is coming, and the number reminds us of all the nations of the earth.

The first instruction is to pray: The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest (Luke 10:2). Then they are sent as lambs among wolves, with no purse, no bag and no sandals, and in pairs, so that no one goes alone. They are to say first: Peace to this house! (Luke 10:5). They are to eat what is set before them, to cure the sick, and to say: The kingdom of God has come near to you (Luke 10:9).

St Paul tells the Corinthians that the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power (2 Corinthians 10:4). The Lord's heralds do not fight with force but with peace, with the word, with prayer and with the care of the sick. The authority given to them is for building up and not for tearing down (2 Corinthians 10:8).

Every baptized person is a herald. So let us pray for laborers for the harvest, let us greet our homes with a blessing of peace, and let us go, in pairs if we can, to someone who needs to hear that the kingdom of God is near. Amen.

For children

Children, once Jesus sent seventy-two of his friends to go ahead of him to the villages. They went in pairs, so nobody was alone. Do you know the first thing they said when they came to a house? “Peace to this house!” That is a beautiful greeting, isn't it? You can do the same: when you come home, say a happy hello and wish peace to your family. And you can ask a friend to go with you when you visit a grandparent or a neighbour who is sick. Then you will be a herald of Jesus, just like those seventy-two!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Lord of the harvest, you appointed seventy-two disciples and sent them ahead of you in pairs to every town where you intended to go. Send laborers into your harvest, and make us laborers, lambs among wolves who trust in you alone. Teach us to bless every house we enter with your peace, to eat what is set before us and to care for the sick, and to say to everyone that the kingdom of God has come near. Give us the weapons that have divine power, the word of truth and the gentleness of patience, and let us use any authority only to build up. Through the prayers of the Seventy-two Heralds, of Mar Addai and of Mar Thoma Shleeha, make our Church a herald of your coming. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of being sent. In Luke the Lord sends out the seventy-two ahead of him, two by two, with no purse and no bag, to say “Peace to this house!” and to announce that the kingdom of God has come near. In Corinthians Paul defends the authority the Lord has given him, an authority “for building you up and not for tearing you down”. The Panchangam remembers the Seventy-two Heralds today, two days after the Dukrono of St Thomas. The key words are sent and building up.

2 Corinthians 10:3–11 · Weapons that are not merely human

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 of this letter Paul's tone becomes sharp. Some people in Corinth, whom he later calls “super-apostles” (11:5), were saying that he was bold on paper but weak in person. Many scholars think these chapters come from a separate letter written in a time of conflict, while others read the whole letter as a unity. Paul answers them, though with great reluctance and often with irony.

In today's verses he says that his struggle is not carried on by human means, defends the authority he has been given, and warns that he will be the same in person as he is in letters.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–6: the weapons of his warfare are not merely human; they bring every thought captive to Christ.
  • vv. 7–8: they belong to Christ, and so does he; his authority is for building up.
  • vv. 9–11: his letters are strong and his presence is said to be weak; he will be the same in person.

Key words.

  • ochyrōmata, strongholds: Fortified places. Paul uses the picture of a siege for the fight against false arguments and pride, which are like walls raised against the knowledge of God.
  • oikodomē, building up: The word for building a house, used by Paul for the growth of the Church. Everything he does as an apostle is to be tested by whether it builds up or breaks down.
  • noēma, thought: The mind and its plans. Paul does not want to destroy thought but to bring it to obey Christ, so that it serves the truth.

Verse by verse.

“the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power to destroy strongholds.” (10:4)

The weapons are the truth, patience, prayer and love (compare 6:6–7; Ephesians 6:11–17). They look weak, but God's power works through them.

“we take every thought captive to obey Christ.” (10:5)

The aim is not to win an argument but to lead the mind to Christ. Faith asks for the whole person, including the mind.

“which the Lord gave for building you up and not for tearing you down,” (10:8)

This is the test of all authority in the Church. It is given by the Lord, for others, and for building up. He says this again at the end of the letter (13:10).

“his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible.” (10:10)

This is what his critics said. Paul does not deny that he is weak; he will say that the Lord's power is made perfect in weakness (12:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read these verses as a charter for her spiritual warfare. The Catechism teaches that prayer is a battle and that it needs faith, humility and perseverance (CCC 2725–2745, in substance). It also teaches that ministry in the Church is service, and the authority of those who lead is given for the good of the faithful (CCC 876, in substance).

Luke 10:1–12 · Peace to this house

Setting.

Jesus has set his face towards Jerusalem (9:51). He sends messengers ahead of him to the places he will visit. Luke alone tells this mission of a larger group, after the earlier mission of the Twelve (9:1–6). The manuscripts differ on the number: some read seventy, others seventy-two. The Panchangam and the Malayalam Bible follow seventy-two. Many scholars see in either number a sign of all the nations of the world, since the list of nations in Genesis 10 has seventy in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in many Greek witnesses.

The disciples go in pairs, to support one another and to give a trustworthy witness (compare Deuteronomy 19:15). They go ahead to the towns and places that Jesus himself intends to visit on his way to Jerusalem, to prepare for his coming. The warnings about towns that do not welcome them use the old language of the prophets, to show the seriousness of refusing the kingdom. They are not a judgment on the Jewish people as a whole.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Jesus appoints and sends them in pairs; the harvest is plentiful, the laborers few; pray to the Lord of the harvest.
  • vv. 3–7: like lambs among wolves, with nothing in their hands; into every house they say “Peace”; they stay in one house.
  • vv. 8–12: if welcomed, eat, heal and proclaim that the kingdom has come near; if not, the warning.

Key words.

  • apesteilen, sent: The same root as “apostle”. The seventy-two are sent as the Twelve are, with Christ's own authority.
  • ergatai, laborers: Workers in the field. The Lord of the harvest owns the field; the laborers serve. The first thing Jesus asks is that his people pray for more laborers.
  • ēngiken, has come near: The kingdom of God is close at hand: in the person of Jesus and in his messengers. The disciples do not bring their own message but his.

Verse by verse.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” (10:2)

Jesus's own answer to the need is prayer. Those who pray for laborers will find themselves sent.

“I am sending you out like lambs into the midst of wolves.” (10:3)

The mission is not safe and is not a show of strength. The messengers have no weapons but peace.

“Whatever house you enter, first say, ‘Peace to this house!’” (10:5)

The first word of the mission is peace, shalom, the same word as in the greeting of the risen Lord (John 20:19). The house comes first because the gospel is carried from home to home.

“the laborer deserves to be paid.” (10:7)

Those who serve the gospel may receive their support from the people they serve. Paul quotes this saying in 1 Timothy 5:18.

“The kingdom of God has come near to you.” (10:9)

The message is that God is near. The healing of the sick is the sign that this is true.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the whole Church is apostolic: she is “sent out” into the whole world, and all her members share in this mission (CCC 863, in substance). St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, wrote that the harvest is plentiful but the laborers few, and that we must pray for more, and sadly he said that there were many to hear and few to preach (Homilies on the Gospels 17, in substance). The Church prays today for vocations, not only to the priesthood but to every way of serving the gospel.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Panchangam remembers today the Seventy-two Heralds, the disciples sent ahead of the Lord in Luke 10:1. Two days ago the Church kept the Dukrono of Mar Thoma Shleeha, who was sent to India. Today the whole Church is reminded that every disciple, and not only the apostles, shares in the Lord's mission. Pray for those who preach the gospel in our parishes and in distant lands.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus first asks his disciples to pray for laborers. Do I pray for vocations, and might I be one of the ones he is sending?
  • Paul's authority is “for building up and not for tearing down”. In my family, work or parish, do my words and decisions build up?
