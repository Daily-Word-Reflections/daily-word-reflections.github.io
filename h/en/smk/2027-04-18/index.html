Word for the day

“Were not our hearts burning within us while he was talking to us on the road, while he was opening the scriptures to us?” Luke 24:32

Reflection

On Easter afternoon two disciples walk away from Jerusalem towards Emmaus, sad and confused. “We had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel.” Jesus himself comes near and walks with them, but their eyes are kept from recognising him. He listens to their sorrow, then opens the Scriptures: “beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things about himself in all the scriptures.”

As evening falls they beg him: “Stay with us, because it is almost evening.” At table “he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them. Then their eyes were opened, and they recognized him.” They had heard him in the Word; they recognise him in the breaking of the bread. Emmaus is the shape of every Holy Qurbono: first the Word that sets hearts burning, then the Bread in which we know him.

The two disciples do not wait until morning. That same hour they return to Jerusalem to tell the others: “The Lord has risen indeed.” A heart that has met the risen Lord cannot stay silent. Peter and John say the same before the council in Acts: “we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard.”

St Paul reminds us that this is all grace: “by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God.” Jesus walks with us on our roads of disappointment, even when we do not recognise him. He waits to be invited: “Stay with us.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on Easter afternoon two disciples are walking away from Jerusalem, sad and disappointed. “We had hoped that he was the one,” they say. Many of us walk such roads: disappointed with life, with the Church, with God. And then a stranger joins them.

It is Jesus, but they do not recognise him. He lets them pour out their sorrow. Then he opens the Scriptures, from Moses and all the prophets, and shows them that the Messiah had to suffer and enter his glory. Later they will say: “Were not our hearts burning within us while he was opening the scriptures?”

As evening falls they invite him in: “Stay with us.” At table he takes bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it to them, and their eyes are opened. This is exactly what happens at every Holy Qurbono: the Word sets our hearts on fire; the breaking of the bread opens our eyes. Emmaus is not just a story; it is our Sunday.

And they could not keep it to themselves. In the dark they ran back seven miles to Jerusalem to tell the others: “The Lord is risen!” Let us read the Gospel each day this week, invite Jesus into our homes, and tell someone what he has done for us. Stay with us, Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Third Sunday after Qyomtho gathers its readings around a road and a table. The Evening Gospel (Mark 16:9–13) tells in a few lines how the risen Lord “appeared in another form to two of them, as they were walking into the country”. Exodus 40:1–15 shows Moses setting up the tabernacle and anointing Aaron and his sons, so that God may dwell among his people. In Joshua 2:1–14 Rahab, an outsider, shelters the spies and confesses the God of Israel. Amos 4:6–13 repeats five times God's sad refrain, “yet you did not return to me”. In Acts 4:5–22 Peter and John, “uneducated and ordinary men”, tell the council, “we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard.”

We study three more closely. Isaiah hears God say, “Here I am, here I am,” to people who were not looking for him. Ephesians tells the baptized that they were dead and have been raised with Christ, by grace. Luke tells how the risen Jesus walked with two disciples who did not recognize him, opened the Scriptures to them, and was known in the breaking of the bread. The key words are found, grace and recognize.

Isaiah 65:1–7 · I was ready to be found

Setting.

Many scholars think that chapters 56–66 of Isaiah speak to the community in Judah after the return from exile in Babylon, in the late sixth century before Christ. The temple was being rebuilt, hopes were high, and the people were divided. Some still followed old pagan rites. Chapter 64 ended with the people's anguished prayer: “Will you keep silent, and punish us so severely?” (64:12). Chapter 65 is God's answer.

The answer begins with a surprise. God has not been hiding. He has been calling out all along, even to those who never called to him.

Structure.

  • v. 1: God ready to be sought and found, saying “Here I am”.
  • v. 2: hands held out all day to a rebellious people.
  • vv. 3–5: the rites they practised and their claim to be holier than others.
  • vv. 6–7: God will not keep silent but will judge.

Key words.

  • nidrashtî, nimṣē’tî, I was ready to be sought, to be found: The Hebrew forms mean “I let myself be sought”, “I let myself be found”. God takes the first step, and makes himself available before anyone looks for him.
  • hinnēnî, Here I am: In the Bible this is usually a person's answer to God: Abraham says it (Genesis 22:1), and Isaiah himself, “Here am I; send me!” (6:8). Here God says it, twice, to people who have not called.
  • ‘am sôrēr, a rebellious people: A people who turn aside, stubborn like a wayward child (see Deuteronomy 21:18). The word describes the conduct of those who follow their own devices, not the worth of a nation.

Verse by verse.

“I was ready to be sought out by those who did not ask, to be found by those who did not seek me.” (65:1)

Grace comes before our searching. The two disciples on the road to Emmaus were not looking for the risen Lord; he came to them.

“I held out my hands all day long to a rebellious people,” (65:2)

The picture is of open arms, waiting. St Justin Martyr and other early Christian writers saw in it the arms of the Lord stretched out on the Cross (First Apology 35, in substance). Those arms are held out to every person.

“who sit inside tombs, and spend the night in secret places; who eat swine’s flesh,” (65:4)

These seem to be practices of consulting the dead and of pagan ritual meals, forbidden by the law (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 18:11). People sought life among the dead.

“who say, “Keep to yourself, do not come near me, for I am too holy for you.”” (65:5)

Those who had turned away from God still claimed a holiness that set them above others. False religion makes people proud and keeps them apart.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul quotes these verses in Romans 10:20–21: he hears verse 1 of the nations who found God without seeking him, and verse 2 of Israel, to whom God's hands remained stretched out. Paul goes on at once: “has God rejected his people? By no means!” (Romans 11:1). The Church reads Isaiah 65 in that spirit, as a word about God's patient love for all, and as a warning to every believer, never as a judgement on the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). The God who says “Here I am” is the God who comes to meet the disciples on the road.

Ephesians 2:1–10 · By grace you have been saved

Setting.

The Letter to the Ephesians has long been received as a letter of St Paul. Many scholars today think it was written by a disciple of his in his name, drawing on his teaching; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. The Church reads it as inspired Scripture in either case. It may have been meant for several churches in the region of Ephesus.

Chapter 1 ended by praising the power God showed when “he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand” (1:20). Chapter 2 says that the same power has worked in the baptized. What God did for Christ, he has done for us with him.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: what we were: dead, following the course of this world.
  • vv. 4–7: “But God”: made alive, raised and seated with Christ.
  • vv. 8–10: saved by grace through faith, created for good works.

Key words.

  • chariti este sesōsmenoi, by grace you have been saved: The Greek perfect tense means saved and remaining in that state. The source is grace, God's undeserved favour.
  • synezōopoiēsen, synēgeiren, synekathisen, made alive with, raised with, seated with: Three verbs joined with “with” (syn). Christians share Christ's own life, Resurrection and glory. Baptism is the moment of this union (see Colossians 2:12).
  • poiēma, what he has made: God's handiwork, his work of art. Our English word “poem” comes from this Greek word. The Christian life is something God creates.

Verse by verse.

“You were dead through the trespasses and sins” (2:1)

Not weak or sick, but dead. Only someone who gives life can help the dead, and that is what the next verses describe.

“But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us” (2:4)

The turning point of the passage. All of salvation hangs on these two words, “But God”.

“For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God” (2:8)

Faith is the hand that receives; grace is the gift. Even faith itself is God's gift.

“For we are what he has made us, created in Christ Jesus for good works,” (2:10)

Our own works cannot earn the grace that saves; good works are the purpose for which God has made us new. Grace that saves also makes us able to do good.

How the Church reads it.

The Catholic Church teaches that justification comes from the grace of God, given to us at Baptism, and that no one can merit the first grace of conversion (CCC 1996, 2010). It also teaches that the good works of the justified are truly good and pleasing to God, because they are the fruit of his grace in us: “The charity of Christ is the source in us of all our merits before God” (CCC 2011). Ephesians 2:8–10 holds both together: we are saved by grace, and created for good works.

Luke 24:13–35 · Known in the breaking of the bread

Setting.

It is still Easter Day. In the morning the women found the tomb empty and heard the angels (24:1–12). The apostles thought their report “an idle tale” (24:11). That afternoon two disciples, one named Cleopas, walk out of Jerusalem toward a village called Emmaus. Luke alone tells this story at length; the Evening Gospel shows that Mark's longer ending knew it in brief.

Where Emmaus lay is not certain. Most manuscripts give the distance as sixty stadia, “about seven miles”, while some give one hundred and sixty. Several places have been proposed, among them Emmaus-Nicopolis (Amwas), El-Qubeibeh and others, and pilgrims honour more than one.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–16: the two on the road; Jesus joins them, unrecognized.
  • vv. 17–24: their story: hope lost and a puzzling report.
  • vv. 25–27: Jesus opens the Scriptures.
  • vv. 28–32: “Stay with us”; the breaking of the bread; eyes opened.
  • vv. 33–35: back to Jerusalem: “The Lord has risen indeed.”

Key words.

  • ekratounto, were kept: Their eyes “were kept” from recognizing him. The passive suggests that God was at work: recognition would come as a gift, at the right moment.
  • dianoigō, to open: The key verb of the chapter. Their eyes were opened (v. 31), he opened the Scriptures (v. 32), and he opened the minds of the Eleven (v. 45). The risen Lord opens what was closed.
  • klasis tou artou, breaking of the bread: The phrase of v. 35, which Luke uses again of the first Christians' gatherings (Acts 2:42). It became one of the earliest names for the Eucharist (CCC 1329).

Verse by verse.

“but their eyes were kept from recognizing him.” (24:16)

The Lord is present before he is recognized. Much of the Christian life is learning to see one who is already beside us.

“But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel.” (24:21)

The saddest sentence in the story, in the past tense. Jesus does not mock their disappointment; he walks with it and listens.

“Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things and then enter into his glory?” (24:26)

“Necessary” is the necessity of God's plan, foretold in the Scriptures. The Cross was not the end of the hope but the way to it.

“Then beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things about himself in all the scriptures.” (24:27)

The first Christian Bible study, given by the Lord himself. The whole Old Testament is read with Christ as its key.

“he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them.” (24:30)

The same four actions as at the feeding of the crowd (9:16) and at the Last Supper (22:19). The guest has become the host.

“Were not our hearts burning within us while he was talking to us on the road, while he was opening the scriptures to us?” (24:32)

Only after recognizing him at table do they understand what was happening on the road. Word and table belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in Emmaus the shape of the Eucharistic celebration: the Lord walks with his people, opens the Scriptures, then takes bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it (CCC 1347). The Second Vatican Council says that the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she has venerated the Body of the Lord, offering the bread of life from the one table of the Word and of the Body of Christ (Dei Verbum 21). St Augustine told his people that the disciples did not recognize the Lord while he explained the Scriptures, but did in the breaking of bread, and that we too, who do not see him in the flesh, know him there (Sermon 235, in substance).

Questions people ask

Why did the two disciples not recognize Jesus?

Luke says that “their eyes were kept from recognizing him”, which suggests that God was at work. Mark's Evening Gospel says he appeared “in another form”. The risen body of Christ is truly his own, but glorified, and he is known only when he gives himself to be known. Recognition came through the Scriptures opened and the bread broken, the way by which we too come to know him.

Was the meal at Emmaus a Eucharist?

Luke tells it with the same words he used at the Last Supper: he took bread, blessed it, broke it and gave it. Many Fathers and the Church read the scene as Eucharistic, and the Catechism uses it to explain the shape of the liturgy (CCC 1347). Whether or not we call that evening meal a Qurbono in the full sense, Luke clearly wanted his readers to recognize the Lord where they broke bread every Sunday.

Does Ephesians 2:9, “not the result of works”, mean good works do not matter?

No. Verse 10 says that we were created “for good works”. Paul's point is that no one earns salvation; it is God's gift. Once given, grace bears fruit, and the good works we do in Christ are pleasing to God and have their reward, because they are his grace at work in us (CCC 2011).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The Syro-Malankara Sunday follows the same road as Emmaus. In the Holy Qurbono the Word is read first, ending with the Gospel proclaimed by the celebrant; then comes the anaphora, in which the priest repeats the Lord's words over the bread and the cup, and the fraction, when the Body of the Lord is broken. Every Sunday of Qyomtho we walk with Cleopas and his companion from the Scriptures to the table. The evening prayer of Saturday, with today's Evening Gospel from Mark, already set us on the road.

For study and teaching

  • When have I been walking away from Jerusalem, saying “we had hoped”? How did the Lord come alongside me?
  • God says “Here I am” to those who did not seek him. Who in my life is not seeking God, and how might I show them his open arms?
  • Ephesians says I am God's handiwork, created for good works. What good work has God prepared for me this week?
  • How can I prepare to hear the Word and receive the bread at next Sunday's Holy Qurbono so that my heart burns within me?
