Word for the day

“Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Luke 1:38

Reflection

Last Sunday the angel came to an old priest in the Temple. Today the same angel, Gabriel, comes to a young virgin in Nazareth, a village never mentioned in the Old Testament. God's greatest work begins in a hidden house, in the heart of a woman who listens.

The readings stretch from the first pages of the Bible to its fulfilment. In the garden, after the first sin, God promises that the offspring of the woman will strike the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). Isaiah cries: “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down” (Isaiah 64:1). St Paul answers: “When the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman” (Galatians 4:4). The Annunciation is the moment when heaven opens and God comes down.

“The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” Mary conceives not by human power but by the Spirit, as Zechariah's vision promised: “Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit” (Zechariah 4:6). Her child is truly God and truly man; that is why the Church calls her Theotokos, Mother of God.

Zechariah asked, “How will I know?” and was silenced. Mary asks, “How can this be?” and then gives her free consent: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” The Fathers saw in her obedience the reversal of Eve's disobedience. Through her “yes”, the Son of God becomes our brother, and we become “children of God; and that is what we are” (1 John 3:1).

Homily outline

Theme: “Let it be with me according to your word.”

Today heaven and earth meet in a small house in Nazareth. God's plan, promised from the first pages of the Bible, waits for the free “yes” of a young woman.

1. God answers humanity's oldest cry

  • After the first sin God promised a woman whose offspring would crush the serpent.
  • Through the centuries the prophets cried: “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down!”
  • In the fullness of time God sent his Son, born of a woman: Mary is that woman.
“I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.” Genesis 3:15
“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law.” Galatians 4:4

Apply: Thank God this week that he did not abandon humanity after sin.

2. Full of grace, by the power of the Spirit

  • The angel greets Mary as the “favored one”: God's grace has prepared her.
  • The Incarnation is not human achievement but the work of the Holy Spirit overshadowing her.
  • God still works “not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit”.
“Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, says the LORD of hosts.” Zechariah 4:6
“The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” Luke 1:35

Apply: Before every important task, pray: “Come, Holy Spirit.”

3. The “yes” of faith

  • Mary asks how, not whether: her question seeks understanding, not proof.
  • She surrenders like clay to the potter: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord.”
  • Her “yes” changes history; our small daily “yes” lets Christ be born in our world.
“Yet, O LORD, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand.” Isaiah 64:8
“Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Luke 1:38

Apply: Pray the Angelus (or three Hail Marys) every day this week, morning, noon and evening.

God never forces his love; he asks. In Mary humanity answered “yes” on behalf of us all.

Let us learn from her to listen, to ask, and to say each day: “Let it be with me according to your word.”

Take-home resolution: Say one hidden “yes” to God this week in something you have been resisting.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, last Sunday an angel came to an old priest in the great Temple of Jerusalem. Today the same angel goes to a little village called Nazareth, to a young girl named Mary. God's greatest work begins in a small house, in a heart that listens.

Our readings remind us that God had promised this from the very beginning. When Adam and Eve sinned, God said that the offspring of the woman would crush the serpent's head. For centuries the prophets cried, “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down!” And St Paul says, “When the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman.” Today that fullness arrives.

Mary asks a question: “How can this be?” Faith may ask questions. But then she says the words that changed the world: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” The Fathers said: Eve's disobedience tied a knot; Mary's obedience untied it. Through her, the Son of God became man; that is why we call her Mother of God.

God is still looking for hearts that say yes. Perhaps he asks you to forgive someone, to accept an illness, to give time to prayer, or to answer a call to the priesthood or religious life. In a few moments the Holy Spirit will come down upon this altar as he came upon Mary. Let us say with her: “Let it be with me according to your word.” Amen.

For children

Children, who visited Mary in her little house? The angel Gabriel! He said: “Mary, God is with you. You will have a baby, and you will call him Jesus.”

Mary said: “Yes, I will do what God wants.” And Jesus, God's own Son, became a tiny baby inside her.

Every day God asks us small things: to obey, to share, to pray. Let's say like Mary: “Yes, God!”

Prayer

Lord God, you sent your angel to the Virgin Mary, and by the power of the Holy Spirit your Son took flesh in her womb. Give us her listening heart and her generous “yes”, that Christ may be formed in us. Through her prayers, Mother of God, keep us faithful. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sunday of the Annunciation to Mary, Mother of God, the West Syriac Church stands at the second step of the Suboro season, the Sundays of announcement that lead to Yaldo, the Nativity. The readings trace one long promise. In the evening Gospel Matthew opens his book with the genealogy (Matthew 1:1–17): forty-two generations of a family through which God kept his word. The Old Testament lessons announce a birth that only God can give: the first promise to the woman in Eden (Genesis 3:8–15), the angel who announces Samson to a barren woman (Judges 13:2–14), the temple rebuilt “not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit” (Zechariah 4:6), and Isaiah's cry, “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down” (Isaiah 64:1). The Catholic Epistle tells us what that coming makes of us: “See what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God” (1 John 3:1).

The three readings studied here form a single line. Genesis gives the promise: the offspring of the woman will strike the serpent's head. Galatians names the moment: “when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman”. Luke shows the woman herself, in Nazareth, saying yes. Promise, fullness, consent: the key words of this Sunday.

Genesis 3:8–15 · Where are you? The first promise

Setting.

The scene comes straight after the sin of Genesis 3:1–7. The man and the woman have eaten from the forbidden tree, their eyes are opened, and they sew fig leaves together. Now God comes into the garden, and what follows has the shape of a trial: the Judge questions, the accused answer, and sentence is given.

Genesis 1–11 is not a history book in the modern sense. It teaches, in images, real truths about God, the world and man, among them a real fall at the beginning of human history (CCC 390). The garden, the tree and the serpent carry that teaching.

Structure.

  • v. 8: the sound of God walking in the garden; the man and woman hide.
  • vv. 9–10: the first question, “Where are you?”, and the first confession of fear.
  • vv. 11–13: two more questions; the man blames the woman (and God who gave her), the woman blames the serpent.
  • vv. 14–15: the sentence on the serpent, which ends in a promise.
  • ¶ God questions the man and the woman, but he does not curse them. He curses only the serpent here (v. 14) and the ground later (v. 17). In the middle of judgment a door to hope is already open.

Key words.

  • ’ayyekkāh, where are you?: One Hebrew word. God is not asking for information. It is the call of a father to a child who is hiding, and it is the first question God asks in the Bible. All of salvation history is God looking for man.
  • ’êbāh, enmity: A lasting hostility, not a passing quarrel. God himself puts it there: “I will put enmity”. The woman and her offspring will not be on the serpent's side.
  • zera‘, offspring, seed: The word can mean a whole line of descendants or one single descendant. The verse begins with the line (“your offspring and hers”) and ends with one person: “he will strike your head”.
  • šûp, strike: The same verb is used for both blows: he strikes the head, the serpent strikes the heel. The struggle is real on both sides, but a wound on the heel is not the same as a blow to the head.

Verse by verse.

“But the Lord God called to the man, and said to him, “Where are you?”” (3:9)

The man ran from God; God comes after the man. The hiding began with fear (v. 10), and fear is still what keeps people away from God's call.

“The man said, “The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate.”” (3:12)

Sin has already broken the unity of the couple. A few verses earlier he called her “bone of my bones” (2:23); now she is “the woman whom you gave”. The blame passes on to the woman, and through her even to God.

“I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.” (3:15)

A note on the text. The Hebrew has “he” (hû’), and the Greek Septuagint uses a masculine pronoun (autos), pointing to one person, the Messiah. Many Latin manuscripts of the Vulgate read ipsa, “she will crush”, and older Catholic art shows Mary with her foot on the serpent. Both readings carry the same faith: the victory is Christ's, and his mother shares in it as the new Eve.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Genesis 3:15 the Protoevangelium, the “first gospel”: the first announcement of the Messiah and Redeemer, of a battle between the serpent and the woman, and of the final victory of her descendant. Many Fathers and Doctors of the Church have seen the woman as Mary, the mother of Christ, the “new Eve” (CCC 410–411).

St Justin Martyr and St Irenaeus set Eve and Mary side by side. St Irenaeus writes that the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by Mary's obedience; what the virgin Eve bound through unbelief, the Virgin Mary loosed through faith (CCC 494; Lumen Gentium 56).

Galatians 4:1–7 · God sent his Son, born of a woman

Setting.

Paul writes to the churches of Galatia, in the centre of Asia Minor, which he had founded. After he left, other teachers told the Gentile believers that they must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to belong fully to God's people. In chapters 3–4 Paul answers: we are children of Abraham and heirs of God through faith in Christ, not through the works of the Law.

This passage is the only place where Paul speaks, even indirectly, of the mother of Jesus. It is also one of the earliest Christian texts about the Incarnation, written within about twenty-five years of the Resurrection.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: a picture from family law: an heir who is still a child lives under guardians until the date set by his father.
  • v. 3: the application: “so with us”; before Christ we were like minors, enslaved to “the elemental spirits of the world”.
  • vv. 4–5: the first sending: God sends his Son, to redeem and to make us children.
  • vv. 6–7: the second sending: God sends the Spirit of his Son into our hearts; so we are no longer slaves but children and heirs.
  • ¶ The passage is built on two sendings with the same verb: God sent his Son (v. 4), God sent the Spirit of his Son (v. 6). The Father sends, the Son redeems, the Spirit cries out in us. It is a short lesson on the Holy Trinity.

Key words.

  • to plērōma tou chronou, the fullness of time: Time is pictured as a vessel being filled. The coming of the Son is not an accident of history but the moment the Father had fixed (compare v. 2, “the date set by the father”).
  • genomenon ek gynaikos, born of a woman: Paul says nothing of a human father. The phrase stresses that the Son of God became truly human, with a real mother, in a real family, “under the law”.
  • huiothesia, adoption as children: A legal word: to be placed as a son, with full rights of inheritance. We are not children of God by nature, as the Son is, but by grace we receive the same name and the same inheritance.
  • Abba, Father: An Aramaic word, the way a son or daughter spoke to their own father. Jesus prayed with it in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36). Paul keeps the Aramaic even in a Greek letter: the Spirit lets us pray with the very words of Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law,” (4:4)

Four short phrases hold the whole mystery of Christmas: the time (fullness), the sender (God), the one sent (his Son), and the way he came (born of a woman, born under the law). He comes in humility, inside the history of Israel.

“And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!”” (4:6)

Notice who cries out. It is the Spirit, crying in our hearts. When we pray “Our Father”, it is the Spirit of the Son praying in us.

“So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if a child then also an heir, through God.” (4:7)

Paul suddenly turns from “we” to “you” in the singular. Each believer is meant to hear it personally: you are no longer a slave.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on Jesus Christ with this text (CCC 422) and says that the Annunciation to Mary inaugurates “the fullness of time” (CCC 484). It also teaches that the One whom the Father sends into our hearts, the Spirit of his Son, is truly God (CCC 689). The verses are a key text of the Church's faith that Christ is true God and true man, and that Mary is truly his mother.

Luke 1:26–38 · Here am I, the servant of the Lord

Setting.

Luke opens his Gospel with two announcements set side by side. The first (1:5–25) takes place in the Temple in Jerusalem, to an old priest, Zechariah, who asks for a sign and is struck dumb. The second takes place in Nazareth, a small village in Galilee never mentioned in the Old Testament, to a young girl engaged to Joseph. The angel is the same, Gabriel, whom the book of Daniel already knows as the messenger of the end times (Daniel 9:21).

In Jewish custom, the engagement was a binding marriage contract. The bride normally stayed in her father's house for some months before she was taken into her husband's home.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: the sending: time (the sixth month), place (Nazareth), person (a virgin, of the house of David through Joseph).
  • vv. 28–29: the greeting and Mary's troubled reflection.
  • vv. 30–33: the first message: a son named Jesus, who will be the Davidic king forever.
  • v. 34: Mary's question.
  • vv. 35–37: the second message: the Holy Spirit, the power of the Most High, the Son of God; and a sign, Elizabeth.
  • v. 38: Mary's answer, and the angel departs.

Key words.

  • chaire, greetings, rejoice: The ordinary Greek greeting, but its root meaning is “rejoice”. Zephaniah 3:14 calls on the “daughter Zion” to rejoice because the Lord is in her midst; many commentators hear that call behind the angel's word.
  • kecharitōmenē, favored one, full of grace: A perfect passive participle: one who has been filled with grace and remains so. The angel uses it in place of her name, as if it were a new name. The Malayalam POC renders it ദൈവകൃപ നിറഞ്ഞവളേ.
  • episkiasei, will overshadow: The word used in the Greek Old Testament for the cloud that covered the tent of meeting when God's glory filled it (Exodus 40:35), and in Luke for the cloud at the Transfiguration (9:34). Mary becomes the new dwelling place of God.
  • doulē, servant: The feminine of doulos, servant or slave. Mary takes the same title as Moses, David and the prophets, “servant of the Lord”, and gives it its fullest meaning.

Verse by verse.

“And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”” (1:28)

“The Lord is with you” was said to Gideon (Judges 6:12) and to others called to a great task. Here the presence of God will be more than help from outside: he will take flesh in her.

“Mary said to the angel, “How can this be, since I am a virgin?”” (1:34)

Literally, “since I do not know a man”, as the POC keeps it. Mary does not doubt, as Zechariah did (1:18); she asks how. Her question shows that she does not expect to conceive through Joseph, and the Church has always read it in the light of her virginity.

“The angel said to her, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” (1:35)
“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Then the angel departed from her.” (1:38)

“Here am I” is the answer of Abraham, Moses, Samuel and Isaiah when God called them. The verb “let it be” (genoito) expresses a wish, not mere acceptance. Mary does not only allow God's plan; she desires it.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in “full of grace” the sign that Mary was prepared by God from the first moment of her life, preserved from all stain of original sin (CCC 490–492). By her “yes”, given in faith and obedience, she became the Mother of God and freely cooperated in the work of salvation (CCC 494; Lumen Gentium 56). St Bernard, preaching on this Gospel, pictures the whole world, Adam and all his children, waiting at Mary's feet for her answer (in substance).

The Council of Ephesus (431) confessed that Mary is Theotokos, Mother of God, because the one she conceived by the Holy Spirit is truly the eternal Son of the Father (CCC 495).

Questions people ask

Why was Zechariah punished for his question, but not Mary?

Zechariah asked for proof: “How will I know that this is so?” (1:18). Mary believed and asked how it would happen. Elizabeth later praises exactly this: “blessed is she who believed” (1:45).

Does Genesis 3:15 speak of Mary?

Its first meaning is the long struggle between humanity and evil, and the promise of a descendant who will win. The Church, reading the whole Bible, sees that descendant in Christ and the woman, in its fullest sense, in Mary, the new Eve (CCC 411).

Why do we call Mary “Mother of God” and not only “Mother of Jesus”?

Because Jesus is one person, the eternal Son of God made man. A mother is mother of a person, not of a nature. So Mary is truly Mother of God (CCC 495).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac year, the season before Yaldo is a chain of announcements: to Zechariah, to Mary, the Visitation, the birth of John, the revelation to Joseph. The Malankara Church walks to Christmas by listening to how God spoke to each person who received him. Today the Church honours Mary as Mother of God, as the great Syriac Fathers did, and St Ephrem sang of her as the one in whom the Lord of all made his dwelling (in substance). In the Holy Qurbono, when the Holy Spirit is invoked upon the gifts, the Church asks for the same overshadowing that came upon Mary at Nazareth.

For study and teaching

  • God's first question is “Where are you?” Where am I hiding from him at this moment?
  • Galatians 4:6 says the Spirit cries “Abba” in our hearts. How can I let my prayer this week be the prayer of a child, not of a slave?
  • Mary asks “How can this be?” and still says yes. What is the difference between doubt and a believing question?
  • Mary's “let it be” is a desire, not only consent. What would it mean for me to desire God's plan, and not just put up with it?
