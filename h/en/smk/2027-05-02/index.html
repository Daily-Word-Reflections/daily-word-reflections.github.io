Word for the day

“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” John 16:22

Reflection

At the Last Supper Jesus prepares his disciples for his going. “A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.” They will weep while the world rejoices, “but your pain will turn into joy.” He compares it to a woman in labour: her pain is forgotten in the joy of a child born into the world. The Resurrection is that birth; Christian joy is born through the Cross.

“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” This is the joy of Qyomtho: not a mood that changes with circumstances, but a gift no one can take away, because it rests on the risen Christ. Acts shows the first disciples, even when expelled from a city, “filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit”.

Jesus promises the Spirit: “When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” As the Ascension and Pentecost approach, the Church is taught to live not by sight but by the Spirit, who leads her into the fullness of truth. And Jesus teaches us to pray with confidence: “Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.”

St Paul gives the practical consequence: “If you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above… Set your minds on things that are above.” Then he lists what must be put to death and what put on. Proverbs adds: “Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offences.” Easter joy shows itself in a changed life.

Homily outline

Theme: “Your pain will turn into joy.”

Before his Passion Jesus prepared his disciples for sorrow, and promised a joy no one could take away. In the Easter season the Church hears these words as fulfilled, and learns that the Spirit of truth leads us into this joy.

1. Sorrow turned into joy

  • Jesus compares the disciples' grief to a mother in labour: pain that gives way to new life.
  • The Psalmist knew: weeping may linger for the night, but joy comes with the morning.
  • Easter joy does not deny suffering; it passes through it.
“Weeping may linger for the night, but joy comes with the morning.” Psalm 30:5
“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” John 16:22

Apply: Offer one present sorrow to the Lord, trusting it will become joy.

2. The Spirit of truth guides

  • God's counsel is wonderful and his wisdom excellent.
  • Jesus promises the Spirit of truth who will guide us into all truth.
  • The Spirit speaks through Scripture and the teaching of the Church, never against them.
“This also comes from the LORD of hosts; he is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in wisdom.” Isaiah 28:29
“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” John 16:13

Apply: Before every decision this week, pray: “Come, Holy Spirit, guide me.”

3. Joy that is shared

  • At the feast of Weeks all rejoiced together: children, servants, strangers, orphans and widows.
  • The first disciples, though persecuted, were filled with joy and the Holy Spirit.
  • Christian joy is never selfish; it includes the poor and the lonely.
“Rejoice before the LORD your God—you and your sons and your daughters, your male and female slaves, the Levites resident in your towns, as well as the strangers, the orphans, and the widows who are among you.” Deuteronomy 16:11
“And the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit.” Acts 13:52

Apply: Invite someone lonely to share a family meal or celebration.

The Risen Lord gives a joy that the world cannot give or take away.

Let us ask in his name, let the Spirit guide us, and share our joy with all.

Take-home resolution: Begin preparing for Pentecost: pray each day “Come, Holy Spirit.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, at the Last Supper Jesus told his friends: “A little while, and you will no longer see me; and again a little while, and you will see me.” They did not understand. He explained with a picture every mother knows: a woman in labour has pain, but when the child is born she forgets the pain for joy.

That is Easter. The Cross was the labour pain; the Resurrection is the birth of new life. And Jesus promised: “I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” Christian joy is not the absence of trouble. It is a joy that rests on the risen Christ, and so no one can take it away.

In Acts the disciples are thrown out of a city, and yet they are “filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit.” Jesus also promises the Spirit of truth to guide us, and invites us to ask in his name, “so that your joy may be complete”. On Thursday we celebrate his Ascension; then we wait for the Spirit.

St Paul tells us how this joy shows: “Seek the things that are above.” Put off anger, lies and impurity; put on compassion and love. As Proverbs says, “love covers all offences.” Let us live this week as people whose joy no one can take away. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told his friends: “You will be sad for a little while, but then you will be very happy!”

They were sad when Jesus died, but so happy when they saw him alive again! Jesus said nobody can take that joy away.

When you feel sad, remember Jesus is alive and loves you. Let's say: “Jesus, you are my joy!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you promised that our pain would turn into joy and that no one would take it from us. Send us your Spirit of truth, teach us to ask in your name, and set our hearts on the things that are above, where you are seated at the right hand of the Father. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Fifth Sunday after Qyomtho stands four days before the Ascension, and its readings speak of a joy that cannot be taken away. In the evening Gospel (John 16:12–15) Jesus promises: “When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” In 2 Samuel 5:9–12 David grows greater and greater, “for the Lord, the God of hosts, was with him.” Proverbs 10:1–13 teaches that “love covers all offenses”. Isaiah 28:23–29 watches a farmer at work and concludes that the Lord “is wonderful in counsel”. In Acts 13:44–52, even when Paul and Barnabas are driven out of Pisidian Antioch, “the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit.”

We study three readings more closely. Deuteronomy 16 commands Israel to rejoice before the Lord at the festival of Weeks, the feast the Greek-speaking Jews called Pentecost. Colossians 3 tells the baptized that their life is now hidden with Christ, who is seated at the right hand of God. In John 16 Jesus tells his disciples that their pain will turn into joy, like the pain of a mother whose child is born. The key words are rejoice, above and a little while.

Deuteronomy 16:10–12 · Rejoice before the Lord at the festival of weeks

Setting.

Deuteronomy is given as Moses' great farewell speech on the plains of Moab, before Israel enters the land. Chapter 16 sets out the three pilgrim feasts, when every Israelite man was to appear before the Lord: the festival of unleavened bread, the festival of weeks and the festival of booths (16:16).

The festival of weeks came seven weeks after the beginning of the grain harvest (16:9). It was a harvest thanksgiving. Because it fell on the fiftieth day, Greek-speaking Jews called it Pentecost, “fiftieth”; the book of Tobit already speaks of “our festival of Pentecost, which is the sacred festival of weeks” (Tobit 2:1). Later Jewish tradition also linked this feast with the giving of the Law at Sinai.

Structure.

  • v. 10: keep the festival with an offering in proportion to the blessing received.
  • v. 11: rejoice before the Lord, the whole household together with the Levite, the stranger, the orphan and the widow.
  • v. 12: remember that you were a slave in Egypt.

Key words.

  • ḥag shāvu‘ôt, festival of weeks: Named after the seven weeks counted from the first sickle in the grain. The counting itself taught Israel that the whole harvest was a gift, measured out day by day from God's hand.
  • śāmaḥ, rejoice: Deuteronomy commands joy again and again at the place God chooses (12:7, 12; 16:11, 14). Joy before God is not only a feeling; it is a duty of worship, and it is meant to be shared.
  • zākar, remember: Memory is the root of Israel's mercy. Because they were once slaves, they must make room at the feast for those who have no land and no protector.

Verse by verse.

“Then you shall keep the festival of weeks to the Lord your God,” (16:10)

The feast belongs “to the Lord”. The harvest is his before it is ours.

“you and your sons and your daughters, your male and female slaves, the Levites resident in your towns, as well as the strangers, the orphans, and the widows who are among you” (16:11)

The list is wide on purpose. No one is to be left outside the joy: not the servant, not the foreigner, not the child without a father, not the woman without a husband. A feast where the poor are missing is not yet the feast God wants.

“Remember that you were a slave in Egypt, and diligently observe these statutes.” (16:12)

Gratitude for one's own rescue becomes kindness to others. The same reason is given for rest on the sabbath (5:15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this feast the background of the Christian Pentecost. The Holy Spirit came upon the disciples “when the day of Pentecost had come” (Acts 2:1), when Jews from every nation were gathered in Jerusalem for the festival. The Catechism teaches that on the day of Pentecost Christ's Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit (CCC 731). The harvest of the fields becomes the first harvest of the Church, when about three thousand were baptized (Acts 2:41). The command to share joy with the stranger, the orphan and the widow still stands: the early Church in Jerusalem shared its goods so that no one was in need (Acts 4:34).

Colossians 3:1–11 · Seek the things that are above

Setting.

Colossae was a small town in the Lycus valley of Asia Minor, in modern Turkey. The church there was founded not by Paul but by his co-worker Epaphras (1:7). The letter speaks in Paul's name from prison. Many scholars think a disciple of Paul wrote it after his time, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

Chapter 2 warns against a “philosophy” of food rules, special days and the worship of angels (2:8, 16–18). Against all this the letter puts Baptism: “you were buried with him in baptism, you were also raised with him” (2:12). Today's verses draw the consequences for daily life.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: you have been raised with Christ; seek what is above, for your life is hidden with him.
  • vv. 5–7: put to death what is earthly: impurity and greed, which is idolatry.
  • vv. 8–9a: put away anger, malice, slander and lies.
  • vv. 9b–11: you have put off the old self and put on the new, in which Christ is all and in all.

Key words.

  • ta anō zēteite, seek the things that are above: “Above” is not a place in the sky. It is where Christ is, “seated at the right hand of God”, a phrase taken from Psalm 110:1. To seek what is above is to let the risen Christ set the direction of the whole life.
  • kekryptai, is hidden: The tense means: has been hidden and stays hidden. The new life of the baptized is real now, but it cannot yet be seen. It will be revealed “when Christ who is your life is revealed”.
  • eikōn, image: The new self is renewed “according to the image of its creator”, recalling Genesis 1:27. In 1:15 Christ himself is called “the image of the invisible God”. Baptism restores in us the likeness that sin had darkened.

Verse by verse.

“So if you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God.” (3:1)

The “if” does not express doubt. It means “since”. Because Baptism has joined us to the risen Lord, our desire must follow him.

“for you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.” (3:3)

Hidden like seed in the earth, or like treasure kept safe. No one can steal what is kept “with Christ in God”.

“Put to death, therefore, whatever in you is earthly: fornication, impurity, passion, evil desire, and greed (which is idolatry).” (3:5)

Greed is called idolatry because it puts money where God should be. Jesus said the same: “You cannot serve God and wealth” (Matthew 6:24).

“In that renewal there is no longer Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and free; but Christ is all and in all!” (3:11)

The Scythians, from north of the Black Sea, were thought by Greeks to be the roughest of all peoples. In Christ the walls that divide human beings lose their power. Galatians 3:28 says the same.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses these verses to teach that through Baptism Christians already share in a real way in the heavenly life of the risen Christ, though this life remains “hidden with Christ in God” (CCC 1003). The putting off of the old self and the putting on of the new was acted out in the early Church's baptisms, where the candidate took off old clothes, went down into the water and came up to be clothed in white. The Church still speaks of the white garment as a sign that the baptized have “put on Christ” (Galatians 3:27; CCC 1243). The list of sins in vv. 5–9 is a reminder that the new life is not only a gift to receive but a way of life to grow into, with the help of grace.

John 16:16–24 · Your pain will turn into joy

Setting.

John 13–17 is the farewell of Jesus at the Last Supper. He has told his disciples that he is going to the Father, and they are filled with sorrow (16:6). Just before today's reading he promised the Spirit of truth, who will guide them into all the truth (16:13), the evening Gospel of this Sunday.

In today's verses Jesus speaks of a short time of absence followed by a seeing again. The disciples do not understand. The reader knows the pattern: his death on Friday, his rising on the first day of the week, and then his going to the Father and his return at the end.

Structure.

  • v. 16: a little while, and you will not see me; again a little while, and you will see me.
  • vv. 17–19: the disciples puzzle over “a little while”, and Jesus knows their question.
  • vv. 20–22: sorrow turned into joy, like a mother after childbirth.
  • vv. 23–24: on that day: ask the Father in my name, so that your joy may be complete.

Key words.

  • mikron, a little while: The phrase comes seven times in four verses (vv. 16–19). The disciples repeat it because it puzzles them. Christian readers have heard in it both the three days of the Passion and the whole time of the Church, which is short when set beside eternity.
  • lypē, pain, sorrow: The same word is used for the sorrow of the disciples and the pain of childbirth. Jesus does not deny their grief. He promises that it will be changed, not simply replaced.
  • chara, joy: The great word of the farewell discourse. Jesus wants “that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete” (15:11). This joy is rooted in seeing him again, and it is guarded by him, so “no one will take your joy from you”.

Verse by verse.

“A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.” (16:16)

Two different Greek verbs stand behind “see” here. The second is the one Mary Magdalene uses on Easter morning: “I have seen the Lord” (20:18). The disciples will see the risen Lord, and through their witness the Church learns to see him with the eyes of faith.

“you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” (16:20)

Not pain followed by a different joy, but the pain itself becoming joy. The Cross that caused their grief becomes the source of their gladness.

“When a woman is in labor, she has pain, because her hour has come.” (16:21)

The prophets used this image for Israel waiting for God's salvation (Isaiah 26:17; 66:7–9). “Her hour” recalls the hour of Jesus, which is the hour of his Passion and his glory (12:23; 13:1).

“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” (16:22)

Jesus does not only say “you will see me”, but “I will see you”. The initiative is his. A joy that depends on him cannot be taken away by others.

“Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.” (16:24)

Prayer in the name of Jesus is new because the disciples now pray as those united to him. Its fruit is not only an answer to a request, but a joy that is full.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, preaching on this passage, took the second “little while” to mean the whole present age, which seems long to us but is short beside eternity (in substance). The Church lives in that “little while”, between the sorrow of the Cross and the full joy of seeing him face to face. The Catechism teaches that, after his Passover, Jesus taught his disciples to pray to the Father in his name, and that this is the newness of Christian prayer (CCC 2614–2615). The image of the woman in labour also helps Christians to understand suffering: not as meaningless, but as something God can turn toward new life (Romans 8:22–23).

Questions people ask

Can a Christian be commanded to rejoice when life is hard?

Deuteronomy commands joy, and Paul writes “Rejoice in the Lord always” (Philippians 4:4). This joy is not pretending that nothing hurts. Jesus himself says, “you will weep and mourn”. Christian joy rests on what God has done and promised, and so it can live alongside tears, as the disciples in Acts 13 were filled with joy even after they were driven out.

Does “if you ask anything of the Father in my name, he will give it to you” mean every prayer will be granted as I ask?

To pray in the name of Jesus is to pray as he prayed, in union with his will: “not what I want, but what you want” (Mark 14:36). The Father always gives what is good to those who ask (Matthew 7:11), and above all he gives the Holy Spirit (Luke 11:13). Sometimes the answer is not the thing asked for, but something greater.

Why do Christians read about a Jewish harvest feast?

Because the God who gave Israel the festival of weeks is the same God who poured out his Spirit on that feast in Jerusalem. The Church does not take this feast away from the Jewish people, who still keep it as Shavuot. She gives thanks that God's gifts to Israel are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; CCC 839), and she learns from Israel to rejoice before God and to remember the poor.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the Antiochene liturgy we pray, at the beginning of the Anaphora, that our minds and hearts may be lifted up on high, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God the Father. These words echo today's Epistle almost exactly. Every Holy Qurbono is a lesson in “seeking the things that are above”. This Sunday also leads us toward Suloko, the feast of the Ascension, on Thursday, and then to Pentecost. The Church of the Qyomtho season prays with the disciples for the Spirit, as Israel once counted the weeks toward the harvest feast.

For study and teaching

  • Whom would Deuteronomy tell me to invite to my family's feasts this year?
  • Paul says that greed is idolatry. Where does money take the place of God in my life or in my home?
  • Jesus says that pain can be turned into joy. When have I seen this happen in my own life or in my family?
  • When the priest invites us in the Holy Qurbono to lift up our minds and hearts on high, what do I usually have on my mind?
