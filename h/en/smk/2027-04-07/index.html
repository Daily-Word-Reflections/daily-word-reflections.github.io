Word for the day

“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Ephesians 2:14

Reflection

Christ is risen! On Easter evening the risen Lord stood among his frightened disciples and said, “Peace be with you.” Today St Paul explains how deep that peace goes. He writes to Christians who were not born Jews: “Remember that you were at that time without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world. But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” Notice what Paul honors here: the commonwealth of Israel and the covenants of promise are a treasure. Gentile believers, which is to say almost all of us, have been brought near to Israel's hope, grafted, as Paul says elsewhere, onto the olive tree whose root supports us (Romans 11:17–18). The Church remembers with gratitude her spiritual bond with the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4). Then the great sentence: “For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” When Paul says that Christ “abolished the law with its commandments and ordinances,” he is speaking of the rules that kept Jew and Gentile apart as a wall; he does not mean that God's commandments no longer matter. Christ fulfills the Law, and in him both have “access in one Spirit to the Father.” Here is the whole Trinity in a single line, and the Cross is the place where hostility was put to death.

What walls still stand among us? Families divided over property, neighbors who have not spoken for years, the old wounds of caste that still mark some Christian communities in Kerala, suspicion between Churches that share one Baptism. Christ has already broken the wall in his flesh; our part is to stop rebuilding it. Our own Church knows something of this grace: in 1930 Mar Ivanios and his companions entered full communion with the Bishop of Rome, and the Syro-Malankara Church still prays daily for the unity of all Christ's disciples. The Gospel adds a word about how this peace spreads. “No one after lighting a lamp hides it under a jar, or puts it under a bed, but puts it on a lampstand, so that those who enter may see the light.” The light of Easter is not for hiding. Then Jesus says something sharp: “Then pay attention to how you listen; for to those who have, more will be given; and from those who do not have, even what they seem to have will be taken away.” The one who listens with faith receives more; the one who hears without receiving loses even the little he thought he had. St Augustine told his hearers that the sound of the preacher's words strikes the ear, but the true Teacher is within, and only he makes the word take root (in substance).

How do we live this Wednesday of Qyomtho? Look honestly for one wall in your life: a relative, a neighbor, a colleague, a Christian of another Church you have kept at a distance. Take one step toward them this week: a greeting, a phone call, a visit, a prayer said for them by name. Pray for the unity of Christians, especially among the Churches of the St Thomas tradition in Kerala. And watch how you listen: at the Holy Qurbono, try to hear the readings as if for the first time; at home, switch off the screen for ten minutes and let the Gospel speak; with others, listen to understand rather than to answer. The lamp is lit; let it stand on the lampstand of a peaceful life.

Full homily

Dear friends, Christ is risen! On Easter evening the Lord said to his disciples, Peace be with you. Today St Paul tells us how deep that peace goes: he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us (Ephesians 2:14).

We who were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ, brought into the hope of Israel's covenants. Through him all of us have access in one Spirit to the Father. Christ has already broken the wall. Our part is to stop rebuilding it in our families, our parishes, and between Churches.

In the Gospel Jesus says that no one hides a lamp under a jar. The light of Easter is not for hiding. And he adds: pay attention to how you listen (Luke 8:18). The one who listens with faith receives more.

So this week let us take one step across a wall: a greeting, a call, a visit, a prayer for someone by name. And let us listen to God's word as if for the first time. Amen.

For children

Children, Christ is risen! St Paul says that Jesus is our peace. When people are angry with each other, it is like building a wall between them. Jesus came to break down walls, so that we can all be friends in God's family. Is there someone you had a fight with? Today you can say sorry, or just say hello with a smile.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you are our peace. In your flesh you made both groups into one and broke down the dividing wall of hostility; through you we all have access in one Spirit to the Father. Bring near all who are far off, heal the divisions in our families, our parishes and our Churches, and keep us from rebuilding the walls you have broken. Set the lamp of your Easter light on the lampstand of our lives, and teach us to listen to your word with faith, so that more may be given us. To you be glory with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul asks Gentile believers to remember where they once stood: far off, “without Christ”, “strangers to the covenants of promise”. Now, by the blood of Christ, they have been brought near, for “he is our peace”. The Gospel from Luke gives the lamp that is set on a lampstand and the warning “pay attention to how you listen”. In this week after New Sunday both readings speak of a light that has come out of the tomb: a peace that breaks down walls, and a word that must be heard well so that it can shine.

Ephesians 2:11–18 · He is our peace

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter about the Church as one body in Christ. Chapter 2 begins with what grace has done for every believer: “by grace you have been saved through faith” (2:8). Now Paul turns to his Gentile readers in particular and asks them to look back at how far they once stood from Israel's God and Israel's hope.

The letter names Paul as its author and says that he writes from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think that it was written by a disciple in Paul's name, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself; in either case the Church receives it as inspired Scripture among the letters of Paul. Many commentators also think that the “dividing wall” would remind a reader of that time of the barrier in the Jerusalem Temple beyond which Gentiles could not go. A stone inscription from it, found in 1871, warns them not to pass.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: remember: once you were far off, without Christ, strangers to the covenants, without hope.
  • v. 13: but now in Christ Jesus you have been brought near by his blood.
  • vv. 14–16: he is our peace; he has made the two into one new humanity and reconciled both to God through the cross.
  • vv. 17–18: he proclaimed peace to the far and the near; through him both have access in one Spirit to the Father.
  • ¶ Verse 18 names the Father, the Son and the Spirit in one sentence: through Christ, in the Spirit, to the Father. This is the shape of all Christian prayer.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: In the Bible peace (Hebrew shalom) is more than the end of a quarrel. It is wholeness, things set right between God and people and among people. Here peace is a person: “he is our peace”.
  • mesotoichon, dividing wall: A word found only here in the New Testament. The letter explains it at once: the wall is “the hostility between us”.
  • prosagōgē, access: The word for being brought into the presence of a king. It appears also in Romans 5:2. Through Christ the Gentile, once a stranger, may now come straight to the Father.

Verse by verse.

“remember that you were at that time without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise” (2:12)

Notice what the Gentiles lacked: the Christ whom Israel awaited, Israel's covenants and Israel's hope. The covenants with Israel are the treasure, and to be outside them was the loss. The Church does not take Israel's place: the Gentiles are brought near to promises that remain Israel's, for “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29).

“But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” (2:13)

“Far” and “near” come from Isaiah 57:19, which verse 17 will quote. Nearness is not earned; it is given by the blood of Christ.

“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” (2:14)

Christ does not only bring peace; he is peace. In his own flesh, offered on the Cross, the enmity is ended.

“He has abolished the law with its commandments and ordinances,” (2:15)

This must be read with care. The letter does not throw away God's moral law, which it quotes in 6:2. What is set aside is the law as a fence that kept the peoples apart, with its rules of separation. In Christ, Jew and Gentile meet in one new humanity.

“for through him both of us have access in one Spirit to the Father.” (2:18)

“Both of us”: the Jew who was near and the Gentile who was far now come to the Father by the same door.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council recalls this passage when it speaks of the Jewish people: the Church believes that by his cross Christ our Peace reconciled Jews and Gentiles, making both one in himself (Nostra Aetate 4). The Catechism teaches that earthly peace is the image and fruit of the peace of Christ, who by the blood of his Cross put the hostility to death (CCC 2305). So the first fruit of Easter is a reconciled people. Every Holy Qurbono, where people of every family, language and background receive the one Body, shows the wall already broken.

Luke 8:16–18 · Pay attention to how you listen

Setting.

Jesus has told the parable of the sower and explained it: the seed is the word of God, and the good soil is those who “hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance” (8:15). These three sayings follow at once. Just after them, Jesus says that his true family are “those who hear the word of God and do it” (8:21). So the whole passage is about hearing.

Luke gives the lamp saying a second time in 11:33, and Mark places the same group of sayings after the sower (Mark 4:21–25).

Structure.

  • v. 16: no one hides a lit lamp; it is put on a lampstand so that those who come in may see.
  • v. 17: what is hidden will be made known.
  • v. 18: so take care how you listen: more will be given to the one who has.

Key words.

  • lychnos, lamp: A small clay oil lamp, lit in the evening and set high so that the whole room had light. In Luke the lamp is the word received and lived, which cannot be kept private.
  • pōs akouete, how you listen: Mark has “what you hear” (Mark 4:24); Luke writes “how”. The question is not only which words reach us, but with what heart we take them in.
  • dokei echein, seem to have: Luke adds the word “seem”, which Mark does not have. A faith that is only heard and never kept was never truly ours.

Verse by verse.

“No one after lighting a lamp hides it under a jar, or puts it under a bed, but puts it on a lampstand, so that those who enter may see the light.” (8:16)

Luke speaks of “those who enter”, people coming into the house. The light is for guests. In Acts the same Luke will show the light of Easter going out to people of every nation.

“For nothing is hidden that will not be disclosed, nor is anything secret that will not become known and come to light.” (8:17)

On the lips of Jesus this is first a promise: the mystery of the Kingdom, still veiled in parables, will be made known. After the Resurrection the apostles proclaim openly what they once heard in private.

“Then pay attention to how you listen;” (8:18)

“Then” ties the warning to what came before. Because the word is meant to shine, the hearer carries a responsibility.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council opens its teaching on revelation with the words “Hearing the word of God with reverence” (Dei Verbum 1), and it asks all the faithful to come often to the Scriptures in reading and prayer (Dei Verbum 25). The Catechism adds that prayer should accompany the reading of Sacred Scripture, so that a dialogue takes place between God and man (CCC 2653). Luke himself shows the Mother of the Lord as the first of those who hear well: she treasured all these things in her heart (Luke 2:19, 51).

Questions people ask

Does “to those who have, more will be given” mean that God favours the rich?

No. Jesus is not speaking of money but of the word. The one who “has” is the one who receives the word and keeps it, and such a person grows in understanding. The one who does not open the heart loses even the little that seemed to be there. It is a law of the spiritual life, and a call to listen now.

For study and teaching

  • Who first set the light of faith where I could see it? How can I do the same for someone who comes into my home?
  • In my family or parish, where does a “dividing wall” still stand? What would it mean to let Christ, our peace, break it down this week?
