Word for the day

“for the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name.” Luke 1:49

Reflection

“When the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman.” To prepare a worthy mother for his Son, God filled Mary with grace from the very first moment of her existence. The Church professes that she was preserved from every stain of original sin, not by her own merit, but “in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the human race” (Ineffabilis Deus, 1854). She too was redeemed by her Son, in the most perfect way: preserved rather than cleansed.

In the Magnificat Mary herself gives the reason for today's feast: “the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name.” She does not boast of her purity; she praises God's mercy. In Genesis God had promised enmity between the serpent and the woman; in Mary that promise begins to be fulfilled. Through her Son we too become children and heirs, able to cry “Abba! Father!”, and in the middle of the Christmas fast we look to her as the pure dwelling God prepared for his coming.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today we celebrate the Immaculate Conception of the Holy Mother of God. The Church believes that from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin (Pope Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854). God prepared a pure dwelling for his Son.

St Paul writes: when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children (Galatians 4:4–5). The woman of whom the Son was born is Mary. Her purity serves the Incarnation.

In the Gospel Mary sings her Magnificat: my soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Saviour. Notice: God my Saviour. Mary too was saved by Christ, in a more perfect way: preserved from sin rather than freed from it after falling.

Mary also sings: he has looked with favour on the lowliness of his servant. From now on all generations will call me blessed (Luke 1:48). Our Syriac tradition loves to call her blessed, the Mother of God, the second heaven who carried the Lord of heaven.

Mary's purity is not distance from us. It is the beauty God wants to restore in each of us. Through baptism, confession and the Eucharist, God cleanses us and makes us his children.

During this fast, let us honour Mary by imitating her: humility, purity of heart, faith and praise. Let us make a good confession before Yaldo, so that our hearts too may be a pure dwelling for her Son. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, the early Christians did not have the dogma of the Immaculate Conception in its later words, but they had a deep intuition about Mary, drawn from the Scriptures. Let us look at two images that the Fathers loved: Mary as the new Eve, and Mary as the Ark of the Covenant.

In the garden, Eve listened to the serpent and believed his lie. Through her disobedience, sin and death entered the world. But God promised that the offspring of a woman would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). In Nazareth another woman listened, not to a serpent but to an angel, and said: here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word (Luke 1:38). St Irenaeus wrote that the knot of Eve's disobedience was untied by Mary's obedience. Where Eve said no, Mary said yes.

The second image is the Ark of the Covenant. God commanded Moses to make it of acacia wood, covered with pure gold inside and out (Exodus 25:10–11). Inside were the tablets of the law, a jar of manna and Aaron's rod (Hebrews 9:4). When the Ark entered a house, it brought blessing; David danced before it. Mary carried in her womb the Word of God, the true Bread from heaven and the eternal High Priest. When she entered Elizabeth's house, the child leaped for joy (Luke 1:41). If the Ark had to be pure gold, how much more the living Ark of the Son of God.

This is what the Church proclaims today: that from the first moment of her conception Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ. She too was saved by her Son, but in a more exalted way: preserved rather than cleansed.

And what God did perfectly for Mary, He wants to begin in us. In Baptism we were washed; in confession we are cleansed again; in the Holy Qurbono we receive Christ as Mary did. In these days before Christmas, let us prepare a clean dwelling for Him. Make a good confession before Christmas, and ask Mary Immaculate to teach you to say yes.

Homily option 3 · Teaching homily: the dogma of the Immaculate Conception

Many people confuse this feast with the virginal conception of Jesus. Let us be clear about what the Church teaches and why.

1. What the dogma says

  • Defined by Pope Pius IX in 1854 (Ineffabilis Deus).
  • Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception (CCC 491).
  • By a singular grace, in view of the merits of Christ.

📖 Luke 1:28

2. Full of grace

The dogma honours Christ, whose grace made Mary all-holy.

Mary Immaculate is the model and mother of the Church.

Dear brothers and sisters, many Catholics think that today's feast is about the conception of Jesus in Mary's womb. It is not. That we celebrate on 25 March, the Annunciation. Today we celebrate the conception of Mary herself in the womb of her mother, whom tradition names Anne.

On 8 December 1854, Pope Pius IX solemnly defined that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God, and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin (CCC 491). Every word matters. Singular grace: it was God's gift, not Mary's achievement. In view of the merits of Jesus Christ: Mary too was saved by her Son.

Where is this in the Bible? The angel greets Mary as full of grace (Luke 1:28). The Greek word means one who has been, and remains, filled with God's favour. The Church understood that the woman chosen to be the Mother of God was prepared by grace from the beginning. The Catechism says that Mary was redeemed from the moment of her conception, and that by God's grace she remained free of every personal sin her whole life long (CCC 492–493). The Eastern Fathers call her Panagia, the All-Holy.

Four years after the definition, in 1858, Our Lady appeared to a poor girl, Bernadette, at Lourdes. When Bernadette asked her name, she answered: I am the Immaculate Conception. Bernadette did not understand the words, but the parish priest did. Heaven confirmed what the Church had taught.

Why does this matter? Because it shows what God's grace can do. Mary is not a goddess; she is a creature, entirely filled with grace. She shows what the Church is called to become: holy and without blemish (Ephesians 5:27). So let us honour her this Advent with the Rosary, and ask her to help us prepare for her Son at this Holy Qurbono and at Christmas.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the middle of the Suboro season, as the Church walks toward Yaldo, she stops to give thanks for the beginning of the woman through whom the Word took flesh. Neither of today's readings tells of the beginning of Mary's life. Scripture does not narrate it. They show instead why it matters. Paul gives the one line in his letters that touches the Mother of the Lord: “God sent his Son, born of a woman”. Luke gives us Mary's own voice, praising the God who “has done great things” for her.

Read together, the two passages explain the feast. Galatians says what God was preparing: a Son born under the law, so that slaves might become children. The Magnificat says how Mary understands her place in it: she calls God “my Savior”, and she gives all the credit to his mercy. The key words are fullness, adoption and mercy.

Galatians 4:1–7 · Born of a woman, so that we might be children

Setting.

Paul wrote to the churches of Galatia, in what is now central Turkey, because teachers had come urging his Gentile converts to accept circumcision and the observance of the Mosaic law as necessary for salvation. Paul answers that faith in Christ has already made them children of God. Just before this passage he has said: “in Christ Jesus you are all children of God through faith” (3:26).

To explain this he uses an everyday legal picture. An heir who is still a child owns everything in principle, yet in practice he lives under others' orders until the date his father has set. That date, Paul says, has now come.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the picture: the young heir under guardians and trustees.
  • v. 3: the application: “while we were minors, we were enslaved”.
  • vv. 4–5: the turning point: God sends his Son, for a double purpose: to redeem and to make us children.
  • vv. 6–7: the proof: the Spirit in our hearts cries “Abba! Father!”
  • ¶ Notice the pattern of the whole passage: God sends his Son (v. 4), and God sends the Spirit of his Son (v. 6). Father, Son and Spirit appear together, and all three are at work in making us children.

Key words.

  • plērōma tou chronou, fullness of time: Time is pictured as a vessel that God fills to the brim. The Son comes not by accident of history but at the date set by the Father, as in v. 2. The Catechism says that the Annunciation to Mary opens this fullness of time (CCC 484).
  • genomenon ek gynaikos, born of a woman: A Hebrew way of saying “a real human being” (compare Job 14:1). Paul does not name Mary and says nothing here about the manner of the birth. His point is that the Son is truly one of us. Even so, the Church has always heard in this phrase the place God gave to the woman from whom his Son took flesh.
  • huiothesia, adoption as children: A word from Greek and Roman law. An adopted child had the full rights of a child and heir. God does not merely release slaves; he brings them into the family.
  • stoicheia tou kosmou, elemental spirits of the world: The phrase is debated. It may mean the basic elements of the world, which people of that age often linked with spiritual powers, or the elementary rules of religion. In both cases it describes life before the coming of the Son as life under a kind of childhood bondage.
  • Abba, Father: The Aramaic word for father, kept in Greek by the early Church because it was Jesus' own word in prayer (Mark 14:36; see also Romans 8:15).

Verse by verse.

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law,” (4:4)

“Sent” shows that the Son already existed before his birth. “Born of a woman” shows that he truly became man. The two phrases hold together the whole mystery of the Incarnation, and the second one is the reason we honour his Mother.

“in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” (4:5)

He entered our condition so that we might enter his. The early Fathers often repeated this exchange: the Son of God became a son of man so that we might become children of God (so St Irenaeus and St Athanasius, in substance; see CCC 460).

“And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!”” (4:6)

The Spirit prays within us with the same word the Son used. Every time a Christian says “Father”, that prayer is the work of the Spirit (CCC 683, 742).

“So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if a child then also an heir, through God.” (4:7)

Paul moves from “we” to a singular “you”, as if speaking to each reader in turn. The last two words matter: this inheritance comes “through God”, as a gift and not as something earned.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Galatians 4:4–5 at the opening of its teaching on Jesus Christ (CCC 422), and it teaches that God, preparing a body for his Son, wanted a creature's willing cooperation, and so chose a daughter of Israel (CCC 488). The doctrine proclaimed today says that this preparation reached back to the first moment of her existence: “by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God and by virtue of the merits of Jesus Christ, Savior of the human race”, Mary was preserved from all stain of original sin (Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854; CCC 491). St Irenaeus, comparing Eve and Mary, says that the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by Mary's obedience (CCC 494).

Luke 1:46–56 · The Mighty One has done great things for me

Setting.

Mary has travelled to the hill country of Judea to help her kinswoman Elizabeth. Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, has just called her “blessed” because she believed (1:42–45). The Magnificat is Mary's reply. She turns the praise away from herself and gives it to God.

The song is woven almost entirely from the Old Testament: above all the song of Hannah, the barren woman who became the mother of Samuel (1 Samuel 2:1–10), and also the Psalms. Mary speaks in the language of Israel's prayer. She stands among the poor of the Lord, whose hope she carries, and the Catechism calls her the purest figure among them (CCC 64).

Structure.

  • vv. 46–49: Mary's own thanksgiving: what God has done for her.
  • v. 50: the bridge: his mercy for every generation.
  • vv. 51–53: what God does in history: the proud scattered, the lowly lifted up, the hungry filled.
  • vv. 54–55: God keeps his promise to Israel and to Abraham.
  • v. 56: the narrative close: three months with Elizabeth.
  • ¶ The song moves outward, from one young woman to all generations, and then back to Abraham. What God has done in Mary is the pattern of what he does for all his people.

Key words.

  • megalynei, magnifies: From megas, great. To magnify the Lord is to declare him great. It adds nothing to God. It clears the soul to see him as he is. The Latin form of the word, Magnificat, gives the song its name.
  • tapeinōsis, lowliness: It means a low condition, not only a humble feeling. It is the very word of Hannah's prayer in the Greek Old Testament, when she asks God to look on the “misery” of his servant (1 Samuel 1:11). Mary is not praising her own humility. She is saying that God looked down on someone small.
  • eleos, mercy: The word appears twice in the song (vv. 50, 54). It stands for the Hebrew ḥesed, God's faithful covenant love. The whole Magnificat rests on it, and so does the Immaculate Conception, which is a work of mercy and not a reward.

Verse by verse.

“And Mary said, “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior,” (1:46–47)

Mary herself calls God “my Savior”. This line answers an old objection to today's feast. The Church does not teach that Mary had no need of a Saviour. She teaches that Mary was redeemed in a more exalted way, “by reason of the merits of her Son” (CCC 492; Lumen Gentium 53). A person can be rescued after falling into the pit, or be held back before reaching it; both owe their lives to the rescuer.

“for he has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant. Surely, from now on all generations will call me blessed;” (1:48)

This is a bold prophecy from a village girl, and the Church has kept it for two thousand years. Our honour to Mary is the fulfilment of her own words (CCC 971).

“for the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name.” (1:49)

Here is the feast in one line. Everything in Mary is what the Mighty One has done. Her holiness is the work of a holy God.

“He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty.” (1:52–53)

The Greek verbs are in the past tense. Mary speaks as if the reversal had already happened, because in the child she carries God has already begun it. St John Paul II wrote that the Church's love for the poor is inscribed in the Magnificat (Redemptoris Mater 37).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls the Magnificat the song both of the Mother of God and of the Church (CCC 2619), and it holds up Mary's words as the model of adoration: to confess with gratitude that God has done great things, and that holy is his name (CCC 2097). St Ambrose, preaching on this passage, prays that the soul of Mary may be in each of us to magnify the Lord, and her spirit in each of us to rejoice in God (in substance). The Eastern Fathers call the Mother of God “the All-Holy”, as the Catechism recalls, “free from any stain of sin, as though fashioned by the Holy Spirit and formed as a new creature” (CCC 493; Lumen Gentium 56).

Questions people ask

Is the Immaculate Conception the same as the Virgin Birth?

No, though many people confuse them. Today's feast is about the beginning of Mary's own life: from the first moment she was preserved from original sin. Her parents, whom tradition names Joachim and Anne, conceived her in the ordinary way. The virginal conception concerns Jesus: he was conceived in Mary's womb by the power of the Holy Spirit, without a human father (CCC 490–491, 496–497).

Where is this in the Bible?

Scripture does not state it in so many words. The Church reads it in the light of God's promise of enmity between the serpent and the woman (Genesis 3:15; CCC 410–411), and of the angel's greeting: “Greetings, favored one” (Luke 1:28), where the Greek kecharitōmenē means one who has been and remains filled with grace (CCC 490–491). The dogma of 1854 expresses what the Church had come to understand slowly over the centuries, through prayer and reflection on Scripture.

Did Mary then not need Christ?

She needed him more than anyone, and she says so: “God my Savior”. She was redeemed by Christ, and in the most perfect way, by being preserved from sin instead of being cleansed from it (CCC 492). All her grace flows from his Cross.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

The West Syriac Fathers sang of the Mother of God, the Yoldath Aloho, long before the Latin definition. St Ephrem says to the Lord in one of his hymns that he and his Mother alone are entirely beautiful: there is no blemish in him, and no stain in his Mother (Nisibene Hymns 27, in substance). The Syro-Malankara Catholic reader therefore receives today's dogma, proclaimed in Rome, as a clear statement of a praise already sung in his own tradition. In the Suboro season the feast has a particular place: Suboro is the time of good news, and here the first good news is that God began to prepare a home for his Son long before the angel came to Nazareth. At the Holy Qurbono we receive the Body born of this pure Virgin, and with her our souls magnify the Lord.
