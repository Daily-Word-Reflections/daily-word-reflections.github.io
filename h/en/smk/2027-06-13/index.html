Word for the day

“Nevertheless, do not rejoice at this, that the spirits submit to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” Luke 10:20

Reflection

In the evening Gospel the Lord appoints seventy others and sends them ahead of him in pairs to every town he means to visit. Before he gives them any instruction about what to do, he tells them what to pray for: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” Mission begins on our knees. The harvest belongs to the Lord, and so do the laborers.

Then comes a strange commission: “See, I am sending you out like lambs into the midst of wolves.” No purse, no bag, no sandals. They are to enter a house and say first, “Peace to this house!” They heal the sick and announce that the kingdom of God has come near. The messenger carries so little so that the message may be seen clearly: the power is God's, not ours. And the Lord binds himself to these poor messengers: “Whoever listens to you listens to me.”

In the Gospel of the Qurbono the seventy come back full of joy: “Lord, in your name even the demons submit to us!” Jesus answers, “I watched Satan fall from heaven like a flash of lightning.” Yet he turns their joy to a deeper source: do not rejoice that spirits submit to you, “but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” Success in ministry passes; belonging to God remains. A catechist, a choir member, a priest, a parent who prays with the children: the deepest joy of each is not what they achieve but that God knows them by name.

The other readings fill out the picture. At Jericho the walls fall not by siege engines but by obedience, procession and the sound of the priests' trumpets: “the LORD has given you the city.” In Acts the apostles hand the daily service of the tables to seven men “full of the Spirit and of wisdom” so that they may give themselves to prayer and to serving the word, and “the word of God continued to spread.” St Paul reminds the Corinthians that the cup we bless and the bread we break are a sharing in the Blood and Body of Christ; because there is one bread, we who are many are one body. The Church is sent out from the altar.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, before Jesus sent out the seventy, he did not give them money or maps. He gave them a prayer: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” Every vocation in the Church begins with someone praying. Somebody prayed for the priest who baptised you. Somebody is praying today for the priest who will one day anoint you.

Then Jesus said: “See, I am sending you out like lambs into the midst of wolves.” No purse, no bag, no sandals. They were to enter a house and first say, “Peace to this house!” Why so poor? So that people would see that the peace and the healing come from God, not from the messenger. Jericho fell not by weapons but by prayer and obedience. The Church grows the same way.

In Acts the apostles chose seven men full of the Spirit and of wisdom to serve the tables, so that the apostles could give themselves to prayer and to the word. Every service in the parish matters: the one who teaches catechism, the one who cleans the church, the one who visits the sick. All of it is the work of the harvest. And all of it flows from the altar, where the one bread makes us one body.

The seventy came back rejoicing: even the demons obey us! Jesus said: rejoice rather that your names are written in heaven. Successes come and go; our name in the heart of God remains. This week, pray every day for vocations, bless the houses you enter with the peace of Christ, and prepare for the Apostles' Fast that begins on Wednesday. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus sent seventy friends to visit many towns. He told them to take no bag and no money, only his peace.

When they entered a house they said: “Peace to this house!” You can say that too, quietly in your heart, when you go into a house.

The friends came back very happy. Jesus said: be happy most of all because God knows your name in heaven. He knows your name too!

Prayer

Lord of the harvest, you sent the seventy ahead of you in poverty and peace. Send laborers into your harvest today: priests, religious, catechists and families who announce your kingdom. Make us one body through the one bread we share, give us your peace to carry into every house, and let our deepest joy be that our names are written in heaven. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Fourth Sunday after Pentecost the readings speak of people whom God sends, blesses and keeps as his own. In the evening Gospel (Luke 10:1–16) the Lord sends seventy others ahead of him in pairs; before he tells them anything else, he tells them to pray: “ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” Joshua 6:14–18 shows Israel circling Jericho until Joshua cries, “Shout! For the Lord has given you the city.” Psalm 15 asks, “O Lord, who may abide in your tent?” and answers with a portrait of the honest and upright person. In Isaiah 65:8–12 God compares his people to a cluster of grapes that is not destroyed, “for there is a blessing in it”. In Acts 6:1–7 the apostles ask the community to choose “seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom” to serve at the tables.

We study three readings more closely. Deuteronomy 33 gives the last blessings of Moses and ends with a hymn: “Happy are you, O Israel! Who is like you, a people saved by the Lord”. Paul tells the Corinthians that the cup we bless and the bread we break are a true sharing in the blood and body of Christ, and so they cannot also sit at the table of idols. In the Gospel the seventy return full of joy because the demons submit to them, and Jesus turns their joy to its deepest ground: “rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” The key words today are blessing, communion and belonging.

Deuteronomy 33:23–29 · Happy are you, O Israel

Setting.

Deuteronomy ends with the farewell of Moses. Before he climbs Mount Nebo to die (chapter 34), he blesses the tribes of Israel one by one, as Jacob had blessed his sons long before (Genesis 49). The blessing is an ancient poem. Many scholars think it is older than the book around it, and that it was placed here as Moses' last words. A short hymn to God opens it (33:2–5) and another closes it (33:26–29); the blessings of the tribes stand in between.

Our verses give the last two tribal blessings, for Naphtali and Asher, and then the closing hymn. Naphtali settled in the hills west of the Sea of Galilee; Asher along the coast to the north, in rich olive country. Centuries later Isaiah promised light to “the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali” (Isaiah 9:1), and Matthew sees that promise fulfilled when Jesus begins to preach in Galilee (Matthew 4:15).

Structure.

  • v. 23: Naphtali, full of the Lord's favour and blessing.
  • vv. 24–25: Asher, blessed among his brothers, with oil in abundance and strength for all his days.
  • vv. 26–27: there is none like God, who rides the heavens to help his people and drives out their enemies.
  • vv. 28–29: Israel dwells in safety; “Happy are you, O Israel!”
  • ¶ The tribal blessings end, and the whole people is gathered into one final word of praise for the God who saves them.

Key words.

  • bᵉrākâ, blessing: In the Bible a blessing is not only a kind wish. It is God's gift of life, fruitfulness and peace, spoken over a person and believed to be effective. Moses blesses because he speaks for the Lord.
  • yᵉšurûn, Jeshurun: A rare poetic name for Israel, found only here, in 32:15 and 33:5, and in Isaiah 44:2. It probably comes from the root meaning “upright”: Israel as God calls it to be.
  • ’ašrêkā, happy are you: The same word opens the Psalter: “Happy are those who do not follow the advice of the wicked” (Psalm 1:1). It describes the person or people who are truly well off because they belong to God.

Verse by verse.

“O Naphtali, sated with favor, full of the blessing of the Lord,” (33:23)

The NRSV's “the west” renders the Hebrew word for “sea”, which here probably means the lake of Galilee. The POC keeps “sea”. Naphtali's land was the land where Jesus would later spend most of his public life.

“There is none like God, O Jeshurun, who rides through the heavens to your help,” (33:26)

The picture of God riding the clouds is old Near Eastern language for the Lord of the storm and the sky. Israel uses it to say that the true God, and no other, is the one who comes to help.

“he drove out the enemy before you, and said, “Destroy!”” (33:27)

The first half of this verse is hard in Hebrew. The NRSV adopts a corrected reading, “He subdues the ancient gods”. The traditional Hebrew text, which the POC follows, reads in substance: “The eternal God is your refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms.” Both say that Israel's safety rests on God alone.

“Happy are you, O Israel! Who is like you, a people saved by the Lord,” (33:29)

Israel's happiness is not its own strength but its Saviour. The verse ends with the enemy under Israel's feet. The Hebrew word there, bāmôt, can mean “backs” (NRSV) or “high places” (POC). The image of treading down the enemy returns in today's Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Moses' blessing as a witness to the faithfulness of God, who keeps the people he has chosen. St Paul says of Israel that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29), and the Catechism teaches that the Jewish people, “to whom the Lord our God spoke first”, keep a unique bond with the Church (CCC 839; Nostra Aetate 4). Christians also hear in this blessing a promise that reaches beyond Israel: in Christ Jesus “the blessing of Abraham” comes to the nations (Galatians 3:14). So the Church does not take Israel's place. She is brought into a blessing that was first spoken over Israel, and she prays with Israel's own words: “Happy are you … a people saved by the Lord.”

1 Corinthians 10:14–22 · One bread, one body

Setting.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus (16:8), around AD 54–55. In chapters 8–10 he answers a real question of the Corinthian Christians: may they eat meat that has been offered to idols? In a city like Corinth, temples had dining rooms where sacrificial meals were held, and such meals were part of ordinary social life: family feasts, guild dinners, celebrations.

Paul first says that idols are nothing, and that the strong must take care not to wound the conscience of the weak (chapter 8). Then he warns from Israel's story in the desert: they all ate spiritual food and drank spiritual drink, and yet many fell into idolatry (10:1–13). Our passage draws the conclusion. Eating at a temple feast is not a neutral act, because every sacred meal joins the eater to the one who is worshipped.

Structure.

  • v. 14: flee from the worship of idols.
  • v. 15: Paul appeals to their own judgment.
  • vv. 16–17: the cup of blessing and the bread that we break: a sharing in the blood and body of Christ; one bread, one body.
  • v. 18: Israel's sacrifices make the eaters partners in the altar.
  • vv. 19–20: idols are nothing, but pagan sacrifice is turned away from God.
  • vv. 21–22: no one can share both the table of the Lord and the table of demons.

Key words.

  • koinōnia, sharing, communion: The word means a real participation in something held in common. It is the word behind “Holy Communion”. Paul does not say the cup is a reminder of Christ's blood; he says it is a sharing in it.
  • potērion tēs eulogias, cup of blessing: In Jewish usage this is the name of the cup of wine over which the blessing of God is said at a festive meal; at the Passover meal it is the third cup. The Lord's Supper was celebrated in the setting of such a meal.
  • trapeza kyriou, table of the Lord: The prophet Malachi already speaks of the altar of the Temple as “the Lord's table” (Malachi 1:7). For Paul the table of the Lord is the altar of the new covenant.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, my dear friends, flee from the worship of idols.” (10:14)

Paul does not say “argue about idols” but “flee”. Idolatry is not only bowing before statues. Anything that takes God's place in the heart can become an idol.

“The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ?” (10:16)

Paul names the cup first, perhaps because the cup was the point of comparison with pagan drink offerings. The question expects the answer “Yes”. The Corinthians already believe it; Paul builds his argument on what they believe.

“Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.” (10:17)

Here Paul moves from the body of Christ received to the body of Christ that we become. Communion with the Lord makes communion with one another.

“You cannot partake of the table of the Lord and the table of demons.” (10:21)

The word “cannot” is strong. It is not only forbidden; it is impossible, because the two tables lead in opposite directions.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains the name “Holy Communion” from these verses: by this sacrament we unite ourselves to Christ, who makes us sharers in his Body and Blood to form a single body (CCC 1331). It also teaches that the Eucharist makes the Church: those who receive it are united more closely to Christ and so to one another (CCC 1396). The Second Vatican Council quotes verse 17 when it describes the Church as the Body of Christ (Lumen Gentium 7). St Augustine, preaching to the newly baptized on this mystery, told them that if they are the body of Christ and his members, it is their own mystery that is placed on the Lord's table: “be what you see, and receive what you are” (Sermon 272, in substance). For Paul's argument to hold, the sharing must be real: in the Holy Qurbono we truly receive the Body and Blood of the Lord.

Luke 10:17–20 · Your names are written in heaven

Setting.

Only Luke tells of this second mission. After sending the Twelve (9:1–6), Jesus sets his face towards Jerusalem (9:51) and appoints seventy others to go ahead of him into every town he means to visit (10:1). The evening Gospel gave his instructions; today's Gospel tells of their return.

The manuscripts are divided between “seventy” and “seventy-two” in 10:1 and 10:17. The NRSV reads “seventy”; the POC reads “seventy-two”. Both numbers recall the list of the nations in Genesis 10, which counts seventy in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in the Greek Septuagint. The number seems to point beyond Israel to the whole world, which the Church will one day be sent to.

Structure.

  • v. 17: the seventy return with joy: even the demons submit.
  • v. 18: Jesus saw Satan fall like lightning.
  • v. 19: authority over all the power of the enemy.
  • v. 20: the deeper joy: names written in heaven.

Key words.

  • hypotassetai, submit, are subject: A word of order and rank: the demons are put under. The disciples' power does not come from themselves but “in your name”.
  • astrapē, lightning: Lightning falls suddenly and all at once, and everyone sees it. The fall of Satan is not slow and doubtful; it is sudden and public.
  • engegraptai, have been written: The Greek perfect tense means written and still standing written. The image of God's book of the living is old (Exodus 32:32; Daniel 12:1), and Paul uses it for his co-workers, “whose names are in the book of life” (Philippians 4:3).

Verse by verse.

“Lord, in your name even the demons submit to us!” (10:17)

The disciples had been told to heal the sick and announce the kingdom (10:9). They found that even the demons gave way. Their joy is real and Jesus does not rebuke it.

“I watched Satan fall from heaven like a flash of lightning.” (10:18)

Jesus does not describe a battle long ago. In the mission of his poor messengers he sees Satan's power broken. Many Fathers linked this verse with Isaiah 14:12, “How you are fallen from heaven, O Day Star”, and with Revelation 12:9.

“See, I have given you authority to tread on snakes and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy; and nothing will hurt you.” (10:19)

The language echoes Psalm 91:13, “You will tread on the lion and the adder”, and Moses' blessing, “you shall tread on their backs” (Deuteronomy 33:29). The enemy now is not a human people but the power of evil.

“but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” (10:20)

Success in ministry can pass, and even great gifts can be lost. What lasts is that God knows us by name and has written us among his own. This is the joy that no failure can take away.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the coming of God's kingdom means the defeat of Satan's, and that the exorcisms of Jesus are signs of his victory over “the ruler of this world” (CCC 550). The Church continues that work in Christ's name, above all in Baptism, where the one to be baptized renounces Satan. The Catechism also teaches that God calls each one by name, and that the name received in Baptism is a name for eternity (CCC 2158–2159). The seventy are a picture of every missionary, catechist and parent who speaks of Christ: they may see wonderful fruit, but their deepest reason for joy is that they belong to God.

Questions people ask

Were there seventy disciples or seventy-two?

The ancient manuscripts of Luke are almost evenly divided, and scholars still do not agree. The NRSV reads “seventy”, the POC “seventy-two”. The meaning does not change: the number recalls the nations of the world in Genesis 10, and perhaps also the seventy elders who shared Moses' spirit (Numbers 11:16–25). The Church has honoured these unnamed disciples as “the Seventy” in her calendars.

Is the Holy Qurbono only a sign of our unity, as some say?

Paul's argument depends on more than a sign. He says the cup is a sharing in the blood of Christ and the bread a sharing in his body (10:16), and in the next chapter he warns that whoever eats or drinks unworthily “will be answerable for the body and blood of the Lord” (11:27). The Church believes that Christ is truly, really and substantially present under the forms of bread and wine (CCC 1374). Because he is truly there, the one bread makes us one body.

Jesus says “nothing will hurt you”. Does that mean believers will never suffer?

No. Luke himself tells how Stephen was stoned and James was killed (Acts 7; 12:2), and Jesus warns the seventy that they go “like lambs into the midst of wolves” (10:3). The promise is that no power of evil can separate those who belong to Christ from God. Paul says the same: neither death nor life nor any power “will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38–39).

For the Syro-Malankara reader

In the West Syriac tradition the Sundays after Pentecost show the life of the Church that the Spirit has gathered and sent. Today that life has three marks: the blessing of God, the one bread of the Qurbono, and the joy of mission. Just before Communion in our Holy Qurbono the celebrant lifts up the Holy Mysteries and proclaims that the holy things are for the holy, and the people answer by confessing the one holy Father, the one holy Son and the one Holy Spirit. Holiness is not our own; we share it at the table of the Lord. This week the Apostles' Fast begins on Wednesday 16 June and is kept until the feast of Sts Peter and Paul on 29 June. The seventy of today's Gospel tell us what the fast is for: that labourers may be sent into the harvest, and that we who are sent may keep our joy where Jesus places it.

For study and teaching

  • Moses blesses each tribe by name before he dies. Whom do I bless, in word and prayer, in my family and parish?
  • Paul says that the one bread makes us one body. How does receiving Holy Communion change the way I treat the people who stand beside me in church?
  • Is there any “idol”, anything that takes God's place in my heart, from which Paul would tell me to flee?
  • Where do I look for my joy in serving the Church: in results, or in the fact that God knows me by name?
