Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” John 20:28

Reflection

On the evening of Easter the disciples are behind locked doors, afraid. Jesus comes and stands among them: “Peace be with you.” He shows them his hands and side, and they rejoice. Then he sends them: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you,” breathes on them and says, “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them.” From the first Easter evening the risen Lord gives his Church peace, mission, the Spirit and the power to forgive sins.

Thomas was not there. When the others say, “We have seen the Lord,” he answers: “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” A week later Jesus comes again and says: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” And Thomas makes the greatest confession of faith in the Gospels: “My Lord and my God!”

For us in India this Gospel is our own story. Thomas, who doubted and then believed, brought that faith to our land. The Church of Malankara, like all the St Thomas Christians, received from him the confession “My Lord and my God.” His doubt became our certainty; his wounded hand-touch became our faith.

Jesus adds a word for all later generations: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” That is us. St Peter says: “Although you have not seen him, you love him… and rejoice with an indescribable and glorious joy.” New Sunday asks: do we live with the joy of believers, or with the fear of locked doors?

Homily outline

Theme: “Peace be with you: sent, forgiven, believing.”

On New Sunday the Risen Lord comes through locked doors. He gives peace, breathes the Spirit, entrusts the power to forgive sins, and leads Thomas from doubt to faith.

1. Peace behind locked doors

  • The disciples hide in fear; Jesus comes and stands among them.
  • His first word is “Peace”, not reproach.
  • Isaiah tells the herald of good tidings: “lift up your voice … do not fear.” The Risen Christ turns fear into mission.
“Get you up to a high mountain, O Zion, herald of good tidings; lift up your voice with strength, O Jerusalem, herald of good tidings, lift it up, do not fear.” Isaiah 40:9
“When it was evening on that day, the first day of the week, and the doors of the house where the disciples had met were locked for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you.”” John 20:19

Apply: Bring your fears to the Risen Lord in prayer; let him say to you: “Peace be with you.”

2. Sent with the Spirit to forgive

  • Elisha asked for a double share of Elijah's spirit to continue his mission.
  • Jesus breathes on the apostles: “Receive the Holy Spirit.”
  • He gives them power to forgive sins: the gift of the sacrament of Reconciliation.
“Elisha said, “Please let me inherit a double share of your spirit.”” 2 Kings 2:9
“Jesus said to them again, “Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them, “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them.”” John 20:21–23

Apply: Make an Easter confession if you have not yet done so.

3. Blessed are those who have not seen

  • The Lord does not abandon doubting Thomas; like a shepherd he comes back for the one.
  • He shows his wounds; Thomas confesses: “My Lord and my God!”
  • We are among those blessed who believe without seeing.
“He will feed his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms, and carry them in his bosom, and gently lead the mother sheep.” Isaiah 40:11
“Then he said to Thomas, “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” John 20:27–28

Apply: At the elevation in the Qurbono, say silently: “My Lord and my God.”

Peace, Spirit, mercy and faith: these are the gifts of the Risen Lord on New Sunday.

Let us receive them and become messengers of his peace.

Take-home resolution: Forgive one person this week, as Christ forgives you.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on the evening of Easter the disciples were behind locked doors, full of fear. Jesus came and stood among them: “Peace be with you.” He showed them his wounds. Then he sent them: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” He breathed on them: “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven.”

Thomas was not there, and he would not believe: “Unless I see the marks of the nails and put my hand in his side, I will not believe.” A week later Jesus came again and invited him: “Put your finger here; reach out your hand. Do not doubt, but believe.” And Thomas cried out: “My Lord and my God!”

Dear friends, this is our own family story. Thomas, who doubted and then believed, brought this faith to India. We are called St Thomas Christians. His doubt, healed by the wounds of Christ, became our faith. In many of our churches people whisper his words when the priest lifts up the holy Mysteries: My Lord and my God.

Jesus then said: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” That is you and me. We have not seen him, but we love him. Let us not live behind locked doors of fear. Let us bring our doubts to his wounds, receive his peace, and go out as witnesses. My Lord and my God! Amen.

For children

Children, after Jesus rose, Thomas said: “I won't believe unless I see him myself!”

A week later Jesus came and said: “Thomas, look at my hands.” Thomas fell down and said: “My Lord and my God!”

Thomas later came to India to tell people about Jesus. That's why we are St Thomas Christians! Let's say together: “My Lord and my God!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus, risen and wounded, you came through locked doors and gave your disciples peace. Breathe your Spirit on us, heal our doubts, and make us confess with St Thomas, our father in faith: My Lord and my God! Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

New Sunday closes the white week, and the Church sets a full table. The Evening Gospel (John 20:19–23) gives the first evening of Easter: “Peace be with you … Receive the Holy Spirit.” Four Old Testament lessons follow. Genesis 41:37–45 raises Joseph, once thrown into a pit, over all the land of Egypt. 2 Kings 2:1–12 shows Elijah taken up and Elisha asking for a double share of his spirit. Wisdom 3:1–9 says, “the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God”. Isaiah 40:9–15 cries to the cities of Judah, “Here is your God!” Then 1 Peter 1:3–12 blesses God who has given us “a new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead”.

We study three of these closely. The lesson from 2 Kings stands nearest to the Gospel: a master taken from his disciple, a condition about seeing, and a spirit handed on. Paul, a prisoner, tells Timothy to “remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead”. John brings us twice into the room with closed doors, and to Thomas, who will not believe until he sees, and then makes the fullest confession of faith in the Gospels. The line runs from Elisha's “if you see me” to the Lord's “Blessed are those who have not seen”, and its key words are spirit, remember and believe.

2 Kings 2:1–12 · If you see me as I am being taken

Setting.

Elijah the Tishbite was the great prophet of the northern kingdom in the ninth century before Christ, in the days of King Ahab. He stood alone against the worship of Baal on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18), and at Horeb he met the Lord not in wind, earthquake or fire but in “a sound of sheer silence” (1 Kings 19:12). There he was told to anoint Elisha as prophet in his place, and he threw his mantle over Elisha as he ploughed (1 Kings 19:16, 19).

Our reading is the handing over. The two men walk from Gilgal to Bethel, from Bethel to Jericho, and on to the Jordan. Each time Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind, and each time Elisha refuses. Groups of prophets watch, and they know what is coming. Readers have long noticed that the parting of the Jordan and the crossing on dry ground recall Israel's entry into the land under Joshua (Joshua 3).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–6: three stages, three times “Stay here”, three times “I will not leave you”.
  • vv. 7–8: by the Jordan, the mantle strikes the water and it parts.
  • vv. 9–10: the request for a double share of the spirit, and its condition.
  • vv. 11–12: chariot and horses of fire; Elijah taken up; Elisha's cry and his torn clothes.
  • ¶ The story's weight lies in faithfulness. Because Elisha refused three times to leave his master, he is there to see, and to receive.

Key words.

  • se‘ārāh, whirlwind: A storm wind. The Lord answered Job out of such a wind (Job 38:1). Elijah is taken up by the wind, while the chariot of fire separates the two men.
  • pî-shenayim, double share: Literally “a mouth of two”, a portion of two. Deuteronomy 21:17 uses it for the inheritance of the firstborn son, who receives twice what each of the others receives. Elisha is not asking to be twice as great as Elijah. He is asking to be his heir, the firstborn among his disciples.
  • ’adderet, mantle: The prophet's cloak, the sign of his office. Elijah threw it over Elisha at his call, uses it here to strike the Jordan, and leaves it behind for him (2:13).

Verse by verse.

“As the Lord lives, and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” (2:2)

An oath, repeated three times (vv. 2, 4, 6). Ruth said something like it to Naomi (Ruth 1:16–17). Faithfulness to the one who has taught you is the road to receiving his gift.

“Please let me inherit a double share of your spirit.” (2:9)

He asks for nothing else: no wealth, no title. The spirit is the Lord's gift that made Elijah a prophet.

“if you see me as I am being taken from you, it will be granted you; if not, it will not.” (2:10)

Seeing is the condition. Elisha must keep his eyes on his master to the end. Today's Gospel also speaks of seeing, and then goes beyond it.

“Father, father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!” (2:12)

Elisha calls Elijah “father”, as the disciples of a prophet did, and names him as Israel's true defence, worth more than an army. The same cry is raised over Elisha himself when he is dying (13:14).

How the Church reads it.

Israel remembered Elijah's going up with hope: Malachi promised that the Lord would send him again before the great day (Malachi 4:5), and Jesus said that Elijah had already come, meaning John the Baptist (Matthew 17:12–13). The Catechism calls Elijah the father of the prophets (CCC 2582). Christian readers from early times have seen in this scene a figure of the Lord's own going up to the Father and of the Spirit he gives to his disciples. The difference is as important as the likeness: Elijah was taken up; Christ rose and ascended by his own power, and with his own breath he gives the Spirit.

2 Timothy 2:1–13 · Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead

Setting.

Second Timothy is written as Paul's last letter. He is a prisoner in Rome (1:17), many have deserted him (1:15), and he knows that his death is near (4:6). He writes to Timothy, his beloved “child”, who is leading the church at Ephesus. Many scholars today think that the Pastoral Letters (1 and 2 Timothy and Titus) were written after Paul's death by a disciple in his name; others, with the long tradition of the Church, hold that they come from Paul himself. Either way the Church receives the letter as inspired Scripture, and its voice is that of the apostle facing death.

Monday's epistle takes up the second half of this passage again, so here we give more time to its opening verses.

Structure.

  • v. 1: be strong in grace.
  • v. 2: hand on what you have heard, to faithful people who will teach others.
  • vv. 3–7: three pictures: the soldier, the athlete, the farmer.
  • vv. 8–10: remember Jesus Christ, raised; the word of God is not chained.
  • vv. 11–13: a sure saying, perhaps from an early hymn.

Key words.

  • paratithēmi, entrust: A word for placing something valuable in the keeping of a trustworthy person. The faith is a deposit (1:12, 14) passed from hand to hand: from Paul to Timothy, to faithful people, and to others. Four generations of teaching appear in one verse.
  • synkakopatheō, share in suffering: A rare word that joins “with” to “suffer hardship”. In the New Testament it appears only in this letter (1:8; 2:3). Timothy is not asked to suffer alone, but alongside Paul and alongside Christ.
  • mnēmoneue, remember: A present command: keep on remembering. Remembering here is not looking back with longing; it is keeping the living Lord before one's eyes.

Verse by verse.

“You then, my child, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus;” (2:1)

Strength comes first from grace, not from willpower.

“what you have heard from me through many witnesses entrust to faithful people who will be able to teach others as well.” (2:2)

A short picture of apostolic tradition. The Church's teaching is not invented anew in each generation. It is received, guarded and handed on.

“And in the case of an athlete, no one is crowned without competing according to the rules.” (2:5)

The soldier teaches single-mindedness, the athlete discipline, the farmer patient work (vv. 4–6). All three wait for a reward that comes at the end.

“Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, a descendant of David” (2:8)

The whole gospel in a line: raised from the dead, and of David's line, so truly human and truly the promised King. The Greek is a perfect participle: he is the one who has been raised and remains risen.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that, so that the Gospel might always be kept whole and alive in the Church, the apostles left bishops as their successors and handed over to them their own teaching office (Dei Verbum 7), and that what the apostles handed on includes everything that helps the People of God to live holily and to grow in faith (Dei Verbum 8). St Irenaeus taught that the tradition of the apostles can be found in every church by looking to the succession of bishops from the apostles (in substance). Timothy stands at the head of that chain.

John 20:19–29 · My Lord and my God!

Setting.

It is the evening of the first day of the week. That morning Mary Magdalene had found the tomb empty, had met the risen Lord, and had told the disciples, “I have seen the Lord” (20:18). Yet they are behind locked doors, John says “for fear of the Jews”. In John's Gospel this phrase usually means the Temple authorities who had handed Jesus over, not the Jewish people, to whom Jesus and his disciples themselves belonged.

The scene comes in two parts, a week apart. Thomas, called the Twin, was absent the first time. He has spoken before in this Gospel: when Jesus decided to go to Bethany, he said, “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (11:16), and at the Supper he asked, “How can we know the way?” (14:5). He is brave, and he wants to understand.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: the first evening: closed doors, peace, the wounds shown, joy.
  • vv. 21–23: the sending, the breath, the Spirit, the power to forgive.
  • vv. 24–25: Thomas absent, and his conditions.
  • vv. 26–27: eight days later the Lord meets Thomas's conditions.
  • vv. 28–29: Thomas's confession, and the blessing for those who have not seen.
  • ¶ The two parts have the same shape: the doors are shut, Jesus stands among them, and he says, “Peace be with you.” His coming does not depend on our opening the door.

Key words.

  • eirēnē hymin, peace be with you: The everyday greeting, shalom. On the risen Lord's lips it is the gift he promised at the Supper: “Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you” (14:27).
  • enephysēsen, he breathed on: The same rare verb is used in the Greek Bible when God breathes the breath of life into the first human being (Genesis 2:7), and in Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones (Ezekiel 37:9). This is the new creation.
  • Didymos, the Twin: The Greek word for “twin”. The name Thomas itself comes from the Aramaic word for twin, so the Gospel gives his name and then translates it.
  • ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, my Lord and my God: The fullest confession of faith in the Gospels. The Gospel began with “the Word was God” (1:1), and now a disciple says it to the risen Jesus face to face.

Verse by verse.

“After he said this, he showed them his hands and his side.” (20:20)

The risen body keeps the wounds. They are not erased but glorified, the signs that the one standing here is the one who was crucified.

“As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” (20:21)

Jesus' own mission becomes theirs. The Church is sent as he was sent.

“If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.” (20:23)

The first gift of the risen Lord, together with the Spirit, is the power to forgive. The Council of Trent taught that these words are to be understood of the power of forgiving and retaining sins in the sacrament of Penance.

“Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” (20:25)

Thomas will not borrow someone else's faith. His demand is precise: he wants to know that the risen one is the crucified one.

“Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” (20:27)

Jesus repeats Thomas's own words back to him; though no one saw him when Thomas spoke them, he knew them. The Gospel does not say that Thomas actually touched him.

“My Lord and my God!” (20:28)
“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

A beatitude for every later generation, and so for us. Elisha received the spirit because he saw; we receive the Spirit believing without seeing, through the word of those who saw: “Although you have not seen him, you love him” (1 Peter 1:8).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this evening when it teaches that the risen Christ gave his apostles his own power to forgive sins when he breathed the Holy Spirit on them (CCC 976; CCC 1485). It also notes that at the appearances of the risen Jesus the title “Lord” becomes a word of adoration, as in Thomas's confession (CCC 448). St Gregory the Great said that the unbelief of Thomas did more for our faith than the faith of the disciples who believed, because when he was led to faith by touching, our minds were released from all doubt (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was Thomas wrong to ask for proof?

He was not alone: the Gospels show that all the apostles struggled to believe, and the Catechism notes that even when faced with the risen Jesus they were still doubtful (CCC 644). Jesus does not reject Thomas. He comes back for him and meets him where he is. Yet he also says that a greater blessing belongs to those who believe without seeing, because faith rests on God's word received through the witnesses he has chosen.

Does John 20:23 have anything to do with confession to a priest?

Yes. The Church has always understood these words as the gift of the power to forgive sins in the Lord's name, given to the apostles. The Catechism teaches that since Christ entrusted the ministry of reconciliation to his apostles, bishops, their successors, and priests, the bishops' co-workers, continue to exercise it (CCC 1461). The Council of Trent taught this explicitly. When the priest says the words of absolution, the risen Lord is still breathing peace into a closed room.

Why is this called New Sunday?

It is the Sunday that closes the white week of Easter, when in the early Church the newly baptized wore their white garments for the last time. In Kerala it is known as Puthu Njayar. The newness is the new creation that Easter has begun, and the Gospel of this day, with the Lord's breath on his disciples, shows that new creation at work.

For the Syro-Malankara reader

For Christians of Kerala this Sunday has a special warmth. The ancient tradition of our Churches holds that Thomas, who confessed “My Lord and my God”, brought that faith to India, and the Thomas Christians have kept it for many centuries. The Malankara Syrian Catholic Church, which prays in the West Syriac (Antiochene) tradition, belongs to this same family of St Thomas. In the Holy Qurbono, when the holy Mysteries are lifted up, many of the faithful quietly say Thomas's words, “My Lord and my God”, as an act of faith in the Lord who is present though unseen. Thomas's doubt became our blessing; his confession is our inheritance.

For study and teaching

  • Elisha refused three times to leave his master. When has faithfulness in an ordinary, repeated way prepared me to receive something from God?
  • What has been handed on to me “through many witnesses”? To whom am I handing it on?
  • The Lord came through closed doors. What doors in my life, fear, shame or a quarrel, have I kept shut, and how might I let his “Peace be with you” in?
  • When I say “My Lord and my God” at the Holy Qurbono, what do I want those words to mean this year?
