Word for the day

“So if you consider me your partner, welcome him as you would welcome me.” Philemon 17

Reflection

The Letter to Philemon is the shortest of St Paul's letters and the most personal. It is a single page written from prison to a friend at Colossae, and its subject is a man named Onesimus, who had been in Philemon's household as a slave and had left it. Somehow Onesimus met Paul, and in that meeting he became a brother in Christ. Paul now sends him back with this letter. Today's verses are the heart of the appeal: “So if you consider me your partner, welcome him as you would welcome me.” Paul does not command, though he could. He puts himself in the place of Onesimus: receive him as you would receive me. Then comes the sentence that Christian preachers have loved for two thousand years: “If he has wronged you in any way, or owes you anything, charge that to my account. I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand: I will repay it.” In a few words Paul is doing what Christ did for us: he takes the debt of another onto his own account and signs for it with his own hand. The verse that comes just before today's reading says what Paul hopes Philemon will see: Onesimus is returning “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother” (Philemon 16). The Church has never read the letter as an approval of slavery; in time the Gospel it carries has undone it, and the Church teaches that the enslavement of human beings is a grave sin (Catechism 2414).

Paul ends with gentle words that show a whole way of life: “Yes, brother, let me have this benefit from you in the Lord! Refresh my heart in Christ. Confident of your obedience, I am writing to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say. One thing more: prepare a guest room for me, for I am hoping through your prayers to be restored to you.” He trusts Philemon to go beyond what is asked, and he counts on the prayer of the house community, for the prayers of Philemon's family are the means by which Paul hopes to be released and to come to them. A guest room, a prayer and a forgiven debt: this is how the Gospel moves into a home. In Kerala, where a guest is treated as a blessing and a house is judged by the welcome it gives, the letter has a special ring. Welcome him as you would welcome me is a rule for the parish, for the family, and for every household that has to receive someone whom it would prefer not to see.

The Gospel looks at the disciples on the lake shore. They have crossed to the other side and “had forgotten to bring any bread.” The Lord, who has just met the Pharisees and Sadducees demanding a sign (Matthew 16:1–4), says: “Watch out, and beware of the yeast of the Pharisees and Sadducees.” The disciples only think of their empty baskets: “It is because we have brought no bread.” Jesus, perceiving this, says: “You of little faith, why are you talking about having no bread? Do you still not perceive? Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many baskets you gathered? Or the seven loaves for the four thousand, and how many baskets you gathered?” The remedy for worry is memory: to count again what the Lord has already done. “Bless the Lord, O my soul, and do not forget all his benefits” (Psalm 103:2). Only then does he explain: “He had not told them to beware of the yeast of bread, but of the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees.” Yeast is a small thing that works in secret and spreads through the whole dough: “A little yeast leavens the whole batch of dough” (Galatians 5:9). Jesus names what can spread in a community, pride, hypocrisy, the demand for signs without faith. He is arguing within his own people, as the prophets did, and the warning is for each one of us, not for others. The yeast is not always bad: in the Antiochene tradition of our Church the bread of the Holy Qurbono is leavened, and the Lord himself says that the kingdom is like yeast. What counts is what spreads. Today we ask the Lord to keep the yeast of our hearts good, and to make us remember.

Full homily

Dear friends, the short Letter to Philemon asks a hard thing in a gentle way. Onesimus, who had been a slave in the house of Philemon, is coming back, and Paul says: So if you consider me your partner, welcome him as you would welcome me (Philemon 17). Then he says: If he has wronged you in any way, or owes you anything, charge that to my account. I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand: I will repay it (Philemon 18–19). This is a picture of Christ, who took our debt on his own account and paid it with his own hand on the Cross. In the verse just before this reading Paul says that Onesimus now comes back no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother (Philemon 16).

Paul adds: Confident of your obedience, I am writing to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say. And he asks: prepare a guest room for me, for I am hoping through your prayers to be restored to you (Philemon 21–22). A welcome, a guest room and a prayer: this is how the Gospel enters a home.

In the Gospel the disciples are crossing the lake, and they have forgotten to bring bread. Jesus warns them of the yeast of the Pharisees and Sadducees, but they think of their empty baskets. He says: You of little faith, why are you talking about having no bread? Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many baskets you gathered? (Matthew 16:8–9). The cure for worry is to remember what the Lord has done. And the yeast he warns of is the teaching of pride and hypocrisy, which a little spreads through the whole dough.

So let us welcome someone whom we find hard to welcome, let us forgive a debt, and let us count again the gifts of God, so that the yeast of his kingdom may work in us. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago a man named Onesimus had gone away from his master's house, and then he became a friend of Jesus. St Paul wrote a letter to the master, Philemon, and asked him to welcome Onesimus back as a brother. Paul even said that he would pay whatever Onesimus owed! That is just what Jesus does for us when we say sorry. In the Gospel the friends of Jesus forgot to take bread and began to worry. Jesus reminded them how he had fed thousands with a few loaves. When you worry, remember how good God has been. And this week, welcome somebody who is new or alone: sit with them and play with them.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you took our debt onto your own account and paid it with your own hand on the Cross. Teach us to welcome one another as you have welcomed us, and to see in every person a brother or sister for whom you died. Make our homes places of welcome, with a guest room and an open door, and let the prayers of our families be heard. When we worry about our empty baskets, remind us of the loaves you have multiplied, and keep us from the yeast of pride, hypocrisy and little faith. Let the yeast of your kingdom work in us until the whole dough is leavened. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are about what we carry and what we forget. In the Epistle Paul, an old man in prison, pleads for Onesimus, a runaway slave who has become his son in the faith, and promises to repay whatever Onesimus owes. In the Gospel the disciples cross the lake having forgotten to bring bread, and Jesus uses their worry about bread to warn them of another kind of yeast, the wrong teaching that can spread through a whole community. The key words are welcome and remember.

Philemon 17–22 · Welcome him as you would welcome me

Setting.

Philemon is the shortest of Paul's letters and the only one addressed chiefly to a single person on a private matter. Paul writes from prison (vv. 1, 9, 13) to Philemon, a Christian of Colossae in whose house the church met (v. 2; compare Colossians 4:9). Philemon's slave Onesimus had left him, perhaps with some debt or loss to his master, and had met Paul, who brought him to faith (v. 10). Where Paul was in prison is not certain: Rome, Ephesus and Caesarea have each been proposed.

Paul now sends Onesimus back, with this letter in his hand. He does not give an order. He asks as a friend, an “old man” (v. 9) and the one who brought Philemon himself to Christ (v. 19). Our verses are the heart of the request.

Structure.

  • v. 17: “welcome him as you would welcome me.”
  • vv. 18–19: if Onesimus owes anything, Paul will repay it.
  • v. 20: “Refresh my heart in Christ.”
  • vv. 21–22: Paul trusts Philemon to do even more, and hopes to visit.

Key words.

  • koinōnos, partner: The word means one who shares in something with another; it is of the same family as koinōnia, “communion”. Paul reminds Philemon that they share a common life in Christ, and that this is stronger than the law about a runaway slave.
  • ellogei, charge to my account: A word from the business of the time, used for putting a debt on someone's account. Paul takes Onesimus's debt on himself, as Christ took on himself the debt of us all.
  • anapauson ta splanchna, refresh my heart: The Greek word splanchna means the inward parts, the seat of strong feeling. Paul asks Philemon to bring peace to his deepest affection, by receiving Onesimus well.

Verse by verse.

“So if you consider me your partner, welcome him as you would welcome me.” (v. 17)

Paul does not say “forgive him” or “spare him”. He says: receive him as you would receive me. Onesimus takes Paul's place at Philemon's door.

“If he has wronged you in any way, or owes you anything, charge that to my account.” (v. 18)

Paul does not pretend there is no wrong. He admits that Onesimus may owe something, and then pays for it himself. This is love that deals honestly with the loss.

“I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand: I will repay it.” (v. 19)

Paul takes the pen from the secretary and writes these words himself, like a signature on a promissory note. Having said this, he gently remembers that Philemon owes him his very self.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this letter as a small page of the Gospel: in Christ a slave becomes “a beloved brother” (v. 16). Paul does not here condemn the institution of slavery in so many words, but by teaching that a slave is a brother he plants the seed that later grew. The Church today teaches that slavery and the trade in human beings are a grave offence against human dignity (CCC 2414; Gaudium et Spes 27). The Fathers also saw in Paul's offer a picture of Christ: as Paul stands between Onesimus and his master, so Christ stands between us and the Father and takes our debt upon himself. An old tradition identifies Onesimus with the bishop of Ephesus of that name, but this is not certain.

Matthew 16:5–12 · Beware of the yeast

Setting.

The scene follows two controversies. The Pharisees and Sadducees have asked Jesus for a sign from heaven, and he has refused them and left (16:1–4). He and his disciples cross the Sea of Galilee to the other side, and the disciples discover they have no bread. Jesus then speaks to them of yeast.

The Pharisees and Sadducees were different groups, often in disagreement with each other. Matthew names them together to show that the teaching Jesus warns against is not one party's alone: it is any teaching that closes the heart to the signs God gives. The Pharisees were pious and respected, and Jesus often agrees with them (Matthew 23:2–3). The warning is about a spirit of testing God and demanding signs, which can arise anywhere.

Structure.

  • v. 5: the disciples have forgotten bread.
  • v. 6: Jesus warns: beware of the yeast of the Pharisees and Sadducees.
  • v. 7: the disciples think he is speaking of bread.
  • vv. 8–11: Jesus reproaches their little faith; he recalls the two feedings.
  • v. 12: they understand: the yeast is teaching.

Key words.

  • zymē, yeast, leaven: Yeast is a little thing that works silently until all the dough is changed. In the Bible it can mean good influence (Matthew 13:33) or bad (1 Corinthians 5:6–8). Here it is the bad: a teaching that spreads quietly.
  • oligopistoi, you of little faith: Matthew uses this word of the disciples several times (6:30; 8:26; 14:31). It is not unbelief but weak, anxious trust. Jesus does not drive them away; he teaches them.
  • kophinoi / spyrides, baskets: Matthew uses a different word for the baskets of the first feeding (14:20, kophinoi) and for the second (15:37, spyrides). The Gospel keeps the memory of what the disciples gathered, as proof that Jesus always gives more than enough.

Verse by verse.

“You of little faith, why are you talking about having no bread?” (16:8)

The disciples have just seen Jesus feed thousands twice from a few loaves. To worry about a lack of bread in his company is to forget who is with them.

“Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many baskets you gathered?” (16:9)

Jesus calls them to remember. Faith in the Gospel is partly memory: remembering what the Lord has done and trusting that he will do it again.

“he had not told them to beware of the yeast of bread, but of the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees.” (16:12)

Matthew gives the answer to the riddle himself. “Teaching” (didachē) is the way of thinking passed from teacher to disciple. Yeast of bread feeds the body; false teaching can spoil the soul.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the two feedings as signs of the Eucharist (CCC 1335): Jesus gives bread that does not run out. The Fathers also took the warning about yeast as a call to guard the purity of faith and of teaching. For this the Church holds the Apostles' teaching in her creeds and teaches that the faithful must recognize true teaching from false (CCC 85–88). Yet yeast is not evil in itself; Jesus says the Kingdom of Heaven is like yeast hidden in flour (Matthew 13:33). The yeast of the Gospel, quiet and strong, is what the Church is called to be in the world.

Questions people ask

Did Paul accept slavery, since he sends Onesimus back?

Paul never calls slavery good. In the world of his day it was part of the whole order of society, and he does not here attack it directly. But he speaks of Onesimus as “my child” (v. 10) and “a beloved brother” (v. 16), and he asks Philemon to receive him as he would receive Paul himself. When a slave is a brother, the old order is already undone from within. The Church teaches today that slavery is a grave evil that offends the dignity of the human person (CCC 2414).

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus “as you would welcome me”. Whom is the Lord asking me to welcome in this way?
  • Jesus says, “Do you not remember?” What has the Lord done for me that I must remember when I am anxious about tomorrow?
