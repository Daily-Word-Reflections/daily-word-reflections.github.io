Word for the day

“Each of you must give as you have made up your mind, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.” 2 Corinthians 9:7

Reflection

St Paul is still speaking about the collection for the poor of Jerusalem, and now he uses the language every farmer understands: “The point is this: the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully.” A farmer who keeps back most of his seed for fear of losing it will have a poor harvest. Giving to the poor is not throwing seed away; it is sowing. Then Paul adds a word about the heart of the giver: “Each of you must give as you have made up your mind, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.” The Greek word for cheerful is hilaros, from which comes our word “hilarious.” God does not want gifts squeezed out of us by guilt or by public pressure; he wants the gift of a glad heart. And the one who gives in this way will never be the poorer: “God is able to provide you with every blessing in abundance, so that by always having enough of everything, you may share abundantly in every good work.” Paul quotes today's Psalm about the just man: “He scatters abroad, he gives to the poor; his righteousness endures forever.” God, “who supplies seed to the sower and bread for food,” will multiply our seed. And the final fruit of all giving is not only bread for the poor but praise for God: our generosity “will produce thanksgiving to God.”

In the Gospel Jesus speaks of the three great works of Jewish piety, which the Church still practises: almsgiving, prayer and fasting. He does not question these practices; he takes for granted that his disciples will give, pray and fast. He asks only one question: for whom are you doing it? “Beware of practicing your piety before others in order to be seen by them; for then you have no reward from your Father in heaven.” The word “hypocrite” in Greek meant a stage actor, someone playing a part behind a mask. Jesus describes the actor's way: sounding a trumpet when giving alms, praying “at the street corners, so that they may be seen by others,” disfiguring the face while fasting. Three times he says: “Truly I tell you, they have received their reward.” They wanted applause, and they got it, and that is all they will get. Then three times he describes the way of the child of God: “do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing”; “go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret”; “put oil on your head and wash your face.” And three times comes the same promise: “your Father who sees in secret will reward you.” This does not forbid public worship or good example; a few verses earlier Jesus said, “let your light shine before others” (Matthew 5:16). The difference is the direction of the heart: the light is to give glory to the Father, not to ourselves.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul speaks to us today like a farmer: the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully (2 Corinthians 9:6). When we give to the poor, we are not losing; we are sowing. And God is the one who gives the harvest. He supplies seed to the sower and bread for food.

Paul also says: God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7). Not a giver who is forced, not a giver who complains, not a giver who gives only because his name will be read out in church. God loves the one who gives with a glad heart. Sometimes the poorest give most cheerfully, because they know what it is to be in need.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks about our good works: almsgiving, prayer and fasting. He says: do not do them to be seen by others. The hypocrites wanted to be praised, and Jesus says: they have received their reward (Matthew 6:2). If we give only to get a photograph in the newspaper, or pray only so that others think we are holy, we already have our reward: the praise of people, which lasts a moment.

Instead, Jesus invites us to a secret life with the Father: go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you (Matthew 6:6). Let us today do one good thing that nobody will ever know about. Let us give one gift with a cheerful heart. And let us find one quiet moment to pray behind a shut door. The Father sees, and that is enough. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: when you do something good, you do not have to show everyone. You can do it in secret! Maybe you can make your sister's bed when she is not looking, or put a coin in the box for the poor without telling anyone. Jesus says God sees everything, even secret good deeds, and he is very happy with them. Try one secret good deed today!

Prayer

Generous Father, you supply seed to the sower and bread for food, and you love a cheerful giver. Release our hearts from fear and meanness, so that we may sow bountifully and share abundantly in every good work. Lord Jesus, you saw the hypocrisy of those who wanted to be seen; purify our alms, our prayer and our fasting, so that we may do them for the Father who sees in secret, and our generosity may produce thanksgiving to God. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

St Paul uses the language of the farmer: the one who sows bountifully will reap bountifully, and God loves a cheerful giver. In the Gospel Jesus speaks of the three good works of Jewish piety: almsgiving, prayer and fasting. Do them for the Father who sees in secret, not to be seen by others. Both readings ask the same question: for whom am I giving?

2 Corinthians 9:6–11 · God loves a cheerful giver

Setting.

Paul continues his appeal for the collection for the poor of Jerusalem. He has praised the Macedonians and sent Titus and two brothers ahead, so that the Corinthians’ gift may be ready as a gift and not as something extorted (9:5). Now he gives the spiritual principle behind giving.

Structure.

  • v. 6: sow sparingly, reap sparingly; sow bountifully, reap bountifully.
  • v. 7: give as you have decided in your heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion.
  • vv. 8–10: God is able to provide every blessing; he supplies seed to the sower (quoting Psalm 112:9).
  • v. 11: enriched for generosity, which produces thanksgiving to God.

Key words.

  • hilaros, cheerful: From it comes our word “hilarious”. The line echoes the Greek Old Testament of Proverbs 22:8, which says God blesses a cheerful giver. God looks at the heart as well as at the gift.
  • ep’ eulogiais, bountifully: Literally “with blessings”. A generous gift is a blessing for the poor and draws down blessing on the giver.
  • autarkeia, enough: Having enough, contentment (v. 8). God gives not so that we may hoard, but so that, having enough, we may share abundantly in every good work.

Verse by verse.

“the one who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and the one who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully.” (9:6)

Giving is not loss but sowing. What is given in love is not wasted; God makes it grow.

“not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.” (9:7)

Paul does not set an amount. He asks for a decision of the heart, made willingly and with joy.

“He who supplies seed to the sower and bread for food” (9:10)

These words recall Isaiah 55:10. All we have to give is first given to us by God.

How the Church reads it.

From the very beginning Christians have brought, along with the bread and wine for the Eucharist, gifts to share with those in need (CCC 1351). The collection at Mass continues Paul’s collection. The Catechism lists almsgiving among the chief works of mercy and a witness to fraternal charity (CCC 2447).

Matthew 6:1–6, 16–18 · Your Father who sees in secret

Setting.

After the six antitheses about the law, the Sermon on the Mount turns to three practices of devotion that Jesus’ hearers knew well: almsgiving, prayer and fasting (compare Tobit 12:8). Jesus does not reject them; he assumes his disciples will do them (“whenever you give… whenever you pray… whenever you fast”). He warns only against doing them to be seen. The Lectionary leaves out the Lord’s Prayer (vv. 7–15), which is read tomorrow.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the principle: do not practise your piety to be seen by others.
  • vv. 2–4: almsgiving: no trumpet; let not the left hand know.
  • vv. 5–6: prayer: not at street corners, but in your room with the door shut.
  • vv. 16–18: fasting: oil on your head, wash your face.

Key words.

  • dikaiosynē, piety: Literally “righteousness”: the right way of living before God. Here it names the acts of devotion.
  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: In Greek, a stage actor who plays a part behind a mask. Jesus warns against making devotion a performance. The warning is for his own disciples, and for every religious person.
  • en tō kryptō, in secret: Used again and again in this passage. God sees what no one else sees, and his reward is himself.

Verse by verse.

“do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing,” (6:3)

A vivid picture: give so quietly that you yourself hardly notice. Do not keep a record of your own goodness.

“go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret;” (6:6)

The word for “room” meant an inner storeroom, often the only room with a door. Jesus does not forbid public prayer, which he himself shared in the synagogue and the Temple; he asks that all prayer come from a heart turned to the Father.

“But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face,” (6:17)

Oil and washing were signs of ordinary daily care, even of joy. Fasting should not become a show of sadness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the New Law practises the acts of religion, almsgiving, prayer and fasting, directing them to the “Father who sees in secret”, in contrast with the desire to be seen by men (CCC 1969). It names fasting, prayer and almsgiving as the three forms of penance that express conversion in relation to oneself, to God and to others (CCC 1434). The Church reads this Gospel on Ash Wednesday, at the start of Lent.

Questions people ask

If good works should be secret, why does Jesus also say “let your light shine before others” (Matthew 5:16)?

The two sayings answer different temptations. In 5:16 the light is seen so that people “give glory to your Father in heaven”. In 6:1 the work is done “in order to be seen”, for one’s own glory. The question is not whether people see, but for whom we act. St Gregory the Great said: let the work be public, but the intention remain hidden (in substance).

For study and teaching

  • Do I give gladly or reluctantly? What would make my giving more cheerful this week?
  • Which of the three, almsgiving, prayer or fasting, do I most need to practise “in secret” for the Father alone?
