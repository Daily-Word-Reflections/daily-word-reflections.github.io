Word for the day

“Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life, which the Son of Man will give you.” John 6:27

Reflection

Stephen first appears in the Acts of the Apostles as a man chosen to wait on tables. The Twelve had asked the community to pick seven men “of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom” to care for the daily distribution of food to the widows, so that the apostles could give themselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word. Stephen's name stands first on the list. But today we see that the man appointed to serve bread is also a man of the word: “full of grace and power,” he “did great wonders and signs among the people.” Members of the synagogue of the Freedmen, Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia and Asia, begin to argue with him, and “they could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke.” When argument fails, accusation begins. False witnesses are brought: “This man never stops saying things against this holy place and the law.” St Luke tells the story so that we hear the trial of Jesus again; there too false witnesses spoke about the Temple (Mark 14:57–58). The disciple is walking the road of his Master. And at the very moment of accusation Luke gives us one quiet, luminous line: all who sat in the council looked at him, “and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.” The man accused of speaking against Moses carries the radiance of Moses, whose face shone when he came down from Sinai after speaking with God. The Psalm gives us Stephen's inner prayer: “Even though princes sit plotting against me, your servant will meditate on your statutes.” The quarrel here is a family quarrel among Jews, for Stephen is himself a Jew; Luke does not blame a whole people, and neither does the Church (Catechism 597).

The Gospel takes us to the morning after the feeding of the five thousand. The crowd has noticed that Jesus did not get into the boat with his disciples, so they take boats from Tiberias and cross to Capernaum looking for him. “Rabbi, when did you come here?” Jesus does not answer the question; he answers the heart: “you are looking for me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.” They had seen bread; they had not seen the sign. They wanted another meal, not the One who gives it. So Jesus lifts their eyes: “Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life, which the Son of Man will give you.” They reply like good, practical people: “What must we do to perform the works of God?” They expect a list. Jesus gives them one word: “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” Faith is both God's work in us and our first work for God; every good deed grows out of it. Through this whole week the Church reads the great discourse on the Bread of Life, step by step, and in the Easter season we read it with the eyes of the newly baptized, who have just received the Eucharist for the first time. Stephen served the bread that perishes to hungry widows and preached the bread that endures. The Church still does both, and both are the work of love.

Full homily

Dear friends, Stephen was chosen to serve food to widows. A humble job. But the Spirit does not stay inside job descriptions: the man who served tables was full of grace and power, and his wisdom was so strong that his opponents could not answer him (Acts 6:10).

So they found false witnesses, as they had for Jesus. And at that moment, when anyone else would be angry or afraid, the whole council looked at Stephen and saw that his face was like the face of an angel (Acts 6:15). That is what Easter does to a person. The risen Lord gives a peace that accusations cannot take away.

In the Gospel a crowd crosses the lake looking for Jesus. It sounds wonderful, until Jesus tells them the truth: you are looking for me because you ate your fill of the loaves (John 6:26). They wanted more bread, not the One who gives it. Many of us pray like that: we come when we need something, and disappear when we have it.

Jesus does not send them away. He offers them more than they asked: work for the food that endures for eternal life. And when they ask what work they must do, he gives them one: believe in him whom God has sent (John 6:29). Faith first, and everything else grows from it. At this Mass he gives us that food that endures, his own Body and Blood. Let us seek him today not only for his gifts but for himself. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, the people followed Jesus because he gave them bread to eat. Jesus said: I have an even better bread for you, a bread that makes you live for ever! That bread is Jesus himself, in Holy Communion. Today let us tell Jesus: I love you, not only for what you give me, but because you are you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, risen from the dead, you filled your servant Stephen with grace and power and made his face shine before his accusers. Deliver us from seeking you only for the bread that perishes. Give us the faith that is your own work in us, and feed us with the bread that endures for eternal life, until we see your face in glory. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Stephen, one of the seven whose choosing we heard on Saturday, now steps forward: full of grace and power, he speaks with a wisdom his opponents cannot withstand, and when he is brought before the council his face is like the face of an angel. In the Gospel the crowd fed in Friday’s reading crosses the lake looking for Jesus, and he tells them to work for the food that endures for eternal life. The work God asks is to believe in the one he has sent. Psalm 119 blesses those who walk in the law of the Lord.

Acts 6:8–15 · The face of an angel

Setting.

Stephen was chosen to serve at table (6:5), but Luke now shows him as a powerful preacher and worker of signs. His opponents come from a synagogue of Greek-speaking Jews in Jerusalem. The “Freedmen” (Greek Libertinoi, from the Latin word) were probably Jews who had been slaves, or the descendants of slaves, freed in Rome or elsewhere; many think of the captives brought to Rome by Pompey in 63 BC. With them were Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia and Asia. Saul of Tarsus came from Cilicia; he will be present at Stephen’s death (7:58).

The dispute is among Jews in Jerusalem about the meaning of the temple and the Law in the light of Jesus. Luke shapes Stephen’s trial so that it recalls the trial of Jesus.

Structure.

  • v. 8: Stephen’s grace and power.
  • vv. 9–10: debate, and wisdom his opponents cannot withstand.
  • vv. 11–14: secret instigation, a stirred crowd, arrest, false witnesses.
  • v. 15: the council sees his face like an angel’s.

Key words.

  • charis kai dynamis, grace and power: Luke says that the favour (charis) of God was upon the child Jesus (Luke 2:40), and that great power and great grace marked the apostles’ witness (Acts 4:33). Stephen, chosen for service, shares the same gifts.
  • pseudomartyres, false witnesses: The same charge as at Jesus’ trial, where false witnesses said that he would destroy the temple (Mark 14:56–58). The disciple walks his Master’s road.
  • ta ethē, the customs: The practices of the Law handed down from Moses. The accusation is that Stephen announces their end. In fact the first Christians in Jerusalem went on praying in the temple (2:46; 3:1).

Verse by verse.

“Stephen, full of grace and power, did great wonders and signs among the people.” (6:8)

The one chosen “to wait on tables” is the first, after the apostles, whom Luke shows working signs. Service and power go together.

“But they could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke.” (6:10)

Jesus had promised: “I will give you words and a wisdom that none of your opponents will be able to withstand or contradict” (Luke 21:15). The promise is kept in Stephen.

“They set up false witnesses” (6:13)

When argument fails, some turn to falsehood. Luke presents this as the work of particular people, not of the Jewish people; many in Jerusalem had come to faith (6:7).

“and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.” (6:15)

Perhaps an echo of Moses, whose face shone after he spoke with God (Exodus 34:29). Stephen, accused of speaking against Moses, shines as Moses did.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours Stephen as the first martyr, the protomartyr, and keeps his feast on 26 December, the day after Christmas, so that the birth of Christ is followed at once by the witness of blood. Luke tells his death as an echo of Jesus’ death: he commends his spirit to the Lord and prays for his persecutors (7:59–60).

John 6:22–29 · The food that endures

Setting.

This begins the long dialogue at Capernaum that will become the discourse on the Bread of Life, spoken in the synagogue there (6:59). The crowd fed on the far shore has noticed that Jesus did not leave in the disciples’ boat, and comes looking for him. Verses 22–24 are complicated, but their purpose is simple: to bring the same crowd face to face with Jesus again.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: the crowd searches and crosses to Capernaum.
  • vv. 25–27: “When did you come here?”: Jesus answers with the true question, what they are seeking.
  • vv. 28–29: the work of God is to believe.

Key words.

  • brōsis, food: Food in the sense of eating and nourishment. Jesus contrasts food that perishes with food that “endures”, a favourite word of John (menein, to remain, abide).
  • sphragizein, to set a seal: God the Father has set his seal on the Son of Man: he has marked him as his own and authorized him, as a seal authorizes a document.
  • ergon, work: The crowd asks about “works” in the plural, things to do. Jesus answers with one “work”: faith. Faith is both God’s work in us and our response.

Verse by verse.

“you are looking for me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.” (6:26)

They saw the bread but not what it signified. Jesus does not send them away; he tries to lead them from the gift to the Giver.

“Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life,” (6:27)

Not a contempt for daily bread, which Jesus himself teaches us to ask for. It is a question of what we live for.

“This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” (6:29)

St Augustine comments on these verses: “Believe, and you have eaten” (in substance, Tractates on John 25). Faith is the first way of receiving the Bread of Life; the Eucharist, which Jesus will speak of in 6:51–58, is received by faith.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith is a gift of God, a supernatural virtue infused by him, and at the same time an authentically human act (CCC 153–154). John 6:29 holds both: it is “the work of God”, and it is something we are called to do. These verses prepare for the words that follow: “I am the bread of life” (6:35).

For study and teaching

  • Stephen served tables and preached with wisdom. How might my ordinary service become a witness?
  • What am I really looking for when I seek Jesus? What “food that perishes” do I work hardest for?
