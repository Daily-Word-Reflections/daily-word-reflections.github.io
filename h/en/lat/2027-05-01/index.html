Word for the day

“If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” John 15:18

Reflection

Paul sets out on his second missionary journey, and the first thing Luke tells us is about a young man. In Lystra, the town where Paul had once been stoned and left for dead, there is a disciple named Timothy, “the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer; but his father was a Greek.” The believers speak well of him, and Paul wants him as a companion. Then the little band travels from town to town, handing on the decisions of the apostles and elders in Jerusalem, “so the churches were strengthened in the faith and increased in numbers daily.” What follows is strange. The missionaries try to preach in the province of Asia, and the Holy Spirit forbids them. They try to go north into Bithynia, and “the Spirit of Jesus did not allow them.” Two doors close, one after the other. They end up at Troas, on the coast, with nowhere obvious to go. And there, in the night, Paul sees a man of Macedonia standing and pleading: “Come over to Macedonia and help us.” At once they cross the sea. Notice the small change in the text: from this verse Luke begins to write “we.” The writer has joined the journey. The Gospel is about to enter Europe, and it enters through closed doors, a dream in the night, and a plea for help.

The Gospel moves from the joy of the mission to its cost. At the Last Supper Jesus warns his friends: “If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” In John's Gospel “the world” here does not mean the earth God made and loves (John 3:16), but human life organised without God and against him. The disciple who belongs to Christ will not always be welcome in it. “Remember the word that I said to you, ‘Servants are not greater than their master.’ If they persecuted me, they will persecute you; if they kept my word, they will keep yours also.” Paul would learn this soon enough in Macedonia: beatings in Philippi, riots in Thessalonica. And yet the plea “help us” was real. The same world that resists the Gospel is crying out for it. Today, on the first of May, the Church also honours St Joseph the Worker. Pope Pius XII gave this memorial to the Church in 1955, so that the workers' day would also be a day of the carpenter of Nazareth. The people of Nazareth once asked of Jesus, “Is not this the carpenter's son?” Joseph's hidden work, with the Blessed Virgin Mary beside him, made a home where the Son of God grew up. On this day we pray for all who work, for all who cannot find work, and for all whose work is unjust or unsafe; and we begin the month of May with the Mother of Jesus.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul and his companions had good plans. They wanted to preach in Asia; the Holy Spirit said no. They tried Bithynia; the Spirit of Jesus did not allow them (Acts 16:7). Two closed doors. And then, at Troas, by the sea, a dream: a man of Macedonia saying, come over to Macedonia and help us (Acts 16:9).

We do not like closed doors. An exam failed, a job refused, a marriage proposal that did not work out, a transfer we did not want. But sometimes God closes a door to keep us for another. Paul did not sit down and sulk at Troas. He prayed, he listened, and when the call came he crossed the sea at once. Because of that crossing, the Gospel came to Europe.

Jesus is honest with us in the Gospel. Not every door will be opened by the world. If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you (John 15:18). The servant is not greater than the master. Paul would be beaten in Philippi only days after he landed. Following the Spirit is not always comfortable. But it is always fruitful.

Today is also the first of May, and the Church shows us St Joseph the Worker. People in Nazareth looked down a little on Jesus: is not this the carpenter's son? But Joseph's hidden, honest work kept the Holy Family alive. Let us offer our work to God today, pray for every worker and for those who have no work, and begin this month of May close to our Mother Mary. Amen.

For children

Children, Paul wanted to go somewhere, but God said no. Then God showed him in a dream a man saying: come and help us! So Paul went, and many people came to know Jesus. Sometimes when we do not get what we want, God has a better plan. And today we remember St Joseph, the carpenter who worked hard for Jesus and Mary. Let us help at home today, like Jesus helped Joseph!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, risen from the dead, you sent your apostles across the sea in answer to the cry “Come over and help us.” When our plans fail and doors close, give us the humility to follow your Spirit. When the world resists your word, keep us faithful, for you were hated before us. Through the prayers of St Joseph, the carpenter of Nazareth, bless all who work and all who look for work, and give us a share in the Easter joy of your Mother, whose month begins today. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul sets out again, now with Silas and a young man from Lystra named Timothy. The Holy Spirit closes one road after another, until a vision in the night opens the way: a man of Macedonia pleading, “Come over to Macedonia and help us.” The Gospel crosses into Europe in answer to a cry for help. In the Gospel Jesus warns his friends that the world which hated him will hate them too. Psalm 100 calls all the earth to make a joyful noise to the Lord: the mission, its cost and its joy belong together.

Acts 16:1–10 · Come over to Macedonia

Setting.

This is the beginning of what is often called Paul’s second missionary journey. After the meeting in Jerusalem (Acts 15), Paul and Barnabas parted company over John Mark, and Paul chose Silas instead (15:36–41). Now he revisits the churches of the first journey and finds at Lystra a disciple named Timothy, whose mother was a Jewish believer and whose father was Greek. Second Timothy 1:5 names his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, women of sincere faith.

Background. “Asia” here is not the continent but the Roman province of Asia, in the west of present-day Turkey, with Ephesus as its chief city. Bithynia lay to the north, along the Black Sea. Troas was a port on the Aegean coast, not far from ancient Troy. Across the sea lay Macedonia, the Roman province in northern Greece, with the cities of Philippi and Thessalonica.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: Timothy joins Paul, and Paul has him circumcised.
  • vv. 4–5: the decisions of Jerusalem are delivered; the churches grow.
  • vv. 6–8: the Spirit closes the way into Asia and Bithynia; they come down to Troas.
  • vv. 9–10: the vision of the man of Macedonia, and the first “we”.

Key words.

  • to pneuma Iēsou, the Spirit of Jesus: Only here in the New Testament (v. 7). In v. 6 Luke says “the Holy Spirit”, in v. 7 “the Spirit of Jesus”: the same Spirit, whom the Risen Lord pours out and through whom he still leads his Church.
  • horama, vision: Luke uses this word for the visions that guide the Church at turning points: to Ananias (9:10), to Cornelius (10:3) and to Peter (10:17). God speaks when the road is unclear.
  • symbibazō, to conclude, be convinced: Literally to put things together (v. 10). The companions weigh the closed doors and the vision together and come to a decision. God’s guidance and careful human thought work side by side.

Verse by verse.

“the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer; but his father was a Greek.” (16:1)

Timothy grew up between two worlds. Many scholars think that the son of a Jewish mother would have been regarded as a Jew, which helps to explain the next verse.

“he took him and had him circumcised because of the Jews who were in those places,” (16:3)

This can surprise us, since Paul refused to have the Gentile Titus circumcised (Galatians 2:3). But Timothy was counted as Jewish, and Paul did not want his companion to be a stumbling block in the synagogues where they would preach. As he says himself, to the Jews he became as a Jew (1 Corinthians 9:20).

“having been forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia.” (16:6)

Luke does not say how: perhaps through a prophet, through circumstances, or through an inner conviction. The mission is not Paul’s own plan. A closed door is also guidance. Paul will preach in Asia later, for more than two years at Ephesus (19:8–10).

““Come over to Macedonia and help us.”” (16:9)

The first call to bring the Gospel into Europe comes as a plea for help. The man does not ask for new ideas; he asks for help. The Gospel is the help people most need, even when they cannot name it.

“we immediately tried to cross over to Macedonia,” (16:10)

Here, without any explanation, the story changes from “they” to “we”. This is the first of the “we” passages of Acts (16:10–17, and later in chapters 20–21 and 27–28). Ancient tradition took them as the voice of Luke himself, Paul’s companion. Scholars still discuss them, but that remains the simplest reading.

How the Church reads it.

Pope St Paul VI wrote in Evangelii Nuntiandi (1975) that the Church exists in order to evangelise (n. 14). The cry of the man of Macedonia is that need spoken aloud. The Catechism calls the Holy Spirit the principal agent of the whole of the Church’s mission, the one who leads her on her missionary paths (CCC 852). Today’s reading shows this in practice: the apostles plan, the Spirit redirects, and the Church goes where she is needed.

John 15:18–21 · It hated me before it hated you

Setting.

We are still at the Last Supper. Jesus has just called the disciples his friends and given them the commandment to love one another (15:12–17). Now he turns to the other side: the hatred of the world. Love within the community and hostility from outside are spoken of in the same breath.

In John, “the world” has more than one sense. God so loved the world that he gave his only Son (3:16). Here the world means humanity as it closes itself against God. The disciples are not taken out of the world; they are sent into it (17:15, 18).

Structure.

  • v. 18: the world hated me first.
  • v. 19: chosen out of the world.
  • v. 20: the servant and the master.
  • v. 21: on account of my name.

Key words.

  • kosmos, world: Here not the earth, nor people as such, but human life arranged without God and against him.
  • miseō, to hate: In the Bible “to hate” can mean to reject, to refuse to belong with. Jesus does not ask his disciples to hate in return; in the same discourse he prays for those who will believe through their word (17:20).
  • eklegomai, to choose: “I have chosen you out of the world” (v. 19) uses the same verb as “You did not choose me but I chose you” (15:16). The disciple’s identity rests on Christ’s choice, not on the world’s approval.

Verse by verse.

“If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” (15:18)

Here too Jesus goes ahead of his disciples. When they suffer for him, they walk where he has already walked.

“If you belonged to the world, the world would love you as its own.” (15:19)

A disciple who is never opposed may ask whether he has simply blended in. This is not a call to look for conflict, only to belong first to Christ.

“Remember the word that I said to you, ‘Servants are not greater than their master.’” (15:20)

He said it after washing their feet (13:16). There it meant that they must serve as he served; here it means that they will be treated as he was treated. Both belong to being a disciple.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read these words in the light of her martyrs. St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to death in Rome, wrote that Christianity shows its greatness when it is hated by the world (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that all must be ready to confess Christ before others and to follow him along the way of the cross amid the persecutions which the Church never lacks (Lumen Gentium 42, in substance). Christians in many lands suffer today for the name of Christ; the Church prays for them, and for those who wrong them.

Questions people ask

Should a Christian expect to be hated?

Not to look for it, and not to be surprised by it. Jesus speaks of hatred “on account of my name” (v. 21), not hatred we earn by harshness or pride. St Peter makes the same distinction: no one should suffer as a wrongdoer, but whoever suffers as a Christian should not be ashamed (1 Peter 4:15–16). The Christian answer to hostility is the love Jesus has just commanded, and prayer for those who persecute (Matthew 5:44).

For the Latin reader

1 May is also the optional memorial of St Joseph the Worker, instituted by Pope Pius XII in 1955; where it is kept, its own readings may be used. The carpenter of Nazareth and Paul the tentmaker (Acts 18:3) remind us that ordinary work, done with love, belongs to God’s plan.

For study and teaching

  • When has a closed door in my life turned out to be God’s guidance?
  • Who around me is saying, in words or without words, “Come over and help us”?
