Word for the day

“And you shall hallow the fiftieth year and you shall proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a jubilee for you: you shall return, every one of you, to your property and every one of you to your family.” Leviticus 25:10

Reflection

The first reading gives the law of the Jubilee, spoken “on Mount Sinai.” Israel is to count seven weeks of years, “seven times seven years,” forty-nine years, and then, “on the tenth day of the seventh month, on the day of atonement,” the trumpet is to be sounded “throughout all your land.” The fiftieth year is holy: “you shall proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a jubilee for you: you shall return, every one of you, to your property and every one of you to your family.” The land is not to be sown or reaped that year; it rests, and the people eat what the field gives by itself. Property that has been sold or lost returns to the family; those who have fallen into debt are released. In effect the Jubilee is a reset, a time when the poor are given back what they have lost and no family is shut out of the land for ever. There are practical rules as well: in buying and selling, “you shall not cheat one another,” and the price is to reflect the number of harvests before the next Jubilee, “but you shall fear your God; for I am the Lord your God.” The year of Jubilee became, for the prophets, a picture of God's saving freedom. Jesus read from Isaiah in the synagogue at Nazareth: “to proclaim release to the captives... to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor” (Luke 4:18–19). The Church keeps Holy Years of Jubilee, the most recent in 2025. The Psalm is a prayer for the blessing to reach beyond Israel: “May God be gracious to us and bless us and make his face to shine upon us, that your way may be known upon earth, your saving power among all nations.”

The Gospel shows the opposite of liberty: a prophet in prison and a king in bondage to his own pride. Herod Antipas, the ruler of Galilee, hears about Jesus and decides that he must be “John the Baptist; he has been raised from the dead.” The Evangelist then tells why he thinks so. John had been arrested because he had said to Herod: “It is not lawful for you to have her,” meaning Herodias, his brother Philip's wife. Herod “wanted to put him to death,” but he “feared the crowd, because they regarded him as a prophet.” St Mark adds that Herod “feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man,” and “liked to listen to him” (Mark 6:20), though he did not act on what he heard. At his birthday banquet Herodias' daughter dances, and Herod, carried away, promises with an oath to give her whatever she asks. Prompted by her mother she says: “Give me the head of John the Baptist here on a platter.” The king is “grieved, yet out of regard for his oaths and for the guests” he orders it. What a picture of a man who is a king and a slave: afraid of the crowd, afraid of the guests, afraid of looking weak, and not afraid of killing a righteous man. John, in prison, had the freedom Herod lacked. The scene ends with a detail of great tenderness: John's disciples “took the body and buried it; then they went and told Jesus.” They bring their grief to him. Today the Church remembers St Ignatius of Loyola, a man who knew the power of such a decision. Born in 1491 in the Basque country, a courtier and soldier, Ignatius was wounded by a cannonball at Pamplona in 1521. While he recovered at Loyola he read the lives of Christ and of the saints, and noticed that the thoughts of serving God left him peaceful and joyful, while worldly dreams left him empty. That was the beginning of his conversion and of his teaching on the discernment of spirits. At Manresa he began to write the Spiritual Exercises; he studied in Spain and Paris, and gathered friends, among them St Francis Xavier and St Peter Faber; and the Society of Jesus was approved by Pope Paul III in 1540. Ignatius died in Rome on 31 July 1556 and was canonised in 1622. His watchword was “for the greater glory of God,” in Latin Ad maiorem Dei gloriam, which is St Paul's line in the proper reading: “whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” The Jesuits have long served India: St Francis Xavier landed at Goa in 1542 and worked among the fishing communities of the coast, and Jesuit schools and colleges are found in every part of the country. The proper Gospel for the memorial is Jesus' call to count the cost: “Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” Ignatius knew that real freedom is the freedom to give oneself wholly to God.

Full homily

Dear friends, God gives Israel a wonderful law in the book of Leviticus: the Jubilee. Every fiftieth year the trumpet is sounded across the land, and everyone is to return to their property and to their family (Leviticus 25:10). Debts are cancelled, slaves are released, the land rests. It is a year of liberty. Jesus would read from Isaiah in Nazareth about this very year: to proclaim release to the captives, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor (Luke 4:18–19). And the Church keeps Jubilee Holy Years to remind us that God's mercy is always opening doors.

The Gospel shows the opposite. Herod is a king, and he has no liberty. He is afraid of the crowd. He is afraid of his guests. He is afraid of his oath, and so he orders the death of a righteous man. And in prison there is John, who has nothing but the truth, and he is the one at liberty. It is not lawful for you to have her (Matthew 14:4). John did not fear to say what was right, and he paid for it with his life. This is the freedom of the Jubilee: not to be spared the consequences, but to be released from fear.

At the end of the passage there is a small detail: his disciples took the body and buried it; then they went and told Jesus (Matthew 14:12). They bring their grief to Jesus. That is where every sorrow should go.

Today we remember a man who found this freedom: St Ignatius of Loyola. A proud soldier, he was wounded at Pamplona. In bed, with nothing to read but the lives of Christ and the saints, he found that thoughts of serving God left him with a deep peace. His whole life was changed. He wrote the Spiritual Exercises, and gathered friends like Francis Xavier, who came to India, to Goa, to the fishermen of our coasts. His motto was for the greater glory of God. St Paul says it: whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God (1 Corinthians 10:31). Dear friends, let us ask for the Jubilee freedom of Ignatius and John: released from fear, ready to give ourselves wholly, for the greater glory of God. Amen.

For children

Children, Ignatius was a soldier who wanted to be famous. But a cannonball hurt his leg and he had to stay in bed for a long time. He read books about Jesus and the saints, and he decided to give his life to God instead. He started a group of friends who wanted to do everything for God's glory. They were called Jesuits, and one of them, Francis Xavier, travelled all the way to India. Ignatius used to say: for the greater glory of God. Today let us do something very small, like tidying our room, and say: for the glory of God!

Prayer

O God, who raised up Saint Ignatius of Loyola in your Church to further the greater glory of your name, grant that by his help we may imitate him in fighting the good fight on earth and merit to receive with him a crown in heaven. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray with Ignatius: Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding and my entire will. Give me only your love and your grace, and I am rich enough. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The first reading gives the law of the Jubilee. After seven times seven years, on the Day of Atonement, a trumpet sounds through the whole land, and the fiftieth year is holy: liberty is proclaimed, each family returns to its property, the land rests, and no one is to cheat a neighbour. In the Gospel, a different kind of power appears: Herod, who has imprisoned John the Baptist for telling him “It is not lawful for you to have her,” gives in to a dancing girl and her mother and has John beheaded. The jubilee is a year of release; Herod’s court is a place of fear and bondage, and John, in prison, is the one truly at liberty. The memorial of St Ignatius of Loyola, whose proper readings (1 Corinthians 10:31–11:1 and Luke 14:25–33) may be used instead, is kept today.

Leviticus 25:1, 8–17 · You shall return, every one of you, to your property

Setting.

Leviticus 25 is part of the Holiness Code. It begins with the sabbatical year, in which the land rests every seventh year (25:1–7), and then gives the law of the Jubilee: after seven sabbaths of years, forty-nine years, the fiftieth year is a year of release. The word “Sinai” in verse 1 recalls that these are laws given by God to Israel at the mountain.

The law rests on the conviction that the land belongs to God (25:23): Israel’s families are tenants and stewards, not absolute owners. If poverty forced a family to sell its land or itself into service, the jubilee returned them to their inheritance. Scholars debate how fully the jubilee was kept in ancient Israel, but the prophets and the Gospels hold it up as an ideal of what God wants.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the Lord speaks to Moses on Mount Sinai.
  • vv. 8–9: seven weeks of years; on the Day of Atonement the trumpet sounds throughout the land.
  • vv. 10–12: the fiftieth year is holy; liberty is proclaimed; each returns to his property and family; the land is not sown.
  • v. 13: “you shall return, every one of you, to your property”.
  • vv. 14–17: honest dealing: prices fixed by the number of harvests until the next jubilee; “you shall not cheat one another, but you shall fear your God”.

Key words.

  • yôbēl, jubilee: Originally the ram’s horn that was blown to announce the year. The Latin iubilaeum, “jubilee”, comes from it, though it later came to be associated with iubilare, “to rejoice”.
  • dĕrôr, liberty, release: The word for the freedom proclaimed in the year of jubilee, found again in Isaiah 61:1 and Jeremiah 34:8. Jesus quotes the Isaiah text in the synagogue of Nazareth (Luke 4:18).
  • tônû, you shall not wrong: From the verb yānâ, “to oppress, to wrong”, used in verses 14 and 17 (“you shall not cheat one another”). The jubilee law forbids it, and verse 17 grounds the prohibition in the fear of God.

Verse by verse.

“you shall proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants.” (25:10)

The liberty is for all the inhabitants of the land. These words were later carved on the Liberty Bell in Philadelphia, but their first meaning is the release of the poor and the return of families to their land.

“you shall return, every one of you, to your property and every one of you to your family.” (25:10)

Property and family go together. The jubilee guards both the means of life and the bond of kinship, so that no family is cut off for ever.

“you shall eat only what the field itself produces.” (25:12)

The year of rest is a year of trust. The land is not worked, and the people depend on what God gives without their labour.

“You shall not cheat one another, but you shall fear your God;” (25:17)

The law of the market is joined to the fear of God. Honest dealing is not only a matter of law but of reverence for the Lord who sees.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the jubilee in the light of Jesus’ first sermon at Nazareth, where he reads from Isaiah 61, “to proclaim release to the captives… to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor,” and says that it is fulfilled today (Luke 4:16–21). Since 1300 the Church has also celebrated Holy Years, as times of pilgrimage, conversion and mercy; Pope John Paul II linked them expressly with Leviticus 25 (Tertio Millennio Adveniente 11–13). The Catechism teaches the universal destination of goods: God gave the earth to the whole human race, and private property does not remove the claim of the poor on what is superfluous (CCC 2402–2403). The jubilee is a standing reminder that every possession is held in trust.

Matthew 14:1–12 · Herod sent and had John beheaded in the prison

Setting.

The account follows the rejection of Jesus at Nazareth. Herod “the ruler” is Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, who governed Galilee and Perea as tetrarch from 4 BC until AD 39. Matthew has just told that Jesus’ fame was spreading, and now Herod hears of it and fears that John the Baptist, whom he killed, has been raised.

The story is a flashback: Matthew tells how John came to die. Herod had married Herodias, the wife of his brother Philip; John said that this was not lawful, as the Law of Moses forbids a man to marry his brother’s wife while the brother is alive (Leviticus 18:16; 20:21). Matthew’s account is brief; the historian Josephus also tells of John’s death and says that Herod put him to death at the fortress of Machaerus.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Herod hears of Jesus and thinks he is John raised from the dead.
  • vv. 3–5: the flashback: John imprisoned for saying “It is not lawful”; Herod wants to kill him but fears the crowd.
  • vv. 6–11: Herod’s birthday, the dance, the oath, the request for John’s head, and the beheading.
  • v. 12: John’s disciples bury him and tell Jesus.

Key words.

  • tetrarchēs, ruler, tetrarch: Literally “ruler of a fourth part”. Matthew calls Herod “the ruler”, and in v. 9 “the king”, as he was called by courtesy though Rome had not given him the title.
  • horkos, oath: Herod binds himself by an oath, and the oath becomes his excuse. Jesus teaches elsewhere that his disciples should not swear at all (Matthew 5:33–37), and this story shows why.
  • pinax, platter: A flat dish for food. The head on a platter is the grim reversal of a banquet, where the guests are fed and a prophet is killed.

Verse by verse.

“It is not lawful for you to have her.” (14:4)

John is not afraid of a king. He speaks the truth about marriage and the Law and he pays for it. The same word “lawful” is used of the Sabbath and divorce elsewhere in Matthew.

“Though Herod wanted to put him to death, he feared the crowd,” (14:5)

Herod’s fear of the crowd restrains him for a while. Later, fear of the guests and of his own oath moves him in the other direction. A man who fears people and not God can be led anywhere.

“The king was grieved, yet out of regard for his oaths and for the guests, he commanded it to be given;” (14:9)

The most tragic line of the story: he is sorry, and he does it. Regret that does not turn into action is no protection.

“His disciples came and took the body and buried it; then they went and told Jesus.” (14:12)

The disciples of John give him a burial and take the news to Jesus. With this, John’s story passes into Jesus’ story: the forerunner’s death foreshadows his own (Matthew 17:12).

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours John the Baptist as the forerunner who prepared the way, and as a martyr for the truth: his death is kept on 29 August as the Passion of St John the Baptist. The Catechism teaches that martyrdom is the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473), and it presents John as the one who goes before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah” (CCC 523). His death is a sign for every Christian who speaks the truth where it is unwelcome. Herod’s story is the other side: one who knew John was a holy man (Mark 6:20) and still chose to protect his own position.

For the Latin reader

St Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556) was born into a Basque noble family. As a soldier he was wounded at Pamplona in 1521, and while he recovered at Loyola he read the lives of Christ and the saints, and his life turned. After a year of prayer at Manresa he wrote the first notes of the Spiritual Exercises, a month of guided prayer that has shaped countless lives. In 1540 Pope Paul III approved his Society of Jesus. Its motto, “for the greater glory of God”, is the thread of the proper readings: “whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31) and the cost of following Christ (Luke 14:25–33). He died in Rome on 31 July 1556 and was canonised in 1622.

For study and teaching

  • The jubilee returns each family to its land and each person to freedom. Is there a debt, a grievance or an old wrong that I can release this week?
  • Herod grieved, and yet obeyed his oath and his guests. When have I done something I knew to be wrong because of what others would think, and what would have helped me to stand firm?
