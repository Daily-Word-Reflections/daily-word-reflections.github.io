Word for the day

“I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” John 6:35

Reflection

Yesterday we saw Stephen accused; today we see him die. At the end of a long speech in which he retells the whole history of Israel, from Abraham to Solomon's Temple, Stephen speaks with the burning voice of the prophets: “You stiff-necked people, uncircumcised in heart and ears, you are forever opposing the Holy Spirit.” These are hard words, but they are the words Moses and the prophets themselves had used in calling their own people back to God (Exodus 33:5; Jeremiah 9:26). Stephen speaks as a son of Israel to his brothers, not as an outsider passing judgment on a nation, and the Church reads his words in that light (Nostra Aetate 4). His hearers are enraged and grind their teeth. But Stephen stands firm: “filled with the Holy Spirit, he gazed into heaven and saw the glory of God and Jesus standing at the right hand of God.” Elsewhere the risen Christ is seated at the Father's right hand; here he stands, as if rising to welcome his first witness, or standing as advocate for the one who is being condemned. They drag Stephen out of the city and stone him, and the witnesses lay their coats at the feet of a young man named Saul. Stephen's last two prayers are an echo of the cross: “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,” and then, on his knees, “Lord, do not hold this sin against them.” Luke ends gently: “When he had said this, he died”; the Greek says he “fell asleep.” The Psalm is the prayer Jesus himself made his own on the cross: “Into your hand I commit my spirit.” And the last line of the reading quietly opens the next chapter of the story: “And Saul approved of their killing him.” Stephen prayed for his killers, and one of them would become the Apostle of the nations.

In the Gospel the crowd that has eaten the loaves now asks Jesus for a sign: “What sign are you going to give us then, so that we may see it and believe you?” They remind him of the great sign of the desert: “Our ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, ‘He gave them bread from heaven to eat.’” Jesus corrects them gently: it was not Moses who gave that bread, “but it is my Father who gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is that which comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.” The crowd, like the Samaritan woman at the well asking for living water, says: “Sir, give us this bread always.” And Jesus answers with one of the great sayings of the Gospel: “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” The manna fed bodies for a day and spoiled by morning; Jesus is bread that gives the life of God and does not spoil. Today the Church also remembers St Martin I, pope from 649 to 655. He defended the truth, against the emperor's policy, that Christ has a true human will as well as his divine will, freely united to the will of the Father. For this he was arrested in Rome, taken to Constantinople, humiliated and condemned, and exiled to the Crimea, where he died in 655 of hunger and hardship. In his letters from exile he wrote of being short of bread. Deprived of the bread that perishes, he lived on the bread of life, and the Church honours him as a martyr.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we stand with Stephen at the moment of his death. Stones are flying, but he is looking somewhere else: he gazed into heaven and saw the glory of God and Jesus standing at the right hand of God (Acts 7:55). Jesus stands up for his witness. No one who suffers for him suffers alone.

Listen to Stephen's last prayers. Lord Jesus, receive my spirit. Then, on his knees: Lord, do not hold this sin against them (Acts 7:59–60). He has learned to pray from the cross. And his prayer was heard in a way no one expected: the young man holding the coats was Saul. St Augustine said, in substance, that if Stephen had not prayed, the Church would not have had Paul.

In the Gospel the people ask Jesus for a sign like the manna. Jesus says: it was not Moses but my Father who gives you the true bread from heaven. And then: I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry (John 6:35). The manna lasted one day. Jesus is the bread that gives the life of God for ever.

Today we also remember St Martin I, a pope who would not change the truth about Jesus to please an emperor. He died in exile, hungry for bread, but fed with the bread of life. Let us ask for their courage and their forgiving hearts, and receive with faith today the bread that never fails. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Stephen was hurt by angry people, but he did not hate them. He prayed: Jesus, forgive them. Jesus is happy when we forgive. And Jesus said: I am the bread of life. When we receive him in Holy Communion, he fills our hearts with his love so that we can forgive too.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, standing at the right hand of the Father, you received the spirit of your servant Stephen and heard his prayer for those who killed him. Give us his forgiving heart, the courage of St Martin your faithful pope, and an ever greater hunger for you, the true bread from heaven, who live and reign for ever and ever. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Acts reaches its first martyr, and the discourse at Capernaum goes on. Stephen, one of the Seven, whose arrest we heard of on Monday, ends his long speech before the council with hard prophetic words, sees heaven opened, and dies praying as Jesus prayed on the cross. In John the crowd asks for a sign like the manna, and Jesus answers that the true bread comes from his Father and that he himself is that bread. Psalm 31 gives Stephen’s last prayer its source: “Into your hands, O Lord, I commend my spirit”.

Acts 7:51–8:1a · Lord Jesus, receive my spirit

Setting.

Stephen was one of the Seven chosen to serve the tables of the Greek-speaking widows (6:1–6), a man “full of grace and power” who also preached (6:8). Accused of speaking against the Temple and the law (6:13–14), he gave before the council the longest speech in Acts (7:2–53), retelling Israel’s story from Abraham to the Temple of Solomon. Today’s reading begins as the speech reaches its sharp end and the hearing turns into a killing.

Stephen speaks as a Jew to fellow Jews, in the voice of the prophets who rebuked their own people. Luke does not describe a formal sentence: the scene ends in a rush of anger outside the city.

Structure.

  • vv. 51–53: Stephen’s rebuke: you resist the Spirit as your ancestors did.
  • vv. 54–56: their rage; his vision of the glory of God and of the Son of Man standing.
  • vv. 57–58: they drag him out and stone him; the witnesses lay their coats at the feet of Saul.
  • 7:59–8:1a: two prayers, and death; Saul approves.

Key words.

  • sklērotrachēloi, stiff-necked: The word God uses of Israel after the golden calf (Exodus 33:3, 5). Stephen takes it from the Torah itself: it is the language of a quarrel inside the family of the covenant, not an outsider’s insult.
  • hestōta, standing: Elsewhere the risen Christ is “seated” at the right hand of God (Psalm 110:1; Luke 22:69). Here he stands. Many interpreters see him rising to help and welcome his witness; others see him standing as witness and advocate in a court where Stephen is condemned.
  • ekoimēthē, he fell asleep: Luke’s word for Stephen’s death (NRSV “he died”). Early Christians often spoke of death as sleep, because they awaited the resurrection (1 Thessalonians 4:13–14). Our word “cemetery” comes from the same Greek family: a place of sleeping.

Verse by verse.

““You stiff-necked people, uncircumcised in heart and ears, you are forever opposing the Holy Spirit, just as your ancestors used to do.” (7:51)

Hard words, but taken from Scripture (Leviticus 26:41; Jeremiah 6:10; 9:26). They are spoken to the members of the council at one hearing. The Church reads them as a warning to every hearer who resists the Spirit, never as a charge against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

““Look,” he said, “I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God!”” (7:56)

This is the only place in Acts where Jesus is called the Son of Man. Stephen’s words echo what Jesus told the council at his own trial (Luke 22:69). What was promised there is seen here.

“While they were stoning Stephen, he prayed, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit.”” (7:59)

On the cross Jesus prayed to the Father, “into your hands I commend my spirit” (Luke 23:46, from Psalm 31). Stephen makes the same prayer to Jesus. Within a few years of Easter, a Christian dies praying to Jesus as to God.

““Lord, do not hold this sin against them.” When he had said this, he died.” (7:60)

Like Jesus in Luke 23:34, he prays for those who kill him. And he kneels, as Jesus knelt in the garden (Luke 22:41).

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours Stephen as the first martyr, the Protomartyr, and keeps his feast on 26 December, the day after Christmas: the Child born at Bethlehem is the Lord for whom his servant died. St Fulgentius of Ruspe, in a sermon the Church reads on that feast, says that the love which brought Christ down from heaven to earth raised Stephen from earth to heaven (in substance). The Catechism calls martyrdom “the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith” (CCC 2473) and teaches that Christian prayer reaches as far as the forgiveness of enemies (CCC 2844). St Augustine said that if Stephen had not prayed, the Church would not have had Paul (in substance). The young man at whose feet the coats were laid would soon be called.

John 6:30–35 · My Father gives you the true bread from heaven

Setting.

After the feeding of the five thousand (6:1–15) and the walking on the sea (6:16–21), the crowd finds Jesus again at Capernaum (6:24–25). He has told them to work for the food that endures for eternal life, and that the work of God is to believe in the one whom he has sent (6:27–29). Now they ask for proof. They have eaten bread in the wilderness; they want manna, the sign of Moses. John notes that the Passover, the feast of the Exodus, was near (6:4).

Structure.

  • vv. 30–31: the crowd asks for a sign and recalls the manna.
  • vv. 32–33: Jesus corrects them: not Moses but my Father; not only in the past but now (“gives”); the bread that comes down and gives life to the world.
  • v. 34: “Sir, give us this bread always.”
  • v. 35: “I am the bread of life.”

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: John’s word for the miracles of Jesus: deeds that point beyond themselves. The crowd has just seen one and asks for another. The sign they need is the person standing before them.
  • ho artos ho alēthinos, the true bread: In John alēthinos means real, the thing itself to which earlier gifts pointed: the true light (1:9), the true vine (15:1). The manna was a real gift of God; it was also a sign of a greater one.
  • egō eimi, I am: The first of the sayings in John where Jesus says “I am” with an image: bread, light, gate, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine. Behind the words many hear the name God revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14).

Verse by verse.

“Our ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, ‘He gave them bread from heaven to eat.’”” (6:31)

The quotation gathers Exodus 16:4, 15 and Psalm 78:24. A Jewish writing from about the same period hoped that the treasury of manna would come down again in the days of the Messiah (2 Baruch 29:8), and the crowd may share that hope.

“it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but it is my Father who gives you the true bread from heaven.” (6:32)

Two corrections in one sentence: the giver is God, not Moses, and the giving is now, not only long ago.

““Sir, give us this bread always.”” (6:34)

The Samaritan woman said almost the same about water (4:15). Both misunderstand at first, and both are led further.

““I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” (6:35)

Coming and believing are one movement here. Bread and drink together already point toward the meal Jesus will speak of later in the chapter.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that while the other Gospels and St Paul hand on the institution of the Eucharist, St John reports the words at Capernaum that prepare for it: Christ calls himself the bread of life, come down from heaven (CCC 1338). It also recalls that the manna in the desert always reminded Israel that it lives by the bread of the Word of God (CCC 1334). Many commentators see two movements in the discourse: in the first, Jesus is the bread received by faith, in his word and person; from verse 51 the language becomes plainly eucharistic. The Church does not set the two against each other. At every Mass we are fed from the one table of the Word of God and of the Body of Christ (Dei Verbum 21).

Questions people ask

Why does Stephen speak so harshly to his own people?

He speaks as an Israelite within Israel, using the words of Moses and the prophets, who also rebuked their own generation. His last words are a prayer for forgiveness. The Church teaches that the Jews are not to be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4), and that all sinners share the responsibility for the Passion (CCC 598).

For study and teaching

  • Stephen prayed for those who were killing him. Whom do I find it hardest to pray for, and can I name that person to the Lord today?
  • Like the crowd, do I ask God for signs when he has already given me himself in his word and in the Eucharist?
