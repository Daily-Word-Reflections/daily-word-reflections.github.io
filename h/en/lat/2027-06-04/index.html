Word for the day

“Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” John 19:34

Reflection

Today the Church contemplates the love of God made visible in a human heart: the Heart of Jesus, pierced on the Cross. The readings of Year B begin not on Calvary but in the house of a father. Through the prophet Hosea, in the eighth century before Christ, God speaks of Israel as a little child: “When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son.” Then come some of the most tender words in the Old Testament: “Yet it was I who taught Ephraim to walk, I took them up in my arms; but they did not know that I healed them. I led them with cords of human kindness, with bands of love. I was to them like those who lift infants to their cheeks. I bent down to them and fed them.” God is pictured as a parent stooping to a toddler, holding its hands as it learns to walk, lifting it to his cheek. The child grows up and walks away to other gods. Justice would demand punishment. But God's heart does something unexpected: “My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender. I will not execute my fierce anger … for I am God and no mortal, the Holy One in your midst, and I will not come in wrath.” The Hebrew says that God's heart is overturned within him, the same word used for the overthrow of Sodom. God's anger is overthrown by his own mercy. God is holy precisely because he does not react like a wounded human being. The responsorial song is from Isaiah: “Surely God is my salvation; I will trust, and will not be afraid … With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation.” In Latin that verse begins Haurietis aquas, and Pope Pius XII took it as the title of his great encyclical on the Sacred Heart in 1956.

St Paul, in prison, writes to the Ephesians in amazement at his own vocation: “Although I am the very least of all the saints, this grace was given to me to bring to the Gentiles the news of the boundless riches of Christ.” The plan of God, hidden for ages, is now revealed: all peoples are called into one Church. Then Paul kneels: “For this reason I bow my knees before the Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth takes its name.” His prayer for the Christians of Ephesus is a prayer for us on this feast: “that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, as you are being rooted and grounded in love. I pray that you may have the power to comprehend, with all the saints, what is the breadth and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, so that you may be filled with all the fullness of God.” The love of Christ has dimensions. Christians have long seen in them the shape of the Cross: its arms stretched wide to embrace all, its beam reaching down to the lowest and up to heaven. We can never measure that love, but we can know it, the way a child knows its mother's love, by being held in it.

The Gospel takes us to Golgotha on the afternoon of Good Friday. It is the day of Preparation before a great sabbath, and the Law of Moses did not allow a body to remain hanging overnight (Deuteronomy 21:23), so the authorities ask Pilate to have the legs of the crucified broken to hasten their death. The soldiers break the legs of the two others, “But when they came to Jesus and saw that he was already dead, they did not break his legs. Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” John, the beloved disciple who stood beneath the Cross with the Mother of Jesus, solemnly insists: “He who saw this has testified so that you also may believe.” He sees two Scriptures fulfilled. “None of his bones shall be broken”: Jesus is the true Passover lamb, whose bones were not to be broken (Exodus 12:46), dying at the hour when the lambs were slaughtered in the Temple. “They will look on the one whom they have pierced” (Zechariah 12:10): the whole world will look at this wound and be drawn to repentance and love. From the opened side flow blood and water. The Fathers saw in them the sacraments of the Church, the water of Baptism and the blood of the Eucharist, and they saw the Church herself born from the side of the new Adam, as Eve was formed from the side of the first Adam while he slept (Catechism 766). This is the Sacred Heart: not a sentimental picture, but the physical heart of the incarnate Son, pierced for us, the sign and the source of the love that Hosea foretold. Jesus said, “learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29). In the visions given to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial (1673–1675), he showed his Heart burning with love and asked for reparation, Holy Communion on the First Fridays, and this feast. Today the Church kneels, as Paul knelt, before the Father, and looks on the one whom we have pierced.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, in many of our homes there is a picture of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, with a small lamp burning before it. We have seen it so often that we may not notice it anymore. Today the Church asks us to look at it again, with new eyes, and to understand what it tells us: that God loves us with a heart, and that this Heart was pierced for us.

The first reading shows us God as a father, or a mother, with a little child. It was I who taught Ephraim to walk, I took them up in my arms … I was to them like those who lift infants to their cheeks. I bent down to them and fed them (Hosea 11:3–4). Think of a parent holding a baby's hands as it takes its first steps, or lifting it up to kiss its cheek. That is how God sees you. But the child grows up and runs away. And what does God say? Not, I will destroy you. He says: my heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender (Hosea 11:8). God's heart is overturned, not against us, but in our favour.

In the second reading St Paul kneels before the Father and prays that we may know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge (Ephesians 3:19). We cannot measure it. It is broad enough to embrace every person, long enough to last for ever, high enough to lift us to heaven, deep enough to reach the lowest sinner. We cannot measure it, but we can know it, the way a child knows its mother's love, by being held.

Then the Gospel takes us to the Cross. Jesus is already dead. A soldier pierces his side with a spear, and at once blood and water come out (John 19:34). John, who stood there with the Mother of Jesus, says: I saw it, and I tell you so that you may believe. From that wound the Church is born. The water reminds us of our Baptism; the blood, of the Eucharist. Every sacrament we receive flows from the open Heart of Jesus.

This Heart asks something of us. It asks for our love in return. When Jesus showed his Heart to St Margaret Mary, he complained gently that so many people treat his love with coldness and indifference. He asked for reparation: that we console him with our love, receive him in Holy Communion on the First Fridays, and spend time with him in adoration. He also said: learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart (Matthew 11:29). Our homes need hearts like that.

Today is also the day of prayer for priests. A priest is a gift from the Heart of Jesus. Pray for your parish priest today. And tonight, gather your family before the picture of the Sacred Heart, light the lamp, and say together: Sacred Heart of Jesus, we trust in you. Let the lamp remind you that his love never goes out. Amen.

For children

Children, have you seen the picture of Jesus pointing to his heart, with a fire and a crown of thorns around it? That is the Sacred Heart. It tells us that Jesus loves us with a real heart, just like ours, but bigger than the whole world! When Jesus died on the cross, a soldier made a hole in his side, and blood and water came out. Jesus gave everything for us.

Today, when you see the Sacred Heart picture at home, stop and say: Jesus, I love you too! And pray for your parish priest, because today is a special day to pray for priests.

Prayer

Grant, we pray, almighty God, that we, who glory in the Heart of your beloved Son and recall the wonders of his love for us, may be made worthy to receive an overflowing measure of grace from that fount of heavenly gifts. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Most Sacred Heart of Jesus, pierced for us, we adore you and we trust in you. You taught us to walk and lifted us to your cheek; you bent down and fed us. Forgive our coldness and ingratitude, and accept our love in reparation. Make our hearts like yours, gentle and humble. Bless our homes, our children, and all the priests of your Church. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Solemnity of the Most Sacred Heart of Jesus the readings of Year B lead us from the heart of God in the Old Testament to the pierced heart of Christ on the Cross. In Hosea, God speaks as a father who taught his little son to walk and cannot bear to give him up: “My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender.” St Paul kneels before the Father and prays that we may know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge. In the Gospel a soldier pierces the side of the dead Jesus, and blood and water flow out.

The response is the song of Isaiah 12: “With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation.” Pope Pius XII took the first words of this verse in Latin, Haurietis aquas, as the title of his letter on devotion to the Sacred Heart (1956). The solemnity is kept on the Friday after the Second Sunday after Pentecost, which this year falls on 4 June.

Hosea 11:1, 3–4, 8c–9 · How can I give you up?

Setting.

Hosea preached in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century before Christ, in the years before it fell to Assyria in 722. His own painful marriage became a parable of God’s love for an unfaithful people (chapters 1–3). Chapter 11 turns from the image of husband and wife to that of father and child.

The Lectionary leaves out the verses of judgment (vv. 2, 5–8b): Israel ran after the Baals, refused to return, and would go into exile. Our verses keep the two ends of the poem: the tender love of the beginning and God’s refusal to destroy at the end.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Israel as a child: “out of Egypt I called my son”.
  • vv. 3–4: God the parent: teaching to walk, carrying, healing, leading with cords of kindness, bending down to feed.
  • v. 8c: the turning point: God’s own heart recoils.
  • v. 9: “I am God and no mortal, the Holy One in your midst.”

Key words.

  • lēb, heart: In Hebrew the heart is not only feelings but the centre of thought, decision and love. When God says “my heart”, he speaks of his innermost self.
  • nehpak, recoils, is turned over: From the same root as the “overthrow” of Sodom and Gomorrah. Instead of overthrowing Israel like the cities of the plain, God’s own heart is turned over within him.
  • qādôš, the Holy One: God’s holiness is his being utterly other. Here it is surprising: precisely because he is God and not a man, he does not act in anger. His holiness shows itself as mercy.

Verse by verse.

“When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son.” (11:1)

The Exodus was an act of fatherly love. St Matthew sees this verse fulfilled when the child Jesus returns from Egypt (Matthew 2:15): the true Son relives the story of Israel.

“Yet it was I who taught Ephraim to walk, I took them up in my arms;” (11:3)

Ephraim is a name for the northern kingdom. God pictures himself holding the hands of a toddler. Few passages in the Old Testament are so tender.

“I led them with cords of human kindness, with bands of love.” (11:4)

Not the ropes used to drive animals, but the bonds of love that draw a child. Textual note: in the next line the Hebrew words for “infant” and “yoke” are written with the same consonants, so some translations read “like those who lift infants to their cheeks” and others “like those who ease the yoke on their jaws”. Both pictures speak of tender care.

“My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender.” (11:8)

Admah and Zeboiim, named just before, were destroyed with Sodom (Deuteronomy 29:23). God’s justice would allow it, but his heart will not.

“for I am God and no mortal, the Holy One in your midst,” (11:9)

A human father might give up a rebellious son. God does not. His love is more faithful than any human love.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Hosea on this day because the heart of God, revealed to the prophets, has taken flesh in Jesus. Pope Pius XII taught in Haurietis Aquas that the Old Testament already speaks of God’s love in such tender human terms, and that these images prepare for the human heart of the Word made flesh (in substance). God’s covenant love for Israel is not withdrawn (Romans 11:29). The Catechism teaches that Jesus has loved us all with a human heart (CCC 478).

Ephesians 3:8–12, 14–19 · The love of Christ that surpasses knowledge

Setting.

The Letter to the Ephesians presents itself as written by Paul in prison. Many scholars think it was written by a disciple of Paul after his death, in his name and spirit; others defend Paul’s own authorship. Either way the Church receives it as inspired Scripture. Its great theme is the mystery of God’s plan: to unite all things in Christ, and to bring Jews and Gentiles together into one body, the Church.

Our passage has two parts. First Paul speaks of the grace given to him, the least of all the saints, to preach the riches of Christ to the Gentiles. Then he kneels and prays for his readers.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–9: the grace given to Paul: to preach the boundless riches of Christ and to make known the hidden plan.
  • vv. 10–12: through the Church God’s wisdom is made known even to the heavenly powers; in Christ we have access to God.
  • vv. 14–15: Paul kneels before the Father, from whom every family takes its name.
  • vv. 16–19: his prayer: strength in the inner being, Christ dwelling in the heart, the knowledge of a love beyond knowledge, the fullness of God.

Key words.

  • anexichniastos, boundless: Literally “that cannot be tracked”, whose footprints cannot be traced to the end. The riches of Christ are beyond exploring.
  • patria, family: A play on words: patēr, father, and patria, family or clan. Every family in heaven and on earth takes its name from the Father.
  • plērōma, fullness: The goal of the prayer is that we be filled with all the fullness of God: not a little of God, but God himself living in us.

Verse by verse.

“this grace was given to me to bring to the Gentiles the news of the boundless riches of Christ,” (3:8)

Paul, who once persecuted the Church, calls himself the very least of the saints. Grace, not his merit, made him an apostle.

“in whom we have access to God in boldness and confidence through faith in him.” (3:12)

Through Christ we may approach God not as frightened strangers but as children who speak openly to their Father.

“For this reason I bow my knees before the Father,” (3:14)

Jews usually prayed standing. Kneeling shows special humility and urgency.

“that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, as you are being rooted and grounded in love.” (3:17)

Two images: a plant rooted in soil and a house built on a foundation. Love is both the soil and the foundation.

“and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge,” (3:19)

A deliberate paradox: we are to know what is beyond knowing. This knowledge is not information but experience, the knowledge of the heart.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has seen in “the breadth and length and height and depth” the dimensions of the Cross, on which Christ’s love stretched out to the whole world; St Augustine read the verse in this way (in substance). Pope Francis, in his letter on the love of the heart of Christ, Dilexit Nos (2024), invited the Church to rediscover this love that surpasses knowledge, and to let it reach the wounded heart of the world.

John 19:31–37 · Blood and water from his side

Setting.

Jesus has just died on the cross with the words “It is finished” (19:30). It is Friday afternoon, the day of Preparation, and that evening a solemn sabbath begins; in John’s Gospel it is also the eve of the Passover. The Law said that a hanged body must not remain overnight (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). So the authorities ask Pilate to have the legs of the crucified men broken, which hastened death, and the bodies taken down.

Background. When John speaks of “the Jews” in this passage, he means the religious authorities who were dealing with Pilate, not the Jewish people as a whole. Jesus, his mother, the disciples and the evangelist were themselves Jews. The Church firmly teaches that the death of Christ cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

Structure.

  • v. 31: the request to break the legs and remove the bodies before the sabbath.
  • vv. 32–33: the soldiers break the legs of the two others, but Jesus is already dead.
  • v. 34: a soldier pierces his side; blood and water come out.
  • v. 35: the eyewitness testifies so that you may believe.
  • vv. 36–37: two Scriptures fulfilled: “None of his bones shall be broken”; “They will look on the one whom they have pierced.”

Key words.

  • pleura, side: The same word is used in the Greek Old Testament for the rib taken from Adam’s side to form Eve (Genesis 2:21–22). The Fathers saw the Church, the new Eve, formed from the side of the new Adam.
  • enyxen, pierced: The Greek word means to prick or pierce. St Augustine, reading the Latin “opened”, said that a door of life was opened there, from which the sacraments of the Church flowed (in substance, Tractates on John 120).
  • martyria, testimony: The eyewitness, understood by the Church as the beloved disciple, stresses that he saw this. It is not a legend but a witness given so that we may believe.

Verse by verse.

“But when they came to Jesus and saw that he was already dead, they did not break his legs.” (19:33)

Jesus has given up his spirit willingly. His death is real and complete.

“Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” (19:34)

Blood and water: the sign of a real human death, and, for the evangelist, a sign of the life that flows from it. Jesus had promised rivers of living water from his heart (7:37–39).

“His testimony is true, and he knows that he tells the truth.” (19:35)

The evangelist turns directly to the reader: “so that you also may believe”. This scene is at the heart of John’s witness.

“None of his bones shall be broken.” (19:36)

The rule for the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12), and also the psalm of the righteous one whom God protects (Psalm 34:20). Jesus is the true Passover Lamb.

“They will look on the one whom they have pierced.” (19:37)

From Zechariah 12:10, where the people mourn over one they pierced, and a spirit of compassion is poured out. To look on the Pierced One is to begin to be converted.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the blood and water that flowed from the pierced side of the crucified Jesus are types of Baptism and the Eucharist, the sacraments of new life (CCC 1225), and that the Church was born from the pierced heart of Christ, as Eve was formed from the side of the sleeping Adam (CCC 766, quoting St Ambrose in substance). St John Chrysostom told the newly baptised that the water and the blood are symbols of Baptism and the Eucharist, and that from these the Church is born (in substance, Catecheses 3).

Devotion to the Sacred Heart is not devotion to an organ apart from the person. The heart of Jesus is the sign of the whole love of the Son of God, divine and human, for the Father and for us. The Catechism says that the Sacred Heart of Jesus, pierced by our sins and for our salvation, is the chief sign and symbol of that love with which the divine Redeemer continually loves the eternal Father and all human beings without exception (CCC 478, in substance).

Questions people ask

Why do we honour the heart of Jesus? Is it not enough to love Jesus himself?

We do honour Jesus himself. The heart is the biblical sign of the person’s innermost self and love. To honour the Sacred Heart is to worship the person of the Word made flesh, under the sign of his human heart, pierced for us. Because his human heart belongs to the divine person of the Son, the Church honours it with the worship given to Christ himself (compare CCC 478).

What are the promises connected with the Sacred Heart, such as the nine first Fridays?

They come from the private revelations given to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial in France between 1673 and 1675. The Church approved the devotion and recommends the practice of receiving Communion on the first Friday of the month in reparation and love. Private revelations do not add to the faith (CCC 67); the heart of the devotion is the love of Christ shown on the Cross and in the Eucharist, and our response of love and reparation.

Does Hosea’s tenderness mean that God does not judge sin?

No. The chapter itself speaks of exile as the result of Israel’s refusal to return (vv. 5–7). But judgment is not God’s last word. His mercy is greater than his anger, and he punishes in order to heal and bring back. On the Cross the justice and the mercy of God meet: the Son bears the sin of the world, and the heart pierced by sin pours out forgiveness.

For the Latin reader

Devotion to the heart of Jesus grew in the Middle Ages among saints such as St Bernard, St Gertrude and St Mechtilde. St John Eudes composed the first liturgical texts for the feast in the seventeenth century, and after the revelations to St Margaret Mary Alacoque, with the support of St Claude de la Colombière, the devotion spread throughout the Church. Blessed Pius IX extended the feast to the whole Latin Church in 1856, and Pope Leo XIII consecrated the human race to the Sacred Heart in 1899. In many Catholic homes in India a picture of the Sacred Heart has a place of honour, with a lamp burning before it, and families renew the enthronement of the Sacred Heart in the home. This day is also kept as a day of prayer for the holiness of priests, as Pope St John Paul II asked.

For study and teaching

  • Hosea pictures God teaching a child to walk. When have I felt God carrying me or bending down to me?
  • Paul prays that Christ may dwell in our hearts through faith. What would need to change in my heart for him to be at home there?
  • The evangelist says, “They will look on the one whom they have pierced.” How can I look at a crucifix today with new eyes?
  • How can our family honour the Sacred Heart in our home, in prayer, and in reparation for the wounds of the world?
