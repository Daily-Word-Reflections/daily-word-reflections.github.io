Word for the day

“As he went ashore, he saw a great crowd; and he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd; and he began to teach them many things.” Mark 6:34

Reflection

Today's readings are about shepherds, and they begin with a thunderclap. The prophet Jeremiah speaks around the year 600 before Christ, when Judah's kings and leaders were failing the people, and the Lord says through him: “Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture!” The accusation is precise: “It is you who have scattered my flock, and have driven them away, and you have not attended to them.” The sheep belong to the Lord (“my flock,” “my pasture”); the shepherds are only stewards, and their failing is neglect: they have not “attended to” the people. Then God turns from judgment to promise, in three steps. First: “I myself will gather the remnant of my flock out of all the lands where I have driven them, and I will bring them back to their fold.” God himself is the first shepherd. Second: “I will raise up shepherds over them who will shepherd them, and they shall not fear any longer, or be dismayed, nor shall any be missing.” Good shepherds are a gift from God, and their sign is that no sheep is lost. Third: “The days are surely coming … when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king and deal wisely.” His name will be “The Lord is our righteousness.” Christians read this as a promise fulfilled in Jesus, the descendant of David and the Good Shepherd. The psalm is the best-loved shepherd prayer in the world: “The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures; he leads me beside still waters; he restores my soul.” It moves from green meadow to “the darkest valley” (“for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me”) to a table “in the presence of my enemies” and a cup that “overflows,” and to the house of the Lord “my whole life long.”

In the second reading St Paul tells the Christians of Ephesus, most of them from non-Jewish families, what the Shepherd has done for them. “You who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” “Far off” and “near” were the old words for Gentiles and Israel. Then he says the sentence at the centre of the letter: “For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Everyone in Ephesus knew about a dividing wall: in the Jerusalem Temple a low stone barrier separated the courtyard of the Gentiles from the inner courts, with a notice warning foreigners on pain of death not to go further. Paul says that Christ has taken down that wall, not by a plan or a treaty, but “in his flesh” and “through the cross.” The result is “one new humanity in place of the two, thus making peace.” This peace is not only between people; it is also with God: “through him both of us have access in one Spirit to the Father.” This is the unity of the Church: Jews and Gentiles, and in our parishes Malayalis and migrants, rich and poor, all brought near by one blood and one Spirit. The Church's unity is not our achievement but his gift, and our job is to keep from rebuilding the walls.

The Gospel has a very human opening. The apostles have just come back from their first mission (Mark 6:7–13), when Jesus had sent them out two by two, and “told him all that they had done and taught.” They are full of stories, and tired. Jesus does not say “well done, now go again.” He says: “Come away to a deserted place all by yourselves and rest a while.” Mark adds why: “many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat.” Here is a Lord who cares about the apostles' bodies and their need for food and quiet. Real ministry depends on rest and on prayer. But the plan fails: the crowds run along the shore and arrive before the boat. A shepherd might say, “They are ruining our retreat.” Jesus does the opposite: “As he went ashore, he saw a great crowd; and he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd; and he began to teach them many things.” The Greek for compassion, splagchnizomai, means to be moved in the gut. This is the same verb used of the Good Samaritan and of the father of the prodigal son. And what does he do for people who are like lost sheep? He teaches them. The first food that he gives is his word, and on the next page of Mark he will feed them with bread. In this one scene, Jeremiah's promise is fulfilled: the Lord himself comes to gather the scattered sheep.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, if you want to know what a shepherd is, imagine a man out on the hillside with a hundred sheep. He knows each by name and he knows which one limps. He walks ahead. At night he counts them, and if one is missing, he goes out into the dark to find it. That is what God says he is in our readings today. Jeremiah is angry at the shepherds who scattered and neglected the flock. But he does not leave it there: I myself will gather the remnant of my flock (Jeremiah 23:3). And in Jesus, God has come to do exactly that.

Psalm 23 gives us the Shepherd's face. He makes me lie down in green pastures, he leads me beside still waters, he restores my soul (Psalm 23:2–3). And even in the darkest valley, I fear no evil; for you are with me (Psalm 23:4). Whoever has sat beside a sickbed or a grave knows how much that line means. The Lord does not promise that there will be no valley. He promises that he will be in it with us.

In the second reading St Paul says: he is our peace; he has broken down the dividing wall (Ephesians 2:14). Think of the walls we build: between castes, between families over land, between rich and poor, between parties and communities. Christ has taken down the greatest wall of all, between Jew and Gentile, and made one new humanity. If he can do that, he can do it in our village. The question is whether we are willing to be wall-breakers, or wall-builders.

And then the Gospel. The apostles come back tired, and the first thing Jesus says is: come away and rest a while (Mark 6:31). What a tender Lord! He knows that a good shepherd who does not rest can no longer shepherd. In our month of Karkidakam, when the rain keeps us at home, our elders always said: rest, look after your body, be quiet. Let that be a chance to be with the Lord. But when the crowd is waiting, he does not complain. He had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd (Mark 6:34). That is the heart of Christ. Let us ask him for it, and let us pray for our shepherds, that they may have it too. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what a shepherd does? He looks after the sheep. He takes them to green grass and cool water. He counts them at night, and if one is lost he goes to find it. The Bible says: the Lord is my shepherd! Jesus is our Good Shepherd. One day his friends came back very tired, and Jesus said: come and rest! But a great crowd of people ran after them, because they needed Jesus so much. Jesus did not send them away. He felt very sorry for them, because they were like sheep with no shepherd, and he taught them. Who looks after you like a shepherd? Your mummy and daddy, your teacher, your priest! Let us say thank you to them today, and pray for them.

Prayer

Almighty God, show favour to your servants and mercifully increase the gifts of your grace, that, made fervent in hope, faith and charity, they may be ever watchful in keeping your commands. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, you saw the crowds and had compassion on them. Look on the scattered and the tired, and gather them in your fold. Raise up for your Church shepherds after your own heart, who attend to the sheep and lose none. You are our peace: take down the walls between peoples, castes and families. Lead us beside still waters, restore our souls, and let us dwell in your house our whole life long. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s readings are about the shepherd. In Jeremiah God speaks against the shepherds, the kings of Judah, who have scattered his flock, and promises to gather the remnant himself, to give them shepherds who will care for them, and to raise up a righteous Branch of David whose name is “The Lord is our righteousness.” In the Gospel Jesus sees a great crowd, like sheep without a shepherd, and is moved with compassion; he begins to teach them. The promise of Jeremiah is being fulfilled before the eyes of the apostles.

Psalm 23, “The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want,” is the people’s answer: the flock that is led to green pastures and still waters, that fears no evil in the darkest valley, and that dwells in the house of the Lord. The second reading, from Ephesians, shows what the Shepherd’s work achieves: he has made those who were far off and those who were near into one new people, and has given both access in one Spirit to the Father. Next Sunday the Lectionary turns from Mark to John 6 for five weeks; today’s Gospel is the introduction to the feeding of the five thousand that follows it.

Jeremiah 23:1–6 · I will raise up shepherds over them

Setting.

Jeremiah was a priest of Anathoth who was called as a young man in 627 before Christ and prophesied through the last decades of the kingdom of Judah until after the fall of Jerusalem in 587. Chapters 21–23 are a collection of sayings about the kings of Judah, who failed to lead the people in justice. In the ancient Near East a king was called the shepherd of his people, so to speak of “shepherds” was to speak of the rulers.

Background. The kings of Jeremiah’s day were Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin and Zedekiah. Under them the people were scattered, first by the exile of 597 and then by the destruction of Jerusalem in 587. This passage looks both back at the failure and forward to God’s own rescue.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: woe to the shepherds who scatter the flock; God will “attend” to their evil doings.
  • v. 3: God himself will gather the remnant from all the lands and bring them back.
  • v. 4: he will set up shepherds who will really shepherd them, and no one will be missing.
  • vv. 5–6: the promise of a righteous Branch of David, who will reign wisely; his name, “The Lord is our righteousness.”

Key words.

  • rōʿîm, shepherds: Kings, princes and leaders. A shepherd leads, feeds, protects and searches for the lost. The prophets measure rulers by these tasks (Ezekiel 34).
  • pāqad, to attend to, to visit: The same verb is used twice in v. 2: “you have not attended to them” and “I will attend to you for your evil doings”. The shepherds did not visit the flock, so God will visit them. The verb can mean care or judgment, depending on what is found.
  • ṣemaḥ ṣaddîq, a righteous Branch: The shoot that comes from a stump. After the fall of the royal line, God promises a new growth from the root of David (compare Isaiah 11:1).

Verse by verse.

“Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture!” (23:1)

The flock is God’s: “my pasture”, “my flock”. Those who lead have been given a trust and are answerable to the owner.

“you have not attended to them. So I will attend to you for your evil doings,” (23:2)

Neglect is a sin. The shepherds are not accused only of cruelty but of not caring at all.

“Then I myself will gather the remnant of my flock out of all the lands” (23:3)

God does not wait for better shepherds before saving his people. He acts himself, and brings them back to their fold, with the promise that they will be fruitful and multiply.

“I will raise up shepherds over them who will shepherd them, and they shall not fear any longer,” (23:4)

God does not abolish leadership. He promises leaders who truly lead, so that the flock lives without fear and no one is lost.

“when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch,” (23:5)

The hope of Israel is bound to the house of David. Jeremiah’s promise is the root of the later Jewish expectation of the Messiah, and Christians have read it as fulfilled in Jesus.

““The Lord is our righteousness.”” (23:6)

The Hebrew is YHWH ṣidqēnû. It is a wordplay on the name of Zedekiah, the last king, whose name means “my righteousness is the Lord”. The true king will live up to the name that the last king failed to honor.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this oracle as a promise of the Messiah, the true Shepherd from David’s line. The Catechism teaches that the Church is the sheepfold whose one door is Christ, and the flock of which God himself foretold that he would be the shepherd (CCC 754; Lumen Gentium 6). Jesus names himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). The Church then asks its pastors to share in his care: the Second Vatican Council says that bishops are to be shepherds who serve and do not rule for themselves (Lumen Gentium 27), and the Church prays that God will give his people shepherds after his own heart, as he promised through Jeremiah (3:15). St Augustine, in his sermon on the shepherds (Sermon 46), reminded the bishops of his time that the flock belongs to Christ and not to them (in substance).

Ephesians 2:13–18 · He is our peace

Setting.

The Letter to the Ephesians was written in the name of Paul, and the Church receives it as his. Many scholars think that it was composed by a disciple of Paul who wrote in his name soon after his death, and that it was meant as a circular letter for several churches in the region of Ephesus; this does not change its authority in the Church. Its first three chapters praise God’s plan: to unite all things in Christ.

Background. In chapter 2 the writer speaks to Gentile Christians, who once were “aliens from the commonwealth of Israel” (2:12), and says that in Christ they have been brought near. The picture is of a great Temple court, with a low wall that separated the court of the Gentiles from the holy places beyond it. Notices on that wall warned Gentiles not to go farther, on pain of death. Many commentators think this wall stands behind the image of the “dividing wall” in v. 14.

Structure.

  • v. 13: those who were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.
  • v. 14: Christ is our peace; he made both groups one and broke down the dividing wall.
  • v. 15: he abolished the law with its commandments and ordinances, to create one new humanity.
  • v. 16: he reconciled both groups to God in one body through the cross.
  • vv. 17–18: he proclaimed peace to those far and near; through him both have access in one Spirit to the Father.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: The Greek word for peace, with the Hebrew šālôm behind it: wholeness, harmony with God and with one another. Here it is a person: “he is our peace”.
  • mesotoichon, dividing wall: Literally “middle wall”. The writer does not say that Israel was a wall, but that the separation between Jew and Gentile was a wall that Christ has taken down.
  • prosagōgē, access: The word for being brought into the presence of a king, introduced at court. Through Christ, both Jew and Gentile are brought into the presence of the Father.

Verse by verse.

“you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” (2:13)

The Gentiles who were far have been brought near, not by their own effort, but by the blood of Christ. The language of near and far is that of the prophets (Isaiah 57:19).

“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall,” (2:14)

Peace is not first a treaty or a feeling but a person. The reconciliation was made in his flesh, in the body that was given on the cross.

“He has abolished the law with its commandments and ordinances,” (2:15)

This does not mean that the law of Moses was bad. The writer means that the observances that set Israel apart and divided Jew from Gentile no longer divide them in Christ. Paul himself says that the law is holy (Romans 7:12), and Jesus did not come to abolish it but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17).

“one new humanity in place of the two,” (2:15)

The new humanity is not the Gentile Church replacing Israel but a new people in which Jews and Gentiles share in one body. Paul says elsewhere that Gentiles are grafted into the olive tree of Israel (Romans 11:17–24).

“reconcile both groups to God in one body through the cross,” (2:16)

The reconciliation has two directions: of the two groups with each other, and of both with God. The cross is the place where hostility is put to death.

“through him both of us have access in one Spirit to the Father;” (2:18)

This verse is a short summary of the Trinity at work in our prayer: through the Son, in the Spirit, we come to the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s peace is the foundation of all true peace among peoples, and that it is the gift of the reconciling cross (CCC 2305). The Second Vatican Council quotes this passage in its teaching on the Jewish people: Christ, through his cross, reconciled Jews and Gentiles and made both one in himself (Nostra Aetate 4). The same document recalls that the Jews remain dear to God, who does not repent of the gifts he has made, and the Catechism teaches that the Old Covenant has never been revoked (CCC 121; 839). The Church sees in v. 18 the shape of all Christian prayer, and of the Mass itself, which is offered to the Father, through Christ, in the Holy Spirit.

Mark 6:30–34 · Like sheep without a shepherd

Setting.

The Twelve have just been sent out two by two to preach repentance, cast out demons and anoint the sick (6:7–13). Between their sending and their return Mark has placed the story of the death of John the Baptist (6:14–29), so that the reader feels the cost of the mission. Now the apostles come back. They are tired, and so is Jesus: the shadow of John’s death lies on them.

Background. The scene is by the Sea of Galilee. Jesus and the apostles cross by boat to a deserted place, probably on the north-east shore near Bethsaida. In the verses that follow the Lectionary’s reading (6:35–44) the feeding of the five thousand takes place there.

Structure.

  • v. 30: the apostles return and tell Jesus all they have done and taught.
  • v. 31: Jesus invites them to come away and rest; there are too many people for them even to eat.
  • vv. 32–33: they go by boat to a deserted place; the crowds see them and run ahead by land.
  • v. 34: Jesus goes ashore, sees the crowd, has compassion because they are like sheep without a shepherd, and begins to teach.

Key words.

  • apostoloi, apostles: “Those sent out”. Mark uses this title here at the moment when they come back from being sent. The Church first reports to the one who sent her.
  • erēmos topos, deserted place: In Mark the desert is a place to meet God (1:12–13; 1:35). It is also where Israel was shepherded by Moses, and so a fitting place for the Shepherd to feed his flock.
  • splanchnizomai, to have compassion: From the Greek word for the inner organs, the seat of deep feeling. In the Gospels it is used only of Jesus and of figures in his parables who show God’s mercy (Mark 1:41; 8:2; Luke 15:20). His compassion is not a passing sympathy but moves him to act.

Verse by verse.

“told him all that they had done and taught.” (6:30)

The apostles bring their work back to Jesus. In pastoral work, prayer and report to the Lord go together with work for the people.

““Come away to a deserted place all by yourselves and rest a while.”” (6:31)

Jesus does not ask the apostles to work without rest. He gives rest because he loves them; and he wants them near him. Rest in his company is part of ministry.

“they hurried there on foot from all the towns and arrived ahead of them.” (6:33)

The crowd’s hunger was greater than the apostles’ tiredness. Plans for rest met people’s need; Jesus lets the need change his plan.

“he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd;” (6:34)

The phrase recalls Moses’ prayer in Numbers 27:17, when he asked God to give the people a leader so that they would not be like sheep without a shepherd. God named Joshua, whose name in Greek is Jesus.

“and he began to teach them many things.” (6:34)

The first gift of the shepherd is the word. Only afterwards does he feed them with bread (6:35–44). Word and bread belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this scene the figure of her own life: the apostles are sent, return to the Lord, rest in him, and are drawn again into the needs of the crowd. The Catechism teaches that the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she venerates the Body of the Lord, and that at Mass she receives from the one table the bread of life from both the word of God and the Body of Christ (CCC 103; Dei Verbum 21). The Church also offers the Lord’s Day as a time for rest and for works of mercy (CCC 2184–2186). Pope St John Paul II took the title of his letter on the priesthood from Jeremiah’s promise, “I will give you shepherds after my own heart” (Pastores Dabo Vobis, 1992, quoting Jeremiah 3:15).

Questions people ask

Does “he has abolished the law” mean that the Old Testament is no longer important?

No. The Church teaches that the Old Testament is the word of God, and that the covenant God made with Israel has never been revoked (CCC 121; Romans 11:29). In Ephesians, what ends is the law as a barrier between Jews and Gentiles: the observances that marked Israel as God’s own people no longer divide those who belong to Christ. Jesus said that he did not come to abolish the law but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17), and the Church still reads the Ten Commandments and the whole Old Testament as the word of God for us.

Is it right to rest when so many people need help?

Jesus himself said, “Come away… and rest a while.” Even when the crowd was waiting, he knew that those who serve need rest, prayer and friendship with him. Rest is not selfish; it makes love last. The Church’s teaching about the Lord’s Day, as a day of worship, rest and charity, is meant to protect this balance (CCC 2184–2186). At the same time Jesus did not send the crowd away; his compassion led him to teach and to feed them.

What should we do when those who lead the Church fail?

Jeremiah and Jesus both show that God sees the failures of shepherds and that the flock is his, not theirs. The Church asks her pastors to serve and to be accountable; the people of God are asked to pray for them, to speak the truth with love, and to take their own part in the Church’s mission. We trust, above all, not in any person but in Christ, the Good Shepherd, who promises that he will not leave his flock (John 10:11–16).

For the Latin reader

In the Latin Church the Sundays of Ordinary Time in Year B follow Mark’s Gospel; from next Sunday the Lectionary reads John 6, the Bread of Life discourse, for five weeks, and today’s verses prepare for it. The image of the shepherd is also close to the Church’s liturgy: the bishop carries a crosier, the shepherd’s staff, as the sign of his ministry, and the Church prays at every Mass for the Pope and the bishop by name. In Kerala many families pray for their parish priests and for vocations. Today’s readings invite the faithful to take part in that prayer and in the care of one another, since all share in the shepherd’s work of looking after those who are tired, lost or alone.

For study and teaching

  • Jeremiah says that God will attend to those who did not attend to the flock. Who in my family, parish or work depends on me to “attend” to them?
  • Jesus invites his tired apostles to rest with him. What would real rest “with Jesus” look like for me this week?
  • Ephesians says that Christ is our peace and has broken down the dividing wall. What wall in my family, parish or village could I help take down?
  • Psalm 23 says, “you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me.” When have I known the Shepherd’s presence in a dark valley?
