Word for the day

“As he went ashore, he saw a great crowd; and he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd; and he began to teach them many things.” Mark 6:34

Reflection

Today's readings are about shepherds, and they begin with a thunderclap. The prophet Jeremiah speaks around the year 600 before Christ, when Judah's kings and leaders were failing the people, and the Lord says through him: “Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture!” The accusation is precise: “It is you who have scattered my flock, and have driven them away, and you have not attended to them.” The sheep belong to the Lord (“my flock,” “my pasture”); the shepherds are only stewards, and their failing is neglect: they have not “attended to” the people. Then God turns from judgment to promise, in three steps. First: “I myself will gather the remnant of my flock out of all the lands where I have driven them, and I will bring them back to their fold.” God himself is the first shepherd. Second: “I will raise up shepherds over them who will shepherd them, and they shall not fear any longer, or be dismayed, nor shall any be missing.” Good shepherds are a gift from God, and their sign is that no sheep is lost. Third: “The days are surely coming … when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king and deal wisely.” His name will be “The Lord is our righteousness.” Christians read this as a promise fulfilled in Jesus, the descendant of David and the Good Shepherd. The psalm is the best-loved shepherd prayer in the world: “The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures; he leads me beside still waters; he restores my soul.” It moves from green meadow to “the darkest valley” (“for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me”) to a table “in the presence of my enemies” and a cup that “overflows,” and to the house of the Lord “my whole life long.”

In the second reading St Paul tells the Christians of Ephesus, most of them from non-Jewish families, what the Shepherd has done for them. “You who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” “Far off” and “near” were the old words for Gentiles and Israel. Then he says the sentence at the centre of the letter: “For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Everyone in Ephesus knew about a dividing wall: in the Jerusalem Temple a low stone barrier separated the courtyard of the Gentiles from the inner courts, with a notice warning foreigners on pain of death not to go further. Paul says that Christ has taken down that wall, not by a plan or a treaty, but “in his flesh” and “through the cross.” The result is “one new humanity in place of the two, thus making peace.” This peace is not only between people; it is also with God: “through him both of us have access in one Spirit to the Father.” This is the unity of the Church: Jews and Gentiles, and in our parishes Malayalis and migrants, rich and poor, all brought near by one blood and one Spirit. The Church's unity is not our achievement but his gift, and our job is to keep from rebuilding the walls.

The Gospel has a very human opening. The apostles have just come back from their first mission (Mark 6:7–13), when Jesus had sent them out two by two, and “told him all that they had done and taught.” They are full of stories, and tired. Jesus does not say “well done, now go again.” He says: “Come away to a deserted place all by yourselves and rest a while.” Mark adds why: “many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat.” Here is a Lord who cares about the apostles' bodies and their need for food and quiet. Real ministry depends on rest and on prayer. But the plan fails: the crowds run along the shore and arrive before the boat. A shepherd might say, “They are ruining our retreat.” Jesus does the opposite: “As he went ashore, he saw a great crowd; and he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd; and he began to teach them many things.” The Greek for compassion, splagchnizomai, means to be moved in the gut. This is the same verb used of the Good Samaritan and of the father of the prodigal son. And what does he do for people who are like lost sheep? He teaches them. The first food that he gives is his word, and on the next page of Mark he will feed them with bread. In this one scene, Jeremiah's promise is fulfilled: the Lord himself comes to gather the scattered sheep.

Homily outline

Theme: “He had compassion for them.”

The Lord who gathers the scattered flock, the Shepherd who walks with us in the dark valley, the peace that breaks down walls, and the compassion of Jesus who rests his tired friends and then teaches the crowd.

1. Woe to the shepherds

  • Neglect and scattering.
  • The sheep are the Lord's.
  • “I myself will gather.”
“Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture! says the Lord.” Jeremiah 23:1
“to tend the flock of God that is in your charge, exercising the oversight, not under compulsion but willingly, as God would have you do it.” 1 Peter 5:2

Apply: Pray by name each day this week for your parish priest and bishop, and ask yourself whom you have been entrusted with at home or at work.

2. The Lord is my shepherd

  • Green pastures and still waters.
  • The darkest valley, and you are with me.
  • A table prepared; my cup overflows.
“The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures; he leads me beside still waters; he restores my soul.” Psalm 23:1–3
““I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” John 10:11

Apply: Pray Psalm 23 slowly each evening this week, and add to it one name of a person for whom you want the Shepherd's care.

3. He is our peace

  • Far off and near.
  • The wall of the Temple.
  • One new humanity, one Spirit.
“Peace, peace, to the far and the near, says the Lord; and I will heal them.” Isaiah 57:19
“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Ephesians 2:14

Apply: Do one thing this week to cross a wall: greet someone from another community, call a relative with whom you are on bad terms, or share a meal.

4. Come away and rest a while

  • Tired and full of stories.
  • No leisure even to eat.
  • The Lord who cares for our bodies.
“It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives sleep to his beloved.” Psalm 127:2
“He said to them, “Come away to a deserted place all by yourselves and rest a while.” For many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat.” Mark 6:31

Apply: Choose one hour this week for real rest with the Lord, with no phone, and receive it as a gift.

5. Compassion

  • Like sheep without a shepherd.
  • Moved in the gut.
  • First he teaches, then he feeds.
“I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, and I will make them lie down, says the Lord God. I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak.” Ezekiel 34:15–16
“As he went ashore, he saw a great crowd; and he had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd; and he began to teach them many things.” Mark 6:34

Apply: Notice one person this week who seems lost or tired, and give them time, attention and a kind word.

God promises to gather his scattered flock and raise up good shepherds; in Jesus he comes as the Good Shepherd and is our peace.

Jesus rests his tired friends and then, moved with compassion, teaches the crowd: the heart of the Shepherd is the model of ours.

Take-home resolution: Pray daily for your shepherds, take one hour of rest with the Lord, and cross one wall this week.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, if you want to know what a shepherd is, imagine a man out on the hillside with a hundred sheep. He knows each by name and he knows which one limps. He walks ahead. At night he counts them, and if one is missing, he goes out into the dark to find it. That is what God says he is in our readings today. Jeremiah is angry at the shepherds who scattered and neglected the flock. But he does not leave it there: I myself will gather the remnant of my flock (Jeremiah 23:3). And in Jesus, God has come to do exactly that.

Psalm 23 gives us the Shepherd's face. He makes me lie down in green pastures, he leads me beside still waters, he restores my soul (Psalm 23:2–3). And even in the darkest valley, I fear no evil; for you are with me (Psalm 23:4). Whoever has sat beside a sickbed or a grave knows how much that line means. The Lord does not promise that there will be no valley. He promises that he will be in it with us.

In the second reading St Paul says: he is our peace; he has broken down the dividing wall (Ephesians 2:14). Think of the walls we build: between castes, between families over land, between rich and poor, between parties and communities. Christ has taken down the greatest wall of all, between Jew and Gentile, and made one new humanity. If he can do that, he can do it in our village. The question is whether we are willing to be wall-breakers, or wall-builders.

And then the Gospel. The apostles come back tired, and the first thing Jesus says is: come away and rest a while (Mark 6:31). What a tender Lord! He knows that a good shepherd who does not rest can no longer shepherd. In our month of Karkidakam, when the rain keeps us at home, our elders always said: rest, look after your body, be quiet. Let that be a chance to be with the Lord. But when the crowd is waiting, he does not complain. He had compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd (Mark 6:34). That is the heart of Christ. Let us ask him for it, and let us pray for our shepherds, that they may have it too. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what a shepherd does? He looks after the sheep. He takes them to green grass and cool water. He counts them at night, and if one is lost he goes to find it. The Bible says: the Lord is my shepherd! Jesus is our Good Shepherd. One day his friends came back very tired, and Jesus said: come and rest! But a great crowd of people ran after them, because they needed Jesus so much. Jesus did not send them away. He felt very sorry for them, because they were like sheep with no shepherd, and he taught them. Who looks after you like a shepherd? Your mummy and daddy, your teacher, your priest! Let us say thank you to them today, and pray for them.

Prayer

Almighty God, show favour to your servants and mercifully increase the gifts of your grace, that, made fervent in hope, faith and charity, they may be ever watchful in keeping your commands. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, you saw the crowds and had compassion on them. Look on the scattered and the tired, and gather them in your fold. Raise up for your Church shepherds after your own heart, who attend to the sheep and lose none. You are our peace: take down the walls between peoples, castes and families. Lead us beside still waters, restore our souls, and let us dwell in your house our whole life long. Amen.
