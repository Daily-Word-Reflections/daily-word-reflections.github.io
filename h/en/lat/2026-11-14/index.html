Word for the day

“Then Jesus told them a parable about their need to pray always and not to lose heart.” Luke 18:1

Reflection

Luke tells us the point before the parable: we need “to pray always and not to lose heart”. A widow, the most powerless person in that society, keeps coming to an unjust judge until he gives in just to be rid of her. If even such a judge yields to persistence, how much more will God, who is not unjust but a loving Father, “grant justice to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night”? Then the haunting question: “when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”

The Third Letter of John praises Gaius for welcoming travelling missionaries, “even though they are strangers to you”. By supporting them, he says, “we may become co-workers with the truth”. Persistent prayer and generous hospitality go together: those who pray for the Kingdom also open their homes and purses to those who spread it.

Full homily

Dear friends, Luke tells us the point before the parable: we need to pray always and not to lose heart (Luke 18:1). A widow, the most powerless person in that society, keeps coming to an unjust judge until he gives her justice just to be rid of her.

If even such a judge gives in to persistence, how much more will God, who is not unjust but a loving Father, grant justice to his chosen ones who cry to him day and night (Luke 18:7)? God is not reluctant. But persistent prayer changes us: it keeps our hope alive and our hearts turned to him.

Then Jesus asks a haunting question: when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth (Luke 18:8)? Faith is not only believing once; it is continuing to pray when the answer is slow. St John, in his third letter, praises Gaius for supporting the missionaries and so becoming a co-worker with the truth (3 John 8).

Choose one intention today, for a person, a healing, a reconciliation, and pray for it every day until Advent begins. Do not lose heart. The Father hears. Amen.

For children

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Elder praises Gaius for welcoming travelling missionaries, so that “we may become co-workers with the truth”. Jesus tells the parable of a widow who keeps asking a judge for justice, to teach his disciples “to pray always and not to lose heart”. Hospitality and prayer are both ways of persevering.

3 John 5–8 · Co-workers with the truth

Setting.

The third letter of John is a personal note from “the elder” to Gaius. Travelling preachers moved from Church to Church and depended on local Christians for food and lodging. Gaius had welcomed them; a leader called Diotrephes had refused them (vv. 9–10).

Structure.

  • vv. 5–6: Gaius has acted faithfully towards the brothers, even strangers; send them on in a way worthy of God.
  • vv. 7–8: they went out for the sake of the Name and took nothing from unbelievers; we ought to support them.

Key words.

  • hyper tou onomatos, for the sake of the Name: The Greek has simply “for the Name”, meaning the name of Jesus. The NRSV says “for the sake of Christ”; the POC keeps “for his Name”.
  • synergoi tē alētheia, co-workers with the truth: Those who receive missionaries share in their work. Hospitality is a form of mission.

Verse by verse.

“Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do for the friends, even though they are strangers to you” (1:5)
“Therefore we ought to support such people, so that we may become co-workers with the truth.” (1:8)

How the Church reads it.

The early Church organised such hospitality carefully; the Didache, an early Christian handbook, gives rules for receiving travelling apostles and prophets (in substance). Today the same work goes on when parishes support missionaries, seminarians and those who preach the Gospel far from home.

Luke 18:1–8 · Pray always and do not lose heart

Setting.

This parable follows Jesus’ words about the day of the Son of Man (17:22–37). While the disciples wait for his coming, they must pray. Luke states the point at the start: “their need to pray always and not to lose heart”.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the purpose of the parable.
  • vv. 2–5: a judge who fears no one, and a widow who keeps coming.
  • vv. 6–8a: the lesson: if even this judge gives in, how much more will God grant justice to his chosen ones.
  • v. 8b: the question: will the Son of Man find faith on earth?

Key words.

  • enkakein, to lose heart: To grow weary and give up. Prayer is the opposite of giving up.
  • hypōpiazein, to wear out: Literally a boxing word, to strike under the eye. The judge jokes that the widow will “give him a black eye” by her persistence.
  • ekdikein, to grant justice: To vindicate, to defend someone’s rights. Widows had no one to defend them; God is their defender (Psalm 68:5).

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus told them a parable about their need to pray always and not to lose heart.” (18:1)
“And yet, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?” (18:8)

The parable ends with a question for us. The danger is not that God will fail to answer, but that we will stop asking.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names this among the parables of prayer that Luke records, together with the friend at midnight and the Pharisee and the tax collector (CCC 2613). It also teaches that we are to “pray constantly” (CCC 2742), because prayer is the life of the new heart (CCC 2697).

Questions people ask

Is God like the unjust judge?

No. The parable argues from the lesser to the greater. If even an unjust judge gives way to persistence, how much more will a loving Father hear his children.

For study and teaching

  • Whom could I welcome or support, so as to become a co-worker with the truth?
  • In what prayer have I lost heart? What would it mean to keep coming, like the widow?
