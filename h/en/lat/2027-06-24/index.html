Word for the day

“All who heard them pondered them and said, “What then will this child become?” For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.” Luke 1:66

Reflection

The Church usually celebrates the saints on the day of their death, which she calls their birthday into heaven. Only three births are kept in the calendar: the birth of the Lord at Christmas, the birth of his Mother on 8 September, and today, the birth of John the Baptist. Why John? Because, as the angel told Zechariah, “even before his birth he will be filled with the Holy Spirit” (Luke 1:15). When Mary, carrying the Son of God, came to the house of Elizabeth, the child leapt for joy in his mother's womb (Luke 1:41–44). The Church's tradition has seen in that moment a sanctification of John before his birth; he was born already rejoicing in the Lord. The date itself preaches. The angel said that Elizabeth was in her sixth month when Mary conceived (Luke 1:36), so John's birth is kept six months before Christmas, close to the June solstice. St Augustine noticed that John is born when the days begin to grow shorter and Christ when they begin to grow longer, and he heard in it John's own words: “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30). The first reading is the second Song of the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah, and it fits John strikingly: “The LORD called me before I was born, while I was in my mother's womb he named me. He made my mouth like a sharp sword … he made me a polished arrow.” The Servant knows discouragement too: “I have labored in vain, I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity.” But God answers with a mission wider than Israel: “I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” The Servant is fulfilled in Christ, the light of the nations; John, called from the womb and named by God, is the polished arrow sent ahead of him.

Luke's Gospel tells the birth with the joy of a village. “Now the time came for Elizabeth to give birth, and she bore a son. Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” Elizabeth had carried the shame of being childless for many years; now the whole hill country of Judea shares her joy. On the eighth day comes the circumcision, when the child receives his name, and the relatives have already decided: he will be Zechariah, like his father. Elizabeth says, “No; he is to be called John.” They protest: “None of your relatives has this name.” They turn to the father, who has been unable to speak since the day he doubted the angel in the Temple. Zechariah asks for a writing tablet and writes four words: “His name is John.” The name in Hebrew, Yohanan, means “the Lord has shown favour.” This child does not belong to the family's plans but to God's grace. And immediately, Luke says, Zechariah's mouth was opened and he began to speak, praising God. The silence of doubt ends in praise. The song he sings, the Benedictus, “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favorably on his people and redeemed them,” is prayed by the whole Church every morning at Lauds. Then Luke records the question of all who heard: “What then will this child become?” For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him. The Gospel closes with a single verse that covers some thirty years: “The child grew and became strong in spirit, and he was in the wilderness until the day he appeared publicly to Israel.” Luke tells us that Mary stayed with Elizabeth about three months (Luke 1:56); many have pictured the Mother of the Lord still in that house to see the child born, though the Gospel does not say so. What is certain is that the two mothers, the two children and the two miracles belong together: the barren woman gives birth, and the Virgin conceives the Son of God.

The Psalm lets us pray John's story as our own: “For it was you who formed my inward parts; you knit me together in my mother's womb. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made.” Every child in the womb is known and loved by God before anyone else sees his face; the feast of John's birth is a feast of the dignity of every human life from conception. In the second reading, St Paul, preaching in the synagogue of Pisidian Antioch, tells the story of Israel from the kings to Christ and gives John a place of honour: “before his coming John had already proclaimed a baptism of repentance to all the people of Israel.” And he quotes John's own words of humility: “What do you suppose that I am? I am not he. No, but one is coming after me; I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.” Untying sandals was the work of the lowest servant. The greatest of the prophets, of whom Jesus said that “among those born of women no one has arisen greater” (Matthew 11:11), measured his greatness by his littleness before Christ. St Augustine put it beautifully (in substance): John is the voice, Christ is the Word. The voice is heard for a moment and passes; the Word remains for ever. So today we pray for parents and for children not yet born, for all who are waiting for a child as Elizabeth and Zechariah waited, and for ourselves, that like John we may point away from ourselves to the Lamb of God.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today is a birthday. The Church usually remembers saints on the day they died, their birthday into heaven. But today, as on Christmas and on Our Lady's birthday, we celebrate a birth: the birth of John the Baptist. Even before he was born, John was filled with the Holy Spirit. When Mary came to visit Elizabeth, carrying Jesus within her, the baby John leapt for joy. He was rejoicing in Jesus before he saw the light of day.

The first reading speaks of God's servant: the Lord called me before I was born, while I was in my mother's womb he named me (Isaiah 49:1). And the Psalm says: you knit me together in my mother's womb. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made (Psalm 139:13–14). This is true of John, and it is true of every one of us. Before your parents saw your face, God knew you. Every child in the womb is a person loved by God. Today we pray for all mothers expecting a child, and for all babies not yet born.

In the Gospel the whole village rejoices with Elizabeth, who had waited so long for a child. On the eighth day the relatives want to call him Zechariah, after his father. But Elizabeth says: no; he is to be called John. And Zechariah, who could not speak, writes on a tablet: his name is John (Luke 1:63). The name means: the Lord has shown favour. This child belongs not to the family's plans but to God's grace. And at once Zechariah's mouth is opened, and he praises God.

Zechariah had been silent because he doubted the angel's message. Now his silence turns into a song, the Benedictus, which the Church sings every morning. Perhaps some of us have become silent before God because of disappointment or doubt. Today God wants to open our mouths again to praise him.

Everyone asked: what then will this child become? (Luke 1:66). He became the greatest of the prophets. And what was his greatness? St Paul tells us in the second reading. John said: I am not he. One is coming after me; I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet (Acts 13:25). John's greatness was his humility. He pointed away from himself to Jesus: he must increase, but I must decrease.

At every Mass we hear John's words again, when the priest lifts the Host and says: Behold the Lamb of God. Let us ask John the Baptist today to teach us his joy and his humility. Let us praise God for the gift of life, protect every child, and point others not to ourselves but to Jesus. Amen.

For children

Children, today is John the Baptist's birthday! His parents were very old, and they thought they would never have a baby. But God gave them a son. Everyone said: call him Zechariah, like his papa. But his mother said: no, his name is John! And his papa, who could not talk, wrote: His name is John. Then he could talk again, and he sang a happy song to God.

Even before John was born, he jumped for joy when Mary came near with baby Jesus. When he grew up, he pointed to Jesus and said: Look, there is the Lamb of God! You can be like John: whenever you can, help your friends to know Jesus.

Prayer

O God, who raised up Saint John the Baptist to make ready a nation fit for Christ the Lord, give your people, we pray, the grace of spiritual joys and direct the hearts of all the faithful into the way of salvation and peace. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord, you called John before he was born, filled him with your Spirit and named him for your grace. Thank you for knitting each of us together in our mother's womb. Bless all parents and all children not yet born, and comfort couples who long for a child. Open our mouths, as you opened Zechariah's, to praise you; and make us, like John, glad to decrease so that Christ may increase in us and in our world. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church celebrates the birth of St John the Baptist. Apart from the Lord himself, John and the Blessed Virgin Mary are the only saints whose birthdays the Church keeps in the liturgy, because tradition holds that John was sanctified even before his birth, when he leapt for joy in his mother’s womb at Mary’s greeting (Luke 1:41, 44). The readings speak of a calling that begins before birth. In Isaiah the Servant says, “The Lord called me before I was born”, and God makes him “a light to the nations”. In Acts Paul, preaching in a synagogue, presents John as the one who prepared for Jesus and said, “I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.” In the Gospel John is born, named, and grows strong in spirit in the wilderness.

Psalm 139 gives thanks to God who knit us together in our mother’s womb: “I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made.” The feast falls six months before Christmas, because the angel told Mary that Elizabeth was in her sixth month (Luke 1:36). St Augustine noted that John was born when the days begin to grow shorter and Christ when they begin to grow longer, and heard in this John’s own word: “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30; in substance).

Isaiah 49:1–6 · A light to the nations

Setting.

This is the second of the four “Servant Songs” in the Book of Isaiah (42:1–9; 49:1–6; 50:4–11; 52:13–53:12). Many scholars think that chapters 40–55 of Isaiah come from the time of the exile in Babylon, in the sixth century before Christ, when Israel needed to hear that God had not forgotten his people.

Background. Who is the Servant? In v. 3 he is called “Israel”, yet in vv. 5–6 he has a mission to Israel, to bring Jacob back. The Servant seems to be both the people and one person who sums up the people’s calling. The Church sees the song fulfilled in Christ, and also, in its own measure, in John, who was called before his birth (Luke 1:15) and was sent to turn many of the people of Israel to the Lord (Luke 1:16).

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Listen to me, O coastlands”; called and named in the womb.
  • v. 2: a sharp sword, a polished arrow, hidden in God’s hand and quiver.
  • v. 3: “You are my servant, Israel.”
  • v. 4: “I have labored in vain”, yet my reward is with my God.
  • vv. 5–6: to bring Jacob back, and more: “a light to the nations” to the end of the earth.

Key words.

  • ʿebed, servant: A title of honour, given to Abraham, Moses and David. The servant belongs wholly to his master and acts in his name.
  • ʾiyyîm, coastlands: The islands and distant shores of the Mediterranean, the furthest lands known. The Servant speaks to the whole world.
  • ʾôr gôyim, a light to the nations: God says that bringing back Israel is “too light a thing”; the Servant’s mission reaches all peoples. Simeon will call the child Jesus “a light for revelation to the Gentiles” (Luke 2:32), and Paul and Barnabas will quote this verse at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:47).

Verse by verse.

“The Lord called me before I was born, while I was in my mother’s womb he named me.” (49:1)

The calling comes before any merit. Jeremiah heard the same (Jeremiah 1:5). John’s name too was given by God before his birth (Luke 1:13).

“he made me a polished arrow, in his quiver he hid me away.” (49:2)

Hidden, then sent. John spent years in the wilderness before he appeared to Israel (Luke 1:80).

“I have labored in vain, I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity;” (49:4)

Even God’s servant knows discouragement. John in prison would send to ask Jesus, “Are you the one who is to come?” (Luke 7:19).

“I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” (49:6)

The circle widens from Israel to the whole world. The mission of the Church is born here.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the Servant Songs as fulfilled in Jesus, the true Servant who gives his life (CCC 713, 601). On this day the Church applies the second song also to John, the herald, called from the womb. The Catechism calls John the Lord’s immediate forerunner, the “prophet of the Most High”, who surpasses all the prophets, of whom he is the last (CCC 523). The Second Vatican Council took the phrase “light of the nations” for the opening words of its document on the Church, Lumen Gentium: Christ is the light of the nations, and the Church reflects his light.

Acts 13:22–26 · One is coming after me

Setting.

On their first missionary journey Paul and Barnabas reach Antioch in Pisidia, in the centre of present-day Turkey. On the Sabbath they go to the synagogue, and after the reading of the Law and the Prophets they are invited to speak (13:13–15). Paul’s sermon (13:16–41) is the first long sermon of his that Acts records. He retells Israel’s story from the Exodus to David, and then announces Jesus, the Saviour from David’s line.

Our verses are the hinge of the sermon: from David to Jesus, by way of John. That even in Asia Minor Paul could speak of John to a synagogue audience shows how widely John was remembered (compare Acts 18:25; 19:3).

Structure.

  • v. 22: God made David king, a man after his heart.
  • v. 23: from David’s posterity God has brought Israel a Saviour, Jesus.
  • v. 24: before his coming John proclaimed a baptism of repentance.
  • v. 25: John: “I am not he… I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.”
  • v. 26: “to us the message of this salvation has been sent.”

Key words.

  • sōtēr, Saviour: God himself is Israel’s Saviour in the Old Testament. Now God brings a Saviour, Jesus, whose name means “the Lord saves”.
  • baptisma metanoias, a baptism of repentance: Metanoia is a change of mind and heart, a turning back to God. John’s baptism prepared hearts; Christian baptism, given in the name of Jesus, gives the Holy Spirit (Acts 19:4–6).
  • hoi phoboumenoi ton theon, those who fear God: Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel and attended the synagogue without becoming Jews. Paul addresses both them and the “descendants of Abraham’s family” (v. 26).

Verse by verse.

“I have found David, son of Jesse, to be a man after my heart, who will carry out all my wishes.” (13:22)

Paul combines words from Psalm 89:20 and 1 Samuel 13:14. The promise to David prepares for Jesus, son of David.

“Of this man’s posterity God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised;” (13:23)

God keeps his promises to Israel. Jesus is first of all the fulfilment of what God promised his people.

“What do you suppose that I am? I am not he.” (13:25)

John’s greatness is his humility. He knows who he is not.

“I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.” (13:25)

Untying a master’s sandals was a slave’s task. John does not think himself worthy even of that.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that John is “more than a prophet”: in him the Holy Spirit concludes his speaking through the prophets, and he goes before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah” (CCC 718–719). John points away from himself; that is why the Church loves him. Every preacher, catechist and parent is called to the same humility: to bring people to Christ, and then step aside.

Luke 1:57–66, 80 · His name is John

Setting.

Luke tells the beginnings of John and of Jesus side by side: two annunciations (1:5–25 and 1:26–38), the meeting of the two mothers (1:39–56), and two births (1:57–80 and 2:1–40). At each step Jesus is shown to be greater, but John is honoured as his forerunner. Zechariah has been unable to speak since he doubted the angel’s word in the Temple (1:20). Now the promise is fulfilled.

Background. A Jewish boy was circumcised on the eighth day (Genesis 17:12; Leviticus 12:3). The naming, which Luke places at the same moment, would normally follow family custom, often a grandfather’s or father’s name. The Lectionary leaves out Zechariah’s song, the Benedictus (vv. 67–79), which the Church sings every morning at Lauds, and jumps to v. 80.

Structure.

  • vv. 57–58: Elizabeth gives birth; neighbours and relatives rejoice at the Lord’s mercy.
  • vv. 59–61: the eighth day; “No; he is to be called John.”
  • vv. 62–64: Zechariah writes, “His name is John”, and his tongue is set loose in praise.
  • vv. 65–66: fear and wonder through the hill country: “What then will this child become?”
  • v. 80: the child grows, strong in spirit, in the wilderness.

Key words.

  • Iōannēs, John: From the Hebrew Yôḥānān, “the Lord has been gracious”. The name, given by the angel (1:13), is the message of the child: God has shown favour to his people.
  • emegalynen to eleos, had shown his great mercy: Literally “the Lord had made great his mercy” with her (v. 58). It is the same verb as in Mary’s song: “My soul magnifies the Lord” (1:46). Elizabeth’s neighbours echo, without knowing it, the Magnificat.
  • cheir kyriou, the hand of the Lord: An Old Testament expression for God’s power at work in a person, especially in a prophet (compare Ezekiel 1:3). The neighbours see that this child is marked by God.

Verse by verse.

“Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” (1:58)

The angel had said, “many will rejoice at his birth” (1:14). The promise begins to come true in a village home.

“No; he is to be called John.” (1:60)

Elizabeth speaks first, against custom. Luke does not say how she knew; she trusts the word God gave.

“He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, “His name is John.”” (1:63)

Not “his name shall be”, but “is”: the name was already given by God. Zechariah’s obedience to the angel’s word opens his mouth.

“Immediately his mouth was opened and his tongue freed, and he began to speak, praising God.” (1:64)

The first use of his returned voice is praise. Silence that began in doubt ends in blessing.

“The child grew and became strong in spirit, and he was in the wilderness until the day he appeared publicly to Israel.” (1:80)

Luke will say much the same of Jesus (2:40). The wilderness is where Israel was formed, and where John is formed for his mission.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that with John the Baptist the Holy Spirit begins the restoration to humanity of the divine likeness, and that John was filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother’s womb (CCC 717, 720). The Church’s tradition holds that John was cleansed from original sin before his birth, at the Visitation (St Thomas Aquinas explains this sanctification in the womb); this is why his birth is a feast. Mary alone was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception (CCC 491). The Benedictus, the song of Zechariah that follows our passage, is prayed every morning in the Liturgy of the Hours: each day the Church joins Zechariah in thanking God for the dawn from on high.

Questions people ask

Why does the Church celebrate John’s birth, when it usually keeps a saint’s day of death?

For most saints the feast is their “birthday into heaven”, the day of their death. John’s birth is celebrated because it was already holy: he was filled with the Holy Spirit before birth (Luke 1:15) and leapt for joy in the womb in the presence of the Lord (Luke 1:41, 44). His martyrdom is also kept, on 29 August. Only Jesus, Mary and John have their birth celebrated in the Roman calendar.

Why 24 June and not 25 June, if it is six months before Christmas?

In the old Roman way of counting days, 25 December was “the eighth day before the Kalends of January”, and 24 June is “the eighth day before the Kalends of July”. So the two feasts fall on matching days in the Roman calendar, even though June has only thirty days.

Why did Zechariah lose his speech, and does God punish doubt?

Zechariah asked the angel for a sign, because he and Elizabeth were old (1:18). The silence was the sign, and also a time of discipline and waiting. It was not a rejection. When the promise was fulfilled and Zechariah obeyed, his voice returned in praise. God is patient with our doubts and leads us through them to faith, as he led Zechariah.

For the Latin reader

The Nativity of St John the Baptist is a solemnity of the Roman calendar, with the Gloria and the Creed, and its own Vigil Mass on the evening before. An eighth-century Latin hymn for this feast, Ut queant laxis, attributed to Paul the Deacon, gave music the names of its notes: the monk Guido of Arezzo took the first syllables of its lines, ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la. The hymn asks St John to cleanse our lips, so that with loosened voices we may sing God’s wonders, as Zechariah’s tongue was loosened. In Goa the feast, called São João, falls in the monsoon and is kept with joy: young men wear crowns of leaves and flowers and leap into the wells and ponds, recalling the child who leapt for joy in his mother’s womb. Every family that names a child, every parent who asks “What then will this child become?”, can make this feast its own.

For study and teaching

  • God called John and named him before he was born. What does this say about the dignity of every child in the womb?
  • Zechariah’s silence ended in praise when he obeyed. Is there a word of God I have doubted that I need to obey?
  • John said, “I am not he.” In my family, parish or work, how can I point people to Christ rather than to myself?
  • The neighbours asked, “What then will this child become?” Which child or young person could I pray for, and encourage, this week?
