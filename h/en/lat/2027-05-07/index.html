Word for the day

“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” John 16:22

Reflection

Paul has been in Corinth for some time. He has been opposed, he has moved next door to the synagogue, and he has seen the synagogue official believe. Yet he is afraid. Writing to the Corinthians later, he admits it: “And I came to you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling” (1 Corinthians 2:3). He knew what usually came next in a city: riots, beatings, flight by night. So the Lord speaks to him in a vision: “Do not be afraid, but speak and do not be silent; for I am with you, and no one will lay a hand on you to harm you, for there are many in this city who are my people.” The Lord does not promise that there will be no trouble. He promises his presence, and he gives Paul a new way of looking at the noisy port city: many in this city are already mine, though they do not know it yet. So Paul stays “a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them,” the longest stay of his journeys so far.

Then the trouble comes. When Gallio becomes proconsul of Achaia, some leaders of the Jewish community bring Paul before the tribunal, accusing him of persuading people to worship God against the law. Gallio, the brother of the philosopher Seneca, will not even hear Paul's defence: this is “a matter of questions about words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves.” An inscription found at Delphi dates Gallio's year in Corinth to about 51 or 52, and so this scene is one of the fixed points by which we date Paul's life. A Roman judge's indifference becomes, for a time, the protection of the Gospel. Paul stays on, then sails for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila, cutting his hair at Cenchreae because of a vow, a sign that he still lived as a devout Jew. Luke's phrase “the Jews” here means the group in that synagogue who opposed Paul, not the Jewish people; the official Crispus had believed, and Sosthenes, perhaps the same “brother Sosthenes” who later writes with Paul (1 Corinthians 1:1), suffers in the uproar.

The Gospel gives the image of a woman in labour: “she has pain, because her hour has come. But when her child is born, she no longer remembers the anguish because of the joy of having brought a human being into the world.” Jesus uses the word “hour,” the word he uses for his own Passion. His cross is a birth. On this First Friday the Church looks at the pierced Heart of Jesus. From his opened side came blood and water (John 19:34), and the Fathers saw there the birth of the Church, as Eve was formed from the side of the sleeping Adam. The pain of Good Friday was a labour, and its child is a new people. That is why the joy Jesus promises is a joy that “no one will take from you”: it is not a mood that comes and goes, but the life of the Risen One born in us. And the Psalm, an ancient song for the Lord's enthronement, already rings with Ascension joy: “Clap your hands, all you peoples; shout to God with loud songs of joy.”

Full homily

Dear friends, we think of St Paul as a man without fear. Today's reading tells us otherwise. One night in Corinth the Lord speaks to him in a vision: do not be afraid, but speak and do not be silent; for I am with you (Acts 18:9–10). The Lord would not say do not be afraid to someone who was not afraid.

Paul himself told the Corinthians later: I came to you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling. He had been beaten in other cities, chased out at night. Now in Corinth he wondered: will it happen again? And the Lord says: I am with you, and there are many in this city who are my people.

Notice what the Lord promises and what he does not. He does not promise that nobody will make trouble. Trouble does come: Paul is dragged before the judge Gallio. But the Lord keeps his word: no one harms him. The judge refuses the case, and Paul goes on preaching. God's protection sometimes comes through strange doors, even through the indifference of a Roman official.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks of a mother in labour. She has pain because her hour has come, but when the child is born she forgets the pain because of her joy. Mothers here know this better than any preacher. Jesus is speaking of his own hour, his cross. His Passion was a birth.

Today is First Friday. We look at the Heart of Jesus, opened by the soldier's spear. From it came blood and water, and from it the Church was born. That is why our joy is a joy that no one can take away: it was born from his love, and his love does not die.

So if you are afraid today, hear the Lord's word to Paul: do not be afraid; I am with you. Come to his Heart in confession and in Communion. And from today, with Mary, let us begin to pray for the Holy Spirit, who will give us courage to speak. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, even St Paul was sometimes afraid! But Jesus told him: do not be afraid, I am with you. Jesus says the same to you when you are scared.

Today is First Friday, a day to remember Jesus' Heart, full of love. Put your hand on your heart and say: Jesus, I love you too!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you said to Paul in the night: do not be afraid, but speak, for I am with you. Speak that word to us. When we are silent from fear, give us courage; when we are opposed, give us patience; when we are protected, give us thanks. On this First Friday we come to your pierced Heart. From your opened side the Church was born; from your labour of love comes our joy. Take our hearts, heal them, and make them like yours. Send us your Holy Spirit, for whom we begin to wait with Mary your Mother, and give us the joy that no one can take from us. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul at Corinth is told in a vision not to be afraid, and he stays a year and a half. When a case is brought against him, the Roman governor refuses to hear it, and the word goes on. In the Gospel Jesus tells the disciples that their sorrow will be like the pain of a woman in labour: real, but turned into a joy that no one can take away. Psalm 47 sings of God, king of all the earth, the Lord whose kingdom the apostle is carrying into the cities of Greece.

Acts 18:9–18 · Do not be afraid, but speak

Setting.

Paul came to Corinth after Athens (18:1). He found work and lodging with Aquila and Priscilla, Jews lately expelled from Rome by the emperor Claudius, who were tentmakers like himself (18:2–3). When many in the synagogue would not accept his message, he moved next door to the house of Titius Justus. Crispus, the official of the synagogue, believed with all his household (18:4–8). Corinth was a large, rich port city, the capital of the Roman province of Achaia.

Background. An inscription found at Delphi, a letter of the emperor Claudius, names Gallio as proconsul of Achaia. From it scholars date his term to about AD 51–52, and this gives one of the surest dates in the life of Paul. Gallio was the brother of the philosopher Seneca.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–11: the vision at night, and Paul stays a year and six months.
  • vv. 12–13: some opponents bring Paul before Gallio’s tribunal.
  • vv. 14–16: Gallio refuses to judge a dispute about their own law.
  • v. 17: Sosthenes is beaten, and Gallio takes no notice.
  • v. 18: Paul sails for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila; the vow at Cenchreae.

Key words.

  • mē phobou, do not be afraid: The word spoken to Abraham, to Mary, to the shepherds, and now to Paul. It is not a promise that nothing will hurt; it is a promise of presence: “for I am with you”.
  • anthypatos, proconsul: The governor of a senatorial province such as Achaia. Luke uses the exact title, as he does for Sergius Paulus in Cyprus (13:7). Such accurate titles are one reason historians take Luke’s account seriously.
  • bēma, tribunal, judgment seat: The raised platform from which a Roman magistrate gave judgment. A large stone platform of this kind still stands in the forum of ancient Corinth. Paul later wrote to the Corinthians that all of us must appear before the judgment seat (bēma) of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10).

Verse by verse.

““Do not be afraid, but speak and do not be silent;” (18:9)

Paul had arrived in Corinth, as he says himself, “in weakness and in fear and in much trembling” (1 Corinthians 2:3). The Lord meets the fear of the preacher at night, before it can silence him.

“for there are many in this city who are my people.” (18:10)

The Lord already counts as his own people many in Corinth who have not yet heard the Gospel. The missionary does not bring God to a city; he finds God already at work there.

“see to it yourselves; I do not wish to be a judge of these matters.” (18:15)

Luke means a particular group of opponents in Corinth, not the Jewish people, and the synagogue official Crispus had already believed (18:8). Gallio sees only a quarrel about words and names. His indifference becomes a protection: for the time being, Roman law will not forbid the preaching.

“At Cenchreae he had his hair cut, for he was under a vow.” (18:18)

Cenchreae was the eastern port of Corinth (Phoebe was a minister (diakonos) of its church, Romans 16:1). The vow was probably a private vow of thanksgiving of the Nazirite kind (Numbers 6). Paul the apostle of the Gentiles still keeps the devout practices of his own people.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the vision at Corinth as a word to every preacher and catechist: fear is not a reason to be silent, because the Lord is with those he sends. The same promise closes Matthew’s Gospel: “I am with you always” (Matthew 28:20). Paul’s long stay at Corinth gave rise to two of his greatest letters, 1 and 2 Corinthians. The names in this chapter, Priscilla, Aquila, Crispus and perhaps Sosthenes, who is named with Paul at the start of 1 Corinthians, remind us that the Gospel was carried by households and friendships, not by one man alone.

John 16:20–23 · Your pain will turn into joy

Setting.

We are at the Last Supper, near the end of the farewell discourse. Jesus has just said, “A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me” (16:16), and the disciples do not understand. He answers with a picture every family knows: a woman in labour.

Structure.

  • v. 20: you will weep, the world will rejoice; your pain will turn into joy.
  • v. 21: the woman in labour and the joy of the child born.
  • v. 22: “I will see you again”, and a joy no one will take away.
  • v. 23: on that day you will ask nothing of me; the Father will give in my name.

Key words.

  • lypē, pain, sorrow: The same word for the disciples’ grief and the mother’s pain. The sorrow is not denied or hurried over. It is turned, the very same sorrow, into joy.
  • hē hōra autēs, her hour: In John “the hour” is the hour of Jesus’ Passion and glory (2:4; 12:23; 13:1). Here the mother has her hour too: the Cross is pictured as a birth.
  • chara, joy: Jesus’ joy runs through the discourse: “so that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete” (15:11). It does not depend on what the world gives or takes.

Verse by verse.

“you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” (16:20)

Jesus does not say that joy will replace the pain, as if it were simply forgotten. The pain itself will be changed, as Good Friday becomes the way to Easter.

“When a woman is in labor, she has pain, because her hour has come.” (16:21)

The prophets used the same picture for the coming of God’s new age: Zion in labour giving birth to a new people (Isaiah 26:17–19; 66:7–14). Many Christian readers have also seen here the Church, and Mary at the foot of the Cross, becoming mother in sorrow.

“and no one will take your joy from you.” (16:22)

Easter joy is not a mood. It rests on a fact: “I will see you again”. What rests on the Risen Lord cannot be taken by any persecutor or loss.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this Gospel in the Easter season because it explains the Easter experience from the inside: the disciples’ grief on Good Friday and their joy on Easter morning (20:20). The Second Vatican Council opens its constitution on the Church in the modern world with the words “the joys and the hopes, the griefs and the anxieties” of the people of this age, which are also those of the followers of Christ (Gaudium et Spes 1). Christian joy does not ignore sorrow; it carries it to the Lord, who turns it.

Questions people ask

Does Christian joy mean we should not grieve?

No. Jesus says plainly, “you will weep and mourn”. He wept himself at the tomb of Lazarus (11:35). The promise is not that we will never grieve, but that our grief is not the end. It will be turned into a joy that no one can take.

For study and teaching

  • What fear keeps me silent when I should speak of my faith? What does the Lord’s “I am with you” change?
  • Which sorrow in my life am I being asked to give to the Lord, so that he may turn it into joy?
