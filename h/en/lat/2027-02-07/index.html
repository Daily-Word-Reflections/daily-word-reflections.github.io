Word for the day

“He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” Mark 1:31

Reflection

Job speaks for every sleepless sufferer: “Do not human beings have a hard service on earth…? I am allotted months of emptiness, and nights of misery are apportioned to me. When I lie down I say, ‘When shall I rise?’ But the night is long, and I am full of tossing until dawn.” And: “My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and come to their end without hope.” Scripture does not hide this cry; it places it in God's own Word. Job is not rebuked for complaining honestly. The Psalm answers him from far ahead: “He heals the brokenhearted, and binds up their wounds.” The God who “determines the number of the stars” also bends down to bandage a wound.

Mark shows us one day in Capernaum. Leaving the synagogue, Jesus enters the house of Simon and Andrew. Simon's mother-in-law lies in bed with a fever. “He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” Mark uses the same verb he will use for the resurrection: Jesus raises her. And her first act as a healed woman is service. By evening “the whole city was gathered around the door”, and Jesus heals many. Job's long night meets the dawn that comes to the door.

Then, “in the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed.” The healer draws his strength from the Father in the dark hours. When the disciples find him (“Everyone is searching for you”) he does not return to the easy success of yesterday: “Let us go on to the neighboring towns, so that I may proclaim the message there also; for that is what I came out to do.” Paul shares that same urgency: “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!… I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” Healing, prayer and mission belong together. This week we begin Lent, and on Thursday we pray with the sick on the feast of Our Lady of Lourdes.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Job speaks for everyone who has lain awake in pain: when I lie down I say, ‘When shall I rise?’ But the night is long, and I am full of tossing until dawn (Job 7:4). God's Word does not hide this cry. It gives words to our suffering.

The Psalm answers: he heals the brokenhearted, and binds up their wounds (Psalm 147:3). The God who counts the stars bends down to bandage a wound.

In the Gospel, Jesus enters Simon's house. Simon's mother-in-law lies with a fever. He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them (Mark 1:31). Healed, she serves. Every grace we receive is meant to become service.

By evening the whole city was gathered around the door (Mark 1:33). And before dawn, while it was still very dark, Jesus went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed (Mark 1:35). The healer draws his strength from the Father in the dark.

When the disciples say everyone is searching for you, he answers: let us go on to the neighboring towns, so that I may proclaim the message there also (Mark 1:38). Paul says: woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel! (1 Corinthians 9:16).

This week Lent begins, and on Thursday we pray with the sick on the feast of Our Lady of Lourdes. Let us take someone by the hand: visit the sick, pray in the early hours, and share the Good News. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday begins in the middle of the night, with Job awake and in pain, counting the hours until dawn. It ends before dawn, with Jesus up and praying in a deserted place. In between, the Psalm answers Job from afar: the God who numbers the stars also bandages a wound. In Mark's Gospel, a woman in bed with a fever is taken by the hand and lifted up, and she begins to serve. Paul, in his turn, has made himself a servant of all, because the good news is too great to keep. So the movement of the day runs from night to healing to mission. Those who are healed are sent.

Job 7:1–4, 6–7 · Nights of misery

Setting.

The Book of Job is one of the great works of the Bible's wisdom tradition, and it cannot be dated with certainty. Its hero is not an Israelite but a man from the land of Uz (1:1). In the prose opening he loses his children, his wealth and his health, and he is not told why. Friends arrive and, after long silence, explain that suffering must be the result of sin. Job refuses this answer.

Chapters 6–7 are his reply to the first friend, Eliphaz. In chapter 7 he turns to speak to God directly. The Lectionary leaves out v. 5, with its description of his sores.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: human life compared with hard service: the soldier, the slave, the hired hand.
  • vv. 3–4: Job's own months and nights.
  • vv. 6–7: days that run out without hope; and then a prayer: remember.

Key words.

  • ṣābā’, hard service: Usually military service: the word behind “hosts” in “Lord of hosts”. Isaiah 40:2 uses it of Jerusalem's term of hardship. Life feels like conscription, something a person never chose and cannot leave.
  • yarḥê-šāw’, months of emptiness: Šāw’ means emptiness or futility. A hired man at least gets his wages at evening. Job's pay is months of nothing.
  • tiqwāh, hope: This word can also mean a cord or thread (Joshua 2:18, 21). Coming right after the weaver's shuttle, it makes a sad play on words: Job's days run out “without thread” and “without hope” at once.

Verse by verse.

““Do not human beings have a hard service on earth, and are not their days like the days of a laborer?” (7:1)

Job starts with everybody, and he speaks for all who work with no rest and no reward. Then, in v. 3, he comes to himself.

“When I lie down I say, ‘When shall I rise?’ But the night is long, and I am full of tossing until dawn.” (7:4)

Anyone who has sat by a sick bed, or has been in one, knows this verse. Scripture does not tidy it up. These words are God's word as well as Job's.

“My days are swifter than a weaver’s shuttle, and come to their end without hope.” (7:6)

The odd thing is that the nights are long and the days are short. That is exactly how suffering feels.

““Remember that my life is a breath; my eye will never again see good.” (7:7)

The verb is singular and addressed to God. Job's despair has turned into a prayer. Even a person who thinks he will never see good again may still say “Remember” to God.

How the Church reads it.

St John Paul II takes Job as the Old Testament's proof that not all suffering is a punishment for sin, even though Job's friends thought so (Salvifici Doloris 11). The Catechism adds that no quick answer will do for the question of evil, and that the whole of the Christian message is the answer to it (CCC 309). St Gregory the Great wrote his long Moralia on this book and read Job as a figure of Christ (in substance). In the older Office of the Dead the Church read from Job, including from this very chapter. She gives the sufferer his words, because he has a right to them.

Psalm 147:1–2, 3–4, 5–6 · He heals the brokenhearted

Setting.

Psalms 146–150 close the Psalter with one long Hallelujah. Psalm 147 speaks of God building up Jerusalem and gathering the outcasts (v. 2), and many scholars therefore place it after the return from exile. In the Greek and Latin numbering these verses belong to Psalm 146. The Psalm comes as an answer to Job: not an explanation, but a description of who God is.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: a call to praise, and the reason: he builds and gathers.
  • vv. 3–4: he heals the wounded and names the stars.
  • vv. 5–6: his greatness, and what he does with it: he lifts up the lowly.

Key words.

  • šĕbûrê lēb, the brokenhearted: Literally “broken of heart”. A similar phrase stands in Psalm 34:18. In the Old Testament the heart is the centre of the person, not just of the feelings.
  • mĕḥabbēš, binds up: The word for tying on a dressing. The same verb appears in Isaiah 61:1, “to bind up the brokenhearted”.
  • ‘ănāwîm, the downtrodden: The lowly, the poor who have no one but God. The Beatitude of the meek (Matthew 5:5) comes from the same idea, through Psalm 37:11.

Verse by verse.

“He heals the brokenhearted, and binds up their wounds.” (147:3)

Put this next to Job 7. The Psalm does not explain why the wound came. It tells us who kneels down beside it.

“He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names.” (147:4)

The two verses are set together on purpose. The one who has the vast sky in order has the time to bandage a wound. Compare Genesis 15:5.

“The Lord lifts up the downtrodden; he casts the wicked to the ground.” (147:6)

“Lifts up” prepares us for Mark, where Jesus lifts up a sick woman by the hand.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls the Psalter the book in which the Word of God becomes the prayer of human beings (CCC 2587), and says that the Psalms mirror both God's great deeds and the experience of the one who prays (CCC 2588). The Church prays Psalm 147 in the Liturgy of the Hours. Today it stands as a promise that Christ fulfils in the Gospel, where his healing begins in a family's home.

1 Corinthians 9:16–19, 22–23 · Woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel

Setting.

In chapters 8–10 Paul is answering a question that divided the Christians of Corinth: may a believer eat meat that has been offered to idols? His answer is that love may give up a right for the sake of a weaker brother or sister. In chapter 9 he gives his own example. As an apostle he has a right to be supported by the Church, and he mentions that Cephas and others take a wife with them on their travels (9:5). But he himself has given up that right. He works with his hands (Acts 18:3) and takes no pay from the Corinthians. The Lectionary omits vv. 20–21, where he speaks of living with Jews and with those outside the Law.

Structure.

  • v. 16: preaching is not a boast but a necessity.
  • vv. 17–18: so what is his reward? To give it away.
  • v. 19: bound to no one, and a slave to all.
  • vv. 22–23: all things to all people, for the sake of the gospel.

Key words.

  • anankē, obligation: Necessity, compulsion. This life was laid on Paul on the Damascus road; he did not choose it for himself (Acts 9:15; Galatians 1:15–16). That is why he cannot boast.
  • adapanon, without charge: Only here in the New Testament. Paul's whole reward is to take no reward. The gift is passed on in the same way as it was received.
  • kerdainein, to win: A word from the world of trade: to gain or to make a profit. Paul is working for a different profit. It is used five times in vv. 19–22.
  • synkoinōnos, one who shares: A partner. Paul does not stand outside the gospel, handing it to others. He needs it too.

Verse by verse.

“If I proclaim the gospel, this gives me no ground for boasting, for an obligation is laid on me, and woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” (9:16)

“Woe” is the prophets' word. Paul speaks like Jeremiah, who could not hold the word in (Jeremiah 20:9).

“What then is my reward? Just this: that in my proclamation I may make the gospel free of charge,” (9:18)

He was paid in joy. His refusal of pay meant that no one could say he preached for money, and the poorest could hear him as easily as the rich.

“For though I am free with respect to all, I have made myself a slave to all, so that I might win more of them.” (9:19)

This is the pattern of Christ himself, who took the form of a slave (Philippians 2:7). In a slave-owning city, these words were not a pretty metaphor.

“To the weak I became weak, so that I might win the weak. I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” (9:22)

He does not say “all”, but “some”. It is honest. The missionary does not expect to convert the world by himself. And this is the mission's measure: it starts from the one who is weak.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council makes Paul's words the Church's own: “Woe to me, if I do not preach the Gospel” (Lumen Gentium 17). St Paul VI wrote that evangelizing is the grace and vocation proper to the Church, her deepest identity: she exists in order to evangelize (Evangelii Nuntiandi 14). It belongs to every baptized person, each according to his or her place.

Mark 1:29–39 · He took her by the hand and lifted her up

Setting.

Last Sunday's Gospel opened Jesus' first day in Capernaum, a fishing town on the northern shore of the lake: his teaching with authority and his casting out of an unclean spirit in the synagogue (1:21–28). Today the same day goes on, from the synagogue to a house, from the house to the door at sunset, and then on to the next morning. Mark gives us one full day as a sample of the whole ministry.

Excavations at Capernaum have uncovered a house near the synagogue that early Christians venerated. Many archaeologists identify it with Peter's house, though this cannot be proved.

Structure.

  • vv. 29–31: the house: the fever, the hand, the service.
  • vv. 32–34: the door: at sunset, the whole city.
  • v. 35: the deserted place: prayer before dawn.
  • vv. 36–38: “Everyone is searching for you.” “Let us go on.”
  • v. 39: throughout Galilee.

Key words.

  • ēgeiren, he lifted up: The verb Mark will use for the Resurrection (16:6: “He has been raised”). Healing is already a small resurrection.
  • diēkonei, she began to serve: The verb that gives us “deacon”. The tense suggests continued action. Jesus will say that the Son of Man came “not to be served but to serve” (10:45), the same verb. This woman is the first human being in Mark to do what Jesus himself came to do, and women who served him are there, watching from a distance, at the Cross (15:40–41).
  • exēlthon, I came out: Jesus says that this is what he “came out” to do (v. 38). The word may simply mean leaving Capernaum, but Mark's readers may also hear more: Luke's parallel says “I was sent” (Luke 4:43).
  • prōi ennycha lian, in the morning, while it was still very dark: Three time-words in Greek. Mark will use similar words for the women coming to the tomb on Easter morning (16:2).

Verse by verse.

“Now Simon’s mother-in-law was in bed with a fever, and they told him about her at once.” (1:30)

A fever at that time could easily be fatal. And notice what the disciples do: they simply tell him. This is intercession at its simplest.

“He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” (1:31)

Three things happen, and Jesus says nothing. Her service is not a sign of being a servant by position; it is the response of one who has been healed.

“That evening, at sunset, they brought to him all who were sick or possessed with demons.” (1:32)

Mark gives the time twice, “evening” and “sunset”, because the sabbath ended at sundown. Only then could people carry the sick through the town.

“and he would not permit the demons to speak, because they knew him.” (1:34)

The demons know his name but do not love him. Jesus does not want witness from them, and his identity is to be understood only at the Cross (15:39).

“In the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed.” (1:35)

After the busiest day, the first thing he does is pray. Mark shows Jesus at prayer alone only three times, each at night and each at a moment of pressure (1:35; 6:46; 14:32–39).

“He answered, “Let us go on to the neighboring towns, so that I may proclaim the message there also; for that is what I came out to do.”” (1:38)

“Everyone is searching for you” sounds like success. Jesus does not stay where he is wanted. Prayer has made him clear about what he was sent for.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christ's compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a clear sign that “God has visited his people” (CCC 1503), and that Jesus often draws apart to pray in solitude, on a mountain and preferably at night (CCC 2602). St Jerome, preaching on this passage, prays that the Lord may come to our house and touch our hand, so that the fever of sin may leave us and we may rise and serve (in substance). Peter's house can stand as a picture of the Church: the Lord comes in, and those who are healed begin to serve.

Questions people ask

Was Job wrong to complain to God like this?

No. At the end of the book God says that Job has spoken of him what is right, while the friends who defended God with neat explanations have not (42:7). Job comes to see that he has spoken of things too great for him (42:3), but he is never condemned for bringing his pain to God. The Psalms of lament teach the same. Honest complaint addressed to God is still prayer.

Peter had a wife. Why then are Latin priests unmarried?

The Gospel takes Peter's marriage for granted, and Paul mentions it as well (1 Corinthians 9:5). Celibacy is a discipline of the Church, not a command of Scripture. In the Latin Church priests are normally chosen from among men who live in celibacy “for the sake of the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 19:12). In many Eastern Churches, married men may be ordained priests, and such priests are held in great respect (CCC 1579–1580). Both are honoured by the Church.

When Paul says “all things to all people”, does he mean changing the message to please people?

No. In Galatians 1:10 he says that if he were still pleasing people he would not be a servant of Christ. He changes his habits, not the gospel: he gives up his rights, his food and his comfort for others. St Augustine says that Paul became all things to all not by the cunning of a liar but by the love of one who feels with others (in substance).

For the Latin reader

This is the last Sunday before Lent: Ash Wednesday falls on 10 February. On Thursday, 11 February, the Church keeps Our Lady of Lourdes and the World Day of the Sick, begun by St John Paul II in 1992. Today's readings fit that day well. A parish may use this Sunday to name its sick in the Prayer of the Faithful, to prepare those who bring Holy Communion to the homebound, and to remind people that the Anointing of the Sick is not only for the dying (CCC 1514). Like Simon's friends, we can tell Jesus about them at once.

For study and teaching

  • Job says “Remember” to God in the middle of his despair. What would my own honest prayer sound like on a bad night?
  • Psalm 147 places the stars next to the wound. How does it help me to see God as both great and close? Whose wound can I help to bind up this week?
  • Paul says that he cannot not preach. Where and to whom has Christ given me the chance to speak of him, and what right might I give up for it?
  • Simon's mother-in-law rose and served. What healing have I received? What service should come from it? Before Lent begins, when will I find my “deserted place” for prayer?
