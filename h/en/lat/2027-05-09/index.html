Word for the day

“They said, “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven? This Jesus, who has been taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven.”” Acts 1:11

Reflection

St Luke wrote two books, and today's first reading is the hinge between them. “In the first book, Theophilus, I wrote about all that Jesus did and taught from the beginning until the day when he was taken up to heaven.” The Gospel of Luke ends with the Ascension; the Acts of the Apostles begins with it. The Ascension is not an epilogue to the story of Jesus; it is the beginning of the story of the Church. Luke tells us that after his suffering Jesus “presented himself alive to them by many convincing proofs, appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God.” Forty is the number of preparation: the flood, Moses on the mountain, Israel in the desert, Elijah's journey, Jesus' fast. These forty days prepare the apostles for a new way of being with their Lord. And their last question shows how much they still have to learn: “Lord, is this the time when you will restore the kingdom to Israel?” Jesus does not scold them. He redirects them: “It is not for you to know the times or periods … But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.” That one verse is the table of contents of the whole Book of Acts.

Then the moment itself: “as they were watching, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight.” The cloud in the Bible is not weather; it is the sign of God's presence. The cloud led Israel through the desert, filled the tabernacle and the Temple, and overshadowed Jesus at the Transfiguration. Daniel saw “one like a human being coming with the clouds of heaven” to receive an everlasting kingdom (Daniel 7:13–14). So Jesus does not go up into the sky as a rocket goes into space; he enters the glory of God, and he takes our human nature, his risen body with its wounds, with him. And immediately two men in white, like the two at the empty tomb, ask the disciples a question that is also for us: “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” The Ascension is not an invitation to stand and stare. The same Jesus “will come in the same way,” and until then there is work to do.

The Psalm is the song of a King's enthronement: “God has gone up with a shout, the Lord with the sound of a trumpet. Sing praises to God, sing praises; sing praises to our King, sing praises.” The Church has sung it on this feast from the earliest centuries. In the second reading Paul prays that the Ephesians may have “the eyes of your heart enlightened” to see what God has done: he “raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion.” To sit at the right hand means to share the Father's power and glory. And Paul adds the astonishing consequence: God “has made him the head over all things for the church, which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all.” The Head has gone into heaven, but the Body is still here, joined to him. Where the Head has gone, the Body is called to follow. (The alternative reading, Ephesians 4, makes the same point another way: “When he ascended on high … he gave gifts to his people,” apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers, to build up the Body.)

Mark's Gospel, in its closing verses, gives the Ascension in a few strong lines. Jesus sends the eleven: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” Belief and baptism will bring salvation; signs will accompany those who believe. Then: “the Lord Jesus, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” And at once, without a pause: “And they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them and confirmed the message by the signs that accompanied it.” That is the key to the whole feast. Jesus sits at the right hand of God, and at the same moment he works with his disciples on earth. He is not further away; he is closer, no longer limited to one place, present to every place where the Gospel is proclaimed. These verses (Mark 16:9–20) are missing from some ancient manuscripts, but the Church received them as inspired Scripture, and Trent confirmed the canon with them.

Today is also World Communications Day, kept on the Sunday before Pentecost since 1967, at the wish of the Second Vatican Council. It is fitting. The Lord who ascends gives his Church a message for “the whole creation” and the ends of the earth. In our age the ends of the earth are reached by phone screens and radio, by newspapers and the internet. Every Christian with a mobile phone is in some way an evangelist, for good or ill. And between the Ascension and Pentecost, the Church does what the apostles did: they returned to Jerusalem and devoted themselves to prayer “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). We are in those nine days. Let us look up to where Christ has gone, and then look around at the world he sends us to, and pray with his Mother: Come, Holy Spirit.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, have you ever stood at an airport and watched a plane carry away someone you love? You stand at the glass and watch until it is only a dot, and then it disappears into the clouds. You keep looking a little longer. Then someone touches your shoulder: come, let us go home.

That is what happened to the apostles on the Mount of Olives. Jesus blessed them, and as they were watching, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight (Acts 1:9). They kept staring. And two men in white touched their shoulders, so to speak: Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven? (Acts 1:11).

First, the Ascension tells us where Jesus has gone. Not to some planet far away. The cloud in the Bible is the sign of God's glory. Jesus has entered the glory of God, and he has taken our human nature with him: his risen body, with his wounds, now sits at the right hand of the Father (Ephesians 1:20). A man like us is in the heart of God. That is our future too. Where the Head has gone, the Body will follow.

Second, the Ascension tells us that Jesus has not left us. St Paul says God made him the head over all things for the church, which is his body (Ephesians 1:22–23). The Head is in heaven; the Body is here. You are that Body. And Mark tells us something wonderful: they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them (Mark 16:20). He sits at the right hand of God and at the same time works with us. He is not further away; he is nearer, because he is no longer in one place but with every disciple everywhere.

Third, the Ascension gives us a job. Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation (Mark 16:15). You will be my witnesses to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). The angels did not let the apostles stand and stare. Our faith is not only looking up; it is also going out.

Today is World Communications Day. Two thousand years ago the apostles walked on dusty roads. Today the ends of the earth are in your pocket, on your phone. What do you send and forward? Rumours, gossip, anger? Or hope, truth, and the Good News? Every Christian with a phone can be a witness, like St Carlo Acutis, the teenager who used the internet to spread love for the Eucharist.

Finally, the apostles did not rush out at once. Jesus told them to wait for the Holy Spirit. So they went back to Jerusalem and prayed, together with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14). We are in those days now. In this Mass the ascended Lord comes down to us in his Body and Blood. Let us receive him, lift up our hearts to where he is, and with Mary pray every day this week: Come, Holy Spirit. Alleluia. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, the Ascension can seem far from daily life. Jesus goes up; we stay here. But St Paul draws a very practical lesson from it. Writing from prison, he says: I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to lead a life worthy of the calling to which you have been called (Ephesians 4:1).

What does such a life look like? With all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, making every effort to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace (Ephesians 4:2–3). The prophet Micah had said the same: to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God (Micah 6:8). The ascended Lord is not honoured by staring at the sky but by patience in the kitchen, in the office and in the parish committee.

Paul continues: there is one body and one Spirit… one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all (Ephesians 4:4–6). Israel prayed every day: hear, O Israel, the LORD is our God, the LORD alone (Deuteronomy 6:4). Because God is one, his people must be one. Every quarrel in the Church wounds the body whose Head has ascended into heaven.

Then Paul quotes a psalm about a king going up in triumph: when he ascended on high he made captivity itself a captive; he gave gifts to his people (Ephesians 4:8; Psalm 68:18). The ascended Christ does not leave us empty-handed. The gifts he gave were that some would be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, some pastors and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ (Ephesians 4:11–12). Every Christian has received a gift for others.

So let us celebrate the Ascension by living worthy of our calling. Be patient with one difficult person. Pray and work for unity. Offer your parish one gift: teaching catechism, singing in the choir, visiting the sick, cleaning the church. And at this Mass, receive the ascended Lord who gives himself as the greatest gift of all. Alleluia. Amen.

Dear friends, in today's second reading St Paul prays a beautiful prayer for us. He asks that God may give us a spirit of wisdom and revelation, so that, with the eyes of your heart enlightened, you may know what is the hope to which he has called you (Ephesians 1:17–18). The Ascension is about hope, and hope needs eyes of the heart to see it.

Our physical eyes saw the cloud take Jesus away. The eyes of the heart see more. They see that the man Jesus, with his wounded and risen body, now sits at the right hand of the Father. The psalm had foretold it: the LORD says to my lord, sit at my right hand (Psalm 110:1). The psalmist prayed: open my eyes, so that I may behold wondrous things (Psalm 119:18). Let us ask for those eyes today.

What is Jesus doing at the right hand of the Father? The Letter to the Hebrews answers: he is able for all time to save those who approach God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them (Hebrews 7:25). At this very moment Jesus is praying for you. He shows the Father his wounds and presents your name. When we pray through Christ our Lord, as the Church does at the end of her prayers, we join a prayer that is already rising in heaven.

And the angels promised the apostles: this Jesus, who has been taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven (Acts 1:11). Daniel had seen one like a human being coming with the clouds of heaven (Daniel 7:13). Christ will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead. We do not know the day. So the hope of the Ascension is also a call to be ready.

So let us lift the eyes of our hearts. Let us remember that Jesus prays for us. Let us live each day so that we would be glad to see him come. And at this Mass, where the ascended Lord comes to us hidden in bread, let us receive a foretaste of the glory to which he has called us. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In India, as in many countries, the Solemnity of the Ascension of the Lord is kept on this Sunday, in place of the Seventh Sunday of Easter. Three readings tell one mystery from three sides. Acts tells the event: forty days, the promise of the Spirit, the cloud and the two men in white. Ephesians tells its meaning: God raised Christ and seated him at his right hand, far above every power, as head of the Church, his body. Mark tells its fruit: the apostles go out and preach everywhere, and the Lord works with them.

Psalm 47 gives the cry of the day: “God has gone up with a shout, the Lord with the sound of a trumpet” (Psalm 47:5). The psalm may first have accompanied the Ark of the Covenant going up to the Temple. The Church hears in it the Risen Lord going up to the Father. For the second reading the Lectionary also allows Ephesians 4:1–13, on the gifts of the ascended Christ to his Church.

Acts 1:1–11 · As they were watching, he was lifted up

Setting.

The Acts of the Apostles is the second volume of Luke’s work. It is addressed, like the Gospel, to Theophilus (Luke 1:3). The “first book” is the Gospel, which also ends with the Ascension, at Bethany, with the disciples returning to Jerusalem with great joy (Luke 24:50–53). Luke tells the Ascension twice: at the end of the Gospel, as the close of Jesus’ earthly life, and at the start of Acts, as the beginning of the Church’s mission.

Background. In the Old Testament the cloud is the sign of God’s presence and glory: the pillar of cloud in the desert (Exodus 13:21), the cloud on Sinai (Exodus 24:15–18), the cloud filling the Temple (1 Kings 8:10–11). In Daniel 7:13 one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient One. Elijah was taken up to heaven, and Elisha received a double share of his spirit because he saw him go (2 Kings 2:9–15). The number forty recalls Israel’s forty years in the desert and Jesus’ forty days of fasting: a full time of preparation.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the prologue: the first book, and the forty days of appearances.
  • vv. 4–5: wait in Jerusalem for the promise of the Father, the baptism with the Holy Spirit.
  • vv. 6–8: the question about the kingdom, and the answer: power from the Spirit and witness to the ends of the earth.
  • vv. 9–11: he is lifted up, the cloud, the two men in white.

Key words.

  • analambanō, to take up: Used in v. 2 and v. 11, and in the Greek Old Testament of Elijah taken up (2 Kings 2:11). The Ascension is not Jesus going away into space; it is his being taken into the glory of God.
  • martys, witness: “You will be my witnesses” (v. 8). The Greek word later came to mean martyr, one whose witness costs life itself. The verse is the plan of the whole book: Jerusalem (chapters 1–7), Judea and Samaria (8–12), and the ends of the earth (13–28).
  • nephelē, cloud: Not weather but the sign of God’s presence, as on Sinai and at the Transfiguration (Luke 9:34–35). The cloud takes Jesus into the presence of God, out of their sight but not away from them.

Verse by verse.

“appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God.” (1:3)

Only Luke gives the number forty. These days were a school: the Risen Lord taught them what the kingdom means, and ate and drank with them (10:41).

““Lord, is this the time when you will restore the kingdom to Israel?”” (1:6)

The question is not foolish. It is the hope of the prophets. Jesus does not deny that God will fulfil his promises to Israel; he says the times belong to the Father, and he gives them a task for now.

“But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.” (1:8)

The answer to “when?” is “you”. The kingdom will come through Spirit-filled witness. The ends of the earth include India; the Christians of this land are a fruit of this verse.

“as they were watching, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight.” (1:9)

Luke stresses that they saw it. Like Elisha, who received the spirit of Elijah because he saw him taken up, the apostles see the Lord go and will receive his Spirit.

““Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” (1:11)

The two men in white recall the two men in dazzling clothes at the empty tomb (Luke 24:4). The Ascension does not invite us to stare at the sky but to turn back to the world with the hope of his return: “will come in the same way”.

How the Church reads it.

The Creed confesses: “He ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father. He will come again in glory.” The Catechism teaches that Christ’s Ascension marks the definitive entrance of his humanity into God’s heavenly domain, from where he will come again (CCC 665), and that he precedes us there so that we, his members, may live in the hope of being with him for ever (CCC 666). St Leo the Great preached that the Ascension of Christ is our own uplifting, and that where the glory of the Head has gone, the body is called to follow in hope (in substance). The days of prayer between the Ascension and Pentecost, when the apostles waited with Mary in the upper room (1:14), are the origin of the Church’s novenas.

Ephesians 1:17–23 · Seated at his right hand

Setting.

The Letter to the Ephesians presents itself as Paul’s, written from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think it was written in Paul’s name by a disciple, near the end of the first century; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. The Church receives it, either way, as the inspired word of God in the Pauline tradition. Some of the oldest manuscripts lack the words “in Ephesus” in 1:1, and many think it was a circular letter for several churches of Asia.

The letter opens with a great blessing of God for his plan to gather all things in Christ (1:3–14). Our passage is the prayer that follows: that the readers may understand what God has done in raising and enthroning Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–18a: the prayer for a spirit of wisdom and revelation, and enlightened hearts.
  • vv. 18b–19: to know three things: the hope, the riches of the inheritance, the greatness of the power.
  • vv. 20–21: the power at work in Christ: raised, seated at the right hand, above every power and name.
  • vv. 22–23: all things under his feet; head over all things for the Church, his body, his fullness.

Key words.

  • kephalē, head: Christ is head of the Church as the source of its life and its Lord. Head and body belong together: the Ascension does not separate Christ from his Church but gives the Church its head in heaven.
  • plērōma, fullness: The Church is “the fullness of him who fills all in all” (v. 23). The phrase is difficult. It may mean that the Church is filled by Christ, or that the Church in some way completes him as a body completes a head. The Fathers held both senses.
  • en tois epouraniois, in the heavenly places: A phrase found only in Ephesians (1:3, 20; 2:6; 3:10; 6:12). In 2:6 the letter says that God has already “seated us with him in the heavenly places”. What is said of Christ in our passage is begun in us.

Verse by verse.

“so that, with the eyes of your heart enlightened, you may know what is the hope to which he has called you,” (1:18)

The heart in the Bible is the centre of the person, where we think and decide. Faith gives the heart eyes, so that it can see what the bodily eyes on Ascension day could not: where Christ has gone.

“God put this power to work in Christ when he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places,” (1:20)

An echo of Psalm 110:1, “Sit at my right hand”, the Old Testament verse most quoted in the New. To sit at the right hand of a king is to share his rule.

“far above all rule and authority and power and dominion,” (1:21)

These were names for the unseen powers that people feared. Whatever powers there are, Christ is above them all. No fear has the last word over one who belongs to him.

“and has made him the head over all things for the church, which is his body,” (1:22–23)

Christ is head “over all things” not for himself but “for the church”. His lordship is a gift to his people. Psalm 8:6, “you have put all things under their feet”, is quoted in v. 22 and fulfilled in the Son of Man.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, explaining Christ seated at the right hand, teaches that Christ is Lord of the cosmos and of history, and head of the Church which is his body, quoting this passage (CCC 668–669). The Second Vatican Council uses the image of head and body to describe the Church (Lumen Gentium 7). St Augustine preached on the Ascension that Christ, though raised above the heavens, still suffers on earth in his members, and that we, though on earth, are already with him in heaven through hope (in substance).

Mark 16:15–20 · The Lord worked with them

Setting.

These verses belong to the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20). The two oldest complete Greek manuscripts end the Gospel at 16:8, and many scholars think that the longer ending was added early in the second century, drawing on the other Gospels and Acts. It was known to St Irenaeus, near the end of the second century, and it stands in most manuscripts. The Church receives it as part of the inspired and canonical Gospel; the Council of Trent defined the canon of the books of Scripture with all their parts, and this ending is read in the liturgy.

The Risen Lord appears to the eleven at table, rebukes their unbelief (16:14), and then sends them out.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: the mission: proclaim the good news to the whole creation; faith and baptism.
  • vv. 17–18: the signs that will accompany those who believe.
  • v. 19: the Lord Jesus is taken up and sits at the right hand of God.
  • v. 20: the disciples go out and preach, and the Lord works with them.

Key words.

  • euangelion, good news, gospel: Mark begins his book with “the good news of Jesus Christ” (1:1) and ends it with the command to proclaim that good news to the whole creation. The whole Gospel is framed by the word.
  • pasē tē ktisei, to the whole creation: Not only to all nations but to all creation. Paul says that creation itself waits with eager longing for the revealing of the children of God (Romans 8:19–21).
  • synergountos, working with: The Lord “worked with them” (v. 20). The word gives us “synergy”. The ascended Lord is not absent from the mission; he is its partner and its power.

Verse by verse.

“Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” (16:15)

The last words of Jesus in Mark are a sending. The disciples who had fled at his arrest (14:50) are now sent to the world.

“The one who believes and is baptized will be saved; but the one who does not believe will be condemned.” (16:16)

Faith and baptism go together. The verse speaks of those who hear the good news and refuse it, not of those who never heard it through no fault of their own (see the question below).

“they will pick up snakes in their hands, and if they drink any deadly thing, it will not hurt them;” (16:18)

This is a promise of protection in the mission, like Paul unharmed by the viper on Malta (Acts 28:3–6). It is not an invitation to test God by handling snakes on purpose, which Jesus himself refused to do (Matthew 4:7).

“So then the Lord Jesus, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” (16:19)

Only here in Mark is Jesus called “the Lord Jesus”, the title of the risen one. “Taken up” echoes Elijah (2 Kings 2:11), and “sat down at the right hand” fulfils Psalm 110:1.

“And they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them” (16:20)

The Gospel ends not with the disciples looking up but going out. The Ascension begins the time of the Church.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 19 at the start of its teaching on the Ascension (CCC 659). It cites v. 16 when it teaches that faith is necessary for salvation (CCC 161) and that baptism is necessary for salvation for those to whom the Gospel has been proclaimed (CCC 1257). St Leo the Great taught that what was visible in our Redeemer has passed over into the sacraments (in substance); the Catechism quotes these words (CCC 1115). The Lord who is no longer seen still works with his Church in the word preached and the sacraments celebrated.

Questions people ask

If Jesus ascended, is he absent from us now?

No. He said, “I am with you always” (Matthew 28:20). He is no longer present in the way he was in Galilee, but he is present in a new way: in his word, in the sacraments, above all in the Eucharist, in the Church gathered in his name, and in the poor. The Catechism says that he precedes us into the Father’s glory so that we may live in the hope of being with him (CCC 666).

Was the Ascension a journey upward into the sky?

Luke describes what the apostles saw: he was lifted up and a cloud took him from their sight. The meaning is not a journey through space but Christ’s humanity entering the glory of God. The cloud is the Bible’s sign of God’s presence. “Heaven” here is not a place above the clouds but life with God.

Does Mark 16:16 mean that everyone who is not baptized is lost?

The Church teaches that baptism is necessary for salvation for those who have heard the Gospel and been able to ask for it (CCC 1257). It also teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel or the Church, but seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will as their conscience knows it, may attain salvation (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). God has bound salvation to the sacrament, but he himself is not bound by his sacraments (CCC 1257).

For the Latin reader

In the Roman Rite the Ascension is a solemnity with its own Preface, which proclaims that Christ ascended not to distance himself from our lowly state but that we, his members, might be confident of following where he, our head, has gone before. In India the Bishops have transferred the solemnity from Thursday to this Sunday so that the faithful may celebrate it together. The Paschal candle remains in the sanctuary until Pentecost, and the Regina Caeli is still prayed. The days from now to Pentecost Sunday, 16 May, are kept as a time of prayer for the Holy Spirit, like the apostles with Mary in the upper room.

For study and teaching

  • The angels asked, “why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” Where am I standing still when I should be going out as a witness?
  • Ephesians prays for “the eyes of your heart enlightened”. What would I like the Lord to help me see more clearly about my hope?
  • Mark says “the Lord worked with them”. Where have I seen the Lord working with someone who shared the faith?
  • How can my family pray together for the Holy Spirit in the days before Pentecost?
