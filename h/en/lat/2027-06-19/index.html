Word for the day

“but he said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” So, I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may dwell in me.” 2 Corinthians 12:9

Reflection

For several days Paul has been answering people in Corinth who compared him with smooth, impressive preachers and found him wanting. Today he reaches the strangest part of his defence. If they want boasting, he will boast, but of what? First he speaks, almost shyly, in the third person: “I know a person in Christ who fourteen years ago was caught up to the third heaven.” He was “caught up into Paradise and heard things that are not to be told, that no mortal is permitted to repeat.” Then he refuses to build anything on it: “On behalf of such a one I will boast, but on my own behalf I will not boast, except of my weaknesses.” The revelation is real, but he will not let the Corinthians judge him by it. And now the heart of the passage: “to keep me from being too elated, a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me.” We do not know what the thorn was. Some of the Fathers thought of a sickness, others of persecution or of a temptation; Paul never tells us, and perhaps that is a mercy, because every reader can place his own thorn beside Paul's. “Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me.” Paul prayed, and prayed again, as Jesus prayed three times in Gethsemane. The answer was not the one he asked for: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” The thorn stayed; the grace came. And Paul's whole way of seeing changed: “Therefore I am content with weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and calamities for the sake of Christ; for whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” The Psalm is the song of a poor man who learned the same thing: “The angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them. O taste and see that the LORD is good; happy are those who take refuge in him.”

The Gospel continues the Sermon on the Mount, and Jesus speaks to people who knew what it was to worry about tomorrow's food. He begins with a choice: “No one can serve two masters … You cannot serve God and wealth.” Then he goes to the root of so much of our anxiety: “do not worry about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, or about your body, what you will wear.” He does not tell us to stop working. He tells us to look: “Look at the birds of the air; they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they?” “Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they neither toil nor spin, yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not clothed like one of these.” Worry adds nothing: “can any of you by worrying add a single hour to your span of life?” The answer is not carelessness but a new order of things: “But strive first for the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well.” And then a word of great kindness: “So do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will bring worries of its own. Today's trouble is enough for today.” Paul and Jesus say the same thing from two sides: God's grace is enough for today's weakness, and today's bread is enough for today. St Romuald, remembered today, was a young nobleman of Ravenna who turned from a violent world to a life of prayer, and spent his long life founding hermitages and monasteries in Italy; the best known is Camaldoli, in the hills near Arezzo. His followers kept a short rule attributed to him which begins, in substance: sit in your cell as in paradise, and watch your thoughts as a good fisherman watches for fish. It is the Gospel of the lilies put into practice: a heart at rest in God, seeking first his kingdom.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul was a great apostle. He had seen visions, he had been caught up into Paradise, as he tells us today. But when the Corinthians asked him for proof that he was a true apostle, he did not show them his visions. He showed them his weakness. On my own behalf I will not boast, except of my weaknesses (2 Corinthians 12:5).

He speaks of a thorn in the flesh. We do not know what it was: an illness, a persecution, a temptation. Paul prayed three times that it would leave him. And the Lord answered: My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). The thorn did not go. But grace came. Sometimes God answers our prayer not by taking away the burden but by giving us the strength to carry it.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks to people who worried, just as we do, about food and clothes and the future. Look at the birds of the air, he says; consider the lilies of the field (Matthew 6:26, 28). He does not say: do not work. He says: do not let worry be your master. You cannot serve God and wealth. Worry cannot add a single hour to our life. It can only steal the peace of today.

So Jesus gives us an order of life: strive first for the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well (Matthew 6:33). And he gives us a measure: today's trouble is enough for today. St Romuald, whom we remember today, taught his monks to sit with God as if they were already in paradise. Let us also find a quiet minute today, give God our thorn and our worries, and receive the grace that is enough. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever watched the crows and sparrows outside your window? They do not go to the market and they do not cook food, but God feeds them every day. Jesus said: if God takes care of the birds, he will take care of you too, because you are much more precious to him! So when you feel worried about an exam or a new class, say: Jesus, I trust you. Then do your best, and let him take care of the rest.

Prayer

Lord God, you know the thorn each of us carries and the worries that keep us awake. When we ask you to take them away and you do not, give us the grace that is sufficient, and let the power of Christ dwell in our weakness. Teach us to look at the birds of the air and the lilies of the field, to seek first your kingdom and its righteousness, and to leave tomorrow in your hands. Through the prayers of St Romuald, give us hearts at rest in you. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul, pushed by rivals in Corinth into a contest of boasting, tells of a vision in which he was caught up into Paradise. Yet the one thing he will really boast of is his weakness, because the Lord said to him, “My grace is sufficient for you.” In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples not to worry about food and clothing, for their heavenly Father knows what they need. Both readings teach the same trust: not in our own strength, and not in wealth, but in God who gives. Psalm 34 sings the invitation: “O taste and see that the Lord is good.” Today the Church may also remember St Romuald, who left everything to seek God alone.

2 Corinthians 12:1–10 · Power made perfect in weakness

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 of this letter Paul defends his ministry against visiting preachers whom he calls, with some irony, “super-apostles” (11:5; 12:11). They impressed the Corinthians with their eloquence, their letters of recommendation and their spiritual experiences, and they looked down on Paul as weak. Paul answers with what is often called his “fool’s speech” (11:1–12:13). If boasting is demanded, he will boast, but of the wrong things: his beatings, shipwrecks and dangers (11:23–29), his escape from Damascus in a basket let down a wall (11:32–33), and now a vision he hardly dares to describe. Some scholars think chapters 10–13 were first a separate letter, later joined to the others; the Church receives the whole as Scripture.

Background. The letter was written in the middle of the 50s, so “fourteen years ago” takes us to the early 40s, years of Paul’s life about which Acts tells us very little. Jewish writings of the time spoke of several heavens; “the third heaven” and “Paradise” here name the place of God’s own presence.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: a man in Christ caught up to the third heaven, into Paradise, who heard things that cannot be told.
  • vv. 5–6: Paul will boast of that man, but of himself only of his weaknesses.
  • vv. 7–9a: the thorn in the flesh; three prayers; the Lord’s answer.
  • vv. 9b–10: so he will gladly boast of his weaknesses: when he is weak, then he is strong.
  • ¶ The passage turns on v. 9. Everything before it climbs up to the third heaven; everything after it comes down to weakness, insults and hardships, and finds Christ there.

Key words.

  • skolops tē sarki, a thorn in the flesh: Skolops can mean a thorn, a splinter, or a pointed stake. Paul never says what it was. Suggestions include an illness (compare Galatians 4:13–15), the opponents who harassed him, or a lasting temptation. St John Chrysostom understood it of the adversaries who attacked Paul’s work (in substance). What is clear is that it was painful, it lasted, and it kept him humble.
  • charis, grace: God’s favour given as a gift, and the strength that comes with it. The Lord does not remove the thorn. He gives something greater: his own presence and help in the weakness.
  • astheneia, dynamis, weakness, power: The two words meet in v. 9. The verb translated “is made perfect” (teleitai) means brought to its goal, completed. Christ’s power does its full work where human strength runs out.

Verse by verse.

“I know a person in Christ who fourteen years ago was caught up to the third heaven” (12:2)

Paul speaks of himself in the third person, as v. 7 makes plain. He will not turn this experience into a credential. What matters is not what he has seen but whom he serves.

“Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me,” (12:8)

The prayer recalls Jesus in Gethsemane, who prayed three times (Mark 14:32–41). Paul’s prayer was answered, though not in the way he asked.

“My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” (12:9)

The Lord’s answer is in the present tense: the grace is enough, now and always. Paul does not say he enjoys weakness; he has learned that it is the place where Christ’s power dwells.

“for whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” (12:10)

This is not a love of suffering for its own sake. It is the experience of an apostle whose sufferings for Christ made room for Christ to act.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only faith can embrace the mysterious ways of God’s almighty power, and that this faith glories in its weaknesses in order to draw to itself Christ’s power; the Virgin Mary is its supreme model (CCC 273). Speaking of the sick, the Catechism also recalls this passage: even the most intense prayers do not always obtain the healing of every illness, and Paul learned from the Lord that his grace is sufficient (CCC 1508). The Church therefore never treats illness or weakness as a sign that God has turned away. Joined to Christ, it can become a place of his strength.

Matthew 6:24–34 · Strive first for the kingdom

Setting.

These verses belong to the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7). Jesus has just spoken of treasure in heaven and of the healthy eye (6:19–23). Now he draws the conclusion: a person cannot belong to two masters. Then he speaks to the anxiety that money so often brings.

The pictures come from the Galilean countryside: birds that do not sow or reap, wild flowers in the fields, and the dry grass that was gathered to heat clay ovens. The listeners were mostly poor farmers and fishermen, for whom food and clothing were daily worries, not distant ideas.

Structure.

  • v. 24: no one can serve two masters; God and wealth.
  • vv. 25–30: do not worry about life and body: the birds, the lilies, the grass of the field.
  • vv. 31–32: your heavenly Father knows that you need these things.
  • v. 33: strive first for the kingdom of God and his righteousness.
  • v. 34: do not worry about tomorrow.

Key words.

  • mamōnas, wealth: An Aramaic word kept in the Greek; older translations say “mammon”. It means money and property, perhaps from a root meaning that in which one puts one’s trust. Jesus speaks of it almost as a rival lord.
  • merimnaō, to worry, to be anxious: The verb comes six times in these verses (vv. 25, 27, 28, 31, and twice in v. 34). Jesus does not forbid work or care; the birds are fed, but they still look for their food. He forbids the anxious care that forgets the Father.
  • oligopistoi, you of little faith: A word Matthew likes (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). Jesus says it to disciples: not to unbelievers, but to believers whose faith is still too small for their troubles.

Verse by verse.

“You cannot serve God and wealth.” (6:24)

To “serve” here is to be a slave. Money is a useful servant but a hard master, and the heart cannot give itself wholly to both.

“Look at the birds of the air; they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them.” (6:26)

Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater. If God cares for birds, how much more for his children.

“But strive first for the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well.” (6:33)

The heart of the passage. “First” does not mean “only”: food and clothing still matter, but they find their right place when God’s reign comes first. In Matthew “righteousness” is the faithful life the kingdom asks for (5:6, 20).

“Today’s trouble is enough for today.” (6:34)

A word of plain wisdom. Grace is given for today; tomorrow will have its own grace.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees here the childlike abandonment Jesus asks for, to the providence of a Father who cares for his children’s smallest needs (CCC 305, quoting 6:31–33). It also lists the love of money among the forms of idolatry: “You cannot serve God and mammon” (CCC 2113). The Church does not read this Gospel as a call to carelessness. Parents must provide for their children, and work is a duty and a dignity. What Jesus removes is the anxiety that makes money a god.

Questions people ask

Does this Gospel mean we should not save money or plan for the future?

No. Scripture praises the ant that stores its food in summer (Proverbs 6:6–8), and Jesus himself speaks of the builder who first sits down to estimate the cost (Luke 14:28). Prudent planning is a virtue. What Jesus warns against is worry that takes the place of trust, and saving that becomes hoarding while others are in need. The question is who is master: God, with money as a servant, or money, with God pushed aside.

For the Latin reader

St Romuald was born at Ravenna about the year 951. As a young man he saw his father kill a relative in a quarrel, and he went to do penance at the monastery of Sant’Apollinare in Classe, where he became a monk. He spent his life founding hermitages and monasteries in Italy; the best known is Camaldoli in Tuscany, from which the Camaldolese monks take their name. He joined the silence of the hermit to the common life of the monastery. A short rule handed down under his name says, in substance: sit in your cell as in paradise. By the traditional reckoning he died on 19 June 1027, so this year marks a thousand years since his death. His life is a commentary on today’s Gospel: he had one Master.
