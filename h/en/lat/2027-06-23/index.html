Word for the day

“He brought him outside and said, “Look toward heaven and count the stars, if you are able to count them.” Then he said to him, “So shall your descendants be.” And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness.” Genesis 15:5–6

Reflection

Some years have passed since Abram left Haran, and the great promise has not moved. He is rich, respected and still childless. Then “the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, ‘Do not be afraid, Abram, I am your shield; your reward shall be very great.’” And for the first time in the Bible we hear Abram answer God with a complaint: “O Lord GOD, what will you give me, for I continue childless, and the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus?” What use is a reward if there is no son to inherit it? The Bible is not afraid of such honest prayers. God does not rebuke him. He takes him outside under the night sky of the desert: “Look toward heaven and count the stars, if you are able to count them.” Then he said to him, “So shall your descendants be.” Nothing in Abram's tent has changed. Sarai is still old and childless. But Abram looks up from his problem to God's sky, and the most important sentence of the chapter follows: “And he believed the LORD; and the LORD reckoned it to him as righteousness.” St Paul will build his teaching on this verse: we are made right with God not by our own achievements but by trusting his promise (Romans 4:3; Galatians 3:6), a faith that, as St James adds, shows itself in works (James 2:22–23). Then God seals the promise with a strange ceremony. Abram brings a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove and a pigeon, cuts the animals in two and lays the halves opposite each other. In the ancient world the two parties to a treaty walked between such pieces, as if to say: may I be cut in two like these if I break my word (compare Jeremiah 34:18). Abram waits all day, driving away the birds of prey. At sunset a deep sleep falls on him, and a terrifying darkness. And then “a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces.” Only God passes through. Abram does not. God binds himself alone to his promise. “On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram.” The Psalm sings the same faithfulness: “He is mindful of his covenant forever, of the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations, the covenant that he made with Abraham.” The Church teaches that this covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked, “for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29; Catechism 121).

In the Gospel Jesus warns his disciples about a danger every generation meets: “Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves.” How can we tell them apart? Jesus gives one simple test, said twice: “You will know them by their fruits.” “Are grapes gathered from thorns, or figs from thistles? In the same way, every good tree bears good fruit, but the bad tree bears bad fruit.” A preacher may be eloquent, a video may draw millions of views, a healing service may be crowded; the question is still the fruit. Does this message lead to humility or to pride, to charity or to money, to unity with the Church or to division, to repentance or to excitement only? St Paul named the fruit of the Spirit: “love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control” (Galatians 5:22–23). Abram's faith was not just a feeling under the stars; its fruit was a whole life of obedience. And tomorrow the Church celebrates the birth of the greatest of the prophets, St John the Baptist, a true prophet whose fruit was this: he pointed away from himself to Christ. This evening, in many parishes, the Vigil Mass of his Nativity is celebrated. Its first reading is the call of Jeremiah, “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you,” and its Gospel is the angel's message to the old priest Zechariah in the Temple: your prayer has been heard, and Elizabeth will bear a son, “and you will name him John.” Abram looking at the stars and Zechariah hearing the angel are two old men to whom God says the same thing: nothing is impossible for him.

Full homily

Dear friends, Abram had left everything at God's word, and years later he still had no child. So he says to God: what will you give me, for I continue childless? (Genesis 15:2). It is a very honest prayer. Many of us have prayed like that: Lord, I have done everything you asked, and still I am waiting.

God does not scold him. He takes him outside and says: look toward heaven and count the stars, if you are able to count them. So shall your descendants be (Genesis 15:5). Nothing had changed in Abram's tent. But he lifted his eyes from his problem to God's sky. And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6). This is faith: not seeing, but trusting the One who promises.

Then God makes a covenant. In those days, people who made a treaty walked between animals cut in two, as if to say: may this happen to me if I break my word. But that night only God passes between the pieces, as a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch. God binds himself alone. His faithfulness does not depend on ours. The Psalm sings: he is mindful of his covenant forever (Psalm 105:8).

In the Gospel Jesus says: beware of false prophets; you will know them by their fruits (Matthew 7:15–16). Not every attractive preacher is a true prophet. Look at the fruit: love, humility, peace, unity. Tomorrow we celebrate the birth of John the Baptist, a true prophet, whose fruit was to point to Jesus. This evening many parishes begin the feast with the Vigil Mass. Let us also prepare: lift up our eyes like Abram, believe God's promise, and let our faith bear good fruit. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever tried to count the stars at night? You cannot, there are too many! God told Abram: your family will be as many as the stars. Abram was old and had no children, but he believed God. And God kept his promise. Jesus said: a good tree gives good fruit. When we believe in God, we should give good fruit too: being kind, sharing, saying sorry. Tonight, look up at the sky and say: God, I believe you!

Prayer

Lord God, you said to Abram: do not be afraid, I am your shield. When we are tired of waiting and the promise seems far away, lead us outside to look at your sky, and give us faith like his, which you reckoned as righteousness. You alone passed between the pieces and bound yourself to your covenant; keep us faithful to the new covenant in the blood of your Son. Protect us from false prophets, and make our lives bear the good fruit of your Spirit. As we prepare to celebrate the birth of St John the Baptist, prepare our hearts for Christ. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

God tells Abram not to be afraid, and promises him a son and descendants as many as the stars. “And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness.” Then, in a strange night rite, God himself passes between the divided animals and makes a covenant with Abram. In the Gospel Jesus warns against false prophets: “You will know them by their fruits.” Abram’s faith bore fruit in obedience; true prophets are known by the same kind of fruit. Psalm 105 sings: “He is mindful of his covenant forever.”

Genesis 15:1–12, 17–18 · He believed the Lord

Setting.

God has promised Abram a land and a great nation (12:1–7; 13:14–17), but years have passed and Abram and Sarai still have no child. Abram’s heir would be a servant, Eliezer of Damascus. In this chapter Abram tells God his fear plainly, and God answers with two promises, each followed by a sign: first descendants (vv. 1–6), then the land (vv. 7–21).

Background. In the ancient Near East a covenant could be sealed by cutting animals in two and passing between the pieces, as if to say, “May I be like these animals if I break my word.” The prophet Jeremiah mentions this rite (Jeremiah 34:18). Here only the smoking fire pot and the flaming torch, signs of God’s presence, pass between the pieces. God binds himself; Abram, in a deep sleep, only receives. The Lectionary leaves out vv. 13–16, where God foretells the years of slavery in Egypt.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: “Do not be afraid”; Abram complains that he has no son.
  • vv. 4–6: “Count the stars”; Abram believes, and it is reckoned to him as righteousness.
  • vv. 7–12: the promise of the land; the animals divided; birds of prey driven off; deep sleep and darkness.
  • vv. 17–18: the fire pot and torch pass between the pieces; the covenant and the land from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates.

Key words.

  • heʾĕmin, he believed: From the root ʾmn, to be firm, to be sure, the root of our word “Amen”. To believe is to lean one’s whole weight on God and his word.
  • ṣĕdāqâ, righteousness: Right relationship, faithfulness to a bond. God counts Abram’s trust as what makes him right with God. St Paul quotes this verse (Romans 4:3; Galatians 3:6), and so does St James (James 2:23).
  • kārat bĕrît, to make a covenant: Literally “to cut a covenant” (v. 18), a phrase that recalls the cutting of the animals in this rite.

Verse by verse.

“Do not be afraid, Abram, I am your shield; your reward shall be very great.” (15:1)

The first words of God to Abram in this chapter are the words God speaks so often to those he calls: do not be afraid.

“Look toward heaven and count the stars, if you are able to count them.” (15:5)

God takes Abram outside, under the night sky of the desert. The promise is as great as the heavens, and Abram has nothing to hold but God’s word.

“And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness.” (15:6)

One of the great verses of the Bible. Abram’s faith is not a work that earns God’s favour; it is the welcome he gives to God’s promise.

“a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces.” (15:17)

Fire and smoke are signs of God’s presence, as later at Sinai (Exodus 19:18). God himself walks the path of the covenant oath.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Abraham fulfils the definition of faith in Hebrews 11:1, and quotes this verse: “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (CCC 146). The Council of Trent taught that faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation and root of all justification (Session 6, chapter 8). Paul and James both quote Genesis 15:6 and do not contradict each other: Paul stresses that justification is a gift received by faith, not earned by works of the law; James shows that true faith lives and acts, as Abraham’s did when he offered Isaac. Faith, hope and charity, and the good works that grow from them, are all gifts of grace (CCC 1987–1992, 2008).

Matthew 7:15–20 · You will know them by their fruits

Setting.

After the narrow gate (7:13–14) Jesus warns against those who would lead people along the wrong road: false prophets. They look harmless, like sheep, but they destroy the flock. The test he gives is simple and practical: look at the fruit of their lives and teaching.

John the Baptist had spoken in almost the same words: “every tree therefore that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire” (3:10). Jesus repeats the warning (7:19).

Structure.

  • v. 15: beware of false prophets in sheep’s clothing.
  • v. 16: you will know them by their fruits: no grapes from thorns, no figs from thistles.
  • vv. 17–18: a good tree bears good fruit, a bad tree bad fruit.
  • vv. 19–20: the tree without good fruit is cut down; you will know them by their fruits.

Key words.

  • pseudoprophētēs, false prophet: One who claims to speak for God but does not. The Old Testament knew them well (Jeremiah 23:16), and the early Church had to discern them too.
  • lykoi harpages, ravenous wolves: Wolves that snatch and tear. Jesus sends his disciples “like sheep into the midst of wolves” (10:16); here the danger is wolves dressed as sheep, inside the flock.
  • karpos, fruit: In the Bible fruit means the deeds and character that grow out of a person’s heart. Paul will speak of the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control (Galatians 5:22–23).

Verse by verse.

“Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves.” (7:15)

The danger is not always obvious. A gentle appearance and pious words are not enough; Jesus asks for discernment.

“You will know them by their fruits.” (7:16)

Fruit takes time to appear. The test is the patient look at a whole life: humility, charity, fidelity to the Gospel.

“A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit.” (7:18)

What we do flows from what we are. To change the fruit, the tree itself must be made good, and that is the work of grace.

How the Church reads it.

The Didache, an early Christian writing, gives a similar rule for testing travelling prophets: not everyone who speaks in the Spirit is a prophet, but only one who lives in the ways of the Lord (Didache 11, in substance). The Catechism speaks of the fruits of the Spirit as perfections that the Holy Spirit forms in us as the first fruits of eternal glory, and lists twelve according to the tradition of the Church (CCC 1832). The Church tests new teachings and private revelations in the same way: by their fidelity to the faith handed down and by the fruits of holiness they bear.

For study and teaching

  • Abram brought his fear and complaint to God honestly. What fear do I need to bring to God in prayer today?
  • Looking at the “fruit” of my life this past month, what does it show about the tree?
