Word for the day

“The Lord GOD helps me; therefore I have not been disgraced; therefore I have set my face like flint, and I know that I shall not be put to shame.” Isaiah 50:7

Reflection

The third Song of the Servant is the song of a disciple. “The Lord GOD has given me the tongue of a teacher, that I may know how to sustain the weary with a word. Morning by morning he wakens, wakens my ear to listen as those who are taught.” Before the Servant speaks, he listens; every morning he lets God open his ear. Because he listens, he does not turn back when suffering comes: “I gave my back to those who struck me, and my cheeks to those who pulled out the beard; I did not hide my face from insult and spitting.” His strength is not hardness but trust: “The Lord GOD helps me… therefore I have set my face like flint.” We heard this same reading on Palm Sunday; today it prepares us for the night of Thursday and the day of Friday, when Jesus will be struck, mocked and spat upon, and will not hide his face. The Psalm is the cry of the righteous sufferer whom even his own family treats as a stranger: “It is zeal for your house that has consumed me… for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink.” The Gospels saw this psalm fulfilled on Calvary (John 2:17; 19:28–29).

The Gospel tells the bargain. Judas goes to the chief priests and asks: “What will you give me if I betray him to you?” They pay him thirty pieces of silver. That was the price of a slave in the Law (Exodus 21:32), and the “lordly price” at which the shepherd of Zechariah was valued (Zechariah 11:12–13). From this bargain the day takes its old name in English, Spy Wednesday. Then the scene moves to preparation: “Where do you want us to make the preparations for you to eat the Passover?” Jesus is not a victim caught by surprise. He says: “My time is near; I will keep the Passover at your house with my disciples.” He walks freely into his hour.

At table he says: “Truly I tell you, one of you will betray me.” And each disciple asks, “Surely not I, Lord?” That is the right question for us too. None of the Eleven was sure of himself; each knew that his heart could fail. Judas also asks, but notice the difference: the others say “Lord”; he says “Rabbi”, teacher. Jesus answers, “You have said so,” leaving the door open even now. The words of Jesus about Judas are solemn: “woe to that one by whom the Son of Man is betrayed!” They are not a curse but a lament, the grief of a friend. The Church does not pretend to know the final state of any soul; she knows that despair was Judas's last and greatest tragedy, and that no betrayal is greater than the mercy of Christ. On this last day before the Triduum, let us ask with the apostles, “Surely not I, Lord?”, and then run to the One who is about to give his life for betrayers, deniers and deserters, which is to say, for all of us.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Servant tells us his secret: the Lord GOD has given me the tongue of a teacher, that I may know how to sustain the weary with a word. Morning by morning he wakens, wakens my ear to listen as those who are taught (Isaiah 50:4). Before he speaks, he listens. And because he listens, he does not turn back: I did not hide my face from insult and spitting (Isaiah 50:6).

In the Gospel Judas goes to the chief priests and asks: what will you give me if I betray him to you? They paid him thirty pieces of silver (Matthew 26:15). The price of a slave. The Son of God, valued at thirty coins.

At the table Jesus says: one of you will betray me. And each disciple asks: surely not I, Lord? (Matthew 26:22). They do not point at each other. Each looks into his own heart. That is the question for us on this last day before the Triduum. For what price would I sell Jesus? For money? For comfort? For the approval of others?

But remember: Jesus did not curse Judas; he grieved over him and even at the table left the door open. No sin is bigger than his mercy. Tomorrow evening the Triduum begins. Let us come to it as the Servant did, with a listening ear and a face set like flint, trusting that the Lord GOD helps us. Amen.

For children

Children, Judas sold Jesus for thirty silver coins. How sad! Jesus is worth more than all the money and toys in the world. Let us tell Jesus today: you are my best friend, and I will never trade you for anything!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Servant who listened morning by morning, you set your face like flint and did not hide it from insult and spitting. You were sold for thirty pieces of silver by a friend. Keep us from selling you for any price. Let us ask humbly, Surely not I, Lord?, and run to your mercy. Bring us with you into these holy days. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In English tradition this day has long been called Spy Wednesday, for the Gospel in which Judas goes to the chief priests. Isaiah’s Servant and Matthew’s Judas stand as opposites. The Servant’s ear is opened every morning, and he turns neither back nor aside. Judas hears the same Teacher every day, and looks for his opportunity. Psalm 69, which the New Testament often quotes of the Passion, prays between them: “Insults have broken my heart”.

Isaiah 50:4–9a · Morning by morning

Setting.

This is the third Servant Song. The word “servant” does not appear in the song itself but in the verse just after it (50:10). Here for the first time we hear plainly of suffering. The speaker is a disciple who is beaten for what he has been given to say, yet he remains sure of God’s help. Part of this reading was also heard on Palm Sunday, as the first reading before the Passion. Today we hear it as far as the question “who will declare me guilty?”

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5a: the disciple’s tongue and ear.
  • vv. 5b–6: he does not resist: back, cheeks, face.
  • vv. 7–9a: God helps him; he faces his accusers as if in court.

Key words.

  • leshôn limmûdîm, the tongue of those taught: The Hebrew word comes twice in v. 4. A teacher is first of all one who is taught: the word that comforts comes from an ear that has listened.
  • babbōqer babbōqer, morning by morning: The Hebrew repeats the word. This is not one great vision but a daily schooling, the prayer of an ordinary morning repeated.
  • ḥallāmîsh, flint: One of the hardest stones of the region. “I have set my face like flint” is not hardness toward others, but a firmness that does not turn back. Luke says that Jesus “set his face to go to Jerusalem” (Luke 9:51).

Verse by verse.

“The Lord God has given me the tongue of a teacher, that I may know how to sustain the weary with a word.” (50:4)

The purpose of this gift is the tired person in front of us.

“I gave my back to those who struck me, and my cheeks to those who pulled out the beard; I did not hide my face from insult and spitting.” (50:6)

To pull out a man’s beard was a deep humiliation in that world. The Gospels recall the blows and the spitting at the trial of Jesus (Matthew 26:67; 27:30). The verbs are active: he gave his back. Suffering is not simply something that befalls him.

“he who vindicates me is near. Who will contend with me?” (50:8)

St Paul takes up the language of this courtroom in Romans 8:33–34: “It is God who justifies. Who is to condemn?”

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this song of Christ, of whom Peter writes, “When he was abused, he did not return abuse” (1 Peter 2:23), and the Catechism counts it among the Songs that proclaim the meaning of his Passion (CCC 713). Anyone who teaches the faith can make v. 4 a morning prayer. It gives the order of a Christian day: first to listen, then to speak.

Matthew 26:14–25 · Surely not I?

Setting.

Matthew has just told of the woman who anointed Jesus at Bethany (26:6–13), and now he sets another disciple’s act beside it. She gives away something costly for him. Judas asks what he can get for him. The scene then moves to the preparation for the Passover and the meal itself.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–16: Judas and the chief priests.
  • vv. 17–19: the Passover is prepared.
  • vv. 20–25: at table: “one of you will betray me”.

Key words.

  • paradidōmi, to betray, to hand over: The key verb of the Passion, here in vv. 15, 16, 21, 23, 24 and 25. It can also mean simply to hand over. People hand Jesus over; yet behind them, as Paul says, God “gave him up for all of us” (Romans 8:32, the same verb).
  • triakonta argyria, thirty pieces of silver: Only Matthew gives the sum. It recalls the price for a slave gored by an ox (Exodus 21:32) and the shepherd’s insulting wage in Zechariah 11:12–13, which Matthew takes up in 27:9–10. A small price for such a man.
  • ho kairos mou, my time: The word means a set, decisive time. The disciples are told to say it to a stranger in the city. Judas is watching for an opportunity (v. 16, eukairia, from the same root), but the true time belongs to Jesus.

Verse by verse.

““What will you give me if I betray him to you?”” (26:15)

Only Matthew has Judas ask this, which fits the charge of greed in John 12:6. Why he really did it the Gospels do not fully explain. This passage names some of the chief priests, not the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

“And they became greatly distressed and began to say to him one after another, “Surely not I, Lord?”” (26:22)

The Greek form expects the answer “no”, yet they ask, and none is sure of himself. It is a healthy fear.

“Judas, who betrayed him, said, “Surely not I, Rabbi?” He replied, “You have said so.”” (26:25)

The others say “Lord”; Judas says “Rabbi”, a title Jesus had told his disciples not to claim (23:8). The answer leaves the responsibility with the one who asks. Even now, there is still time.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has never taught that God made Judas betray: God “predestines no one to go to hell” (CCC 1037). Verse 24 joins two truths: “as it is written” means that God’s plan is not defeated, and “woe” means that the human act remains guilty. The Mass, too, is set in this story: Paul begins his account of it with “on the night when he was betrayed” (1 Corinthians 11:23). We approach the table knowing that we might be the one who asks, “Surely not I?”

Questions people ask

Does “better not to have been born” mean that Judas is in hell?

The words are a grave warning, and Christian writers have often been severe about Judas. But the Church has never declared of any named person that he is lost, and she leaves the judgment to God. What we must take from the verse is the seriousness of sin, and our own need of mercy.

For study and teaching

  • What is my “morning by morning”? When and how does God open my ear each day?
  • Judas asked, “What will you give me?” Where in my life am I tempted to set a price on my fidelity?
