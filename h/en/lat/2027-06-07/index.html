Word for the day

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction with the consolation with which we ourselves are consoled by God.” 2 Corinthians 1:3–4

Reflection

The Second Letter to the Corinthians is the most personal of all Paul's letters. He wrote it around the year 55 or 56, probably from Macedonia, after a painful time with the church he had founded in Corinth: a visit that went badly, a letter written “with many tears,” rumours that he was unreliable, rivals who mocked his weak appearance. We will read it for the next two weeks, and we will meet a Paul who is tired, wounded and yet full of hope. He does not begin with a defence. He begins with a blessing: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction.” The Greek word for consolation, paraklesis, rings through these few verses again and again. It is the word from which the Holy Spirit gets his name, the Paraclete, the one called to stand beside us. Paul does not say that God keeps him out of trouble. He says that God meets him inside it, and that the comfort he receives is not meant to stop with him: “so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction with the consolation with which we ourselves are consoled by God.” Suffering and comfort both flow outward. “For just as the sufferings of Christ are abundant for us, so also our consolation is abundant through Christ.” The Christian who has been comforted becomes a channel of comfort. The Psalm sings the same experience in the voice of a poor man rescued: “This poor soul cried, and was heard by the Lord, and was saved from every trouble.” And it ends with an invitation: “O taste and see that the Lord is good.”

In the Gospel Jesus sees the crowds, goes up the mountain and sits down, as a teacher sat in his day, and his disciples come close. Matthew wants us to remember Moses, who went up Sinai and brought down the Law. Here a greater than Moses gives the law of the kingdom, and he begins not with a command but with a blessing, eight times over. “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.” The Beatitudes turn the world's idea of happiness upside down. The world calls happy the rich, the strong, the people who never cry and never lose. Jesus calls happy the poor in spirit, who know they need God; the meek; those who hunger and thirst for righteousness; the merciful; the pure in heart; the peacemakers; those persecuted for righteousness' sake. The Beatitudes are first of all a portrait of Jesus himself, the poor, meek, merciful, pure and persecuted Son. They are also a promise. “Those who mourn” are not told to stop crying; they are told “they will be comforted.” Paul and Matthew meet in the same word. The God of all consolation is the one who fulfils the Beatitude. In Kerala this is the week when schools reopen after the summer and the monsoon rain has arrived: new books, new uniforms, wet umbrellas at the gate. The Church opens her own school today, the Sermon on the Mount, and its first lesson is that happiness is a gift of God given to the humble.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we begin to read St Paul's Second Letter to the Corinthians. Paul had been through a very difficult time with this church. People were saying bad things about him; he had been hurt, and he had hurt them with a strong letter. You might expect him to begin with complaints. Instead he begins with praise: Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation (2 Corinthians 1:3).

Notice what Paul does not say. He does not say that God took away all his problems. He says that God consoles us in all our affliction. God does not always take us out of the storm; he comes and sits with us in the boat. And Paul says why: so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction (2 Corinthians 1:4). The comfort God gives us is not a private treasure. It is meant to be passed on.

Think of the people who comforted you best when you were in pain. Very often they were people who had suffered something similar. A mother who had lost a child, a man who had lost his job, a friend who had been sick. Their own wounds, healed by God, made them gentle with yours. That is what Paul means. Our sufferings, united with the sufferings of Christ, can become a source of comfort for others.

In the Gospel Jesus begins the Sermon on the Mount with a list of surprising blessings. Blessed are the poor in spirit. Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted (Matthew 5:4). The world does not call these people happy. Jesus does, because God is close to them. This week, as children go back to school, let us go back to the school of Jesus. Let us be humble, merciful, peacemaking. And let us pass on the comfort we have received. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever fallen down and hurt your knee? Who came running to help you? Maybe your mother, or a friend. St Paul says God is the God of all comfort: he comforts us when we are sad, so that we can comfort others. And Jesus told his friends on a mountain who the truly happy people are: people who are kind, gentle, who make peace and who have clean hearts. This week at school, look for a child who is sad or alone, and be a comforter for Jesus!

Prayer

Blessed are you, God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, Father of mercies and God of all consolation. You have comforted us in our troubles more times than we can count. Make us channels of your comfort to all who are afflicted: the sick, the grieving, the lonely, the poor. Lord Jesus, you taught on the mountain that the poor in spirit, the meek and the merciful are blessed; give us hearts like yours, emptied of pride and open to your kingdom. Bless the children and teachers beginning a new school year, and keep them safe in the rains. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This week the weekday Mass begins two great readings side by side: St Paul’s Second Letter to the Corinthians and the Sermon on the Mount. Today Paul opens his letter with a blessing of “the God of all consolation”, who comforts us in affliction so that we may comfort others. In the Gospel Jesus sits on the mountain and calls blessed the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek and the persecuted. Paul shows what the Beatitudes look like in a real life, and Psalm 34 sums up the day: the Lord is close to the brokenhearted; taste and see that he is good.

2 Corinthians 1:1–7 · The God of all consolation

Setting.

Paul wrote this letter from Macedonia, most likely in the mid fifties of the first century, after a hard time with the church he had founded at Corinth. There had been a painful visit and a letter written “with many tears” (2:4). Some in Corinth doubted his authority and mocked his weakness. Many scholars think that 2 Corinthians as we have it gathers more than one of Paul’s letters to Corinth; others defend its unity. Either way, its voice is Paul at his most personal.

Ancient letters began with a greeting and usually a thanksgiving. Paul turns the thanksgiving into a blessing of God, in the form of the Jewish prayer “Blessed are you, Lord”. In five verses the word “consolation” and its verb come again and again.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: greeting from Paul and Timothy to the church in Corinth and all Achaia.
  • vv. 3–4: God is blessed as the Father of mercies, who consoles us so that we can console others.
  • vv. 5–7: the sufferings of Christ overflow to us, and so does his consolation; Paul and the Corinthians share both.

Key words.

  • paraklēsis, consolation, encouragement: The word means comfort, but also strengthening and urging on: someone called to stand beside you. It belongs to the same family as Paraclete, the name Jesus gives the Holy Spirit in John’s Gospel (John 14:16).
  • thlipsis, affliction: Literally pressure, being crushed. Paul uses it for persecution, hardship and inner distress. The next verses speak of an affliction in Asia so heavy that he “despaired of life itself” (1:8).
  • koinōnos, sharer, partner: The Corinthians are partners in Paul’s sufferings and in his consolation (v. 7). The same root gives koinōnia, communion.

Verse by verse.

“the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation,” (1:3)

Two names for God built on the Old Testament. In Hebrew, mercies (raḥamim) are linked with the word for a mother’s womb. God’s mercy is tender, close and active.

“who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction” (1:4)

Consolation is not meant to stop with us. The comfort we receive becomes a gift we can pass on. Those who have been through grief often become the best comforters of others.

“For just as the sufferings of Christ are abundant for us, so also our consolation is abundant through Christ.” (1:5)

The sufferings of the apostle are joined to Christ’s own. Paul does not say that faith removes suffering; he says that in Christ suffering and comfort both overflow.

“Our hope for you is unshaken;” (1:7)

After all the tension with Corinth, Paul begins with hope, not with complaint. This is a lesson for every pastor and parent.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this passage the Christian meaning of suffering. St John Paul II, in his apostolic letter on suffering, Salvifici Doloris (1984), wrote that those who share in the sufferings of Christ also share in his consolation and in the work of redemption. The Catechism teaches that the Holy Spirit is the Paraclete, the Consoler (CCC 692). Every visit to the sick, every word of comfort at a funeral and every patient bearing of pain can become what Paul describes: consolation received from God and handed on.

Matthew 5:1–12 · Blessed are the poor in spirit

Setting.

Matthew gathers Jesus’ teaching into five great discourses, and the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7) is the first. Jesus goes up the mountain and sits down to teach, as a rabbi sat. Many commentators see Jesus here as a new Moses, who received the Law on Sinai; but Jesus does not receive the word, he speaks it with his own authority.

The Beatitudes follow a form well known from the Psalms and the wisdom books: “Happy are those who …” (Psalm 1:1). Jesus fills that old form with the good news of the kingdom.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Jesus goes up the mountain, sits down and teaches the disciples, with the crowds listening.
  • vv. 3–10: eight Beatitudes, framed by the same promise, “theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (vv. 3 and 10).
  • vv. 11–12: a ninth, spoken directly to the disciples: blessed are you when you are persecuted for my sake.

Key words.

  • makarios, blessed, happy: Not a wish but a declaration. Jesus announces that these people are already in God’s favour, because the kingdom is coming to them.
  • ptōchoi tō pneumati, poor in spirit: In the Old Testament the poor (‘anawim) are those who have nothing to rely on but God (Psalm 34:6; Zephaniah 3:12). Matthew’s “in spirit” shows the inner attitude: humble dependence on God, which material poverty often teaches.
  • basileia tōn ouranōn, kingdom of heaven: Matthew’s usual phrase. Following the Jewish custom of reverence, “heaven” stands for God. It means God’s reign, not only a place after death.

Verse by verse.

“he went up the mountain; and after he sat down, his disciples came to him.” (5:1)

The scene recalls Sinai. The disciples come close, and the crowds also hear him (7:28). The Sermon is first of all for those who follow him.

“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” (5:3)

The first Beatitude is the door to all the others. Only an empty hand can receive the kingdom as a gift.

“Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth.” (5:5)

Almost word for word from Psalm 37:11. The meek are not weak; they do not seize by force what God will give.

“Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God.” (5:8)

In the Bible the heart is the centre of the person, where decisions are made. Purity of heart means an undivided heart, wanting God alone. Its promise is the highest of all: the vision of God.

“Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven,” (5:12)

Joy in persecution is possible because the disciple stands in the line of the prophets and belongs to Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls the Beatitudes the heart of Jesus’ preaching; they depict the face of Jesus Christ and portray his love (CCC 1716–1717). Christ himself is the poor, meek, merciful and persecuted one. St Augustine, in his commentary on the Sermon on the Mount, called it a perfect standard of the Christian life, and he linked the Beatitudes with the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit (in substance). The saints are simply the Beatitudes lived out in very different lives.

Questions people ask

Are the Beatitudes commands or promises?

Both, in a way. In form they are promises: Jesus declares people blessed and tells what God will give them. But they also describe the kind of person a disciple becomes, and so they call us to conversion. The Catechism says they respond to the desire for happiness that God has placed in every human heart (CCC 1718).
