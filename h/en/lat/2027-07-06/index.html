Word for the day

“When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd. Then he said to his disciples, “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.”” Matthew 9:36–38

Reflection

Twenty years have passed since Jacob ran away from Esau, and now he is coming home. News has reached him that his brother is coming to meet him with four hundred men. Jacob has done what a man can do: he has prayed (Genesis 32:9–12), sent gifts ahead in droves, divided his camp, and sent his wives and children across the ford of the Jabbok. And then: “Jacob was left alone; and a man wrestled with him until daybreak.” The Bible does not explain who the stranger is. When the man cannot win, he strikes Jacob's hip and puts it out of joint. And Jacob, in pain, holds on: “I will not let you go, unless you bless me.” This is the wrestler's prayer. Then comes the question of the name: “What is your name?” “Jacob.” In Hebrew the name means “heel-grabber,” and at his birth it recalled how he clung to Esau's heel. The man gives him a new name: “You shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel, for you have striven with God and with humans, and have prevailed.” Jacob asks for the stranger's name and receives only a blessing; and he calls the place Peniel, “face of God”: “I have seen God face to face, and yet my life is preserved.” “The sun rose upon him as he passed Penuel, limping because of his hip.” The prophet Hosea remembers the story and adds a detail that Genesis does not give: “He strove with the angel and prevailed, he wept and sought his favor.” Prayer, says the Catechism, can be a struggle in the night, in which we do not let go until God blesses us (Catechism 2573). The one who wrestled and was wounded walks away with a blessing and a limp: he who grabbed at the heel is now the father of the people of Israel. And the Israelites to this day do not eat the thigh muscle, in remembrance. We honour the Jewish people who bear that name. The psalm adds the prayer of a man who has come through the night and looks forward to the morning: “As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake I shall be satisfied, beholding your likeness.”

Matthew is gathering the Lord's mighty works, and this is the last of the series. A man who is mute and possessed is brought to Jesus; the demon is driven out; “the one who had been mute spoke.” The crowds are astonished: “Never has anything like this been seen in Israel.” But some of the Pharisees say, “By the ruler of the demons he casts out the demons.” This is the first sign of the opposition that will grow in the Gospel, and it is a warning that a heart may see a miracle and still close itself. Then Matthew gives a summary of the Lord's work: Jesus “went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and proclaiming the good news of the kingdom, and curing every disease and every sickness.” And a sentence follows that shows his heart: “When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” The Greek verb for compassion means to be moved in one's innermost being, in the gut. The crowds are people who have been worn down by hunger, taxes, illness and false leaders, and Jesus feels it. He takes up the promise of the prophet Ezekiel: “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep ... I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak.” And then he turns to his disciples: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” The first thing he asks is that we pray, and in the next chapter the Twelve will be sent. Many people in India, and in Kerala especially, know what a harvest is, and know that the rains are only the start. The harvest of souls is also great today, and it needs workers, priests, religious, catechists and lay people. St Maria Goretti, whom the Church remembers today, was a field that bore a harvest at a very early age. Born in 1890 in a poor farming family in Italy, she was an eleven-year-old girl when, on 5 July 1902, a young man living in the same house, Alessandro Serenelli, tried to force her into sin. She refused: “No, it is a sin. God does not want it.” He stabbed her repeatedly, and she died the next day in the hospital at Nettuno, forgiving him. Alessandro was sentenced to thirty years in prison; some years later he dreamed that Maria offered him lilies, and he changed. Released in 1929, he asked her mother Assunta for forgiveness, and was present at Maria's canonisation in 1950. He died in 1970, as a lay brother of the Capuchins. Her life shows both the cost of purity and the power of forgiveness.

Full homily

Dear friends, have you ever prayed all night? Not just words, but a real struggle? Jacob did. He is returning home after twenty years, and his brother Esau is coming with four hundred men. Jacob has prayed, sent gifts, arranged his camp. And now, alone at the river in the dark, a man wrestles with him until morning (Genesis 32:25). We do not know exactly who he is. But in the end Jacob says: I have seen God face to face.

Why does God wrestle with Jacob? Because Jacob has to be changed. All his life, he has gripped, grabbed and bargained. That is the meaning of his name: heel-grabber. Now he is gripping God. And God lets him, because this struggle is a prayer. The man touches his hip, and Jacob will limp for the rest of his life. And still he says: I will not let you go, unless you bless me (Genesis 32:26). And God blesses him and gives him a new name: Israel. He walks away limping, but he walks away blessed.

Dear friends, are there nights in your life like that? A sick child, a failing business, a broken marriage, a doubt that will not leave? Do not be afraid to wrestle with God. He can take it. And he does not mind if we say, I will not let you go. In the morning we may be limping, but we will have a blessing, and we will have seen his face.

In the Gospel Jesus walks through the towns and villages, and he sees the crowds. And Matthew says he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36). What does Jesus do with that compassion? He turns to his disciples and says: The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest (Matthew 9:37–38). Notice, he does not say: go! He says: pray! The first step is prayer. Let us pray today, for priests, religious sisters, brothers, catechists and for our own children, that they may hear the call.

Today the Church remembers St Maria Goretti. She was eleven. She was attacked by a young man in her own home, and she said no, because she knew that it was a sin. She died the next day, forgiving him. And that forgiveness bore fruit: Alessandro repented, and years later he knelt beside her mother, asking pardon. That is a harvest. A girl of eleven, a poor farm child, was a labourer in the Lord's harvest. May all our young people have her courage and her forgiving heart.

For children

Children, Jacob was alone by a river in the night, and a stranger wrestled with him until morning. Jacob would not let go! He said: bless me! And God blessed him and gave him a new name, Israel. Jacob walked away with a limp, but happy, because he had seen God. When we pray for something, we can keep on praying, like Jacob. Today we remember St Maria Goretti. She was only eleven. When someone wanted her to do something bad, she said: no, God does not want it! And before she died, she forgave the man who hurt her. Let us say no to sin, and let us forgive.

Prayer

God of Jacob, who wrestled with your servant in the night and blessed him with a new name, teach us to hold on to you in prayer, even when it costs us, and to walk in the morning of your blessing. Lord Jesus, shepherd of the harassed and helpless, move our hearts with your compassion, and send labourers into your harvest. Through the intercession of St Maria Goretti, who kept her purity and forgave her murderer, give our young people a love for what is pure and a heart ready to forgive. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two portraits of compassion and struggle. In the dark at the river Jabbok, Jacob wrestles with a mysterious stranger until dawn, refuses to let go until he is blessed, and leaves limping with a new name, Israel. In the Gospel Jesus sees crowds “harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd”, and tells his disciples to pray for workers; in the next verse he will send them. Psalm 17 gives the hope of both: “I shall behold your face in righteousness.”

Genesis 32:22–32 (Lectionary 32:23–33) · I will not let you go unless you bless me

Setting.

After twenty years with Laban, Jacob is going home, and he learns that Esau is coming to meet him with four hundred men (32:6). He is afraid. He prays one of the great prayers of Genesis (32:9–12), sends gifts ahead, and on the last night divides his family and possessions across the ford of the Jabbok. Then he is left alone.

Background. The Hebrew carries a play on three words: the river Jabbok (yabbōq), Jacob (ya‘ăqōb), and “wrestled” (ye’ābēq). The mysterious opponent is called simply “a man”. Jacob later says he has “seen God face to face”, and the prophet Hosea speaks of the same story as a struggle with God and with the angel (Hosea 12:3–4).

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24a: Jacob sends everyone across the ford and stays alone.
  • vv. 24b–26a: the night wrestling; the wound at the hip.
  • vv. 26b–29: “I will not let you go unless you bless me”; the change of name; the stranger will not give his own name.
  • vv. 30–32: Peniel; Jacob limps into the sunrise; the custom about the thigh muscle.

Key words.

  • yiśrā’ēl, Israel: The text explains the name as “you have striven with God”. Scholars note that it more likely means “God strives” or “God rules”, but the biblical explanation is what shapes the story. The people of Israel inherit a name that means struggle and blessing together.
  • pĕnî-’ēl, Peniel, face of God: The place where Jacob saw God’s face and lived. The next chapter returns to this when he meets Esau and says that seeing his brother’s face is “like seeing the face of God” (33:10).
  • bārak, bless: Jacob’s demand. After a life of seizing a blessing by trickery, he now asks for it openly, in the dark, from one who holds it.

Verse by verse.

“Jacob was left alone; and a man wrestled with him until daybreak.” (32:24)

The stranger comes when Jacob is alone and at the end of his own resources. In the Bible, encounters with God often come in such places.

“I will not let you go, unless you bless me.” (32:26)

Wounded, Jacob will not give up. His persistence is not arrogance but need: he knows that his future depends on the blessing.

“You shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel, for you have striven with God and with humans, and have prevailed.” (32:28)

A new name is a new identity. Jacob, “the heel-grabber”, becomes Israel, and the people who bear his name will carry the same story of struggle, blessing and survival.

“For I have seen God face to face, and yet my life is preserved.” (32:30)

In the Old Testament, to see God and live is a wonder (Exodus 33:20; Judges 13:22). Jacob’s limp is the sign that the encounter was real.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that from this account the spiritual tradition of the Church has kept a symbol of prayer as a battle of faith and as a triumph of perseverance (CCC 2573). The story does not teach that we can force God’s hand, but that God invites honest, persistent prayer from those who truly need him, and that such prayer changes the one who prays. Jacob receives both a blessing and a wound: an encounter with God leaves a mark. The Jewish custom still kept of not eating the thigh muscle (gid hanasheh) remembers this night; the Church honours Israel’s faithfulness to the memory of its own story.

Matthew 9:32–38 · The harvest is plentiful

Setting.

Here ends the section of Matthew’s Gospel that shows Jesus’ works of power, chapters 8 and 9: ten miracles, as they are usually counted. Together with the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5 to 7) they show the Messiah in word and deed. The final scene is the healing of a man who could not speak, brought to Jesus by others, followed by Jesus’ summary of his whole ministry, which is almost word for word the same as 4:23. The passage prepares the sending of the Twelve in chapter 10.

Background. The crowds respond with wonder: “Never has anything like this been seen in Israel.” Some Pharisees answer with an accusation, that his power comes from the prince of demons. This charge is repeated in 12:24. Matthew’s Gospel was written when his community, Jewish believers in Jesus, was in painful disagreement with other Jewish leaders; the harsh words belong to that family argument and are not a judgment on the Jewish people.

Structure.

  • vv. 32–33: the mute man speaks; the crowds are amazed.
  • v. 34: the accusation by some Pharisees.
  • v. 35: the summary: teaching, proclaiming, healing.
  • v. 36: Jesus’ compassion for the crowds.
  • vv. 37–38: the harvest and the call to pray for labourers.

Key words.

  • esplanchnisthē, he had compassion: From splanchna, the inner organs, the seat of deep feeling. It is a word in the Gospels used almost only of Jesus (and of characters in his parables who represent God). His compassion moves him, in the body, to act.
  • eskylmenoi kai errimmenoi, harassed and helpless: Literally “worn out and thrown down”: people battered by life and left without anyone to lead them. The picture of the shepherdless sheep recalls Numbers 27:17 and Ezekiel 34.
  • therismos, harvest: A common biblical image of God’s final gathering. The harvest here is not a threat but a promise: God’s people are ready to be gathered, and the workers are few.

Verse by verse.

“Never has anything like this been seen in Israel.” (9:33)

The crowds recognize the newness of what they see. In Matthew they speak in the name of Israel; they are not an enemy of Jesus.

“he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd” (9:36)

Jesus sees what the crowds need before they say it. He sees them as a flock; he is the shepherd whom Israel was promised (Ezekiel 34:23).

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few” (9:37)

Jesus is not complaining about the harvest but about the shortage of workers. The field is ripe; those who will gather it are few.

“therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” (9:38)

The first response to need is prayer. The disciples who are told to pray are in the next verse the ones sent (10:1), so that the praying and the going belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christ’s compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a resplendent sign that God has visited his people (CCC 1503). The Second Vatican Council, drawing on this passage, taught that the duty of fostering vocations belongs to the whole Christian community, which must do so above all by a fully Christian life (Optatam Totius 2). That is why the Church keeps a day of prayer for vocations on the Fourth Sunday of Easter, Good Shepherd Sunday. Every Christian is both asked to pray for labourers and called to be one.

Questions people ask

Who was the man who wrestled with Jacob?

The text calls him “a man”, and Jacob concludes that he has seen God. Hosea 12:4 speaks of an angel. The story keeps the identity veiled: the opponent is God’s own messenger, speaking with God’s authority, who will not give his name (32:29). Christian readers have often seen in him a figure of God drawing close to humanity. What matters is the outcome: Jacob is blessed and renamed Israel.

For study and teaching

  • Jacob would not let go until he was blessed. What blessing am I asking God for, and am I willing to persist in asking?
  • Jesus saw the crowds with compassion. Who around me looks “harassed and helpless”, and what can I do for them or pray for them?
