Word for the day

“There was a leper who came to him and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.” He stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, “I do choose. Be made clean!”” Matthew 8:2–3

Reflection

Twenty-four years have passed since Abram left Haran at the word of the Lord. He is now ninety-nine years old, and the son he was promised has still not been born. Then God appears to him again and gives him a name for himself: “I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless.” The Hebrew is El Shaddai, the God who is enough. The command is simple and searching: live your whole life before my face. Then God renews the covenant and seals it in two ways. First, a new name: Abram, “exalted father,” becomes Abraham, “father of a multitude” (Genesis 17:5), and Sarai becomes Sarah, “princess.” In the Bible a new name means a new mission. Second, a sign in the flesh: “This is my covenant, which you shall keep, between me and you and your offspring after you: Every male among you shall be circumcised.” The covenant is not only an idea in the mind; it is carried in the body, in the family, from generation to generation. Then comes the promise that seems impossible: “I will bless her, and moreover I will give you a son by her.” Abraham falls on his face, and laughs. “Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? Can Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?” He even suggests an easier plan: “O that Ishmael might live in your sight!” God does not reject Ishmael; he blesses him and promises to make him a great nation. But the covenant will pass through the child of the impossible: “your wife Sarah shall bear you a son, and you shall name him Isaac.” Isaac means “he laughs.” Abraham's laugh of disbelief will become the laugh of joy at his son's birth. St Paul will say that Abraham “grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God, being fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised” (Romans 4:20–21). The Psalm paints the blessing of a home that walks before the Lord: “Happy is everyone who fears the LORD, who walks in his ways,” with children “like olive shoots around your table.”

The Sermon on the Mount is over. “When Jesus had come down from the mountain, great crowds followed him.” St Matthew now shows us that the one who taught with authority also acts with authority: chapters 8 and 9 are a chain of ten mighty works. The first is a meeting with a man nobody wanted to meet. The Law of Moses required a person with a leprous disease to wear torn clothes, cover his lip and cry out “Unclean, unclean,” and to “live alone” outside the camp (Leviticus 13:45–46). The disease was feared; the loneliness was perhaps worse. This man breaks the distance. He comes, kneels, and says one of the humblest prayers in the Gospel: “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.” He does not doubt that Jesus can; he only leaves it to Jesus' will. And Jesus does what no one had done for years: “He stretched out his hand and touched him.” By the Law, touching a leper made a person unclean. Here the opposite happens: the clean hand of Jesus does not become unclean; the man becomes clean. “I do choose. Be made clean!” Then Jesus sends him to the priest, to offer “the gift that Moses commanded” (Leviticus 14). Jesus does not despise the Law; he fulfils it, and gives the man back his place in the worshipping community. Abraham laughed because the promise seemed too great; the leper knelt because he trusted it. Both show us the God who wants to bless: I will establish my covenant, says God to Abraham; I do choose, says Jesus to the leper. In this month of the Sacred Heart, the outstretched hand of Jesus is a picture of his heart: he wants us clean, whole and at home.

Full homily

Dear friends, Abraham is ninety-nine years old. For twenty-four years he has waited for the son God promised. Then God speaks again: I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless (Genesis 17:1). God gives him a new name, Abraham, father of a multitude. And he gives Sarai a new name too, Sarah. A new name means a new beginning, even at ninety-nine.

And what does Abraham do when God promises a son by Sarah? He falls on his face and laughs. Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? (Genesis 17:17). We understand him. We too sometimes laugh quietly at God's promises, or we offer God an easier plan, like Abraham offering Ishmael. But God says: your wife Sarah shall bear you a son, and you shall name him Isaac. Isaac means he laughs. God turns the laugh of doubt into the laugh of joy.

In the Gospel Jesus comes down from the mountain, and a leper comes to him. By the Law he had to live alone, outside the village, crying unclean. Nobody touched him. He kneels and says: Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean (Matthew 8:2). What a beautiful prayer. He does not command Jesus. He trusts both his power and his will.

And Jesus stretches out his hand and touches him. I do choose. Be made clean! (Matthew 8:3). The hand that touched the leper is the hand of the Sacred Heart, whom we honour this month. He chooses to heal, to cleanse, to bring us back. Today let us bring him the thing we have stopped hoping for, and let us stretch out our own hands to someone nobody touches. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago Abraham and Sarah were very, very old, and God said: you will have a baby boy! Abraham laughed. A baby at a hundred years? But God kept his promise, and the baby was called Isaac, which means he laughs. In the Gospel a sick man came to Jesus. Nobody wanted to go near him. But Jesus touched him and made him well. Jesus loves to touch and heal the ones nobody wants. Let us be kind to the child who is left alone at school!

Prayer

God Almighty, you called Abraham to walk before you and be blameless, and you kept your promise when it seemed too late. Give us a faith that does not waver, that grows strong as it gives you glory. Lord Jesus, you came down from the mountain and stretched out your hand to the leper whom everyone avoided; stretch out your hand to us today. Where we are unclean, make us clean; where we are lonely, bring us home; and make our hands like yours, ready to touch those whom others will not touch. Sacred Heart of Jesus, we trust in you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

God renews his covenant with Abraham: he gives Abram and Sarai new names, the sign of circumcision, and the promise of a son, Isaac, though both are old. Abraham falls on his face and laughs. In the Gospel a leper kneels before Jesus and says, “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.” Jesus chooses, and touches him. In both readings God keeps his word where human strength has run out, and Psalm 128 sings of the blessing on the house of those who fear the Lord.

Genesis 17:1, 9–10, 15–22 · Walk before me, and be blameless

Setting.

Twenty-four years have passed since God first called Abram from Haran (12:4). Ishmael, the son of Hagar, is now thirteen. God appears again and makes the covenant solemn and lasting: Abram becomes Abraham, Sarai becomes Sarah, every male is to be circumcised, and the son of the promise will be born of Sarah within a year. Many scholars think this chapter comes from a priestly tradition in Israel that loved covenants, names and signs. The Lectionary leaves out the verses on the new name of Abraham and the details of the rite.

Structure.

  • v. 1: God Almighty appears: “walk before me, and be blameless.”
  • vv. 9–10: Abraham’s side of the covenant: circumcision of every male.
  • vv. 15–16: Sarai becomes Sarah; she will bear a son and be the mother of nations.
  • vv. 17–18: Abraham laughs, and prays for Ishmael.
  • vv. 19–22: the son will be called Isaac; Ishmael too is blessed; the covenant passes through Isaac.

Key words.

  • ʾēl šadday, God Almighty: An ancient name of God used to the patriarchs (compare Exodus 6:3). Its exact meaning is uncertain; the Latin translation and later tradition render it “Almighty”. Here it fits: only the Almighty can give a child to a man of ninety-nine and a woman of ninety.
  • tāmîm, blameless: Whole, complete, without defect. The same word is used for an animal fit for sacrifice. God does not ask Abraham for a flawless record but for a whole heart, a life lived entirely before him.
  • yiṣḥāq, Isaac: The name means “he laughs”. Abraham laughs here (v. 17), Sarah will laugh at Mamre (18:12), and at the birth Sarah will say, “God has brought laughter for me” (21:6). Doubt becomes joy.

Verse by verse.

“I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless.” (17:1)

The covenant begins with God naming himself, and then with a call. To walk before someone is to live in his sight, as a servant walks before a king.

“Every male among you shall be circumcised.” (17:10)

The sign is cut into the body of every family. It marks the people as belonging to God for all generations.

“Then Abraham fell on his face and laughed,” (17:17)

He bows in worship and laughs in disbelief at the same moment. God does not withdraw the promise; he names the child after the laughter.

“As for Ishmael, I have heard you; I will bless him” (17:20)

Abraham’s prayer for his first son is heard. Ishmael is not the son of the covenant, but he is not forgotten. The name Ishmael means “God hears” (16:11).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Abraham the model of the obedience of faith, “the father of all who believe” (CCC 145–146), and says that because he walked in God’s presence and in covenant with him he was ready to welcome the mysterious Guest at Mamre (CCC 2571). The Church reads circumcision as a sign that prepared for baptism: Jesus himself was circumcised as a son of Abraham, and his circumcision prefigures the “circumcision of Christ”, which is baptism (CCC 527; Colossians 2:11–12). God’s covenant with Abraham’s descendants remains, for “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29).

Matthew 8:1–4 · I do choose. Be made clean!

Setting.

Jesus comes down from the mountain after the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7). Matthew now gathers ten mighty works in chapters 8–9. Having shown Jesus as teacher with authority, he shows him as healer with authority. The first person healed is a man shut out from ordinary life.

Background. The law of Leviticus 13–14 deals with skin diseases that made a person ritually unclean. The Greek word lepra covered several skin conditions, not only what we call leprosy today. The sick person lived apart, and if healed showed himself to the priest and offered the prescribed sacrifice before returning to the community.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the crowds follow Jesus down from the mountain.
  • v. 2: the leper kneels: “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.”
  • v. 3: Jesus stretches out his hand, touches him, and he is cleansed.
  • v. 4: say nothing; go to the priest; offer the gift Moses commanded.

Key words.

  • prosekynei, knelt before him: The word means to bow down in homage, and Matthew uses it for worship of God (4:10) and of Jesus (2:11; 28:9, 17). The leper approaches as one who believes.
  • thelō, I choose, I will: The leper does not doubt Jesus’ power, only his will. Jesus answers with the same word: “I do choose.” His will is to heal.
  • katharizō, make clean: More than healing of the skin: it means restoration to worship and to the community. That is why the man must go to the priest.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.” (8:2)

A prayer of complete trust. The man leaves everything to Jesus’ will. Christians have prayed in these words for centuries.

“He stretched out his hand and touched him,” (8:3)

Jesus could have healed with a word, as he will for the centurion’s servant. He chooses to touch the one no one touched. Holiness here does not fear contact with impurity; it overcomes it.

“go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded,” (8:4)

Jesus respects the law of Moses. The healing is to be confirmed in the proper way, so that the man can take his place again among his people and in worship.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism speaks of Christ’s compassion towards the sick and his many healings as a sign that God has visited his people (CCC 1503), and notes that the sick try to touch him, for power comes from him (CCC 1504). The Church continues his touch in the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick, and in every hand stretched out to those whom illness or stigma has set apart. Many missionaries, such as St Damien of Molokai, gave their lives to people with leprosy because of this Gospel.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus tell the man to say nothing to anyone?

In Matthew, as in Mark, Jesus often asks that his mighty works not be spread about. He does not want to be followed as a wonder-worker or a political Messiah. His identity will be fully shown only on the Cross and in the Resurrection. The first duty of the healed man is to go to the priest and give thanks to God.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham was told to “walk before me, and be blameless.” What would it mean for me to live this day consciously before God?
  • Who in my parish or neighbourhood is “untouchable” in practice? How can I stretch out my hand to them?
