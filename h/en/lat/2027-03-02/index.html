Word for the day

“Then Peter came and said to him, “Lord, if another member of the church sins against me, how often should I forgive? As many as seven times?” Jesus said to him, “Not seven times, but, I tell you, seventy-seven times.”” Matthew 18:21–22

Reflection

The first reading comes from the part of Daniel preserved in Greek, the prayer of Azariah in the fiery furnace. Three young Jews have refused to worship the golden statue of King Nebuchadnezzar and have been thrown into the flames. There, walking in the fire, Azariah does not complain or argue. He confesses: we have sinned; we have no ruler, no prophet, no leader, no burnt offering, no sacrifice, “no place to make an offering before you and to find mercy.” The Temple is gone. Everything Israel used to bring to God has been taken away. And so Azariah offers the only thing left: “Yet with a contrite heart and a humble spirit may we be accepted, as though it were with burnt offerings of rams and bulls… such may our sacrifice be in your sight today.” This is one of the great discoveries of the Old Testament, and one of the great lessons of Lent: when we have nothing else to give, God accepts a broken and humble heart. Azariah appeals not to his own goodness but to God's faithfulness, “for the sake of Abraham your beloved,” and to his “abundant mercy.” The Psalm makes the same plea: “Be mindful of your mercy, O LORD… Do not remember the sins of my youth.”

The Gospel shows what such mercy must do in us. Peter thinks he is being generous when he offers to forgive seven times. Jesus answers: “Not seven times, but… seventy-seven times,” that is, without counting. Then he tells the story of a servant who owed the king ten thousand talents, a debt so vast that a labourer could not repay it in many lifetimes. The king, “out of pity for him,” forgives it all. The same servant goes out, finds a fellow servant who owes him a hundred denarii, a few months' wages, seizes him by the throat and throws him into prison. The king's verdict is simple: “Should you not have had mercy on your fellow slave, as I had mercy on you?” The parable is not about a God who loves to punish; it is about a heart that has received mercy and refuses to let it flow. We pray every day, “forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us.” Lent asks us to make those words true. Forgiving from the heart does not mean pretending that a wrong was not wrong, or staying in danger; it means letting go of revenge and handing the debt over to God, as he has cancelled ours.

Homily outline

Theme: “Not seven times, but seventy-seven times.”

A contrite heart in the furnace, and the mercy that must flow on.

1. Prayer in the furnace

  • No temple, no sacrifice.
  • Confession, not complaint.
  • For the sake of Abraham your beloved.
“Be mindful of your mercy, O LORD, and of your steadfast love, for they have been from of old. Do not remember the sins of my youth or my transgressions;” Psalm 25:6–7
“God's love was revealed among us in this way: God sent his only Son into the world so that we might live through him.” 1 John 4:9

Apply: When you feel you have nothing to offer God, offer him that emptiness in prayer.

2. A contrite heart and a humble spirit

  • The only sacrifice left.
  • God does not despise the broken.
  • The heart of Lent.
“The sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise.” Psalm 51:17
“But he gives all the more grace; therefore it says, “God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.”” James 4:6

Apply: Pray Psalm 51 slowly once this week, as Azariah would have prayed it.

3. Seventy-seven times

  • Peter counts.
  • Ten thousand talents and a hundred denarii.
  • Forgiving from the heart.
“Forgive your neighbor the wrong he has done, and then your sins will be pardoned when you pray.” Sirach 28:2
“Should you not have had mercy on your fellow slave, as I had mercy on you?” Matthew 18:33

Apply: Pray the Our Father slowly tonight and stop at the words “as we forgive”; name before God the person you need to forgive.

God accepts a humble heart when we have nothing else to give.

The mercy we receive must become the mercy we give.

Take-home resolution: Forgive one person from the heart before the end of this week.

Full homily

Dear friends, the young men in the furnace had lost everything: their land, their Temple, their sacrifices. In the fire Azariah prays: we have no place to make an offering before you and to find mercy. Yet with a contrite heart and a humble spirit may we be accepted (Daniel 3:38–39).

This is good news for us. There are days when we feel we have nothing to bring to God: no virtues, no success, only failures. God accepts that broken heart. The sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit (Psalm 51:17).

But mercy received must become mercy given. Peter asks: how often should I forgive? Seven times? Jesus says: not seven times, but seventy-seven times (Matthew 18:22). Then he tells of a servant forgiven a debt he could never pay, who goes out and seizes a fellow servant by the throat over a small sum.

The king asks him: should you not have had mercy on your fellow slave, as I had mercy on you? (Matthew 18:33). Each day we pray: forgive us our trespasses as we forgive those who trespass against us. This Lent, let us cancel one debt from the heart. It may be hard; ask for the grace, and God will give it. Amen.

For children

Children, Peter asked Jesus: how many times do I have to forgive? Jesus said: seventy-seven times! That means every time. When someone hurts you and says sorry, say: I forgive you. Jesus forgives us every time too!

Prayer

Lord God, merciful and faithful, you did not abandon the young men in the furnace, and you accept a contrite heart and a humble spirit. Remember your mercies, forget the sins of our youth, and teach us to forgive as we have been forgiven, not seven times but without counting. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.
