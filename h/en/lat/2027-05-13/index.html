Word for the day

“As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” John 17:21

Reflection

Paul has been arrested in the Temple in Jerusalem, and the Roman tribune, who “wanted to find out what Paul was being accused of,” brings him before the council. Paul looks at the assembly and sees two groups. The Sadducees, the priestly party, held that there is no resurrection, no angel, no spirit; the Pharisees believed in all three. So Paul cries out: “Brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. I am on trial concerning the hope of the resurrection of the dead.” This is not a trick. It is the heart of his message: Jesus is risen, and in him the hope of Israel for the resurrection has begun to come true. The council divides, and some scribes of the Pharisees even say, “We find nothing wrong with this man.” Luke is careful: the quarrel is a debate within Israel about Israel's own hope, not a story of one people against another. The meeting ends in uproar, and the soldiers drag Paul to the barracks. Then, in the night, comes the real word of the day: “the Lord stood near him and said, ‘Keep up your courage! For just as you have testified for me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness also in Rome.’” The Psalm puts that night into a prayer: “I keep the Lord always before me; because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved.” And it ends with the hope Paul preached: “You show me the path of life. In your presence there is fullness of joy.”

In the Gospel Jesus lifts his eyes beyond the Upper Room, beyond the apostles, to us: “I ask not only on behalf of these, but also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one.” Every Christian who has ever believed through the preaching of the apostles was in that prayer. The unity he asks for is not a mere agreement; it is a share in the life of the Holy Trinity: “As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us.” And it has a purpose: “so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” Divided Christians make the Gospel hard to believe; Christians who love one another make it shine. Today the Church also remembers Our Lady of Fatima. On 13 May 1917, during the First World War, three shepherd children of Portugal, Lucia dos Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto, saw a Lady at the Cova da Iria who asked them to pray the Rosary every day for peace and to offer prayers and sacrifices for sinners. The Church approved the apparitions in 1930. On 13 May 1981 St John Paul II was shot in St Peter's Square; he believed (in substance) that a mother's hand had guided the bullet, and a year later he went to Fatima to give thanks. Francisco and Jacinta were canonised there by Pope Francis on 13 May 2017. Mary's message at Fatima is the Gospel's own: prayer, conversion, and the peace that comes when hearts are made one in her Son.

Full homily

Dear friends, imagine Paul in the barracks of Jerusalem that night. He has been beaten, arrested, shouted at in the council. He is alone. And then the Lord stood near him and said, Keep up your courage! For just as you have testified for me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness also in Rome (Acts 23:11). The risen Jesus does not take the danger away. He stands beside his servant in it.

Why was Paul on trial? He said it himself: I am on trial concerning the hope of the resurrection of the dead (Acts 23:6). That is our hope too. Death does not have the last word. The Psalm sings it: you show me the path of life; in your presence there is fullness of joy (Psalm 16:11). When we are afraid, we can say with the psalmist: I keep the Lord always before me; I shall not be moved.

In the Gospel, Jesus prays for us by name, so to speak: not only for these, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one (John 17:20–21). You and I were in that prayer on the night before he died. And he asks for a unity like that of the Father and the Son, so that the world may believe. Our quarrels in families and parishes are not small things; they hide Jesus from the world. Our love makes him visible.

Today we also remember Our Lady of Fatima. To three shepherd children in a time of war she asked for simple things: pray the Rosary every day, pray for sinners, pray for peace. Let us do what she asked. As we wait for Pentecost, let us gather with Mary in prayer, so that the Holy Spirit may make us one and give us courage to bear witness, in Jerusalem, in Rome, and wherever the Lord sends us. Amen.

For children

Children, Paul was in big trouble and all alone. But that night Jesus came and stood beside him and said: be brave! Jesus is beside you too when you are afraid. Jesus also prayed that all his friends would be one big loving family. And at Fatima, Mother Mary asked three children to pray the Rosary for peace. Can you say one Hail Mary for peace today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, risen from the dead, you stood beside Paul in the night and said: Keep up your courage. Stand beside us in our fears and keep us steady, for you are at our right hand. You prayed to the Father for all who would believe through the apostles' word: make us one, as you and the Father are one, so that the world may believe. Through the prayers of Our Lady of Fatima, Mother of mercy, give us hearts converted, faithful in prayer and eager for peace. Come, Holy Spirit. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul stands before the council in Jerusalem and cries out that he is on trial for the hope of the resurrection of the dead. The council divides, the tribune has to rescue him, and that night the Lord stands near him: as he has borne witness in Jerusalem, so he must bear witness in Rome. In the Gospel Jesus prays for those who will believe through the apostles’ word, that is, for us: that we may be one, and be with him where he is. Psalm 16, which Peter quoted on Pentecost of the risen Christ, sings: “you do not give me up to Sheol” (Psalm 16:10). Today is also the optional memorial of Our Lady of Fatima.

Acts 22:30; 23:6–11 · I am on trial concerning the hope of the resurrection

Setting.

Paul was arrested in the Temple when part of the crowd accused him of bringing Gentiles into it (21:27–36). He spoke to the people (22:1–21), and when the tribune was about to have him flogged, Paul revealed that he was a Roman citizen (22:25–29). The tribune, whose name we later learn is Claudius Lysias (23:26), still wants to know the real charge, so he calls the chief priests and the council. In 22:30 “the Jews” means Paul’s accusers in Jerusalem; Paul himself is a Jew and says so proudly. The Lectionary leaves out 23:1–5, where the high priest Ananias has Paul struck on the mouth.

Background. The council (Sanhedrin) was the high court of the Jewish people in Jerusalem, presided over by the high priest. Two groups sat in it. The Sadducees, mostly from the priestly families, held to the written Law and did not accept the resurrection (see Luke 20:27). The Pharisees, teachers of the Law close to the ordinary people, believed in the resurrection of the dead, in angels and in spirits, as the Jewish historian Josephus also reports.

Structure.

  • 22:30: the tribune releases Paul and brings him before the council.
  • 23:6: Paul’s cry: I am a Pharisee, on trial for the hope of the resurrection.
  • 23:7–9: the council divides; some Pharisee scribes defend him.
  • 23:10: the tribune takes him by force into the barracks.
  • 23:11: the Lord stands near him at night: you must bear witness also in Rome.

Key words.

  • synedrion, council: The Sanhedrin. Jesus, Peter and John, and Stephen had stood before it (Luke 22:66; Acts 4:15; 6:12). Paul now stands where his Lord stood.
  • elpis, hope: For Paul, the hope of Israel and the resurrection belong together (24:15; 26:6–8; 28:20). Christ risen is the first fruits of the resurrection that Israel hoped for (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • dei, must: “So you must bear witness also in Rome.” Luke uses this little word for God’s plan: “Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things?” (Luke 24:26). Paul’s road to Rome is not an accident of politics but part of God’s design.

Verse by verse.

““Brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. I am on trial concerning the hope of the resurrection of the dead.”” (23:6)

Some readers see only a clever move to divide his judges. It is more than that. The resurrection is the centre of Paul’s faith, and it is the point where the Pharisees’ hope and the Gospel meet. Paul calls them “brothers”: he speaks as a son of Israel to his own people.

““We find nothing wrong with this man. What if a spirit or an angel has spoken to him?”” (23:9)

Some Pharisee scribes take Paul’s side, as the Pharisee Gamaliel had once counselled caution about the apostles (5:34–39). Luke shows that the Pharisees were not simply enemies of the Gospel.

“That night the Lord stood near him and said, “Keep up your courage! For just as you have testified for me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness also in Rome.”” (23:11)

Alone in the barracks, Paul is not alone. The Lord’s word fulfils the plan of Acts 1:8: Jerusalem, then the ends of the earth. On Saturday the Lectionary gives the last verses of Acts, with Paul in Rome.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that belief in the resurrection of the dead has been an essential element of the Christian faith from its beginnings, and it quotes Tertullian: the confidence of Christians is the resurrection of the dead (CCC 991). It also recalls that the Pharisees and many of the Lord’s contemporaries hoped for the resurrection, and that Jesus taught it firmly (CCC 993). The Church honours this hope that the Gospel shares with the faith of Israel.

John 17:20–26 · That they may become completely one

Setting.

This is the last part of the prayer of Jesus at the Last Supper, before he crosses the Kidron valley to the garden (18:1). The prayer has three movements: Jesus prays for himself and his glorification (17:1–5), for the disciples who are with him (17:6–19), and now for all who will come to believe through their word (17:20–26).

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: for future believers: one, as the Father and the Son are one, so that the world may believe.
  • vv. 22–23: the glory given, so that they may be completely one, and the world may know.
  • v. 24: that they may be with me and see my glory.
  • vv. 25–26: the world does not know you; I made your name known, so that your love may be in them.

Key words.

  • hen, one: The Greek is neuter, “one thing”, as in “The Father and I are one” (10:30). The unity of believers is modelled on the unity of the Father and the Son, and it comes from it.
  • teteleiōmenoi eis hen, completely one: Literally “made perfect into one”. Unity is not only given at the start; it grows towards completion.
  • thelō, I desire: In v. 24 Jesus does not only ask; he says “I desire”, or “I will”. He speaks as the Son who shares the Father’s will.

Verse by verse.

“I ask not only on behalf of these, but also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word,” (17:20)

Jesus prays for the Church of every age. Each believer comes to faith “through their word”, the word of the apostles handed on in the Church. Before we prayed, we were prayed for.

“that they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” (17:21)

Unity is not sameness; it is communion, like the communion of the Father and the Son. And it has a purpose: the world’s faith. Division among Christians makes the Gospel harder to believe.

“Father, I desire that those also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory,” (17:24)

The goal of the whole prayer is to be with Christ. Heaven is first of all a person and a presence: “with me where I am”.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council opens its decree on ecumenism by saying that division among Christians openly contradicts the will of Christ and is a scandal to the world (Unitatis Redintegratio 1), and it quotes this prayer (UR 2). Saint John Paul II took the title of his encyclical on Christian unity, Ut Unum Sint (1995), from v. 21. The Council also saw in v. 21–22 a certain likeness between the union of the divine Persons and the union of God’s children in truth and love (Gaudium et Spes 24). The Catechism teaches that Christ gives his Church the gift of unity, but the Church must always pray and work to keep it and complete it (CCC 820).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus’ prayer for unity mean that all Catholics should pray and worship in the same way?

No. The unity of the Church is unity in the one faith, the same sacraments, and the communion of the bishops with the successor of Peter (CCC 815). Within this unity there is a rich variety of rites and traditions. In India the Latin, Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches are one Catholic Church, and the Council teaches that such variety does not harm unity but shows it (Orientalium Ecclesiarum 2).

For the Latin reader

Today the Roman calendar keeps the optional memorial of Our Lady of Fatima. On 13 May 1917, and in the months that followed until October, three shepherd children in Portugal, Lucia dos Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto, saw the Blessed Virgin, who asked for prayer, especially the Rosary, for penance and for the conversion of sinners. Francisco and Jacinta were canonised by Pope Francis at Fatima in 2017. The message of Fatima fits today’s Gospel: Mary, who prayed with the apostles, still calls her children to pray for one another and for the world.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus prayed for me “through their word”. Who handed on the word of faith to me, and how can I thank God for them?
  • Where do I see division among Christians or within my own family, and what one step can I take this week for unity?
