Word for the day

“As he came near and saw the city, he wept over it, saying, “If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes.”” Luke 19:41–42

Reflection

In heaven no one is found worthy to open the sealed scroll of history, and John weeps. Then he is told: the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered. He looks, and sees not a lion but “a Lamb standing as if it had been slaughtered”. Christ's victory is the victory of self-giving love. The heavenly court sings a new song: “by your blood you ransomed for God saints from every tribe and language and people and nation”.

On earth, the Lamb weeps. Coming down the Mount of Olives, Jesus sees Jerusalem and weeps over it: “If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace!” He foresees its destruction (fulfilled in AD 70), “because you did not recognize the time of your visitation”. God's tears are not weakness; they are the grief of love refused. Today too he visits us; the question is whether we recognise him.

Full homily

Dear friends, in heaven no one is found worthy to open the sealed scroll of history, and John weeps bitterly. Then he is told: the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered. He looks, and sees not a lion but a Lamb standing as if it had been slaughtered (Revelation 5:5–6).

This is the surprise at the heart of our faith. Christ's victory is not the victory of force but of self-giving love. The heavenly court sings a new song: by your blood you ransomed for God saints from every tribe and language and people and nation (Revelation 5:9). Kerala, Vietnam, Germany, Italy: all are in that song.

In the Gospel, Jesus comes near Jerusalem, sees the city and weeps over it: if you had only recognised on this day the things that make for peace (Luke 19:42). God does not watch our wars and divisions coldly. He weeps over them.

The Lamb conquers by love, and he calls us to be people of peace. Pray for peace in the world each day this week, and make peace in your own home: one apology, one kind word, one forgiven hurt. Amen.

For children

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Yesterday's vision of the throne continues: in God's right hand is a sealed scroll, and no one can open it until the Lamb who was slaughtered comes forward. In the Gospel Jesus comes near Jerusalem and weeps over the city that did not recognise the time of its visitation. The Lamb who opens the meaning of history is the same Lord who weeps over those who will not receive his peace. Psalm 149 sings the new song of the faithful.

Revelation 5:1–10 · Worthy is the Lamb

Setting.

The scroll in God's right hand holds his plan for history and its judgment. It is written on both sides, like the scroll Ezekiel saw (Ezekiel 2:9–10), and sealed with seven seals, that is, completely closed. The question of the chapter is who can open it: who can make sense of history and bring it to its goal.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the sealed scroll; no one is worthy; John weeps.
  • v. 5: an elder announces the Lion of Judah, the Root of David.
  • vv. 6–7: John sees a Lamb standing as if slaughtered, who takes the scroll.
  • vv. 8–10: the living creatures and the elders fall down and sing a new song.

Key words.

  • arnion, Lamb: Revelation calls Christ the Lamb about thirty times. The title recalls the Passover lamb (Exodus 12) and the servant led like a lamb to the slaughter (Isaiah 53:7).
  • esphagmenon, slaughtered: A sacrificial word. The Lamb is standing, that is, risen, yet he bears the marks of his death. The glorified Christ keeps his wounds (compare John 20:27).
  • agorazein, to ransom, to buy: The language of the market where slaves were bought. Christ's blood paid the price that made us God's own.

Verse by verse.

“See, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered” (5:5)

John hears “Lion”: the messianic titles of Genesis 49:9 and Isaiah 11:1, 10.

“Then I saw between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders a Lamb standing as if it had been slaughtered” (5:6)

He turns and sees a Lamb. This is the heart of the book: the victory of the Lion is the self-giving of the Lamb. God conquers by love that suffers.

“for you were slaughtered and by your blood you ransomed for God saints from every tribe and language and people and nation; you have made them to be a kingdom and priests serving our God” (5:9–10)

Four words for humanity describe a Church gathered from every people. The redeemed share Christ's royal priesthood (Exodus 19:6; 1 Peter 2:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the whole community of believers is priestly, sharing in Christ's priesthood through baptism, and it cites these verses (CCC 1546). At every Mass the priest shows the Host and says, “Behold the Lamb of God… Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb,” joining the words of John the Baptist (John 1:29) to the wedding feast of the Lamb in Revelation (19:9). The golden bowls of incense, “which are the prayers of the saints” (5:8), show that the prayers of the Church on earth rise before God's throne.

Luke 19:41–44 · If you had only recognized the things that make for peace

Setting.

Jesus has come down the Mount of Olives amid the praise of his disciples (19:37–40). As the city comes into view, the shouts give way to tears. Only Luke records this weeping. A small church on the Mount of Olives called Dominus Flevit (“the Lord wept”), built in the shape of a tear, marks the place by tradition. About forty years later, in AD 70, the Roman army under Titus surrounded Jerusalem with a siege wall and destroyed the city and the Temple, as Josephus describes.

Structure.

  • v. 41: Jesus sees the city and weeps.
  • v. 42: the lament: if only you had known the things that make for peace.
  • vv. 43–44: the siege foretold, and its cause: the time of visitation was not recognised.

Key words.

  • klaiein, to weep: Luke uses the word for loud weeping, not quiet tears. The King who is being acclaimed weeps for those who will reject him.
  • eirēnē, peace: The name Jerusalem was popularly heard as “city of peace” (shalom). The city of peace does not see the things that make for peace.
  • episkopē, visitation: God's coming to save. Zechariah sang that God “has looked favorably on his people” (1:68); now God visits them in Jesus himself.

Verse by verse.

“As he came near and saw the city, he wept over it” (19:41)

Like Jeremiah before him (Jeremiah 9:1), Jesus mourns for his own city. His prophecy is a lament, not a curse.

“If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes.” (19:42)
“because you did not recognize the time of your visitation from God.” (19:44)

The tragedy is not that God did not come, but that his coming was not recognised.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes this verse with great care: “Jerusalem did not recognize the time of her visitation”, and adds at once that the Jews remain most dear to God for the sake of their fathers, and that what happened in Christ's Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598). The tears of Jesus are a warning to every disciple and every Church: the day of visitation is always “this day”, and anyone can miss the things that make for peace.

For study and teaching

  • The Lion conquers as a Lamb. Where am I tempted to seek victory by force rather than by self-giving love?
  • What are “the things that make for peace” in my family or community today, and am I recognising them?
