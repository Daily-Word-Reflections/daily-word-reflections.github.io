Word for the day

“As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’” Matthew 10:7

Reflection

The famine that Joseph foretold has come, and it has spread beyond Egypt. When the Egyptians cry to Pharaoh for bread, his answer is only seven words: “Go to Joseph; what he says to you, do.” The young man who was sold as a slave and forgotten in prison now opens the storehouses, and “all the world came to Joseph in Egypt to buy grain.” In Canaan old Jacob hears that there is grain in Egypt and sends ten of his sons, keeping Benjamin at home, for the youngest son of his beloved Rachel is all he has left of her. Then Genesis shows us the scene it has been preparing since chapter 37: “Joseph's brothers came and bowed themselves before him with their faces to the ground.” The dream of the sheaves, which they had mocked, comes true, though not in any way they could have imagined. Joseph recognises them; they do not recognise him. He “treated them like strangers and spoke harshly to them,” and he tests them. It is not cruelty for its own sake. He wants to know whether these men have changed, and whether they will again abandon a favoured brother as they once abandoned him. After three days in prison he sends them home on one condition: one brother stays behind, and the rest must bring Benjamin. And now the guilt that more than twenty years of silence could not bury comes to the surface. “Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother; we saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen.” Reuben adds, “So now there comes a reckoning for his blood.” They suppose that Joseph cannot understand them, since he speaks through an interpreter. But he understands every word, and “he turned away from them and wept.” The reading does not say what the tears meant: sorrow for what was done, or hope that his brothers have begun to repent. Nothing in the story is wasted: the famine, the prison and the awakened conscience are the road by which God will bring the family home. The Psalm names the secret of it: “The counsel of the Lord stands forever,” and “the eye of the Lord is on those who fear him, ... to keep them alive in famine.”

In the Gospel Jesus “summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” St Matthew gives their names, and he gives them with some honesty: Simon Peter and Andrew, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew the tax collector, James son of Alphaeus and Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean, and “Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” A fisherman, a tax collector who worked for Rome, a Cananaean who may have belonged to the zealot party, and a man who would betray his Master: Jesus builds his Church from the most unlikely materials. The number is not an accident. Jacob had twelve sons, and from them came the twelve tribes of Israel; now Jesus calls twelve, the foundation of a renewed people of God. We have just heard about those twelve sons and how their family was almost broken by jealousy and cruelty; here are twelve others who are sent out to bring peace and healing. Then comes the first instruction: “Go nowhere among the Gentiles, and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” This does not close the door on the nations. God's promise came through Abraham to Israel, and the Messiah comes first to his own people, to gather them. After the resurrection the same Jesus will say, “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations” (Matthew 28:19), and the Gospel itself has already shown a Roman centurion and the Magi from the East honoured for their faith. The message the Twelve carry is the message of Jesus himself, and of John the Baptist before him: “The kingdom of heaven has come near.” They do not preach their own wisdom, and they do not heal by their own power; the authority was given to them. Pharaoh sent the hungry to Joseph; Jesus sends his apostles to the hungry of every age, with the word that heals and the bread that feeds. Christian devotion has long heard in Pharaoh's command, “Go to Joseph,” an echo of the trust we place in St Joseph, the guardian of the Holy Family and the protector of the Church. And only four days after the feast of St Thomas, the Apostle of India, we may notice his name in the list, one of the Twelve who were sent.

Full homily

Dear friends, the story of Joseph and his brothers is a great story of conscience. Twenty years and more have gone by. The brothers have a family, flocks and a good name. They have told their father a lie and they have kept it. And then a famine comes, and they stand before a powerful man in Egypt, and suddenly they remember. Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother (Genesis 42:21). Nothing had been forgotten. A sin we do not confess does not die; it sleeps, and it wakes when we are afraid.

Notice what Joseph does. He knows them at once, and he does not take revenge. He tests them. He speaks harshly, he puts them in prison for three days, and then he sends them home, keeping only one of them. Why? Because he wants his brothers back, not their punishment. And when he hears them confess, he turns away and weeps (Genesis 42:24). That is how God works with us too. God sometimes lets the famine come, so that we may stop and remember, and in secret God weeps for us and waits.

The Gospel today begins the long instruction of Jesus to his twelve apostles. He calls them by name. A fisherman, a tax collector, a zealot, even one who will betray him. Jesus gives them authority, and sends them with one message: the kingdom of heaven has come near (Matthew 10:7). It has come near like a brother who forgives, like bread for the hungry, like a door that is open.

What are we sent to do? The same as Joseph and the Twelve: to feed and to forgive. If there is a wrong in your past that you have never put right, this is a good week to go to a brother, to a sister, to a priest in the sacrament of Reconciliation. And if someone has wronged you, remember Joseph's tears. Pharaoh said: go to Joseph. Jesus says to us: go to the lost. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph's brothers were not kind to him. Years later they were hungry, and they came to ask him for food. Joseph knew them, but they did not know him! He wanted to see whether they were sorry. And they were. They said: we were wrong to hurt our brother. Joseph cried when he heard it. In the Gospel Jesus chooses twelve friends and sends them to tell everybody that God is near. Jesus wants us to be his friends too. When we say sorry and forgive each other, we are telling people that God is near!

Prayer

Lord God, you were at work in a famine, in a prison and in a brother's tears, to bring a divided family home. Awaken our conscience when it sleeps, and give us the courage to say: we have sinned. Lord Jesus, you called the Twelve by name and gave them your own authority; you still call ordinary and imperfect people and send them to the lost. Make us messengers of your kingdom, patient with those who wrong us, generous to the hungry, and glad to hear your word: the kingdom of heaven has come near. St Joseph, guardian of the Holy Family, pray for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This week the weekday Mass begins the last great story of Genesis, the story of Joseph and his brothers, and at the same time the mission discourse of St Matthew, chapter 10. Today the famine reaches Egypt and Pharaoh sends the hungry to Joseph, the brother who was sold. In the Gospel Jesus names the Twelve and sends them out with his own authority to proclaim that the kingdom of heaven has come near. Psalm 33 gives the thread: the eye of the Lord is on those who fear him, to deliver them from death and keep them alive in famine.

Genesis 41:55–57; 42:5–7a, 17–24a · Go to Joseph

Setting.

The story of Joseph begins in chapter 37, where the favourite son of Jacob is thrown into a pit by his brothers and sold to traders. In Egypt he rises from slavery and prison to be governor under Pharaoh, after he has interpreted Pharaoh’s dream of seven good years and seven lean years (41:1–36). The lean years have now come.

Background. The reading joins two scenes. First, Egypt is hungry and Joseph opens the storehouses, so that “all the world” comes to him for grain. Then Jacob’s sons come from Canaan, where the famine has also reached, and bow to the ground before the brother they sold, who is now a stranger in Egyptian dress. The dream of Joseph’s boyhood, of sheaves bowing down (37:7), is fulfilled, but Joseph does not use his power for revenge. The passage skips the middle of chapter 42, in which the brothers are accused as spies.

Structure.

  • 41:55–57: Pharaoh sends the starving to Joseph; he opens the storehouses and the whole world comes to him.
  • 42:5–7: the sons of Israel come to buy grain and bow to Joseph, who recognizes them and speaks harshly.
  • 42:17–20: three days in prison, and then the condition: one brother stays, the others bring the youngest.
  • 42:21–24: the brothers speak of their guilt, Reuben reminds them, and Joseph, who understands, turns away and weeps.

Key words.

  • šeber, grain: From the verb to break. Genesis 41–42 uses this one word again and again for grain, as if to say: the bread that breaks the hunger.
  • ʾăšēmîm, guilty: The brothers’ own word in 42:21. After more than twenty years, a hard word from a stranger makes them remember what they did, and say it aloud.
  • mēlîṣ, interpreter: In 42:23 the brothers do not know that Joseph understands them, because an interpreter stands between them. The man whose language they think he cannot follow hears every word of their remorse.

Verse by verse.

“Go to Joseph; what he says to you, do.” (41:55)

Nine words in English, and a pointer for the whole story. Pharaoh sends the hungry to the one who was rejected. Christian readers have often heard in these words an echo of Mary’s word at Cana, “Do whatever he tells you” (John 2:5).

“And Joseph’s brothers came and bowed themselves before him with their faces to the ground.” (42:6)

The reader remembers the dream of chapter 37. The brothers do not know whom they bow to, but Joseph knows.

“Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother; we saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen.” (42:21)

A new detail: only now do we learn that Joseph pleaded with them. Guilt that was buried for more than twenty years is brought up by hardship, and this is the beginning of their change.

“He turned away from them and wept;” (42:24)

The harsh governor weeps in secret. His severity is not hatred. Joseph is testing the brothers, as the following chapters show, and he longs for the family to be whole.

How the Church reads it.

Since early times Christians have seen in Joseph a figure of Christ: the beloved son who is handed over by his brothers, falls low, and then becomes the one who feeds the world. Preachers have often heard in Pharaoh’s word “Go to Joseph” a call to go to Christ, the bread for the hungry. Remember that the story is first the Jewish people’s own story of how Israel came to Egypt and was preserved there by God; Christians read it as part of the one history of salvation (Nostra Aetate 4). The Catechism teaches that God is Lord of history and is able to draw good even from the sin of men (CCC 312, quoting Genesis 50:20).

Matthew 10:1–7 · The Twelve are sent

Setting.

Matthew has gathered Jesus’ teaching into five great discourses. The second is the mission discourse of chapter 10, which we read this week. It is introduced by Jesus’ compassion at the sight of the crowds, “harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd”, and his word that the harvest is plentiful and the labourers few (9:36–38). Chapter 10 is the answer: the Twelve are the labourers.

Background. The number twelve is not accidental. It recalls the twelve tribes of Israel; Jesus is gathering and renewing Israel. The mission is first to “the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (v. 6), as the first stage in the history of salvation, before the Gospel goes to the nations after Easter (Matthew 28:19). The list of names is one of four in the New Testament (compare Mark 3:16–19; Luke 6:14–16; Acts 1:13), and each names Peter first.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Jesus calls the twelve disciples and gives them authority over unclean spirits and every sickness.
  • vv. 2–4: the names of the twelve apostles, in pairs.
  • vv. 5–6: the mission is first to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.
  • v. 7: the message: the kingdom of heaven has come near.

Key words.

  • apostolos, apostle, one who is sent: From the verb to send. Matthew uses this noun only here (10:2). The apostles do not act on their own authority but on that of the one who sends them.
  • exousia, authority: Not their own power but a share in Jesus’ authority over evil spirits and sickness, which Matthew has just shown in chapters 8 and 9.
  • ēngiken, has come near: The same message as that of John the Baptist (3:2) and of Jesus himself (4:17). The kingdom is not only coming; it is at the door.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority” (10:1)

Authority is given, not taken. The disciples go out as those who were first with him.

“Simon, also known as Peter,” (10:2)

Matthew writes “first, Simon”, and so places Peter at the head of the list. Matthew himself is named in v. 3 as “the tax collector”, a reminder that Jesus calls the unlikely.

“Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” (10:4)

The Gospel does not hide that a traitor stood among the Twelve. Even a call from Jesus must be answered every day.

“As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’” (10:7)

The disciples’ task is simple: to proclaim. The healings and the freedom from evil (v. 8) follow the word as signs of the kingdom.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that from the beginning of his public life Jesus chose twelve men to be with him and to share in his mission, giving them a share in his authority (CCC 551), and that the Lord endowed his community with a structure that remains until the kingdom is fully achieved, beginning with the choice of the Twelve with Peter at their head (CCC 765). The kingdom was first announced to the children of Israel and is meant to receive people of all nations (CCC 543). Bishops are sent in the line of the apostles, and priests share in their ministry (CCC 861).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus first send his disciples only to Israel?

In the plan of God, Israel was the people to whom the promises had been given, and the Messiah was sent first to them (compare Romans 15:8). The mission of the Twelve to Israel is the first step; after the Resurrection the risen Lord sends the same disciples to all nations (Matthew 28:19). The earlier command does not close the door on others; it follows the order of God’s revealing.

For study and teaching

  • Joseph wept in secret over the brothers who had harmed him. What does this teach me about dealing with a wrong that was done to me?
  • To whom has Jesus sent me, in my family, my parish or my work, and what is the simple message I am to carry?
