Word for the day

“As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’” Matthew 10:7

Reflection

The famine that Joseph foretold has come, and it has spread beyond Egypt. When the Egyptians cry to Pharaoh for bread, his answer is only seven words: “Go to Joseph; what he says to you, do.” The young man who was sold as a slave and forgotten in prison now opens the storehouses, and “all the world came to Joseph in Egypt to buy grain.” In Canaan old Jacob hears that there is grain in Egypt and sends ten of his sons, keeping Benjamin at home, for the youngest son of his beloved Rachel is all he has left of her. Then Genesis shows us the scene it has been preparing since chapter 37: “Joseph's brothers came and bowed themselves before him with their faces to the ground.” The dream of the sheaves, which they had mocked, comes true, though not in any way they could have imagined. Joseph recognises them; they do not recognise him. He “treated them like strangers and spoke harshly to them,” and he tests them. It is not cruelty for its own sake. He wants to know whether these men have changed, and whether they will again abandon a favoured brother as they once abandoned him. After three days in prison he sends them home on one condition: one brother stays behind, and the rest must bring Benjamin. And now the guilt that more than twenty years of silence could not bury comes to the surface. “Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother; we saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen.” Reuben adds, “So now there comes a reckoning for his blood.” They suppose that Joseph cannot understand them, since he speaks through an interpreter. But he understands every word, and “he turned away from them and wept.” The reading does not say what the tears meant: sorrow for what was done, or hope that his brothers have begun to repent. Nothing in the story is wasted: the famine, the prison and the awakened conscience are the road by which God will bring the family home. The Psalm names the secret of it: “The counsel of the Lord stands forever,” and “the eye of the Lord is on those who fear him, ... to keep them alive in famine.”

In the Gospel Jesus “summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” St Matthew gives their names, and he gives them with some honesty: Simon Peter and Andrew, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew the tax collector, James son of Alphaeus and Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean, and “Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” A fisherman, a tax collector who worked for Rome, a Cananaean who may have belonged to the zealot party, and a man who would betray his Master: Jesus builds his Church from the most unlikely materials. The number is not an accident. Jacob had twelve sons, and from them came the twelve tribes of Israel; now Jesus calls twelve, the foundation of a renewed people of God. We have just heard about those twelve sons and how their family was almost broken by jealousy and cruelty; here are twelve others who are sent out to bring peace and healing. Then comes the first instruction: “Go nowhere among the Gentiles, and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” This does not close the door on the nations. God's promise came through Abraham to Israel, and the Messiah comes first to his own people, to gather them. After the resurrection the same Jesus will say, “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations” (Matthew 28:19), and the Gospel itself has already shown a Roman centurion and the Magi from the East honoured for their faith. The message the Twelve carry is the message of Jesus himself, and of John the Baptist before him: “The kingdom of heaven has come near.” They do not preach their own wisdom, and they do not heal by their own power; the authority was given to them. Pharaoh sent the hungry to Joseph; Jesus sends his apostles to the hungry of every age, with the word that heals and the bread that feeds. Christian devotion has long heard in Pharaoh's command, “Go to Joseph,” an echo of the trust we place in St Joseph, the guardian of the Holy Family and the protector of the Church. And only four days after the feast of St Thomas, the Apostle of India, we may notice his name in the list, one of the Twelve who were sent.

Homily outline

Theme: “The kingdom of heaven has come near.”

A famine, a conscience that wakes, a brother who weeps in secret, and twelve men sent out by Jesus.

1. Go to Joseph

  • The world comes for grain.
  • The pit has become a throne.
  • Whom do we go to in need?
“When all the land of Egypt was famished, the people cried to Pharaoh for bread. Pharaoh said to all the Egyptians, “Go to Joseph; what he says to you, do.”” Genesis 41:55
“His mother said to the servants, “Do whatever he tells you.”” John 2:5

Apply: Bring one real need to Jesus in prayer today, and ask what he wants you to do about it.

2. We are paying the penalty

  • More than twenty years of silence.
  • A sin sleeps, it does not die.
  • Reuben remembers.
“They said to one another, “Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother; we saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen. That is why this anguish has come upon us.”” Genesis 42:21
“If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness.” 1 John 1:9

Apply: Make a good examination of conscience this week and go to confession; do not carry the old weight alone.

3. He turned away and wept

  • A test made of love.
  • Tears kept secret.
  • The God who waits.
“He turned away from them and wept; then he returned and spoke to them.” Genesis 42:24
“Jesus began to weep.” John 11:35

Apply: When you must correct or test someone you love, do it as Joseph did: with the aim of bringing them home.

4. The kingdom has come near

  • Twelve names.
  • Authority given, not owned.
  • Sent first to the lost.
“Truly the eye of the Lord is on those who fear him, on those who hope in his steadfast love, to deliver their soul from death, and to keep them alive in famine.” Psalm 33:18–19
“Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” Matthew 10:1

Apply: Say one sentence this week to someone who is discouraged: God is near you.

God uses famine, a prison and a brother's tears to bring a family back together, and he is patient with a conscience that wakes late.

Jesus calls the Twelve, gives them his own authority and sends them to the lost with the news that the kingdom has come near.

Take-home resolution: Put right one old wrong this week, and speak one word of hope to one person who needs it.

Full homily

Dear friends, the story of Joseph and his brothers is a great story of conscience. Twenty years and more have gone by. The brothers have a family, flocks and a good name. They have told their father a lie and they have kept it. And then a famine comes, and they stand before a powerful man in Egypt, and suddenly they remember. Alas, we are paying the penalty for what we did to our brother (Genesis 42:21). Nothing had been forgotten. A sin we do not confess does not die; it sleeps, and it wakes when we are afraid.

Notice what Joseph does. He knows them at once, and he does not take revenge. He tests them. He speaks harshly, he puts them in prison for three days, and then he sends them home, keeping only one of them. Why? Because he wants his brothers back, not their punishment. And when he hears them confess, he turns away and weeps (Genesis 42:24). That is how God works with us too. God sometimes lets the famine come, so that we may stop and remember, and in secret God weeps for us and waits.

The Gospel today begins the long instruction of Jesus to his twelve apostles. He calls them by name. A fisherman, a tax collector, a zealot, even one who will betray him. Jesus gives them authority, and sends them with one message: the kingdom of heaven has come near (Matthew 10:7). It has come near like a brother who forgives, like bread for the hungry, like a door that is open.

What are we sent to do? The same as Joseph and the Twelve: to feed and to forgive. If there is a wrong in your past that you have never put right, this is a good week to go to a brother, to a sister, to a priest in the sacrament of Reconciliation. And if someone has wronged you, remember Joseph's tears. Pharaoh said: go to Joseph. Jesus says to us: go to the lost. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph's brothers were not kind to him. Years later they were hungry, and they came to ask him for food. Joseph knew them, but they did not know him! He wanted to see whether they were sorry. And they were. They said: we were wrong to hurt our brother. Joseph cried when he heard it. In the Gospel Jesus chooses twelve friends and sends them to tell everybody that God is near. Jesus wants us to be his friends too. When we say sorry and forgive each other, we are telling people that God is near!

Prayer

Lord God, you were at work in a famine, in a prison and in a brother's tears, to bring a divided family home. Awaken our conscience when it sleeps, and give us the courage to say: we have sinned. Lord Jesus, you called the Twelve by name and gave them your own authority; you still call ordinary and imperfect people and send them to the lost. Make us messengers of your kingdom, patient with those who wrong us, generous to the hungry, and glad to hear your word: the kingdom of heaven has come near. St Joseph, guardian of the Holy Family, pray for us. Amen.
