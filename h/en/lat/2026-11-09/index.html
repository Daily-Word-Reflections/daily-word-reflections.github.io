Reflection

Why does the whole Church celebrate the anniversary of one building in Rome? Because the Lateran Basilica, dedicated to Christ the Saviour (and later also to St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist), is the cathedral of the Bishop of Rome. Its inscription calls it “mother and head of all the churches of the city and of the world”. Today's feast is a sign of our unity with the Pope and of love for the Church.

Ezekiel sees water flowing from the Temple, growing into a river that makes the Dead Sea fresh: “everything will live where the river goes”. In the Gospel Jesus cleanses the Temple and then speaks of “the temple of his body”. The true Temple is Christ himself, and from his pierced side flowed blood and water (John 19:34), the sacraments of the Church.

Paul adds the third step: “Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?” (1 Corinthians 3:16). Church buildings are holy because they house the Eucharist and gather the living temple, the people of God. Celebrating a church's dedication reminds us that we too have been consecrated, and that zeal for God's house must begin with our own hearts.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today the whole Church celebrates the birthday of a church building in Rome: the Basilica of St John Lateran. Why? Because it is the Pope's cathedral, “the mother and head of all churches”. To celebrate it is to celebrate our unity with Peter's successor.

Ezekiel saw a river flowing from the Temple. It started small and became deep, and wherever it flowed, everything lived; even the salty Dead Sea became fresh. That is what the Church is meant to be: a source of life flowing out into the world.

In the Gospel Jesus makes a whip and drives out the sellers. He is not against worship; he is against turning God's house into a market. Then he says something astonishing: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” He was speaking of his body. The true Temple is Jesus himself.

St Paul then says to us: “You are God's temple, and God's Spirit dwells in you.” Every baptised person is a temple. So we must ask: what is being sold in my temple? Greed, impurity, anger, gossip? Jesus wants to cleanse us too, not to punish but to make room for God.

Let us love our churches, keep them holy and silent, and come early to pray. Let us pray for the Pope and our bishop. And let us allow the river of grace to flow through us to others, so that wherever we go, life may grow.

Dear brothers and sisters, why does the whole Church celebrate the dedication of a basilica in Rome? Because St John Lateran is the cathedral of the Bishop of Rome, the Pope. Its inscription calls it the mother and head of all the churches of the city and of the world. Today's feast is a sign of our unity with the Successor of Peter.

But St Paul takes us further. You are God's building, he says, and no one can lay any foundation other than the one that has been laid, Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 3:9–11). The real temple of God is not made of marble. It is made of people. Each of us is a stone placed by God in His building. Some stones are visible, some hidden in the foundations; all are necessary.

Then Paul asks a startling question: do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you? (1 Corinthians 3:16). Since Baptism, the Holy Spirit lives in us. That gives our bodies a great dignity and calls for respect: for our own body, for the bodies of others, for the poor and the sick. Sin defiles the temple; confession cleanses it.

In the Gospel Jesus enters the Temple and drives out the traders with a whip of cords (John 2:13–17). The disciples remembered the psalm: zeal for your house will consume me. What clutters the temple of our hearts? Greed, resentment, screens that leave no room for prayer, gossip. And what clutters our parishes? Divisions, pride, business without prayer. Let Jesus cleanse His house.

Ezekiel saw water flowing from the Temple, bringing life wherever it went (Ezekiel 47:1–12). When the Church is clean and united, grace flows from it into the world. Today let us pray for the Pope and for unity in the Church, and let us offer ourselves as living stones in the house of God.

Dear brothers and sisters, some people say: God is everywhere, so why do we need churches? I can pray at home or under a tree. It is true that God is everywhere, and that we can pray anywhere. But today's feast reminds us why church buildings matter.

From the beginning God chose to dwell among His people in a special way: the tent of meeting in the desert, then the Temple in Jerusalem. Solomon himself admitted that the highest heaven cannot contain God, much less a house built by human hands, yet he asked God to keep His eyes open toward that house night and day (1 Kings 8:27–30). In Christ, God's dwelling with us became a person: the Word became flesh and lived among us (John 1:14). The church building is a sign of that presence and of the Church, the people gathered by God (CCC 1179–1180).

A Catholic church is first of all the house of the Eucharist. The altar is the place where the sacrifice of the Cross is made present and the table of the Lord to which the people are invited (CCC 1182). The tabernacle holds the Blessed Sacrament, Christ truly present, for the sick and for adoration (CCC 1183). The ambo is the place of the Word; the baptismal font, the place of new birth; the confessional, the place of mercy (CCC 1184–1185). Every part of the building teaches the faith.

When a church is dedicated, the bishop anoints the altar and the walls with chrism, just as a Christian is anointed. That is why we treat the church with respect: we keep silence, dress modestly, genuflect before the tabernacle, and do not use the church as a social hall.

The Lateran Basilica, the Pope's cathedral, reminds us that every parish church is united to the bishop's cathedral, and every cathedral to Rome. And every church on earth points beyond itself to the heavenly Jerusalem. So love your parish church. Visit it during the week, even for five minutes. Kneel before the tabernacle. There, as nowhere else, Christ waits for you.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the whole Latin Church keeps the anniversary of the dedication of a church in Rome: the Basilica of St John Lateran, the cathedral of the Pope as Bishop of Rome. An inscription on its front calls it the mother and head of all the churches of the city and of the world. Yet the readings are not about stones. They move from a building to a living people and to the body of Christ.

Ezekiel sees a river flowing out of the Temple and bringing life wherever it goes. The psalm sings of a river that gladdens the city of God, because God is in her midst. Paul tells the Corinthians that they themselves are God’s temple, built on the one foundation, Jesus Christ. In John, Jesus cleanses the Temple and speaks of the temple of his body, destroyed and raised in three days. The line runs from the Temple to the Christ, and from the Christ to the Church: water, foundation and body.

Ezekiel 47:1–2, 8–9, 12 · The river from the sanctuary

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest taken to Babylon with the first exiles in 597 BC. He saw Jerusalem and its Temple destroyed in 587 BC. In the last part of his book (chapters 40–48), dated to the twenty-fifth year of the exile (40:1), he is shown in vision a new Temple, a renewed land and a renewed people. In chapter 43 the glory of the Lord returns to the Temple by the east gate. Now, from the same Temple, water begins to flow.

The river runs east, down through the desert of Judah to the Arabah and the Dead Sea, the lowest and saltiest water on earth, where nothing lives. This is the place the vision chooses to heal.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: water comes out from under the threshold of the Temple, on the south side of the altar, and flows east.
  • vv. 3–7 (not read): measured every thousand cubits, the stream grows from ankle-deep to a river no one can cross.
  • vv. 8–9: the river reaches the Dead Sea and makes its water fresh; wherever it goes, everything lives.
  • v. 12: fruit trees on both banks, fruit every month and leaves for healing, because the water comes from the sanctuary.

Key words.

  • naḥal, river, torrent: Usually a wadi, a valley that runs with water only after rain. Here it is a stream that grows deeper the farther it goes from its source, the opposite of every river in the desert.
  • ḥāy, to live: The word for life returns again and again in v. 9: every creature “will live”, and “everything will live where the river goes”.
  • terûpāh, healing: The leaves of the trees are “for healing”. The vision is not only of plenty but of a cure for what was wounded.

Verse by verse.

“there, water was flowing from below the threshold of the temple toward the east” (47:1)

The Hebrew word for south here also means “right”. The ancient Latin version reads “from the right side” of the Temple, and the Church’s Easter antiphon takes up these words: “I saw water flowing from the right side of the temple”.

“Wherever the river goes, every living creature that swarms will live, and there will be very many fish, once these waters reach there.” (47:9)

The Dead Sea is named here only as “the sea of stagnant waters” (v. 8). The vision promises fish where there were none. Verse 10 even pictures fishermen spreading their nets along its shore.

“Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for healing.” (47:12)

How the Church reads it.

Jesus takes up this image in John 7:37–39: “out of the believer’s heart shall flow rivers of living water”, and John explains that he meant the Spirit. At the Cross, from the pierced side of Christ, “blood and water” flow out (John 19:34), and the Fathers saw there the birth of the Church and her sacraments of Baptism and the Eucharist (in substance). The Book of Revelation closes with the same river and trees whose leaves are “for the healing of the nations” (Revelation 22:1–2). In the Easter season the Roman rite sings the antiphon “Vidi aquam” as the people are sprinkled with water: the Temple from which the water flows is the risen Christ.

Psalm 46:2–3, 5–6, 8–9 · God is in the midst of the city

Setting.

Psalm 46 is one of the Songs of Zion, which praise Jerusalem as the city where God dwells. It pictures the world shaking, mountains falling into the sea and nations in uproar, and in the middle of it all a city that does not move.

Structure.

  • vv. 2–3: God our refuge, even if the earth changes and the mountains shake.
  • vv. 5–6: the river that gladdens the city; God in her midst.
  • vv. 8–9: the refrain, and an invitation to see the works of the Lord.

Key words.

  • maḥseh, refuge: A place of shelter from storm or attack. The psalm says that God himself, not walls, is the city’s refuge.
  • nāhār, river: Jerusalem had no great river, only the spring of Gihon. The river of the psalm is God’s presence, which waters the city as a river waters a garden.
  • ‘immānû, with us: “The Lord of hosts is with us” uses the same words as the name Immanuel, “God is with us” (Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23).

Verse by verse.

“God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.” (46:2)
“There is a river whose streams make glad the city of God, the holy habitation of the Most High.” (46:5)

On this feast the psalm answers the first reading: the river of Ezekiel and the river of the psalm are one water, flowing from God’s dwelling.

“The Lord of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge.” (46:8)

How the Church reads it.

The Church applies the Songs of Zion to herself, the city of God built on the apostles, and above all to the presence of Christ in her midst. In every church that is dedicated, the psalm’s promise is made concrete: “God is in the midst of the city; it shall not be moved”.

1 Corinthians 3:9c–11, 16–17 · You are God’s temple

Setting.

The Church at Corinth was gifted and divided. Some said “I belong to Paul”, others “I belong to Apollos” or “to Cephas” (1:12). In chapter 3 Paul answers that all preachers are servants. The Church does not belong to them; it belongs to God. He uses two pictures: a field that God makes grow, and a building that God builds.

Structure.

  • v. 9c: you are God’s building.
  • v. 10: Paul laid the foundation as a skilled master builder; others build on it with care.
  • v. 11: the only foundation is Jesus Christ.
  • vv. 12–15 (not read): the work of each builder will be tested by fire on the Day.
  • vv. 16–17: you are God’s temple; the Spirit of God dwells in you; God’s temple is holy.

Key words.

  • oikodomē, building: Both the building and the act of building. The Church is never finished; it is always under construction.
  • architektōn, master builder: The chief builder who lays out the foundation. Paul does not boast of it: he did it “according to the grace of God given to me”.
  • naos, temple, sanctuary: Not the whole Temple precinct but the sanctuary, the inner house where God dwells. In Greek “you” is plural: the community together is the dwelling of God.

Verse by verse.

“For no one can lay any foundation other than the one that has been laid; that foundation is Jesus Christ.” (3:11)

Every bishop and priest, every catechist and parent, builds on a foundation he did not lay. The Lateran inscription honours the mother church, but the foundation even of the mother church is Christ.

“Do you not know that you are God’s temple and that God’s Spirit dwells in you?” (3:16)

In the Old Testament the glory of God filled the Temple. Now the Spirit fills a people.

“If anyone destroys God’s temple, God will destroy that person. For God’s temple is holy, and you are that temple.” (3:17)

The same Greek verb is used twice, “destroy” and “will destroy”. In context Paul is thinking of those who tear the Church apart by division. To wound the unity of the Church is to damage God’s own house.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists “the building of God” among the images of the Church and quotes this passage (CCC 756). It also teaches that the Holy Spirit is to the Church what the soul is to the body (CCC 797). St Augustine, preaching at the dedication of a church, told his people that what was done to these walls must be done in them: they too are being built, joined and dedicated (in substance).

John 2:13–22 · The temple of his body

Setting.

John places the cleansing of the Temple at the start of Jesus’ ministry, at the first of three Passovers in his Gospel. Matthew, Mark and Luke place it in the last week before the Passion. John puts it at the beginning like a heading over the whole Gospel: Jesus is the new Temple.

The Temple of Herod the Great was one of the wonders of the ancient world. Its outer courts held animal sellers and money changers, because pilgrims needed animals without blemish for sacrifice and the Temple tax had to be paid in a particular coin. The trade served the worship, but it had filled the house of prayer with the noise of a market.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–16: at Passover, Jesus drives out the traders: “Stop making my Father’s house a marketplace!”
  • v. 17: the disciples remember Psalm 69.
  • vv. 18–20: the authorities ask for a sign; Jesus answers: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.”
  • vv. 21–22: the evangelist explains: he spoke of his body, and after the Resurrection the disciples believed.

Key words.

  • hieron / naos, temple precinct / sanctuary: In vv. 14–15 John uses hieron, the whole Temple area with its courts. In vv. 19–21 he changes to naos, the sanctuary where God dwells. It is this naos that Jesus identifies with his body.
  • oikos tou patros mou, my Father’s house: Jesus speaks as the Son in his Father’s house, not as a visitor. Zechariah had foretold a day when “there shall no longer be traders in the house of the Lord” (Zechariah 14:21).
  • egeirein, to raise up: The verb is used both for raising a building and for raising the dead. Jesus’ words carry both meanings.

Verse by verse.

“Stop making my Father’s house a marketplace!” (2:16)
“His disciples remembered that it was written, “Zeal for your house will consume me.”” (2:17)

Psalm 69:9. In the psalm the righteous man suffers because of his love for God’s house. The disciples see that this zeal will cost Jesus his life.

“Jesus answered them, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.”” (2:19)

The word “Jews” in vv. 18 and 20 refers, as often in John, to the Temple authorities who questioned Jesus, not to the Jewish people as such.

“But he was speaking of the temple of his body.” (2:21)

The forty-six years of v. 20 fit the history: Herod began rebuilding the Temple about 20 BC, which places this Passover near AD 27–28. The stone Temple took decades; the new Temple is raised in three days.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus loved the Temple, went up to it for the feasts, and was angered that its outer court had become a place of trade; and that he identified himself with the Temple, presenting himself as God’s definitive dwelling among us (CCC 583–586). Through Christ the Church is “the temple of the Holy Spirit”, and the church building is the house where the people of God gather and Christ is present in the Eucharist (CCC 797, 1179–1181).

Questions people ask

Why does the whole Church celebrate a building in Rome?

The Lateran is the cathedral of the Bishop of Rome, the Pope. Keeping its dedication everywhere is a sign of love for and unity with the Chair of Peter. The feast is less about a building than about the one Church gathered around Christ and around Peter’s successor.

Was Jesus against the Temple?

No. Jesus was presented in the Temple as a child, found there at twelve, and went up for the feasts. He cleansed it because he loved it as his Father’s house (CCC 583–584). After the Resurrection the apostles continued to pray there (Acts 2:46; 3:1).

Was it right for Jesus to use a whip?

John says he made a whip of cords to drive out the sheep and cattle. It was a prophetic sign, like the actions of the prophets, done with authority and zeal for God’s honour. It does not justify violence against persons.

For the Latin reader

The Lateran Basilica was given to the Church by the emperor Constantine, and tradition says it was dedicated by Pope Sylvester I on 9 November in the early fourth century. It was dedicated to Christ the Saviour, and later also to St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist, which is why it is called St John Lateran. Its baptistery, built in the same age, became a model for baptisteries across the West. For Latin Catholics this day is also a lesson about our own parish church. When a church is dedicated, the bishop anoints the altar and the walls with chrism, at twelve or four places marked with crosses, before which candles are lit on each anniversary. Every church building is a sign of the living Church, and every baptised person is a living stone.
