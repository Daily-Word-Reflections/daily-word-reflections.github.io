Word for the day

“Then Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them to those who were seated; so also the fish, as much as they wanted.” John 6:11

Reflection

The council is enraged and wants to put the apostles to death. Then one man stands up: Gamaliel, “a Pharisee in the council… a teacher of the law, respected by all the people.” He is the teacher at whose feet Saul of Tarsus was trained, and later Jewish tradition honours him as one of the great masters of the Law. He has the apostles sent out and gives the council a lesson in patience drawn from recent history: Theudas and Judas the Galilean both gathered followers and both came to nothing. “So in the present case, I tell you, keep away from these men and let them alone; because if this plan or this undertaking is of human origin, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them.” Gamaliel is not a Christian, but he is a just man who fears God, and his counsel saves the apostles' lives. The Church has been grateful to him ever since; a later Christian legend even counted him among the friends of the first disciples. The apostles are flogged and released, and Luke records the astonishing reaction: “they rejoiced that they were considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name. And every day in the temple and at home they did not cease to teach and proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.” The Psalm is their song: “The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?”

From today until the end of the Third Week of Easter the weekday Gospels walk through the sixth chapter of St John, the great chapter of the Bread of Life. It begins with a sign. “The Passover, the festival of the Jews, was near,” John notes, and a large crowd follows Jesus up the mountain by the Sea of Galilee. Jesus tests Philip: “Where are we to buy bread for these people to eat?” Philip calculates: six months' wages would not be enough. Andrew finds “a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish. But what are they among so many people?” Barley loaves were the bread of the poor, and they recall the prophet Elisha, who fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves. Jesus has the people sit on the green grass; then he “took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them.” The Greek word for giving thanks is eucharistein. The evangelist wants us to see the Eucharist in this meal, celebrated near the Passover. After all have eaten “as much as they wanted,” Jesus says: “Gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost,” and twelve baskets are filled. The Church still treats every fragment of the consecrated Host with that same care. When the crowd tries to seize him and make him king, Jesus withdraws alone to the mountain. He will not be a king who only hands out bread; he will be the King who gives his own flesh as bread.

Full homily

Dear friends, the council wants to kill the apostles, and a wise teacher named Gamaliel stands up. He says: if this plan is of human origin, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them (Acts 5:38–39). Two thousand years later, the Church is still here. Not because we are strong, but because the work is God's.

The apostles are flogged, and they go out rejoicing that they were worthy to suffer for the name of Jesus. What a change from the frightened men behind locked doors! The risen Lord does not take away the suffering, but he fills it with joy.

In the Gospel a boy gives Jesus five barley loaves and two fish. It seems like nothing. Jesus takes them, gives thanks, and distributes them, and five thousand people eat as much as they want (John 6:11). Our gifts always seem small. But in Jesus' hands they become enough, and more than enough.

Notice the words: he took, he gave thanks, he distributed. These are the words of the Mass. And at the end Jesus says: gather up the fragments, so that nothing may be lost. At the altar today he will feed us with the true Bread, his own Body. Let us bring him our five loaves, and leave nothing of his gift wasted. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a little boy had a small lunch: five small breads and two fish. He gave it all to Jesus. Jesus thanked God and shared it, and more than five thousand people ate! When we give Jesus even a little, he can do great things with it. What will you give Jesus today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you fed the multitude with five barley loaves and gave thanks to the Father for the bread. Give us the wisdom of Gamaliel to recognize the work of God, the joy of the apostles in suffering for your name, and the generosity of the boy who gave you all he had. Feed us with the Bread of Life, and let nothing of your gifts be lost. Who live and reign for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A wise teacher of the law gives the council a sober piece of advice: if this movement is of God, you cannot overthrow it. The apostles are flogged and leave rejoicing. In the Gospel, Jesus feeds five thousand people from five barley loaves, and the crowd wants to make him king; he withdraws to the mountain alone. With this sign John’s great chapter on the Bread of Life begins, read at Mass over the coming days. Psalm 27 gives the prayer of one who wants only to dwell in the house of the Lord.

Acts 5:34–42 · If it is of God

Setting.

Gamaliel, a Pharisee, was one of the most honoured teachers of the law of his time; Jewish tradition remembers him as Rabban Gamaliel the Elder. Paul later says that he was brought up “at the feet of Gamaliel” (22:3). The Pharisees, unlike the Sadducees, believed in the resurrection, and Gamaliel urges caution.

Gamaliel gives two examples of movements that came to nothing. Judas the Galilean led a revolt at the time of the Roman census in AD 6. The Jewish historian Josephus tells of a Theudas who rose up under the governor Fadus, around AD 45, which would be after this speech. Some think Gamaliel means an earlier man of the same name, a common one; others think Luke’s order here is loose. The point of the speech does not depend on it.

Structure.

  • vv. 34–35: Gamaliel has the apostles put outside and speaks.
  • vv. 36–37: two movements that failed.
  • vv. 38–39: his counsel: let them alone.
  • v. 40: flogging and a fresh order of silence.
  • vv. 41–42: the apostles rejoice and keep preaching.

Key words.

  • nomodidaskalos, teacher of the law: A recognized master of the Law of Moses. Luke uses the word for the teachers of the law who sat listening to Jesus when he healed the paralysed man (Luke 5:17). Gamaliel is “respected by all the people”.
  • theomachoi, fighting against God: One Greek word: “God-fighters”. Gamaliel warns that resistance to a work of God is a fight that cannot be won.
  • hyper tou onomatos, for the sake of the name: “The name” stands for Jesus himself. In Acts the apostles heal, preach and suffer “in the name” (3:6; 4:12). To suffer for the name is to share his lot.

Verse by verse.

“if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them” (5:39)

A principle of discernment the Church has often recalled: time and fruit reveal what is of God. It is not the whole answer, since evil can last a long while, but it guards against hasty violence against what one does not understand.

“they had them flogged.” (5:40)

Probably the synagogue punishment of thirty-nine lashes, based on Deuteronomy 25:3. Paul says he received it five times (2 Corinthians 11:24).

“they rejoiced that they were considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name.” (5:41)

Jesus had said, “Blessed are you when people hate you... on account of the Son of Man. Rejoice in that day” (Luke 6:22–23). The apostles find the beatitude true in their own bodies.

“they did not cease to teach and proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.” (5:42)

“In the temple and at home”: the two places of the first Church, public worship and the house church. Luke ends this section with the word still going on.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls martyrdom the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473). Today’s apostles are not yet martyrs, but their joy in dishonour is the same spirit. Christians who are mocked or excluded for their faith in many lands today can read verse 41 as their own story, and pray, as the apostles did, for those who wrong them.

John 6:1–15 · Five barley loaves for five thousand

Setting.

The feeding of the five thousand is the only miracle of Jesus’ ministry told in all four Gospels. John alone notes that it was near Passover, names Philip and Andrew, and mentions the boy and the barley loaves. Barley bread was the bread of the poor.

In John the miracle is a “sign”: it points beyond itself. It opens chapter 6, where Jesus will say, “I am the bread of life” (6:35), and speak of eating his flesh and drinking his blood (6:51–58).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: Galilee, the mountain, the crowd, Passover near.
  • vv. 5–9: Jesus tests Philip; Andrew brings the boy’s loaves.
  • vv. 10–13: the people sit, eat their fill, and twelve baskets of fragments remain.
  • vv. 14–15: the crowd’s acclaim; Jesus withdraws to the mountain alone.

Key words.

  • eucharistēsas, having given thanks: From the verb that gives us the word Eucharist. John uses it here and again when he recalls this meal (6:23). Early readers could not miss the echo of the Lord’s Supper.
  • klasmata, fragments: The broken pieces. The Didache, an early Christian writing, uses the same word for the eucharistic bread and prays that, as the broken bread once scattered on the hills was gathered into one, the Church may be gathered into God’s kingdom (in substance).
  • sēmeion, sign: John’s word for the miracles of Jesus. A sign is meant to be read: the crowd sees the bread but must learn to see the giver.

Verse by verse.

“There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish.” (6:9)

An echo of Elisha, who fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves, and “they ate, and had some left” (2 Kings 4:42–44). One greater than Elisha is here. The boy’s small gift, given up, becomes enough for all.

“Then Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them” (6:11)

In the other Gospels the disciples hand out the bread. In John Jesus himself gives it, as he will give himself.

“Gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost.” (6:12)

The Greek verb “be lost” returns in 6:39: the Father’s will is that Jesus “should lose nothing of all that he has given me”. The care for fragments is a picture of his care for every person.

“to make him king, he withdrew again to the mountain by himself.” (6:15)

The crowd wants a king who fills stomachs. Jesus will be proclaimed king only on the cross (19:19). He refuses a kingship that is not the Father’s way.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the multiplication of the loaves, when the Lord says the blessing, breaks and distributes the loaves through his disciples to feed the multitude, prefigures the superabundance of the unique bread of his Eucharist (CCC 1335). The Church has also always read here a call to share bread with the hungry, since the Lord who feeds us with himself sends us to feed others.

Questions people ask

Was the miracle really just people sharing their food?

That reading is sometimes heard, but it is not what the text says. All four Gospels present a sign of Jesus’ power, and the crowd’s response, “This is indeed the prophet”, would make no sense if they had simply shared their lunches. Sharing is a true lesson drawn from the scene, but the Church believes in the miracle itself, as a sign of the Bread he gives.

For study and teaching

  • What is my “five barley loaves”, the small thing I hesitate to hand over to Jesus?
  • How do I tell whether something new in my parish or my life is “of God”?
