Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “Go; your son will live.” The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and started on his way.” John 4:50

Reflection

Laetare Sunday has just lifted our eyes toward Easter, and today Isaiah carries that joy further. God speaks like an artist about to begin his greatest work: “For I am about to create new heavens and a new earth; the former things shall not be remembered or come to mind.” Jerusalem will be a joy and its people a delight; “no more shall the sound of weeping be heard in it, or the cry of distress.” No infant will die after a few days, and the old will live out their years; people will build houses and live in them, plant vineyards and eat their fruit. It is the picture of a world healed of death and of wasted labour. The Psalm answers with the song of someone who has been brought back from the edge of the grave: “O LORD my God, I cried to you for help, and you have healed me.” And it gives Lent one of its loveliest lines: “Weeping may linger for the night, but joy comes with the morning.”

The Gospel shows the new creation beginning in one household. Jesus has come back to Cana, where he changed water into wine. A royal official, probably a servant of Herod Antipas, walks some twenty-five kilometres up from Capernaum, because his son is dying. Jesus seems to answer coldly: “Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe.” The father does not argue; he simply pleads: “Sir, come down before my little boy dies.” Then Jesus gives him not a sign but a word: “Go; your son will live.” And the man does the hardest thing a worried father can do: he turns round and walks home with nothing in his hands but that word. “The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and started on his way.” On the road his servants meet him: the boy is alive, and the fever left him at one in the afternoon, the very hour Jesus spoke. Faith that began as a cry becomes trust in a word, and ends as the faith of a whole household. Today we also remember St John of God (1495–1550), a Portuguese soldier and bookseller who, converted by a sermon of St John of Avila, spent the rest of his life gathering the sick and the poor of Granada into a hospital and caring for them with his own hands. From his companions grew the Brothers Hospitallers. He believed that every sick person was Christ, and he served them like a father carrying home a child.

Full homily

Dear friends, today God speaks through Isaiah like a builder about to begin: for I am about to create new heavens and a new earth (Isaiah 65:17). No more weeping, no more children dying, no more wasted work. That is where God is taking the world, and that is where Lent is taking us: to Easter, the first morning of the new creation.

In the Gospel a father comes to Jesus because his little boy is dying. Jesus says: unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe. The father does not argue; he only begs: sir, come down before my little boy dies.

And Jesus says only: go; your son will live (John 4:50). Think of that walk home. No sign, no proof, only a word. The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and started on his way. On the road the good news meets him: the fever left the boy at the very hour Jesus spoke. That is what faith is: walking on the strength of Christ's word.

Today we remember St John of God, who once lived a wild and wandering life. After his conversion he filled a house in Granada with the sick and the poor and served them with his own hands, because he saw Christ in every one of them. Let us believe the word of Jesus, and let that faith become hands that serve the sick. Amen.

For children

Children, a father's son was very sick. Jesus said: go home, your son will live! The father believed Jesus and went home, and his son was well. When Jesus says something, we can trust him. Let us pray today for a child who is sick.

Prayer

Lord God, you make all things new and turn our mourning into dancing. Give us the faith of the royal official, to believe your Son's word and set out on our way. Through the prayers of St John of God, give the sick tender care and make our hands ready to serve them. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

From this Monday until Holy Week the weekday Gospels at Mass are taken mostly from St John, and the first scene is a father begging for his dying son. Isaiah promises a Jerusalem where no more weeping is heard and no child dies young. John shows that promise beginning in one house in Capernaum, through a word heard at a distance and believed. Psalm 30 gives the response of someone brought back from the edge of the grave: “You have turned my mourning into dancing.” The halfway point of Lent has passed, and the Church now looks toward what God will make new.

Isaiah 65:17–21 · New heavens and a new earth

Setting.

Many scholars think that the last chapters of Isaiah (56–66) speak to the community that came home from exile in Babylon, in the late sixth century BC or after. The return had been announced in glorious words, but the reality was poor: a ruined city, hard harvests, quarrels among the people. Into this disappointment God says that he has not finished. What he is about to do will be as great as creation itself.

Structure.

  • v. 17: God announces a new creation; the former troubles will not even come to mind.
  • v. 18: the people are told to rejoice in what God is making.
  • v. 19: God himself rejoices, and weeping ends.
  • v. 20: no early death; a hundred years will count as youth.
  • v. 21: people will live in the houses they build and eat what they plant.

Key words.

  • bārā’, to create: The verb of Genesis 1:1, used only with God as subject. It comes three times in vv. 17–18. Going home from exile is only the beginning: God is doing again what only God can do.
  • śûś / gîl, to be glad, to rejoice: In v. 18 these two verbs are a command to the people. In v. 19 the same two verbs come back with God as subject: “I will rejoice … and delight”. God's own joy is the source of theirs.

Verse by verse.

“For I am about to create new heavens and a new earth; the former things shall not be remembered or come to mind.” (65:17)

The Hebrew form suggests something already under way: God is at work now. 2 Peter 3:13 and Revelation 21:1 take up these very words for the world to come.

“I will rejoice in Jerusalem, and delight in my people; no more shall the sound of weeping be heard in it, or the cry of distress.” (65:19)

Revelation 21:4 carries this further: “Death will be no more”. Isaiah's picture, as v. 20 shows, still has old age and dying in it, though long delayed. The promise grows clearer as revelation goes on, until the Resurrection of Christ shows its full measure.

“They shall build houses and inhabit them; they shall plant vineyards and eat their fruit.” (65:21)

This reverses the curse of Deuteronomy 28:30, where a person builds a house and does not live in it, plants a vineyard and does not enjoy its fruit. God's new world is pictured in very ordinary terms: a home, a harvest, a family that lives to see its grandchildren. Salvation does not despise daily life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes up the language of “new heavens and a new earth” for the final renewal of all things (CCC 1042–1050). It says that creation itself will be transformed, and that this hope does not weaken our care for the present world but gives it strength (Gaudium et Spes 39; CCC 1049). In Lent the Church hears this page as a reminder of where her penance is leading: to Easter, and through Easter to a world without tears.

John 4:43–54 · Your son will live

Setting.

Jesus has just spent two days among the Samaritans (4:40) and now goes back to Galilee. He comes again to Cana, where he changed water into wine (2:1–11). The man who comes to him is a royal official, probably in the service of Herod Antipas, the ruler of Galilee, who was popularly called a king (Mark 6:14). Capernaum lies down by the lake, some hours away on foot, so the man has walked far and asks Jesus to “come down”.

The remark in v. 44 about a prophet in his own country has puzzled commentators, because the Galileans then welcome him. John may be hinting that their welcome is shallow, since it rests on what they saw at the festival (v. 45; compare 2:23–25). Those who think so read “his own country” as Galilee; others take it as Judea. The question remains open.

Structure.

  • vv. 43–45: back to Galilee and the Galileans' welcome.
  • vv. 46–47: the official and his request.
  • vv. 48–49: Jesus' hard word, and the father's repeated plea.
  • v. 50: the word, and the man's faith in it.
  • vv. 51–53: the news on the road; the hour is compared; the whole household believes.
  • v. 54: John numbers it the second sign.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: John's word for Jesus' miracles. A sign points beyond itself to who Jesus is. Here it shows that his word gives life, and that distance is no obstacle to it.
  • zē, lives: The Greek at vv. 50 and 53 is literally “your son lives”, in the present tense, and v. 51 repeats the verb. The English “will live” is right as a promise, but the Greek shows the word taking effect as it is spoken.
  • basilikos, royal official: A person from the ruler's circle, a man used to giving orders. Here he has no power at all over what matters most, and he begs.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus said to him, “Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe.”” (4:48)

In Greek the “you” here is plural, so the words reach beyond this man to the crowd that wants a show. The father does not argue. He only repeats what he needs, and this humble persistence is the beginning of his faith.

“Jesus said to him, “Go; your son will live.” The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and started on his way.” (4:50)

He had asked Jesus to come; he receives only a sentence. He walks home with nothing to hold except the word. This is the faith of every Christian who has not seen, and John will end his Gospel by blessing it (20:29).

“So he himself believed, along with his whole household.” (4:53)

He already believed in v. 50, and now he believes again, more fully. Faith here is a road with stages: from need, to trust in a word, to belief in the person, and from one man to a whole family.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the signs worked by Jesus attest that the Father has sent him and invite belief in him (CCC 548). St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, notes that Jesus refused to go in person to this official's son, yet offered to go to the centurion's servant (Matthew 8:7): he did not honour rank (in substance). The same word still reaches us at a distance from Galilee: in the Scripture read at Mass and in the sacraments, Christ is present and speaking.

Questions people ask

Is this the same story as the centurion's servant in Matthew 8 and Luke 7?

The stories are similar: a man of some standing, a sick boy at Capernaum, a healing at a distance. Many scholars think that they may come from one memory told in different ways; others treat them as two events, and there are real differences. The Church has not decided this, and nothing in the faith depends on it. Each Gospel uses the story for its own lesson, and in John the lesson is faith in Jesus' word.

For study and teaching

  • The official had to walk home with only a word. When has God asked me to go on without seeing the result of my prayer?
  • Isaiah pictures God's new world as houses, vineyards and long lives. Where in my ordinary life can I already help someone to have a home, a meal or less reason to weep?
