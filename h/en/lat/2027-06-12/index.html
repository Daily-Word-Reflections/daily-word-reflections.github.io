Word for the day

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” 2 Corinthians 5:17

Reflection

Today's reading is the heart of the Second Letter to the Corinthians, and one of the deepest pages Paul ever wrote. Why does he work so hard, suffer so much, put up with misunderstanding? “For the love of Christ urges us on, because we are convinced that one has died for all; therefore all have died. And he died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and was raised for them.” The love of Christ is not only the love we have for him; it is first his love for us, which presses on us and drives us forward. Because of it Paul looks at everyone differently: “From now on, therefore, we regard no one from a human point of view.” Not by caste, wealth, education or usefulness, but as someone for whom Christ died. Then the great announcement: “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” Baptism is not a coat of paint on an old house; it is a new creation. And all of this comes from God, “who reconciled us to himself through Christ, and has given us the ministry of reconciliation.” God was not waiting for us to make the first move: “in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them.” Paul sees himself as an ambassador, carrying a message from the King: “we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” The reading ends with a mysterious and wonderful exchange: “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” The sinless One took our place so that we could take his. The Psalm answers with pure gratitude: “as far as the east is from the west, so far he removes our transgressions from us.”

In the Gospel Jesus continues his teaching on the Law. The Law forbade false oaths and commanded that vows made to the Lord be kept. Jesus goes further: “But I say to you, Do not swear at all,” neither by heaven, nor by earth, nor by Jerusalem, nor by your own head. People swore by these things to make their words sound more solid, and to leave themselves a way out. Jesus wants disciples whose every word can be trusted: “Let your word be ‘Yes, Yes’ or ‘No, No’; anything more than this comes from the evil one.” (The Church has always understood that this does not forbid a solemn oath for a grave reason, such as in a court of law; Jesus is condemning the habit of swearing, and the lies that make it necessary.) Paul's ambassador and Jesus' plain-spoken disciple belong together: the one who carries the message of reconciliation must be someone whose word is true. On this Saturday many parishes keep the memorial of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Her whole life is the perfect Yes: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” And Saturday evening is the time when many Catholics go to confession before Sunday. The sacrament of Reconciliation is exactly Paul's ministry: a priest, an ambassador for Christ, saying in his name, I absolve you, and the old passing away, and everything becoming new.

Full homily

Dear friends, why did St Paul work so hard? Why did he travel, suffer, write letters with tears? He tells us today: for the love of Christ urges us on (2 Corinthians 5:14). Christ died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and was raised for them (2 Corinthians 5:15). When you really know that someone has died for you, you cannot go on living only for yourself.

Then Paul says one of the most hopeful sentences in the Bible: if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new! (2 Corinthians 5:17). God does not just repair us. He makes us new. That happened at our Baptism, and it happens again every time we come back to him in the sacrament of Reconciliation.

And notice who makes the first move. Not us. In Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them (2 Corinthians 5:19). God came looking for us. And he has given this message to the Church, so that Paul can say: we are ambassadors for Christ; be reconciled to God. Every priest in the confessional is such an ambassador. If you have been away from confession for a long time, this weekend is a good time to come home.

In the Gospel Jesus asks for honesty: let your word be Yes, Yes or No, No (Matthew 5:37). We do not need to swear by God or by our mother's head. A Christian's simple word should be enough. Today is Saturday, a day the Church often gives to Our Lady. Her Yes to God was simple and complete: let it be with me according to your word. May she help us to say Yes to God's reconciliation, and to be people whose word is true. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever broken something and wished you could have a brand-new one? St Paul says that when we belong to Jesus, God makes us new inside: the old is gone, the new has come! When we say sorry to God in confession, he makes our hearts new and clean again. Jesus also says: always tell the truth. When you say yes, mean yes; when you say no, mean no. Mother Mary said a big Yes to God. Let us say Yes to him too!

Prayer

Father of mercy, in Christ you reconciled the world to yourself, not counting our trespasses against us. You made him who knew no sin to be sin for our sake, so that in him we might become your righteousness. We thank you for the new creation that began in our Baptism, and for every time you have made us new in the sacrament of Reconciliation. Let the love of Christ urge us on, so that we live no longer for ourselves. Make our words simple and true, and teach us to say Yes to you as Mary did. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul reaches one of the summits of his letter: the love of Christ urges us on; if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation; God has reconciled us to himself and given us the ministry of reconciliation. “Be reconciled to God.” In the Gospel Jesus asks for words so true that oaths are not needed: let your “Yes” mean yes and your “No” mean no. A reconciled person becomes a truthful person. Psalm 103 sings: the Lord is kind and merciful.

2 Corinthians 5:14–21 · Ambassadors for Christ

Setting.

Paul is still defending his ministry. Why does he work so hard, endure so much, and refuse to boast about outward things? Because the love of Christ has taken hold of him. These verses gather Paul’s whole gospel into a few lines: Christ’s death for all, a new creation, and reconciliation as God’s gift and the Church’s mission.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: the love of Christ urges us on; one died for all, so that all may live for him.
  • vv. 16–17: we no longer regard anyone from a human point of view; in Christ, a new creation.
  • vv. 18–19: God reconciled us to himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation.
  • vv. 20–21: we are ambassadors; be reconciled to God; he made him to be sin who knew no sin.

Key words.

  • synechei, urges on, holds fast: The verb means to press, to hold together, to control. Christ’s love for us, shown on the cross, presses on Paul and directs his whole life.
  • katallagē, reconciliation: From ordinary life: the making up of a broken relationship. Notice who acts: God reconciles us to himself. We do not win God back; he comes to us.
  • presbeuomen, we are ambassadors: An ambassador speaks with the authority of the one who sent him. Through the apostles God himself is making his appeal.

Verse by verse.

“For the love of Christ urges us on, because we are convinced that one has died for all;” (5:14)

Christ died for all, without exception. The love that moved him is the love that now moves his apostle.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (5:17)

Paul does not say only that the person is renewed but that “there is a new creation”. Baptism opens a new world. Isaiah had heard God say, “I am about to do a new thing” (Isaiah 43:19).

“in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself,” (5:19)

The whole world is the horizon of God’s mercy. The cross is not God changing his mind about us, but God himself coming to make peace.

“So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us; we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” (5:20)

Paul writes to Christians. Even the baptised need to hear again and again: be reconciled.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (5:21)

Christ was sinless, but he took the place of sinners. Several Fathers, including St Augustine, read “sin” here as “a sacrifice for sin”, since the same Hebrew word can mean both sin and sin offering (in substance).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse in teaching that Christ, without sin, took our place: “For our sake God made him to be sin who knew no sin” (CCC 602). It teaches that Christ entrusted the ministry of reconciliation to his apostles, and quotes Paul’s appeal, “be reconciled to God” (CCC 1442). Every Christian becomes a new creature in Baptism (CCC 1265). In the sacrament of Penance the priest still speaks as an ambassador of Christ: the words of absolution begin, “God, the Father of mercies, through the death and resurrection of his Son has reconciled the world to himself.”

Matthew 5:33–37 · Let your Yes be Yes

Setting.

This is the fourth of the six contrasts in Matthew 5. The Law forbade false oaths and required vows made to the Lord to be kept (Leviticus 19:12; Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21). In practice people sometimes swore by heaven, by earth or by Jerusalem, thinking that such oaths, which avoided God’s name, were less binding. Jesus answers that all of these belong to God, and goes further: a disciple’s plain word should be so reliable that no oath is needed.

Structure.

  • v. 33: you have heard: do not swear falsely; keep your vows.
  • vv. 34–36: do not swear at all: not by heaven, earth, Jerusalem or your own head.
  • v. 37: let your word be “Yes, Yes” or “No, No”; anything more comes from the evil one.

Key words.

  • omnyō, to swear: To call God or something sacred as witness to the truth of what one says. An oath assumes that ordinary words cannot be trusted.
  • thronos, hypopodion, throne, footstool: Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:1: “Heaven is my throne and the earth is my footstool.” Every so-called lesser oath still touches God.
  • ho ponēros, the evil one: Grammatically it can mean “evil” or “the evil one”. The need for oaths arises from a world where lying is common, the work of the father of lies (John 8:44).

Verse by verse.

“You shall not swear falsely, but carry out the vows you have made to the Lord.” (5:33)

Jesus does not deny the Law; he takes it to its goal. If no word is false, the oath becomes unnecessary.

“But I say to you, Do not swear at all,” (5:34)

The aim is truthfulness in every word, not only in words spoken under oath.

“And do not swear by your head, for you cannot make one hair white or black.” (5:36)

Even our own life is not in our control; it belongs to God.

“Let your word be ‘Yes, Yes’ or ‘No, No’; anything more than this comes from the evil one.” (5:37)

St Paul uses the same language for his own honesty and for God’s faithfulness: in Christ “every one of God’s promises is a ‘Yes’” (2 Corinthians 1:20). The disciple’s truthfulness reflects the faithfulness of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that Jesus’ teaching on oaths belongs to the second commandment (CCC 2153). Following St Paul, who calls God as witness (2 Corinthians 1:23; Galatians 1:20), the tradition of the Church has understood Jesus’ words as not excluding oaths made for grave and right reasons, for example in court (CCC 2154). An oath may be refused when it is demanded for purposes contrary to the dignity of persons or to the communion of the Church (CCC 2155). The heart of the teaching is simple: Christians are to be people whose word can be trusted.

Questions people ask

Is it wrong for a Christian to take an oath in court or at an ordination?

No. The Church teaches that an oath for a grave and just reason, such as in a court of law or when taking up an office, is permitted, because it honours God as the witness of truth (CCC 2154). What Jesus forbids is careless or false swearing, and a double standard where only sworn words are true.

For study and teaching

  • Where do I need to be reconciled with God? When did I last receive the sacrament of Penance?
  • Do people know that my “Yes” means yes? Where do I exaggerate, shade the truth or make promises I do not keep?
