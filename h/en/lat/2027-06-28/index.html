Word for the day

“Then Abraham came near and said, “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?” Genesis 18:23

Reflection

The three visitors leave Abraham's tent and look toward Sodom, and the Lord shares a secret with his friend: “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?” Abraham has been chosen so that through him “all the nations of the earth shall be blessed,” and so that he may teach his children “to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice.” A man with such a calling must learn how God judges. “How great is the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah and how very grave their sin!” The word outcry is the cry of victims; the Bible later names the sins of Sodom as pride, excess of food, prosperous ease and neglect of the poor and needy (Ezekiel 16:49), together with the violence against strangers told in the next chapter. God says he will “go down and see,” like a just judge who examines before he sentences. Then comes one of the most remarkable prayers in the Old Testament. “Abraham remained standing before the LORD.” He comes near and asks: “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?” He does not ask only that the righteous be spared; he asks that the whole city be forgiven for their sake. Six times he presses, from fifty to forty-five, to forty, thirty, twenty, ten, each time with deep humility, “I who am but dust and ashes,” and with great boldness: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?” Each time God agrees. The story teaches that the presence of a few righteous people can protect a whole community, and that God is more ready to forgive than we are to ask. Abraham stopped at ten. The prophets would go further (Jeremiah 5:1), and St Paul would see the fulfilment in one righteous man: “by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19). The Psalm sings the heart of the God Abraham was speaking to: “He does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities.”

In the Gospel two men offer to follow Jesus, and he answers each with a hard saying. To an eager scribe who promises, “Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go,” Jesus says, “Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests; but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.” Following him is not a comfortable career; it means sharing the life of a homeless wanderer. To a disciple who asks first to bury his father, Jesus says, “Follow me, and let the dead bury their own dead.” The words are shocking, for burying one's parents was a sacred duty in Israel. Jesus is not teaching contempt for parents; he is saying that the call of the kingdom comes before everything, even the holiest human duties, and that those who do not follow him are, in a sense, the dead. St Irenaeus, whom the Church honours today, followed Christ wherever he went. Born in Smyrna in Asia Minor around the year 130, he heard as a boy the preaching of St Polycarp, who had known St John the Apostle. He went to Gaul, became a priest of Lyons, and after the bishop Pothinus died in the persecution of 177 he became bishop there himself. Against the Gnostics, who claimed a secret knowledge and despised the body and the created world, he wrote his great work Against Heresies. He taught that the true faith is the one handed on publicly by the apostles through the bishops who succeeded them, above all in the Church of Rome, founded, he wrote, by the two most glorious apostles Peter and Paul. He loved the body God created and the flesh the Word took on; he wrote that the glory of God is a human being fully alive. Tradition honours him as a martyr, and in 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church with the title Doctor of Unity. Like Abraham, he stood before God for others; like the disciples, he left home to follow Christ to the ends of the known world. And tonight, with the Vigil of Sts Peter and Paul, the Church begins to celebrate the very apostles whose teaching he guarded.

Full homily

Dear friends, God says something beautiful in today's first reading: shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do? (Genesis 18:17). God treats Abraham as a friend, and tells him that he is going down to see the sin of Sodom. And Abraham does what friends of God do. He does not say: good, they deserve it. He stays standing before the Lord, and he prays for the city.

Listen to his prayer. Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked? Suppose there are fifty righteous. And when God agrees, he goes on: forty-five? forty? thirty? twenty? ten? He calls himself dust and ashes, but he does not stop. And each time God says yes. God is more ready to forgive than Abraham is to ask. The prayer of a righteous person, even for a wicked city, is precious to God.

This is a lesson for us. We complain a lot about our towns, our politics, our young people. Do we pray for them as much as we complain? Abraham stopped at ten. But God found one righteous man, Jesus, and for his sake he forgives all who turn to him. When we pray for others, we stand beside Jesus, the great intercessor.

In the Gospel Jesus tells those who want to follow him: the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head (Matthew 8:20). St Irenaeus, whom we honour today, took that seriously. He left his home in Asia, went to the far west, served the Church in Lyons in a time of persecution, and spent his life guarding the faith handed down from the apostles. He said: the glory of God is a human being fully alive. Today let us be fully alive in prayer for others, and in following Jesus without delay. Amen.

For children

Children, Abraham was God's friend. One day God told him that a city called Sodom was very, very bad. Abraham did not say: good, punish them! He prayed for them. He asked God: if there are fifty good people, will you save the city? Then forty? Thirty? Ten? And God said yes every time! When you hear about people doing wrong, do not just say they are bad. Pray for them, like Abraham!

Prayer

O God, who called the Bishop Saint Irenaeus to confirm true doctrine and peace in the Church, grant, we pray, through his intercession, that, being renewed in faith and charity, we may always be intent on fostering unity and concord. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, Judge of all the earth, you are merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. Give us Abraham's courage to stand before you for our cities and our families, and the readiness of true disciples to follow your Son without delay. Through the prayers of St Irenaeus, keep us faithful to the faith of the apostles Peter and Paul. Amen.
