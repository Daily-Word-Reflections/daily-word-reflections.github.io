Word for the day

“Then Abraham came near and said, “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?” Genesis 18:23

Reflection

The three visitors leave Abraham's tent and look toward Sodom, and the Lord shares a secret with his friend: “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?” Abraham has been chosen so that through him “all the nations of the earth shall be blessed,” and so that he may teach his children “to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice.” A man with such a calling must learn how God judges. “How great is the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah and how very grave their sin!” The word outcry is the cry of victims; the Bible later names the sins of Sodom as pride, excess of food, prosperous ease and neglect of the poor and needy (Ezekiel 16:49), together with the violence against strangers told in the next chapter. God says he will “go down and see,” like a just judge who examines before he sentences. Then comes one of the most remarkable prayers in the Old Testament. “Abraham remained standing before the LORD.” He comes near and asks: “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?” He does not ask only that the righteous be spared; he asks that the whole city be forgiven for their sake. Six times he presses, from fifty to forty-five, to forty, thirty, twenty, ten, each time with deep humility, “I who am but dust and ashes,” and with great boldness: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?” Each time God agrees. The story teaches that the presence of a few righteous people can protect a whole community, and that God is more ready to forgive than we are to ask. Abraham stopped at ten. The prophets would go further (Jeremiah 5:1), and St Paul would see the fulfilment in one righteous man: “by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19). The Psalm sings the heart of the God Abraham was speaking to: “He does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities.”

In the Gospel two men offer to follow Jesus, and he answers each with a hard saying. To an eager scribe who promises, “Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go,” Jesus says, “Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests; but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.” Following him is not a comfortable career; it means sharing the life of a homeless wanderer. To a disciple who asks first to bury his father, Jesus says, “Follow me, and let the dead bury their own dead.” The words are shocking, for burying one's parents was a sacred duty in Israel. Jesus is not teaching contempt for parents; he is saying that the call of the kingdom comes before everything, even the holiest human duties, and that those who do not follow him are, in a sense, the dead. St Irenaeus, whom the Church honours today, followed Christ wherever he went. Born in Smyrna in Asia Minor around the year 130, he heard as a boy the preaching of St Polycarp, who had known St John the Apostle. He went to Gaul, became a priest of Lyons, and after the bishop Pothinus died in the persecution of 177 he became bishop there himself. Against the Gnostics, who claimed a secret knowledge and despised the body and the created world, he wrote his great work Against Heresies. He taught that the true faith is the one handed on publicly by the apostles through the bishops who succeeded them, above all in the Church of Rome, founded, he wrote, by the two most glorious apostles Peter and Paul. He loved the body God created and the flesh the Word took on; he wrote that the glory of God is a human being fully alive. Tradition honours him as a martyr, and in 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church with the title Doctor of Unity. Like Abraham, he stood before God for others; like the disciples, he left home to follow Christ to the ends of the known world. And tonight, with the Vigil of Sts Peter and Paul, the Church begins to celebrate the very apostles whose teaching he guarded.

Full homily

Dear friends, God says something beautiful in today's first reading: shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do? (Genesis 18:17). God treats Abraham as a friend, and tells him that he is going down to see the sin of Sodom. And Abraham does what friends of God do. He does not say: good, they deserve it. He stays standing before the Lord, and he prays for the city.

Listen to his prayer. Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked? Suppose there are fifty righteous. And when God agrees, he goes on: forty-five? forty? thirty? twenty? ten? He calls himself dust and ashes, but he does not stop. And each time God says yes. God is more ready to forgive than Abraham is to ask. The prayer of a righteous person, even for a wicked city, is precious to God.

This is a lesson for us. We complain a lot about our towns, our politics, our young people. Do we pray for them as much as we complain? Abraham stopped at ten. But God found one righteous man, Jesus, and for his sake he forgives all who turn to him. When we pray for others, we stand beside Jesus, the great intercessor.

In the Gospel Jesus tells those who want to follow him: the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head (Matthew 8:20). St Irenaeus, whom we honour today, took that seriously. He left his home in Asia, went to the far west, served the Church in Lyons in a time of persecution, and spent his life guarding the faith handed down from the apostles. He said: the glory of God is a human being fully alive. Today let us be fully alive in prayer for others, and in following Jesus without delay. Amen.

For children

Children, Abraham was God's friend. One day God told him that a city called Sodom was very, very bad. Abraham did not say: good, punish them! He prayed for them. He asked God: if there are fifty good people, will you save the city? Then forty? Thirty? Ten? And God said yes every time! When you hear about people doing wrong, do not just say they are bad. Pray for them, like Abraham!

Prayer

O God, who called the Bishop Saint Irenaeus to confirm true doctrine and peace in the Church, grant, we pray, through his intercession, that, being renewed in faith and charity, we may always be intent on fostering unity and concord. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, Judge of all the earth, you are merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. Give us Abraham's courage to stand before you for our cities and our families, and the readiness of true disciples to follow your Son without delay. Through the prayers of St Irenaeus, keep us faithful to the faith of the apostles Peter and Paul. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Abraham walks with his three visitors towards Sodom, and the Lord shares his plan with him. Abraham then dares to intercede: will the Judge of all the earth sweep away the righteous with the wicked? From fifty down to ten, God agrees to spare the city. In the Gospel Jesus tells a would-be follower that the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head, and tells another, “Follow me.” Psalm 103 sings of the Lord who is kind and merciful. Today the Church keeps the memorial of St Irenaeus, bishop and Doctor of the Church.

Genesis 18:16–33 · Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?

Setting.

The visitors who promised Isaac now look toward Sodom, the city of the plain whose sin had become notorious (13:13). God decides to share his purpose with Abraham, because Abraham is to teach his children “righteousness and justice”. What follows is the first great prayer of intercession in the Bible.

Scripture describes the sin of Sodom in more than one way: the violence and abuse against guests in chapter 19, and, in Ezekiel 16:49, pride, excess of food and prosperous ease, and failing to help the poor and needy.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–19: God decides not to hide his plan from Abraham, chosen to teach justice.
  • vv. 20–21: the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah.
  • vv. 22–25: Abraham stands before the Lord: “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?”
  • vv. 26–32: from fifty to forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten; each time God agrees.
  • v. 33: the Lord goes his way; Abraham returns home.

Key words.

  • zəʿāqâ, outcry: The cry of the oppressed for help, the same kind of cry as Israel’s in Egypt (Exodus 3:7–9). God hears the voice of the victims.
  • ṣaddîq, righteous: One who is right with God and with neighbour. Abraham’s question is whether the presence of a few righteous people can save a whole city.
  • ʿāpār wāʾēper, dust and ashes: Abraham knows how small he is before God (v. 27), yet he speaks. Humility and boldness go together in true prayer.

Verse by verse.

“Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do,” (18:17)

God treats Abraham as a friend, sharing his counsel with him. Later Scripture calls Abraham the friend of God (Isaiah 41:8; James 2:23).

“Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?” (18:23)

Abraham appeals to God’s own justice. He is not arguing against God but holding God to what God is.

“Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?” (18:25)

The key question of the chapter. God is the judge not only of Israel but of all nations, and his judgment is never unjust.

“For the sake of ten I will not destroy it.” (18:32)

Abraham stops at ten, and the Lord would have spared the city for ten. The righteous few protect the many.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that once God had confided his plan, Abraham’s heart was attuned to his Lord’s compassion for men, and he dared to intercede for them with bold confidence (CCC 2571). It adds that since Abraham, intercession, asking on behalf of another, has been characteristic of a heart attuned to God’s mercy (CCC 2635). The Church sees the fullness of this prayer in Christ, the one righteous man, for whose sake the many are spared, who “always lives to make intercession” for us (Hebrews 7:25).

Matthew 8:18–22 · Nowhere to lay his head

Setting.

Crowds press around Jesus, and he gives orders to cross the lake. Before he gets into the boat, two men speak to him about following. Matthew places these sayings just before the storm on the lake (8:23–27), where following Jesus will mean being in the boat with him in danger.

Structure.

  • v. 18: Jesus gives orders to cross to the other side.
  • vv. 19–20: a scribe: “I will follow you wherever you go”; the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.
  • vv. 21–22: a disciple: “let me first bury my father”; “Follow me, and let the dead bury their own dead.”

Key words.

  • grammateus, scribe: A teacher of the law. The scribe calls Jesus “Teacher” and offers to follow, with good will. Jesus wants him to know the cost.
  • ho huios tou anthrōpou, the Son of Man: Jesus’ favourite way of speaking of himself. Here it is used for the first time in Matthew, of his homelessness. The same title will be used of his suffering and of his coming in glory.
  • akoloutheō, to follow: The word of discipleship. It appears in both scenes and again in v. 23, when the disciples follow him into the boat.

Verse by verse.

“Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go.” (8:19)

A generous promise. Jesus does not refuse him, but makes sure he knows where Jesus goes.

“Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests; but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.” (8:20)

The wild animals have more security than he does. To follow him is to share his poverty and his trust in the Father.

“Follow me, and let the dead bury their own dead.” (8:22)

Burial of a father was among the most sacred duties in Jewish life. Jesus’ words are deliberately shocking, to show the urgency of the kingdom. Many Fathers and commentators take “the dead” to mean those who are spiritually dead, who have not yet heard the call.

How the Church reads it.

The Church does not read this Gospel as a rejection of care for parents, which the fourth commandment requires (CCC 2214–2220). She reads it as a call to put Christ first. The Catechism teaches that Jesus calls his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544). Priests, religious and missionaries who leave home and family live this saying in a visible way; every Christian lives it when the call of Christ comes before comfort.

For the Latin reader

St Irenaeus was born in Asia Minor, probably at Smyrna, between about 130 and 140. As a young man he heard St Polycarp, who had known the apostle John, so he stands very close to the apostles. He became a priest at Lyons in Gaul and, after the martyrdom of Bishop Pothinus in 177, its bishop. His great work, usually called Against Heresies, defended the faith against the Gnostic teachers by pointing to Scripture, the rule of faith and the succession of bishops from the apostles, above all in the Church of Rome, which he called founded and organised by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul (Against Heresies 3.3.2); the Catechism quotes the same passage on the communion of all the Churches with Rome (CCC 834). He wrote that “the glory of God is man fully alive” (CCC 294), and that the knot of Eve’s disobedience was loosed by Mary’s obedience (CCC 494). He is honoured as a martyr, though the details of his death are not known. In 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church, calling him the Doctor of Unity. Like Abraham, he spoke for the whole Church; like the disciples in the Gospel, he left his homeland to follow Christ.

For study and teaching

  • What “first let me…” do I put between myself and Christ’s call?
