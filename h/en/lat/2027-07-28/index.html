Word for the day

“Moses came down from Mount Sinai. As he came down from the mountain with the two tablets of the covenant in his hand, Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone because he had been talking with God.” Exodus 34:29

Reflection

Moses comes down Mount Sinai for the second time, with the new tablets in his hands, and he does not know that his face is shining. “Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone because he had been talking with God.” He has spent forty days in the presence of the Lord, and the glory has left its mark on him, though he himself is the last to notice. Aaron and the Israelites see it and are afraid to come near, so Moses calls them, speaks to them, and gives them the commands he has received; then he puts a veil over his face. Whenever he goes in to speak with the Lord he takes the veil off, and when he comes out he tells the people what he has been told, and they see again that his face is shining. The Psalm recalls this friendship: “Moses and Aaron were among his priests, Samuel also was among those who called on his name. They cried to the Lord, and he answered them. He spoke to them in the pillar of cloud.” The holiest people in Scripture are those who have been with God and do not realise how much of him they carry. St Paul will say that in Christ the veil is removed and that “all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another” (2 Corinthians 3:18).

The Gospel gives two tiny parables, each of one sentence. A man finds a treasure hidden in a field, hides it again, and “in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.” A merchant who knows pearls finds “one pearl of great value” and sells everything to buy it. Notice the word “joy.” Neither man calls it a sacrifice. They give up everything because they have found something better, and the finding comes first. This is how the Church understands the call to follow Christ, and the consecrated life in particular: not a loss but a discovery. St Paul says the same of himself: “whatever gains I had, these I have come to regard as loss because of Christ... because of the surpassing value of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord” (Philippians 3:7–8). The poor man with the field and the rich merchant show that the kingdom is for every kind of person, and that the price is the same for both: all that one has.

Today the Church in India keeps the memorial of a woman who found the pearl early and whose face shone without her knowing it. Alphonsa was born Anna, called Annakutty, on 19 August 1910 at Kudamaloor, near Kottayam in Kerala, into a Syro-Malabar Catholic family. Her mother died when she was small, and she grew up in the care of relatives. From childhood she wanted to belong wholly to Jesus. As a young girl, to avoid a marriage that others were arranging, she injured her feet in a fire, a rash act that the Church does not hold up for imitation, but which shows how single her desire was. She entered the convent of the Franciscan Clarist Congregation at Bharananganam in 1927, received the habit in 1928 and the name Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception, and made her first vows in 1931 and her final vows in 1936. Her life was short and hidden, and full of illness. She taught young children for a time and could do little of the work that the world counts as important. Yet in her letters and in the memory of her sisters she appears as a woman who took her sufferings in union with the Cross of Christ, offered them for others, and met everyone with gentleness. She died at Bharananganam on 28 July 1946, aged thirty-five. Her tomb in the church of St Mary there became a place of pilgrimage. Pope St John Paul II beatified her at Kottayam on 8 February 1986, and Pope Benedict XVI canonised her in Rome on 12 October 2008: she is the first woman of Indian origin to be canonised and the first canonised saint of the Syro-Malabar Church. Like Moses, she did not know that her face was shining; the people of Kerala saw it and came near. Like the merchant, she found one pearl and sold everything. And like the grain of wheat of the Gospel, she fell into the earth and bore much fruit (John 12:24).

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, there is something very beautiful about a face that has been in the presence of God. Moses comes down the mountain, and his face is shining. And the Bible says: Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone because he had been talking with God (Exodus 34:29). The people could see it, but he could not. Holiness is like that. It does not know itself.

Today the Church in India remembers a woman whose face shone like that. Annakutty was born in 1910 in a village near Kottayam. Her mother died when she was small. She grew up wanting to belong to Jesus, and in 1927 she entered the convent of the Clarist sisters at Bharananganam. She took the name Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception. She was never strong. She was often ill, and she could do little of what the world calls important. She died at thirty-five. And the people of Kerala came to her tomb, and the Church has called her a saint.

What was her secret? The Gospel today gives us the answer: the treasure and the pearl. A man finds a treasure in a field, and in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field (Matthew 13:44). In his joy: that is the key. Alphonsa did not give up everything out of sadness. She had found Jesus, and he was worth more than anything else. St Paul says the same: I regard everything as loss because of the surpassing value of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord (Philippians 3:8).

And she gave her suffering a meaning. She offered her pain, in union with the Cross of Christ, for others. The Lord himself says: unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24). Alphonsa's life was like a grain in the earth, hidden, small, and falling. And look at the harvest: pilgrims at her tomb, sick people finding peace, young women hearing the call, a whole Church in India rejoicing, and the Pope in Rome calling her a saint in 2008.

What about us? Perhaps we feel that our lives are small and hidden, with nothing great to show. But this is exactly where holiness grows. Let us offer our small pains and ordinary duties with love. Let us look for the pearl. And let us be gentle, because, like Moses, we may be carrying a light we do not see. St Alphonsa, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember St Alphonsa, who lived in Kerala. Her name when she was little was Annakutty. She loved Jesus very much, and she became a sister. She was often ill, but she was never cross; she offered her pain to Jesus for other people. When she died, people started praying at her grave, and the Pope said she is a saint. She is the first woman from India to be made a saint! Jesus said that heaven is like a treasure in a field. Alphonsa found the treasure. Who is the treasure? Jesus! Let us try to be kind today, even if we are tired, and offer it to him.

Prayer

Lord God, you hid the life of St Alphonsa in a quiet convent and let it shine on the whole Church in India. Through her prayers, grant that we too may find the pearl of great value, offer our sufferings in love for others, and carry your light with gentleness, so that those who see us may come near and give glory to you. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. St Alphonsa, pray for us.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Moses comes down from Sinai with a shining face and does not know it: he has been talking with God, and the light has stayed on him. The people are afraid to come near, so he veils his face when he speaks with them and takes the veil off when he goes in before the Lord. In the Gospel Jesus tells two short parables of the kingdom: a hidden treasure and a pearl of great price, each found and bought at the cost of everything. Psalm 99 repeats, “Holy is the Lord our God.”

Today is the memorial of St Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception, a Franciscan Clarist sister of Kerala who died on 28 July 1946 after a short life of great suffering, and who in 2008 was canonised, the first woman of India raised to the altars. These readings are the weekday readings; the prayers of the Mass may be taken from the Common of Virgins. Her life is a living commentary on both: a face that shone without her knowing it, and a heart that gave everything for the treasure.

Exodus 34:29–35 · Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone

Setting.

Moses has come down Sinai for the second time, carrying the new tablets after the golden calf. For forty days and nights he has been in the presence of the Lord (34:28), and the people see the mark of it on him.

The passage is the end of a long section about the renewal of the covenant. It also prepares the building of the tabernacle in the last chapters of Exodus: the people have a mediator who goes in before the Lord and comes out to tell them what he has been commanded.

Structure.

  • v. 29: Moses comes down with the tablets, his face shining; he does not know it.
  • v. 30: Aaron and the people see the light and are afraid.
  • vv. 31–32: Moses calls them; the leaders and then all the people come near, and he gives them the Lord’s commands.
  • vv. 33–35: Moses puts a veil on his face when he has finished speaking, and removes it each time he goes in to speak with the Lord.

Key words.

  • qāran ʿôr pānāw, the skin of his face shone: The verb qāran is related to qeren, “horn” or “ray”. Moses’ face sent out rays of light. St Jerome’s Latin translation understood “horned”, and for this reason Moses is painted and carved with horns in much medieval art, as in Michelangelo’s statue.
  • masweh, veil: A covering used only in this passage in the Old Testament. Moses wears it when speaking to the people and removes it before the Lord.
  • yārēʾ, to be afraid: Aaron and the people are afraid to come near. Holiness both attracts and frightens, and the one who has been with God carries something of it.

Verse by verse.

“Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone because he had been talking with God.” (34:29)

The most beautiful thing about the verse is what it says of Moses: he does not know. Holiness does not look at itself. The glory is the result of being with God, not of trying to shine.

“they were afraid to come near him.” (34:30)

The people who sinned with the calf cannot bear the sight of the light. The light of God shows what is in us, and Moses must call them to come near.

“Moses called to them;” (34:31)

The mediator does not leave the people afraid. He calls them, and they come. The one who has been with God brings others near and does not keep them at a distance.

“whenever Moses went in before the Lord to speak with him, he would take the veil off,” (34:34)

Before the Lord there is no veil. Moses speaks with God unhidden, and then returns to the people who need the veil.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul reads this passage in 2 Corinthians 3:7–18. The glory on Moses’ face was real and was fading; the glory of the new covenant is greater and lasting, and Christians, with “unveiled faces”, are transformed from glory to glory by the Spirit. This is not a rejection of Moses or of the Old Covenant, which remains God’s gift (Romans 11:29); it is a statement that the light of the Law finds its fulfilment in Christ. The Church also reads the shining face of Moses in the light of the Transfiguration, where Moses and Elijah appear with Jesus and the face of Jesus shines like the sun (Matthew 17:2–3). The Catechism teaches that the saints share in the light of Christ and that by their lives the Church recognises the power of the Spirit of holiness within her (CCC 828). The saints are those whose faces shine because they have been with God.

Matthew 13:44–46 · In his joy he goes and sells all that he has

Setting.

These are the shortest parables in the chapter. They are spoken to the disciples in the house, after Jesus has explained the weeds (13:36–43), and they come in a pair, as the mustard seed and the yeast did: a man and then a merchant.

The treasure is found by chance; the pearl is found after a search. In both cases the discovery is followed by a decision, and the decision is joyful. They tell us what the kingdom is worth, and how the one who finds it should act.

Structure.

  • v. 44: the treasure hidden in a field; the finder hides it again, and in his joy sells all that he has and buys the field.
  • vv. 45–46: the merchant looking for fine pearls finds one of great value, sells all that he has and buys it.

Key words.

  • thēsauros, treasure: Money or valuables, often buried in a field for safety, since there were no banks. A man who finds one has found more than he could ever earn.
  • charas, joy: The joy that causes the man to sell everything. The cost is not a grim obligation but the natural response of one who has seen the value.
  • margaritēs, pearl: Pearls were the most prized of jewels in the ancient world. The merchant knows pearls, which is why he recognises this one as worth everything.

Verse by verse.

“The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field,” (13:44)

The kingdom is already present but hidden. It must be found, and when found, it is worth more than everything else.

“then in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.” (13:44)

The finder is not forced to sell; he sells in joy. For the one who has seen what the kingdom is, giving up other things no longer feels like loss.

“on finding one pearl of great value, he went and sold all that he had and bought it.” (13:46)

The merchant sells all for one pearl. The Church has always seen in these words the way of those who give everything to Christ, as martyrs, religious and all who put him first.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that, in the parables, Jesus invites people to enter the kingdom, and that to enter it one must make a radical choice, selling all that one has (CCC 546). Many Fathers and saints read the pearl as Christ himself (in substance), and the kingdom as his own presence in the soul. In the Church’s tradition the vows of poverty, chastity and obedience are one way to “sell all” for the kingdom, but the parable speaks to every Christian: whoever has found Christ is asked to prefer nothing to him, and to do it in joy. This is why the Church keeps the memorials of the saints: they show what this looks like in a real life.

Questions people ask

What is a memorial, and why does the Church keep St Alphonsa’s today?

A memorial is the lowest of the three ranks of feasts in the Roman calendar, below a solemnity and a feast. Memorials are obligatory or optional. For the Church in India, St Alphonsa’s is an obligatory memorial on 28 July, the day of her death. Her prayers at Mass may be taken from the Common of Virgins, but the readings are the weekday readings, which is why we hear Moses and the parables today.

Is it healthy to speak of offering one’s suffering to God?

Yes, when it is understood rightly. The Church never says that suffering is good in itself or that God wants people to suffer. Christ has given suffering a new meaning: it can unite us to his redemptive Passion (CCC 1521). The Church also asks us to relieve suffering by every means, and the sick have a right to treatment and care. The saints did both: they sought relief where it could be had, and offered with Christ what could not be removed.

Why is she called “of the Immaculate Conception”?

Religious often take a name that places them under the protection of a mystery of faith or of the Blessed Virgin. Alphonsa received the name when she was given the habit as a Franciscan Clarist sister in 1928. The Immaculate Conception, defined in 1854, is the teaching that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her existence, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Christ (CCC 491). Devotion to Mary was part of her spirituality.

For the Latin reader

St Alphonsa was born Annakutty on 19 August 1910 at Kudamaloor in Kerala, in the Syro-Malabar Church. Her mother died soon after her birth, and she was raised by relatives. Accounts of her life tell of a severe burn to her feet in her girlhood, at a time when a marriage was being arranged for her, and of her wish to belong to Christ alone. She entered the Franciscan Clarist Congregation at Bharananganam in 1927, received the habit in 1928, made her first vows in 1931 and her perpetual profession in 1936. Her life was marked by illness, and she died at Bharananganam on 28 July 1946, at the age of thirty-five. She was beatified by Pope John Paul II in Kottayam in 1986 and canonised by Pope Benedict XVI in Rome on 12 October 2008, the first woman of India raised to the altars. Her tomb at Bharananganam is a place of pilgrimage for people of every faith, and every Christian can pray for the sick through her intercession. The Latin Church in India keeps her memorial today, and she is honoured above all by her own Syro-Malabar Church. The Gospel today fits: she is remembered as one who gave everything, quietly and in joy, for the treasure.

For study and teaching

  • Moses did not know that his face shone. What might others see in me that I cannot see myself, and how can I stay close enough to God to carry his light?
  • The people were afraid, yet Moses called them near. Who is afraid to come near to God or to the Church, and how can I call them, as Moses did?
  • The finder of the treasure sold all “in his joy”. What would I give up if I truly saw the worth of the kingdom, and what stops me from doing it joyfully?
  • St Alphonsa offered her suffering with Christ. For whom can I offer something small and hidden today, a pain, a tiredness or a patience?
