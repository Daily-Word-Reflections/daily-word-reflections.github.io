Word for the day

“A bruised reed he will not break, and a dimly burning wick he will not quench; he will faithfully bring forth justice.” Isaiah 42:3

Reflection

Today's first reading is the departure. After the last night in Egypt the people of Israel march out from Rameses to Succoth, “about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children.” They carry unleavened dough, because they were “driven out of Egypt and could not wait.” The Bible records the exact length of the slavery, “four hundred thirty years,” and says that on the very day the years were completed, “all the companies of the Lord went out from the land of Egypt.” There is one detail that is easy to miss: “A mixed crowd also went up with them.” Not all who left Egypt were descendants of Jacob; there were other slaves and foreigners who saw that the God of Israel was acting and went along with them. From the start the people of God has been bigger than a single family. And then comes the heart of the passage: “That was for the Lord a night of vigil, to bring them out of the land of Egypt. That same night is a vigil to be kept for the Lord by all the Israelites throughout their generations.” The night belonged to the Lord, who kept watch; now the people keep watch in return. For this reason the Jewish people stay up on Passover night to tell the story, and the Church, in the same spirit, keeps the greatest vigil of the year on Holy Saturday night, when the Easter Candle is lit and the deacon sings that this is the night when God led our ancestors out of Egypt and the night when Christ broke the chains of death. The psalm repeats the refrain after every line, “for his steadfast love endures forever,” and names what he did: “brought Israel out from among them … with a strong hand and an outstretched arm,” and “remembered us in our low estate.”

In the Gospel the shadow falls. After the argument about the sabbath, “the Pharisees went out and conspired against him, how to destroy him.” Some of the Pharisees, we must remember, were friends of Jesus, and some warned him of danger; Matthew is speaking of the party that plotted against him. What does Jesus do? “When Jesus became aware of this, he departed.” He does not argue, he does not threaten, and he does not run away in fear. He withdraws, and goes on healing: “Many crowds followed him, and he cured all of them.” And he commands silence: “he ordered them not to make him known.” Matthew explains why by quoting the first Servant Song of Isaiah: “Here is my servant, whom I have chosen, my beloved, with whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit upon him, and he will proclaim justice to the Gentiles.” The King is a Servant, and the Servant is gentle: “He will not wrangle or cry aloud, nor will anyone hear his voice in the streets. He will not break a bruised reed or quench a smoldering wick until he brings justice to victory.” A bruised reed is a stem that is cracked and about to snap, and a smoldering wick is a flame about to die; they are pictures of the weak, the discouraged and the sinful, and Jesus does not throw them away: he bends over them to heal. And the passage ends with the word that rings like the “mixed crowd” of Exodus: “in his name the Gentiles will hope.” The people who left Egypt were already a mixed company, and the Servant opens the way for all nations. Both readings are about God's quiet, patient strength: the night watch of the Lord over his people, and the gentle hand over the bruised reed.

Full homily

Dear friends, picture the night the people leave Egypt. After four hundred and thirty years, in a hurry, with unleavened dough on their backs, six hundred thousand men on foot, besides women and children. And the Bible says: that was for the Lord a night of vigil (Exodus 12:42). Think of that: while the people are rushing and afraid, God is awake. He is keeping watch. That is the first good news: when you cannot sleep, when you are afraid, someone is awake over you. And so every year the people keep that night, and every year at Easter we keep the greatest vigil of all.

Notice also: a mixed crowd also went up with them (Exodus 12:38). It was not only the family of Jacob. Other slaves, other peoples, saw what God was doing and joined in. God's people has never been a closed club. In Jesus, the prophet says, the Gentiles will hope (Matthew 12:21). The door is wide. Who is the mixed crowd today, the migrant worker, the newcomer in the parish, the one who does not look like us? Let us make room.

In the Gospel there is a plot. The enemies of Jesus meet and decide to destroy him. And what does Jesus do? He leaves. He does not shout, he does not fight. He goes on healing. Matthew says that he fulfils Isaiah: he will not wrangle or cry aloud; he will not break a bruised reed or quench a smoldering wick (Matthew 12:19–20). Brothers and sisters, we are all bruised reeds in some way: weak, discouraged, close to snapping. And he does not break us. He does not put out the little flame of our faith, even when it is only smoking. He bends over it and protects it with his hand.

Let us take that gentleness with us. When we meet someone who is weak, let us not snap them; let us shield the flame. And when we ourselves are bruised, let us remember that the Lord who watched over Israel in the night is watching over us, and that his steadfast love endures for ever. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago God's people had to leave Egypt in a great hurry, in the middle of the night. They were scared. But God stayed awake all night, looking after them! Many other people joined them too, because they saw that God was good. Years later, Jesus was kind in a special way. The prophet said that Jesus would never break a bent reed, or put out a little flame. Imagine a candle nearly out: Jesus shields it with his hand and it shines again. When a friend is sad or a bit broken, you can be like Jesus. Be gentle, say something kind, and help the little flame to shine.

Prayer

Lord God, you kept a night of vigil to bring your people out of slavery, and your steadfast love endures for ever. Watch over us tonight and always, and teach us to keep watch with you in prayer. Lord Jesus, chosen Servant, beloved of the Father, you did not break the bruised reed or quench the smoldering wick: be gentle with our weakness, and make us gentle with the weakness of others. Open your house to the mixed crowd of all nations, and let your name be the hope of every people. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After the Passover night comes the departure. The first reading tells of Israel marching out of Egypt with unleavened dough on their backs, and calls the night a vigil kept for the Lord for all generations. In the Gospel the Pharisees plot against Jesus after the sabbath healing, and he quietly withdraws, healing all who follow him. Matthew sees in this gentleness the Servant of Isaiah, who will not break a bruised reed or quench a smoldering wick. Both readings show God’s way of saving: not with noise and force of arms, but in a night of watching and in a quiet service of the weak.

Exodus 12:37–42 · A night of vigil

Setting.

Yesterday’s reading (Exodus 11:10–12:14) gave the instructions for the Passover; today’s tells of the departure itself. The night of the tenth plague has passed, and Pharaoh has let Israel go (12:29–32). The people leave in haste from Rameses, the royal city in the eastern delta, and make their first stop at Succoth. The passage is a short narrative summary, written with a liturgical tone, and its last verse turns from the past to the feast kept for ever.

Structure.

  • v. 37: the march from Rameses to Succoth, with six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children.
  • v. 38: a mixed crowd and great flocks and herds go with them.
  • v. 39: unleavened cakes baked from the dough, because there was no time to prepare.
  • vv. 40–41: the time in Egypt: four hundred and thirty years, ending exactly on that day.
  • v. 42: it was a night of vigil for the Lord, to be kept by all the Israelites for ever.

Key words.

  • ʿēreb rab, a mixed crowd: Other peoples, probably slaves and poor workers of different origins, joined Israel in its going out. The people of God has always been open to those who attach themselves to it (compare Exodus 12:48–49).
  • maṣṣôt, unleavened cakes: Bread made without yeast, because there was no time to wait for it to rise. Israel keeps this bread each year as a reminder of the haste of the deliverance.
  • lēl šimmurîm, a night of vigil: Literally “a night of keeping” or “watching”. God kept watch to bring them out, and Israel is to keep watch in return. The word suggests both God’s care and the people’s attention.

Verse by verse.

“about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children.” (12:37)

The number is very large, and scholars debate how to understand it (some think the Hebrew word translated “thousand” may also mean a clan or military unit). What the verse says for sure is that the whole people went out, as one people.

“A mixed crowd also went up with them,” (12:38)

From the very beginning those who join Israel are among the people God leads out. Salvation is not shut to any who come with them.

“because they were driven out of Egypt and could not wait,” (12:39)

The people did not plan their own liberation. God’s act came suddenly, and they had to follow.

“That was for the Lord a night of vigil, to bring them out of the land of Egypt.” (12:42)

The night of the exodus is a night when God was watching over his people, and every year those who keep it watch with him.

How the Church reads it.

The Church remembers this night every year at the Easter Vigil, the “mother of all vigils”, which begins in the dark and reads the story of the exodus. The Exsultet, the great proclamation of Easter, sings in substance that this is the night when God first saved our fathers and delivered the children of Israel from slavery in Egypt. The Church reads the Passover as the great type of Christ’s own passing from death to life, and it also honors the Jewish people who have kept the vigil of Passover through every century. Paul recalls the 430 years when he explains that the promise to Abraham came long before the law (Galatians 3:17).

Matthew 12:14–21 · He will not break a bruised reed

Setting.

The passage follows the sabbath healing of the man with the withered hand (12:9–13). Some Pharisees are angered and plan how to destroy Jesus. The Pharisees were a lay movement of devout Jews; Matthew names those in this scene, not the whole group and certainly not the Jewish people. Jesus does not answer with a counter-attack. He withdraws, and he goes on healing, and Matthew finds in this the first Servant Song of Isaiah (Isaiah 42:1–4).

Structure.

  • v. 14: the plot of the Pharisees.
  • vv. 15–16: Jesus withdraws; the crowds follow; he heals them all and asks that he not be made known.
  • vv. 17–21: Matthew’s quotation of Isaiah 42:1–4: the chosen servant, filled with the Spirit, brings justice to the nations without quarrel or noise, and the Gentiles will hope in his name.

Key words.

  • pais, servant, child: The Greek word can mean both. It renders the Hebrew ʿebed, the servant who belongs to the Lord and does his will. At the baptism of Jesus the Father’s voice uses the same words of love (3:17).
  • krisis, justice, judgment: The right order God brings to the world, especially in defense of the poor and the wronged. The servant brings it “to victory”, not by force but by faithful gentleness.
  • kalamos syntetrimmenos, bruised reed: A reed already cracked, which any pressure would snap. The picture is of something weak and damaged, and the servant treats it with care.

Verse by verse.

“the Pharisees went out and conspired against him, how to destroy him.” (12:14)

The sabbath healing, an act of mercy, is the occasion of the plot. Here begins the road that leads to the Cross.

“he cured all of them,” (12:15)

Jesus does not withdraw to rest alone. Even when he leaves the scene of conflict, he serves those who follow.

“he ordered them not to make him known.” (12:16)

Jesus does not wish to be known first as a wonder-worker or as a political figure. He lets his mission unfold in its own way, as that of a servant who will suffer.

“Here is my servant, whom I have chosen, my beloved,” (12:18)

The words recall the voice at the baptism, “This is my beloved Son” (3:17). Matthew shows that the Son is also the Servant.

“He will not break a bruised reed or quench a smoldering wick until he brings justice to victory.” (12:20)

This is the heart of the passage: strength is shown in gentleness. Jesus will not crush those who are weak in faith or in strength.

“in his name the Gentiles will hope.” (12:21)

The scene of conflict with some of his own people ends with a word of hope for the nations. Matthew’s Gospel, written for a community of Jewish and Gentile believers, holds both.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to the Servant of Isaiah as one of the ways the Spirit prepared the coming of the Messiah (CCC 713). The Church sees in the bruised reed and smoldering wick the way Jesus meets sinners and the weak: he calls them, forgives them, and does not break them. Pastors and confessors are asked to imitate this gentleness. The Church recalls too that the Servant’s mission is to bring justice to the nations, which is why the Gospel is preached to all peoples.

Questions people ask

Does this Gospel say that the Pharisees as a group wanted to kill Jesus?

No. Matthew speaks of some Pharisees in this scene. In the Gospels other Pharisees invite Jesus to dinner and warn him of danger (Luke 7:36; 13:31), and Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea, who were of the leading class, honor him at the end. The Church teaches that what happened to Jesus in his Passion cannot be blamed on all the Jews of that time, nor on the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4).

For study and teaching

  • The Israelites left Egypt in haste, with only dough and a mixed crowd. When has God asked me to move before I felt ready?
  • Jesus will not break a bruised reed. Whose weakness do I treat roughly, in my family or parish, and how could I treat it with his gentleness?
