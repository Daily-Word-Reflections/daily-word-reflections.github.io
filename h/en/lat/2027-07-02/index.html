Readings

  • First Reading Genesis 23:1–4, 19; 24:1–8, 62–67 · Isaac loved Rebekah, and was comforted after his mother's death

  • Gospel Matthew 9:9–13 · I desire mercy, not sacrifice

Word for the day

““Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’ For I have come to call not the righteous but sinners.”” Matthew 9:13

Reflection

The first reading moves in two steps: from a grave to a wedding. “Sarah lived one hundred twenty-seven years.” She dies at Hebron, and Abraham, the man of faith, does what every human being does: he goes in “to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her.” Holy Scripture does not hurry grief. Then he rises and does something practical and dignified: he asks the Hittites for a place to bury his dead. “I am a stranger and an alien residing among you; give me property among you for a burying place.” After more than sixty years in the land God had promised him, the only piece of it Abraham owns is a cave and a field at Machpelah, bought for “four hundred shekels of silver” (Genesis 23:16). It is a lesson in the pilgrim life. The Letter to the Hebrews comments: all these died in faith, and “they confessed that they were strangers and foreigners on the earth,” seeking “a better country, that is, a heavenly one.” Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions all honour that tomb of the patriarchs. The Church too teaches us to respect the bodies of the dead and to pray at their graves (Catechism 2300). The second part of the reading, from chapter 24, is a very different scene. Abraham is old; he sends his oldest servant to find a wife for Isaac among his own kin, and trusts that God “will send his angel before you.” After the long story of the well and Rebekah's kindness, which the Lectionary leaves out, we see the meeting: “Isaac went out in the evening to walk in the field; and looking up, he saw camels coming.” Rebekah looks up too, sees him, and veils herself. Then comes a sentence of great tenderness: “Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent. He took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother's death.” The covenant goes on through the love of husband and wife. The Church sees in the home built on such love a small image of the love of Christ for his people (Catechism 1602–1605).

The Gospel is the call of a man whom everybody despised. Matthew was a tax collector, one who collected tolls for the Roman authorities and for his own pocket, a collaborator in the eyes of his people. “As Jesus was walking along, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth; and he said to him, ‘Follow me.’ And he got up and followed him.” Mark and Luke tell the same story about a tax collector named Levi, and the Church has long understood that this is the apostle Matthew, the author of the Gospel that we read this week. Two words in the story are worth pausing on: he saw, and he said. Jesus looked at the man behind the counter and saw a disciple. Then comes the dinner, and with it the scandal. Jesus sits at table with “many tax collectors and sinners.” In the East to eat with someone was to accept him as a friend. The Pharisees ask the disciples, “Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?” Jesus answers with a proverb and a quotation. The proverb: “Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick.” The quotation is from the prophet Hosea: “Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’” Go and learn: the teachers of the Law are sent back to school! God does not want religion without love. And then the mission statement of Jesus: “I have come to call not the righteous but sinners.” On First Fridays the Church thinks especially of the Heart that has this mercy. Since the revelations to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial in France in 1673–75, Catholics have honoured the Sacred Heart pierced by the lance, from which came “blood and water,” and many keep the nine First Fridays with Mass and Communion. The prayer of St Paul fits the day: that we may “know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today's first reading begins with a funeral. Sarah has died, and Abraham goes in to weep for her. The Bible does not tell us to hide our tears. Abraham mourns, and then he does the practical thing. He asks for a place to bury his wife. And we learn something that is almost strange: after all those years with God's promise of the whole land, Abraham owns one cave. I am a stranger and an alien residing among you (Genesis 23:4).

That is the life of faith. We are pilgrims. The Letter to the Hebrews says that the patriarchs confessed that they were strangers and foreigners on the earth, because they desired a better country, that is, a heavenly one (Hebrews 11:13–16). We have homes, jobs and plans, and we should look after them well. But our true home is where Christ is, and our dead are not lost: they have gone ahead of us.

And God comforts his pilgrims through each other. Isaac goes out in the evening to walk in the field. He is grieving for his mother. And he looks up and sees camels coming. Rebekah looks up too. Then the Bible says something simple and beautiful: he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother's death (Genesis 24:67). Husbands and wives, parents and children, friends: love is the way God consoles. Let us not forget to say to those near us that they are a comfort.

In the Gospel Jesus walks by a tax booth and sees a man. Not a tax collector, not a thief, but a man, and he says: Follow me. Matthew gets up. Then at the dinner Jesus eats with sinners, and when he is criticised he answers: Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick (Matthew 9:12). Go and learn what this means: I desire mercy, not sacrifice. On this First Friday we look at the Heart that was pierced for sinners. If you feel you are too bad to come, this Gospel was written for you. And if you think you are too good to need him, it was written for you too. Let us all come to the physician, and let us make room at our own table for those whom he has called.

For children

Children, a man named Matthew collected taxes, and nobody liked him. One day Jesus walked by and said: follow me. Matthew stood up and went with Jesus! Then he gave a big dinner, and Jesus sat down with all his friends, even those whom others did not like. Some grown-ups were cross. But Jesus said: I came for those who need help, like a doctor for sick people. Jesus loves everybody, and today is First Friday, the day we thank him for his Sacred Heart. Let us be kind to a child who sits alone!

Prayer

God of Abraham and Sarah, you comfort those who mourn and you make the love of husband and wife a sign of your faithfulness. Teach us to live as pilgrims, strangers and aliens on earth, looking for the better country that you have prepared. Lord Jesus, who saw a tax collector at his table and said, Follow me, call us from whatever keeps us sitting at our counters. Sacred Heart of Jesus, meek and humble, gentle and merciful, make our hearts like yours, and teach us to desire mercy more than sacrifice. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two tables and a tomb. In Genesis, Abraham buries Sarah in the only piece of land he ever owned in Canaan, and later his servant brings Rebekah to Isaac, who “was comforted after his mother’s death”. The promise of God continues through grief and through a marriage. In the Gospel Jesus calls a tax collector, Matthew, from his booth and sits at table with him and his friends. When he is questioned, he answers with a word from the prophet Hosea: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.” Psalm 106 begins the response: “Give thanks to the Lord, for he is good.”

Genesis 23:1–4, 19; 24:1–8, 62–67 · Buried with honour, comforted by love

Setting.

Sarah has died at Hebron, aged one hundred twenty-seven; she is the only woman in Genesis whose age at death is recorded. Abraham, who has lived as a wanderer for more than sixty years, now has to ask for land to bury her. The lectionary leaves out the long and courteous bargaining with Ephron the Hittite (23:5–18), and the long story of the servant at the well (24:9–61), and gives us the beginning and the end of each chapter.

Background. The cave of Machpelah was the first piece of the promised land that Abraham owned. Abraham himself, Isaac and Rebekah, and later Jacob and Leah were buried there (Genesis 25:9–10; 49:29–32). The servant in chapter 24 is not named; Jewish tradition identifies him with Eliezer, mentioned in 15:2. The oath with the hand “under the thigh” (24:2) was a solemn form of swearing in the patriarchal age.

Structure.

  • 23:1–4: Sarah dies; Abraham mourns, then asks the Hittites for a burial place.
  • 23:19: the burial in the cave of Machpelah.
  • 24:1–8: Abraham, now old, makes his servant swear to seek a wife for Isaac among his own kindred, and not to take Isaac back to Mesopotamia.
  • 24:62–67: Isaac meets Rebekah in the evening; he takes her into Sarah’s tent, loves her, and is comforted.

Key words.

  • gēr wĕtôšāb, stranger and alien: The two words describe a foreigner without land rights. Abraham uses them of himself. The Letter to the Hebrews says that the patriarchs confessed themselves “strangers and foreigners on the earth” (Hebrews 11:13).
  • lāśûaḥ, to walk, to meditate: Found only here in the Old Testament and of uncertain meaning. The ancient Greek translation read “to meditate”, which is also how the Malayalam Bible understands it. Either way, Isaac is shown in quiet evening solitude when his bride arrives.
  • nāḥam, he was comforted: The verb of consolation. Rebekah does not replace Sarah; she is the sign that life and the promise go on.

Verse by verse.

“I am a stranger and an alien residing among you” (23:4)

Abraham has been in Canaan for decades and still owns no land. He asks politely, as a guest, and will insist on paying the full price (23:16).

“he will send his angel before you” (24:7)

Abraham trusts that the God who brought him out of his father’s house will guide the servant. The same God who once demanded Isaac now provides for him.

“Isaac went out in the evening to walk in the field” (24:63)

A textual note: the Hebrew verb is rare and its meaning uncertain. “Walk” and “meditate” are both ancient readings, and the second has made this verse a favourite picture of prayer.

“He took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother’s death.” (24:67)

Marriage is shown as a place of love and consolation, not only of family planning. The tent of Sarah, empty since her death, is filled again.

How the Church reads it.

The Church treats burial as a work of mercy and an act of faith in the resurrection: the bodies of the dead are to be treated with respect and charity (CCC 2300). Abraham’s grief and his care for Sarah’s grave are a first example. The reading from Genesis 24 is one of those offered for the celebration of marriage in the Roman Rite, because it shows a marriage received from God’s hand, begun in prayer and ending in love. The Letter to the Hebrews sees the patriarchs, living in tents as strangers and looking for a city with foundations, as a sign of the Church’s pilgrim journey (Hebrews 11:9–10, 13–16).

Matthew 9:9–13 · I desire mercy, not sacrifice

Setting.

Right after the healing of the paralyzed man (9:1–8), Jesus passes the tax booth at Capernaum and calls Matthew. Mark (2:14) and Luke (5:27) give the man’s name as Levi; the Church has always understood him to be the apostle Matthew, who is called “the tax collector” in Matthew 10:3.

Background. Tax collectors collected duties on goods and travellers on behalf of Herod Antipas or of Rome. They were widely resented as collaborators and often as extortioners. “Sinners” here means those regarded as living outside the law as it was taught, or openly bad. To eat with someone was to share fellowship, which is why the question is asked. The Pharisees are men who take holiness seriously; their question comes from that concern, not from simple hostility.

Structure.

  • v. 9: the call: “Follow me”, and Matthew rises at once.
  • v. 10: a meal at the house, with many tax collectors and sinners.
  • v. 11: the Pharisees ask the disciples why their teacher eats with such people.
  • vv. 12–13: Jesus’ answer: the physician, the word of Hosea, and his mission.

Key words.

  • akolouthei moi, follow me: The ordinary word for a disciple following a teacher. In the Gospels it is also the call to share his way and, in the end, his Cross.
  • eleos, mercy: Greek for the Hebrew hesed in Hosea 6:6: faithful, loving kindness. Hosea sets it against sacrifice offered without it. Jesus does not reject sacrifice; he reminds the listeners what it is for.
  • hoi kakōs echontes, those who are sick: Literally “those who have it badly”. The image of the physician is Jesus’ own description of his mission, and the Church has taken it up in many prayers.

Verse by verse.

“he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth; and he said to him, “Follow me.”” (9:9)

Matthew’s call is told in two verbs: he saw, and he said. Jesus sees the man behind the booth. Matthew’s response is equally brief: “he got up and followed him”.

“Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?” (9:11)

The Pharisees ask the disciples, not Jesus. The question assumes that holiness means keeping apart. Jesus’ answer shows that his holiness reaches towards sinners to heal them.

“Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick.” (9:12)

Jesus does not say that the Pharisees need no healing. He takes the common saying and makes it a statement of why he has come.

“Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’” (9:13)

“Go and learn” was a teacher’s way of sending someone to study Scripture. Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 to show that he stands within the prophets, not against them.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus scandalized the Pharisees by eating with tax collectors and sinners as familiarly as with themselves (CCC 589), and that he invites sinners to the table of the kingdom (CCC 545). The Fathers delighted in the call of Matthew. Bede, in a homily on this passage, says that Jesus saw him with mercy and so chose him (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 1.21). Pope Francis took the words of this homily as his episcopal motto. The Mass keeps the same pattern: the Church, a company of sinners, is called to the table of the Lord, and before Holy Communion confesses, “Lord, I am not worthy”.

Questions people ask

Does “I desire mercy, not sacrifice” mean that worship does not matter?

No. Hosea was criticizing worship that was offered without love of neighbour and fidelity to God. Jesus, who worshipped in the Temple and kept the feasts, quotes the verse to show that worship and mercy belong together. Sacrifice without mercy is empty; mercy is what God has always wanted at the heart of every offering.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham called himself a stranger and still kept his hope. When have I felt a stranger, and what held my hope?
  • Jesus saw Matthew, and Matthew got up. What would it mean for me to rise from my own “tax booth” today?
