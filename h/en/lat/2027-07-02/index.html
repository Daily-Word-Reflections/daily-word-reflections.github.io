Readings

  • First Reading Genesis 23:1–4, 19; 24:1–8, 62–67 · Isaac loved Rebekah, and was comforted after his mother's death

  • Gospel Matthew 9:9–13 · I desire mercy, not sacrifice

Word for the day

““Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’ For I have come to call not the righteous but sinners.”” Matthew 9:13

Reflection

The first reading moves in two steps: from a grave to a wedding. “Sarah lived one hundred twenty-seven years.” She dies at Hebron, and Abraham, the man of faith, does what every human being does: he goes in “to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her.” Holy Scripture does not hurry grief. Then he rises and does something practical and dignified: he asks the Hittites for a place to bury his dead. “I am a stranger and an alien residing among you; give me property among you for a burying place.” After more than sixty years in the land God had promised him, the only piece of it Abraham owns is a cave and a field at Machpelah, bought for “four hundred shekels of silver” (Genesis 23:16). It is a lesson in the pilgrim life. The Letter to the Hebrews comments: all these died in faith, and “they confessed that they were strangers and foreigners on the earth,” seeking “a better country, that is, a heavenly one.” Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions all honour that tomb of the patriarchs. The Church too teaches us to respect the bodies of the dead and to pray at their graves (Catechism 2300). The second part of the reading, from chapter 24, is a very different scene. Abraham is old; he sends his oldest servant to find a wife for Isaac among his own kin, and trusts that God “will send his angel before you.” After the long story of the well and Rebekah's kindness, which the Lectionary leaves out, we see the meeting: “Isaac went out in the evening to walk in the field; and looking up, he saw camels coming.” Rebekah looks up too, sees him, and veils herself. Then comes a sentence of great tenderness: “Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent. He took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother's death.” The covenant goes on through the love of husband and wife. The Church sees in the home built on such love a small image of the love of Christ for his people (Catechism 1602–1605).

The Gospel is the call of a man whom everybody despised. Matthew was a tax collector, one who collected tolls for the Roman authorities and for his own pocket, a collaborator in the eyes of his people. “As Jesus was walking along, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth; and he said to him, ‘Follow me.’ And he got up and followed him.” Mark and Luke tell the same story about a tax collector named Levi, and the Church has long understood that this is the apostle Matthew, the author of the Gospel that we read this week. Two words in the story are worth pausing on: he saw, and he said. Jesus looked at the man behind the counter and saw a disciple. Then comes the dinner, and with it the scandal. Jesus sits at table with “many tax collectors and sinners.” In the East to eat with someone was to accept him as a friend. The Pharisees ask the disciples, “Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?” Jesus answers with a proverb and a quotation. The proverb: “Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick.” The quotation is from the prophet Hosea: “Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’” Go and learn: the teachers of the Law are sent back to school! God does not want religion without love. And then the mission statement of Jesus: “I have come to call not the righteous but sinners.” On First Fridays the Church thinks especially of the Heart that has this mercy. Since the revelations to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial in France in 1673–75, Catholics have honoured the Sacred Heart pierced by the lance, from which came “blood and water,” and many keep the nine First Fridays with Mass and Communion. The prayer of St Paul fits the day: that we may “know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge.”

Homily outline

Theme: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.”

A grave and a wedding, a pilgrim people comforted by love, and the Heart of Jesus that calls the sinner to his table.

1. Strangers and aliens

  • Sarah dies; Abraham weeps.
  • One cave in the land of the promise.
  • A better country, a heavenly one.
““I am a stranger and an alien residing among you; give me property among you for a burying place, so that I may bury my dead out of my sight.”” Genesis 23:4
“All of these died in faith without having received the promises, but from a distance they saw and greeted them. They confessed that they were strangers and foreigners on the earth,” Hebrews 11:13

Apply: Pray this week for the dead of your family by name, at the grave or at home.

2. Comforted by love

  • Isaac walks in the field in the evening.
  • He looked up, and she looked up.
  • Love is how God consoles.
“Then Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent. He took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother’s death.” Genesis 24:67
“So they are no longer two, but one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate.” Matthew 19:6

Apply: Say aloud to someone in your family this week, “You are a comfort to me.”

3. Follow me

  • He saw a man at the tax booth.
  • He ate with sinners.
  • Go and learn: mercy, not sacrifice.
“For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.” Hosea 6:6
“As Jesus was walking along, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth; and he said to him, “Follow me.” And he got up and followed him.” Matthew 9:9

Apply: Spend one hour before the Blessed Sacrament this First Friday, and ask the Heart of Jesus to give you a heart like his.

Abraham buries Sarah as a stranger in the land of the promise, and Isaac is comforted by Rebekah's love: the covenant continues through a home built on love.

Jesus calls Matthew and eats with sinners, because he came not for the righteous but for sinners; the Sacred Heart is the heart of that mercy.

Take-home resolution: Keep this First Friday with Mass, confession or adoration, and share a meal with someone who is alone.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's first reading begins with a funeral. Sarah has died, and Abraham goes in to weep for her. The Bible does not tell us to hide our tears. Abraham mourns, and then he does the practical thing. He asks for a place to bury his wife. And we learn something that is almost strange: after all those years with God's promise of the whole land, Abraham owns one cave. I am a stranger and an alien residing among you (Genesis 23:4).

That is the life of faith. We are pilgrims. The Letter to the Hebrews says that the patriarchs confessed that they were strangers and foreigners on the earth, because they desired a better country, that is, a heavenly one (Hebrews 11:13–16). We have homes, jobs and plans, and we should look after them well. But our true home is where Christ is, and our dead are not lost: they have gone ahead of us.

And God comforts his pilgrims through each other. Isaac goes out in the evening to walk in the field. He is grieving for his mother. And he looks up and sees camels coming. Rebekah looks up too. Then the Bible says something simple and beautiful: he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother's death (Genesis 24:67). Husbands and wives, parents and children, friends: love is the way God consoles. Let us not forget to say to those near us that they are a comfort.

In the Gospel Jesus walks by a tax booth and sees a man. Not a tax collector, not a thief, but a man, and he says: Follow me. Matthew gets up. Then at the dinner Jesus eats with sinners, and when he is criticised he answers: Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick (Matthew 9:12). Go and learn what this means: I desire mercy, not sacrifice. On this First Friday we look at the Heart that was pierced for sinners. If you feel you are too bad to come, this Gospel was written for you. And if you think you are too good to need him, it was written for you too. Let us all come to the physician, and let us make room at our own table for those whom he has called.

For children

Children, a man named Matthew collected taxes, and nobody liked him. One day Jesus walked by and said: follow me. Matthew stood up and went with Jesus! Then he gave a big dinner, and Jesus sat down with all his friends, even those whom others did not like. Some grown-ups were cross. But Jesus said: I came for those who need help, like a doctor for sick people. Jesus loves everybody, and today is First Friday, the day we thank him for his Sacred Heart. Let us be kind to a child who sits alone!

Prayer

God of Abraham and Sarah, you comfort those who mourn and you make the love of husband and wife a sign of your faithfulness. Teach us to live as pilgrims, strangers and aliens on earth, looking for the better country that you have prepared. Lord Jesus, who saw a tax collector at his table and said, Follow me, call us from whatever keeps us sitting at our counters. Sacred Heart of Jesus, meek and humble, gentle and merciful, make our hearts like yours, and teach us to desire mercy more than sacrifice. Amen.
