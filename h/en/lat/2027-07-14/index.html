Word for the day

“Then he said, “Come no closer! Remove the sandals from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground.” He said further, “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.”” Exodus 3:5–6

Reflection

Forty years have gone by since Moses fled from Egypt. He is now a shepherd in Midian, looking after the sheep of his father-in-law Jethro, and he has led the flock “beyond the wilderness” to Horeb, “the mountain of God.” He is an old man doing the humblest work; the prince of Egypt has become a herdsman, and it is to the herdsman that God appears. “The angel of the Lord appeared to him in a flame of fire out of a bush; he looked, and the bush was blazing, yet it was not consumed.” Notice what Moses does: “I must turn aside and look at this great sight.” The whole story turns on that small decision. “When the Lord saw that he had turned aside to see, God called to him out of the bush, ‘Moses, Moses!’ And he said, ‘Here I am.’” God speaks only to someone who stops. Then come two commands that shape all worship: “Come no closer! Remove the sandals from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground.” Ground becomes holy because God is present in it. And God names himself, not first by power but by relationship: “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.” Moses hides his face. The Lectionary skips two verses, and they are the heart of it: “I have observed the misery of my people who are in Egypt; I have heard their cry on account of their taskmasters. Indeed, I know their sufferings” (Exodus 3:7). The holy God is the God who sees suffering, hears the cry, and knows it from the inside. And so he sends Moses. Moses objects: “Who am I?” and God answers, not with a list of Moses' strengths, but with himself: “I will be with you.” The psalm sings it: “The Lord works vindication and justice for all who are oppressed. He made known his ways to Moses.”

In the Gospel Jesus bursts into thanksgiving: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants.” The passage comes right after he has grieved over the towns that saw most and turned least, and the contrast is deliberate: those who thought they knew did not see, and the little ones did. “Infants” (Greek nepioi) are the unlearned, the humble, those with nothing to defend. The Bible has many of them: a shepherd at a bush, a boy David, fishermen from Galilee. St Paul would write that God chose “what is low and despised in the world” (1 Corinthians 1:28). Then Jesus lifts the veil on his own mystery: “All things have been handed over to me by my Father; and no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him.” This is the most intimate sentence in the first three Gospels; it sounds like the Gospel of John. Jesus is not only a messenger of God: he knows the Father as only a Son can, and he opens that knowledge to those he chooses, and he chooses the humble. The burning bush and this prayer fit together. At the bush God revealed his name and his compassion to a shepherd; in Jesus the Father reveals his whole heart to those who come like children. And the holy ground is no longer only a mountain in Sinai: it is wherever the Son is, and wherever the least of his brothers and sisters are found.

Full homily

Dear friends, imagine Moses at the end of a long ordinary day. He is eighty years old, with the sheep, in the desert. Nothing special is happening. And then he sees a bush that burns and does not burn up. He could have walked on. A lot of us walk on. But Moses says: I must turn aside and look at this great sight (Exodus 3:3). And when the Lord saw that he had turned aside to see, God called to him: Moses, Moses! (Exodus 3:4). God does not shout; he waits for someone who stops.

Then God says: Remove the sandals from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground (Exodus 3:5). It is just a patch of desert, and it is holy because God is there. When we enter a church we feel it. But God's holy ground is also the sickroom, the kitchen where someone is crying, the hospital bed. And hear what God says in the verses we did not read: I have observed the misery of my people; I have heard their cry; I know their sufferings (Exodus 3:7). Our God is no distant ruler. He knows suffering from the inside, and in his Son he entered it.

That is why St Camillus saw what he saw. A soldier with a wound that would not heal, he lay in a hospital and saw how the poor sick were treated, and he said: this is holy ground. He gave his life to nursing the sick, because Jesus himself said: I was sick and you took care of me (Matthew 25:36). Moses asked: who am I? God answered: I will be with you (Exodus 3:12). That is the only answer any of us needs when we are afraid to help.

And Jesus thanks the Father today that these things are revealed not to the wise and learned but to infants (Matthew 11:25). You do not need a degree to meet God. You need the heart of a child, which stops, looks and says: Here I am. Let us turn aside today and ask for that heart. Amen.

For children

Children, Moses was looking after the sheep one day when he saw a bush on fire, but the bush was not burning up! He went to have a look. And God called: Moses! Moses! God said: take off your sandals, this is holy ground. Then God told him: I have seen my people hurting, and I will send you to help them. Moses was afraid, but God said: I will be with you. A man called Camillus knew that God is with sick people. He looked after them with all his heart. When we are kind to someone who is sick, we are standing on holy ground too.

Prayer

Lord God, you appeared to Moses in the burning bush and said: I have heard the cry of my people and I know their sufferings. Make us people who turn aside to look, who take off their sandals before holy ground, and who see in every sickroom a place where your Son is present. Father, Lord of heaven and earth, we thank you that you reveal yourself to the little ones. Through the intercession of St Camillus, give strength and tenderness to all who nurse the sick, healing to those who suffer, and to each of us the knowledge that you are with us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the first reading God calls Moses out of a burning bush and sends him to deliver his people: God has seen their misery and will be with Moses. In the Gospel Jesus thanks his Father for hiding these things from the wise and revealing them to infants, and tells of the knowledge that only the Father and the Son share. Both readings show a God who comes down to the lowly. He reveals himself to a shepherd in a wilderness and to the little ones of Galilee. Today the Church also remembers St Camillus de Lellis (died 1614), founder of the Ministers of the Sick; the weekday readings are kept.

Exodus 3:1–6, 9–12 · The bush that was not consumed

Setting.

Moses, rescued as a baby and raised in Pharaoh’s house, fled to Midian after killing the Egyptian (2:11–15). There he married Zipporah, the daughter of the priest of Midian, and became a shepherd for his father-in-law. The call at the bush comes in the middle of ordinary work. The Lectionary leaves out vv. 7–8, in which God says he has seen the misery of his people and has come down to deliver them; v. 9 repeats the same message.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: Moses leads the flock to Horeb and sees a bush burning that is not consumed.
  • vv. 4–6: God calls him by name, tells him to take off his sandals, and says who he is.
  • vv. 9–10: God has heard Israel’s cry and sends Moses to Pharaoh.
  • vv. 11–12: Moses objects, “Who am I?”, and God answers, “I will be with you.”

Key words.

  • seneh, bush: A thorn bush of the desert. Many scholars see a play on the name Sinai, the mountain where Israel will later worship (v. 12).
  • qōdeš, holy: What belongs to God and is set apart from ordinary use. The ground is holy not because of the place but because God is present there.
  • ʾehyeh ʿimmāk, I will be with you: The verb is the same as in the divine name of v. 14. God does not answer “Who am I?” with a list of Moses’ gifts. He answers with his own presence.

Verse by verse.

“the bush was blazing, yet it was not consumed.” (3:2)

Fire in the Bible is a sign of God’s presence (Genesis 15:17; Exodus 19:18). A fire that burns without destroying shows a God who is near, yet does not consume those who come before him.

“God called to him out of the bush, “Moses, Moses!” And he said, “Here I am.”” (3:4)

God calls by name, and the answer is the answer of every prophet: hinnēnî, “Here I am”. It is the readiness of Abraham (Genesis 22:1) and of Samuel (1 Samuel 3:4).

“Remove the sandals from your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground.” (3:5)

The one who comes near God does so with reverence. Joshua will be told the same near Jericho (Joshua 5:15). Holiness is not frightening for those who come as invited.

“The cry of the Israelites has now come to me;” (3:9)

God is not far from suffering. He hears the cry of the enslaved, and he chooses a human being to carry out his rescue.

“Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh” (3:11)

The humility of Moses is real. God does not argue it away. He gives the one promise that is enough: “I will be with you” (v. 12).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this scene a revelation of God’s holiness and nearness. The Catechism teaches that before the burning bush Moses learns that God is the Holy One, and it speaks of the prayer of Moses that begins here (CCC 205; 2575). Jesus himself uses the words of v. 6 when he teaches the resurrection: God is not the God of the dead but of the living (Mark 12:26–27). Early Christian writers, including St Gregory of Nyssa (in substance), saw in the bush that burned without being consumed a figure of the Virgin Mary, who carried the divine fire of the Word in her womb and remained a virgin; this reading is still honored in the liturgical hymns of the East and the West.

Matthew 11:25–27 · Revealed to infants

Setting.

The prayer of Jesus follows his warning to the towns that did not repent (11:20–24). At such a hard moment he does not complain; he gives thanks. He prays in the same chapter where John the Baptist’s doubts and Galilee’s hardness of heart have been described. The prayer is short, but scholars often call it the “Johannine thunderbolt” in the Synoptic Gospels, because it speaks of the Father and the Son with the language we know best from the Gospel of John.

Structure.

  • v. 25: Jesus thanks the Father who hides these things from the wise and reveals them to infants.
  • v. 26: “Yes, Father, for such was your gracious will.”
  • v. 27: all things have been handed over to the Son; the Father and the Son know each other, and the Son reveals the Father to whom he chooses.

Key words.

  • exomologoumai, I thank, I praise: The word means to acknowledge openly. It is the praise of the psalms, a confession of what God has done.
  • nēpioi, infants: Babies, the little ones who cannot speak. In Matthew these are the humble disciples, the ones who need God and know it. The “wise and intelligent” are those who trust their own wisdom.
  • eudokia, gracious will: God’s good pleasure. The mysteries of the kingdom are given not because of human cleverness but because the Father chooses to give them.

Verse by verse.

“you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants;” (11:25)

Jesus does not despise learning; the learned are warned only when learning becomes pride. The Gospel is given to anyone with a humble and open heart.

“for such was your gracious will.” (11:26)

Jesus accepts the Father’s way. Revelation is the Father’s gift, and he gives it as he chooses.

“no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son” (11:27)

The Son and the Father know each other completely. This is the heart of Jesus’ identity: he is not one prophet among others but the Son who alone can show us the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 27 to teach that Jesus revealed God as Father in an unheard-of sense, eternally Father in relation to his only Son (CCC 240). It also points to this prayer as the model of Jesus’ own thanksgiving to the Father (CCC 2603). The Church’s great teachers have often pointed to the “little ones” of this passage. St Thérèse of Lisieux, a Doctor of the Church, built her “little way” on the same trust: that God gives himself to the small and humble.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus say that education and learning are bad?

No. Jesus does not blame the wise and the intelligent for being learned; he warns against a pride that thinks it needs no one. The Church has always honored learning: many of its saints, such as Augustine, Thomas Aquinas and Edith Stein, were great scholars who approached God as little children.

For study and teaching

  • God called Moses in the middle of his ordinary work. Where might God be calling me in the middle of mine?
  • Jesus rejoices that God reveals himself to the little ones. What would it mean for me to be “small” before God this week?
