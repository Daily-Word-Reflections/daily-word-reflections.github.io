Word for the day

“Elijah went up to the top of Carmel … At the seventh time he said, “Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea.”” 1 Kings 18:42–44

Reflection

Today's first reading is the last night in Egypt, and it is the most important night in the Old Testament. Nine plagues have fallen; “the Lord hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he did not let the people of Israel go out of his land.” Now God gives Moses and Aaron the instructions for the night of liberation. First, a new calendar: “This month shall mark for you the beginning of months.” The liberation of the people becomes the beginning of the year. Then, a meal in each home: “a lamb for each family,” “without blemish, a year-old male,” kept until the fourteenth day and then slaughtered at twilight by the whole assembly. Some of its blood is put “on the two doorposts and the lintel” of the house. It is eaten “with unleavened bread and bitter herbs,” in haste: “your loins girded, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand.” “It is the passover of the Lord.” The word means passing over: “when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague shall destroy you.” And then the command that turns an event into a tradition: “This day shall be a day of remembrance for you. You shall celebrate it as a festival to the Lord; throughout your generations you shall observe it as a perpetual ordinance.” Jews celebrate this meal today, in every generation, in the Seder; and the Church, from the beginning, has seen in the unblemished lamb a picture of Christ: John the Baptist calls him “the Lamb of God,” and Paul writes, “our paschal lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7). The psalm is the answer of someone who has been saved: “What shall I return to the Lord for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the Lord.” We say these words at the Mass, because they are the grateful prayer of the Passover and the Eucharist.

In the Gospel the disciples walk through the grainfields on the sabbath and, being hungry, pluck some heads of grain. The Pharisees object: “Look, your disciples are doing what is not lawful to do on the sabbath.” Plucking grain was counted as reaping, one of the kinds of work forbidden on the day of rest. Jesus does not abolish the sabbath; he explains it, with three arguments from Scripture. First, David and his men, when hungry, ate the bread of the Presence, which only the priests might eat. Need and mercy come before the letter. Second, the priests in the temple work on the sabbath, offering the sacrifices, and are guiltless: the service of God takes precedence. Then the astonishing sentence: “I tell you, something greater than the temple is here.” He means himself. And then he quotes the prophet Hosea: “I desire mercy and not sacrifice.” Mercy is the key that opens the law. The passage ends with a claim of authority: “the Son of Man is lord of the sabbath.” The Passover lamb and this Gospel meet in the same truth: the God who passes over his people in mercy has come among them in his Son, who is greater than the temple, greater than the sabbath, and who will be the Lamb.

Today the Church also remembers Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the day has its own beauty. Carmel is a mountain ridge by the sea in the Holy Land, near present-day Haifa, and its name is bound up with Elijah. On Carmel he called down fire on the altar and challenged the prophets of Baal; and at the end of the drought he “went up to the top of Carmel; there he bowed himself down upon the earth and put his face between his knees,” and sent his servant to look toward the sea. Six times there was nothing. “At the seventh time he said, ‘Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea.’” In a little while the heavens grew black with rain. Christian tradition, and the Carmelites in particular, have seen in that little cloud a sign of Mary, a small and humble figure out of whom came the rain of grace, her Son. In the twelfth century hermits settled on Mount Carmel and built a chapel in honour of Our Lady, and they became the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel, who gave the Church Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross and Thérèse of Lisieux, and whose spirituality also shaped, in Kerala, the religious family of St Kuriakose Elias Chavara. The proper readings for today are the Magnificat, in which Mary sings “the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name”; the prophet Zechariah's words, “Sing and rejoice, O daughter Zion! For lo, I will come and dwell in your midst”; and the Gospel in which Jesus says that his true family are those who do the will of his Father. Mary is the first and best of them: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). The brown scapular, a small piece of cloth worn on the shoulders, is a sign of belonging to Mary and of the wish to follow her way. A tradition says that Our Lady gave it to the English Carmelite St Simon Stock in 1251, though historians are cautious about the details. The Church teaches that the scapular is a sacramental: a sign that helps us to pray, to trust Mary's care and to imitate her, not a charm. Salvation is always Christ's gift. In July, when Kerala watches the sky for the monsoon, a cloud bringing rain is a good picture of Mary: small, humble, and carrying the water of life.

Homily outline

Theme: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord.”

The blood on the doorposts and the Lamb, mercy greater than sacrifice, and the little cloud over Carmel that is Mary.

1. When I see the blood

  • The first month, the first night.
  • A lamb without blemish.
  • A day of remembrance for ever.
“The blood shall be a sign for you on the houses where you live: when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague shall destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.” Exodus 12:13
“Clean out the old yeast so that you may be a new batch, as you really are unleavened. For our paschal lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed.” 1 Corinthians 5:7

Apply: At the next Mass, listen to the words “Behold the Lamb of God” and receive Holy Communion as a Passover.

2. Mercy and not sacrifice

  • Hungry disciples in the grain.
  • David and the bread of the Presence.
  • Something greater than the temple.
“For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.” Hosea 6:6
“I tell you, something greater than the temple is here. But if you had known what this means, ‘I desire mercy and not sacrifice,’ you would not have condemned the guiltless. For the Son of Man is lord of the sabbath.” Matthew 12:6–8

Apply: This week, let mercy win over a rule once: feed someone, forgive someone, or help someone in need.

3. A little cloud

  • Elijah on top of Carmel.
  • Six times nothing, the seventh time a cloud.
  • Mary, small and humble, who brought the rain of grace.
“At the seventh time he said, “Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea.”” 1 Kings 18:44
“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”” Luke 1:38

Apply: Pray daily this week, and do not stop at the first “nothing”: go back seven times if you need to.

4. Do whatever he tells you

  • The first disciple.
  • The scapular as a sign of belonging.
  • Hearing the word and keeping it.
“Sing and rejoice, O daughter Zion! For lo, I will come and dwell in your midst, says the Lord.” Zechariah 2:10
“But he said, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!”” Luke 11:28

Apply: Say a Hail Mary each morning this week, asking: Mother, show me what he wants me to do today.

God passes over his people at the Passover; in Christ, the Lamb, he has saved us, and he desires mercy more than sacrifice.

As a little cloud brought rain to Carmel, Mary brought Jesus to the world, and she teaches us to hear the word and keep it.

Take-home resolution: Each day this week say a Hail Mary to Our Lady of Mount Carmel for a need that is still unanswered, and do one act of mercy.

Full homily

Dear friends, imagine the night of the Passover. In every Hebrew home a family is eating in haste, shoes on, staff in hand. On the doorpost is the blood of a lamb without blemish. And God says: when I see the blood, I will pass over you (Exodus 12:13). Every year, for three thousand years, the Jewish people have kept that night. And we, in the Mass, keep our Passover: Christ is the Lamb without blemish, his blood saves us, and every time we receive him we pass from slavery to life. Let us remember what Paul says: our paschal lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed (1 Corinthians 5:7).

In the Gospel the disciples are hungry and pluck some grain, and the Pharisees say: it is not lawful! And Jesus answers with a sentence that should wake us up: I desire mercy and not sacrifice (Matthew 12:7). Rules matter; worship matters. But if a rule makes us forget a hungry person, we have lost its point. Something greater than the temple is here, he says (Matthew 12:6). It is he himself, the Lord of the sabbath. Mercy is the key to the whole law.

Today we also remember Our Lady of Mount Carmel. On Mount Carmel the prophet Elijah prayed for rain, and he said to his servant: look toward the sea. Six times: there is nothing. The seventh time: a little cloud no bigger than a person's hand (1 Kings 18:44). Dear friends, sometimes our prayer feels like that. We look and see nothing. Elijah teaches us to go back, again and again. And the tradition of the Church saw in that little cloud Mary: a humble young woman from Nazareth, no bigger than a hand in the eyes of the world, and out of her came Jesus, the rain of grace for the whole world.

The Carmelites took Mary as their mother and their model. They wear the scapular, a small cloth, as a sign that they belong to her and want to live as she lived. And what was her life? Here am I, the servant of the Lord (Luke 1:38). And at Cana: do whatever he tells you (John 2:5). Today let us ask Our Lady of Carmel to teach us that, to pray like Elijah, to be merciful like Jesus and to say yes like her. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago on a mountain called Mount Carmel there was a prophet named Elijah. There was no rain, and everything was dry. Elijah prayed. He told his helper: look at the sea! The helper looked and said: nothing! Elijah said: look again! Seven times! The seventh time the helper saw a little cloud, as small as a hand. And then came the rain! We remember Mary today as Our Lady of Carmel. She is like the little cloud: small and humble, and she brought us Jesus. Some people wear a little brown scapular to remember that Mary is our mother. Let us say a Hail Mary and ask her to help us to wait and pray like Elijah, and not give up.

Prayer

Lord God, on the night of the Passover you saw the blood on the doorposts and passed over your people. Through Christ, our Passover lamb, you have saved us and given us the cup of salvation. Teach us mercy rather than the letter. Lord Jesus, you who are greater than the temple, and lord of the sabbath, receive our thanks. Father, as Elijah looked to the sea and saw a little cloud, so we look to Our Lady of Mount Carmel and see in her the one who bore your Son and who did your will. Through her prayers, make us faithful in waiting, humble in service and generous in mercy. Amen.
