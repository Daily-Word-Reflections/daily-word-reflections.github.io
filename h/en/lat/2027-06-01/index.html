Word for the day

“Jesus said to them, “Give to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's.” And they were utterly amazed at him.” Mark 12:17

Reflection

For the first days of June the Church reads the Book of Tobit, a family story told among the Jews scattered in exile. Tobit is a good man living in Nineveh, far from Jerusalem. He gives alms, shares his bread with the hungry and, at the risk of his life, buries the bodies of fellow Israelites left in the street. Today's passage begins after one such burial. Tobit sleeps outside by the courtyard wall, and a stupid accident changes his life: “I did not know that there were sparrows on the wall; their fresh droppings fell into my eyes and produced white films.” The physicians only make it worse, “until I became completely blind.” A rich man becomes a dependent. His wife Anna goes out to work: “my wife Anna earned money at women's work.” One day her employers pay her wages and add a young goat as a gift. Tobit hears it bleat and suspects the worst: “Where did you get this goat? It is surely not stolen, is it? Return it to the owners.” He will not believe her. And Anna, hurt, answers with words that cut deep: “Where are your acts of charity? Where are your righteous deeds? These things are known about you!” It is a painfully honest scene. A good man, humiliated by blindness and dependence, becomes suspicious and hard at home. His goodness in the street has not yet reached his own courtyard. The book does not hide this; it lets us see a holy man in his weakness, so that we may see how God will heal him. The Psalm describes the just man as Tobit wanted to be and will become again: “Happy are those who fear the Lord, who greatly delight in his commandments … They are not afraid of evil tidings; their hearts are firm, secure in the Lord.”

In the Gospel the enemies of Jesus come with a trap wrapped in flattery. Pharisees and Herodians, who normally disliked each other, unite: “Teacher, we know that you are sincere, and show deference to no one … Is it lawful to pay taxes to the emperor, or not?” If Jesus says yes, he betrays the people who hate the Roman tax; if he says no, he is a rebel against Rome. Jesus asks for a coin. “Whose head is this, and whose title?” “The emperor's.” Then comes the answer that has echoed through history: “Give to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's.” The coin bears the image of Caesar, so it may go back to Caesar. But the human person bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27), so the whole person belongs to God. Caesar may have taxes; he may not have our worship. St Justin, whom we remember today, lived this answer to the end. Born of pagan parents at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria, he searched for truth among the philosophers, until an old man showed him the prophets and Christ. He wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius that Christians pay their taxes more readily than anyone, because Christ taught them to give to Caesar what is Caesar's, but that they worship God alone (First Apology 17, in substance). When the prefect Rusticus ordered him to sacrifice to the gods, he refused, and was beheaded with six companions. He gave Caesar his obedience in all things lawful, and gave God his life.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we meet Tobit, a man everyone praised. He gave alms, he fed the hungry, he buried the dead at the risk of his life. And then one night, sleeping by a wall, droppings from some sparrows fell into his eyes. The doctors made it worse, and he became completely blind (Tobit 2:10). In one moment the generous man became the man who must be helped.

Suffering can make us holy, but it can also make us hard. Tobit's wife Anna goes out to work to keep the family. One day she comes home with a young goat, a gift from her employers. Tobit, sitting in his darkness, does not believe her: it is surely stolen, return it. He is ashamed of being poor and dependent, so he suspects the one who is carrying him. And Anna answers: where are your acts of charity? Where are your righteous deeds? (Tobit 2:14). The man who was kind to strangers had become unkind at home. Many of us know that temptation.

In the Gospel Jesus is trapped by a question about taxes. He asks for a coin and says: whose head is this? The emperor's. Then give to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's (Mark 12:17). The coin has Caesar's face, so let it go back to him. But you, my friend, have God's image stamped on you. Caesar may have your coin. God wants you, all of you.

St Justin understood this. He was a philosopher, a seeker of truth, who found Christ. He told the emperor: we Christians pay our taxes more willingly than anyone, because Christ taught us; but we worship God alone. When he was ordered to sacrifice to idols, he refused, and gave his life. Today let us give God what bears his image: our heart, our thoughts, our home, our kindness to those closest to us. And when suffering comes, let us pray with the Psalm that our hearts be firm, secure in the Lord. Amen.

For children

Children, take a coin and look at it. Whose picture is on it? Jesus once held a coin like that and said: give the coin to the king whose picture is on it. Then he said: give to God what belongs to God. And what belongs to God? You do! God made you to look a little like him, inside your heart. So give God your heart, your prayers, your kindness. You are God's treasure!

Prayer

O God, who through the folly of the Cross wondrously taught Saint Justin the Martyr the surpassing knowledge of Jesus Christ, grant us, through his intercession, that, having rejected deception, we may become steadfast in the faith. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Lord, we bear your image; take our whole selves, our minds, our work and our homes. Heal what is blind and hard in us, as you healed Tobit, and make our hearts firm and secure in you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Tobit, the man who risked everything to bury the dead, is struck blind, and in his bitterness he suspects his own wife of theft. Anna answers him with a question that cuts deep: “Where are your acts of charity?” In the Gospel the Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Jesus with a question about paying taxes to the emperor. He answers by pointing to the image on the coin, and reminds them that something else bears the image of God. On the memorial of St Justin, a philosopher who gave his life for Christ in Rome, Psalm 112 sings of the righteous whose hearts are firm, secure in the Lord.

Tobit 2:9–14 · Where are your acts of charity?

Setting.

The Book of Tobit is set among the exiles of the northern tribes in Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. Tobit, of the tribe of Naphtali, tells his own story: he gave alms, fed the hungry, and buried the bodies of his fellow Israelites when the king left them unburied. Just before our passage, at the feast of Pentecost, he left his dinner to bury a murdered man (2:1–8). Having touched a corpse, he was ritually unclean and slept outside in the courtyard.

The book was written in Aramaic or Hebrew, probably two or three centuries before Christ; fragments in both languages were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran. The Catholic Church receives it among the books of the Old Testament (CCC 120). The passage shows a good man in the dark: blind, poor, dependent on his wife, and quick to suspect.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: Tobit sleeps in the courtyard; bird droppings blind him; physicians make it worse; four years of blindness.
  • vv. 11–12: Anna works at weaving and is given a young goat with her wages.
  • vv. 13–14: Tobit accuses her; Anna answers, “Where are your acts of charity?”

Key words.

  • eleēmosynai, acts of charity, alms: A key word of the whole book (compare 4:7–11; 12:8–9). It comes from eleos, mercy. Anna turns Tobit’s favourite virtue back on him: where is his mercy now, toward her?
  • dikaiosynai, righteous deeds: Righteousness here means deeds that are right before God and neighbour. Tobit had them in abundance (1:3), yet he fails in the one place where it is hardest: at home.

Verse by verse.

“For four years I remained unable to see.” (2:10)

Tobit does not explain his suffering. The book never says that God sent the blindness as a punishment. It is the trial of a just man, as with Job.

“It is surely not stolen, is it? Return it to the owners; for we have no right to eat anything stolen.” (2:13)

His honesty is real, but it has turned hard. Suffering has made him suspicious of the one person who is caring for him.

“Where are your acts of charity? Where are your righteous deeds? These things are known about you!” (2:14)

Anna’s words sting because they are partly true. Charity to strangers must also become patience and trust toward those closest to us.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Tobit as a story of providence: God is at work even when his servants cannot see. Tobit is a great teacher of almsgiving (4:7–11; 12:8–9), and the Catechism names almsgiving among the three chief forms of penance (CCC 1434). Tobit’s burying of the dead is remembered among the corporal works of mercy, and the Catechism notes that burying the dead is a work of mercy that honours the children of God (CCC 2300). Today’s passage adds a quieter lesson: mercy begins at home. Pope Francis, commenting on St Paul’s hymn to love in Amoris Laetitia, speaks of the patience and trust that love needs within the family (in substance).

Mark 12:13–17 · The image on the coin

Setting.

Jesus is in the Temple at Jerusalem in the last days before his Passion. The chief priests, scribes and elders have just heard the parable of the wicked tenants and understood that it was told against them (12:12). Now they send some Pharisees and Herodians. The two groups were usually far apart: the Pharisees resented Roman rule, while the Herodians supported the house of Herod, which ruled with Rome’s consent.

Background. The tax in question was the poll tax paid directly to Rome by the people of Judea. It was bitterly resented, and some years before, a rising under Judas the Galilean had opposed it. If Jesus said “pay”, he would offend the people; if he said “do not pay”, he could be denounced to the Romans.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–15a: flattering words and a trap: is it lawful to pay taxes to the emperor?
  • vv. 15b–16: Jesus asks for a denarius: whose head and whose title?
  • v. 17: “Give to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” They are amazed.

Key words.

  • dēnarion, denarius: A Roman silver coin, a day’s wage for a labourer. The coin of the emperor Tiberius bore his head and a title calling him son of the divine Augustus. Jesus does not carry one; he asks for it.
  • eikōn, image, head: The word used for the image of God in the Greek of Genesis 1:26–27. The coin bears Caesar’s image; the human person bears God’s.
  • apodote, give back, render: Not simply “give” but “give back what is owed”. The coin already belongs, in a sense, to the one whose image it carries.

Verse by verse.

“Teacher, we know that you are sincere, and show deference to no one;” (12:14)

The flattery is true, though it is meant as a trap. Jesus is indeed not swayed by anyone’s rank.

“Bring me a denarius and let me see it.” (12:15)

His questioners produce the coin themselves, in the Temple courts. They already use Caesar’s money.

“Give to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” (12:17)

The State has a real but limited claim. God’s claim is total, because we carry his image. Tertullian drew the lesson: give to Caesar the image of Caesar on the coin, and to God the image of God in yourself (in substance, On Idolatry 15).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that it is a moral duty to pay taxes, to vote and to defend one’s country (CCC 2240), and that citizens are obliged in conscience not to follow the directives of civil authorities when they are contrary to the demands of the moral order; here the Catechism quotes this saying of Jesus (CCC 2242). St Justin, whose memorial we keep, wrote to the emperor that Christians pay their taxes more readily than anyone, because Christ taught them to, but worship God alone (in substance, First Apology 17). The coin goes to Caesar; the heart and the whole person go to God.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus divide life into two separate parts, one for God and one for the State?

No. Everything belongs to God, including the emperor. Jesus accepts that the State has a real role and real claims, such as taxes and just laws. But it may never claim what belongs only to God: worship, conscience and the whole person made in his image. When a law commands what is against God’s law, the Christian must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29; CCC 2242).

For the Latin reader

St Justin was born about the year 100 at Flavia Neapolis (present-day Nablus) in Samaria. He searched for truth among the philosophers and found it in Christ, whom he called the Word, a seed of whose truth was scattered among all peoples. He taught in Rome and wrote two Apologies and the Dialogue with Trypho. His First Apology contains the oldest full description of the Sunday Eucharist (chapters 65–67), quoted in the Catechism (CCC 1345). He was beheaded in Rome about 165 with several companions, under the prefect Rusticus.

For study and teaching

  • Tobit was generous to strangers and harsh at home. Where am I more patient with outsiders than with my own family?
  • What in my life bears God’s image and needs to be given back to him?
