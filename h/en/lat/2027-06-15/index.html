Word for the day

“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the righteous and on the unrighteous.” Matthew 5:44–45

Reflection

For several years St Paul organised a collection among his Gentile churches for the poor Christians of Jerusalem (Romans 15:26; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4). It mattered to him greatly: it was a sign that Jews and Gentiles in Christ were one family. In today's reading he tells the Corinthians, who were fairly prosperous, about the churches of Macedonia, Philippi and Thessalonica, who were not: “during a severe ordeal of affliction, their abundant joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part.” Poverty and joy together produced generosity. “They voluntarily gave according to their means, and even beyond their means, begging us earnestly for the privilege of sharing in this ministry to the saints.” The poor begged for the privilege of giving! And Paul gives the secret: “they gave themselves first to the Lord.” When the heart is given, the purse follows. Paul does not command the Corinthians; he invites them to “excel also in this generous undertaking,” and then he points to the source of all giving: “For you know the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich.” The word Paul uses for this “generous act” is charis, grace. The Son of God emptied himself, was born in a manger and died on a cross, and by his poverty we receive the riches of God. The Psalm sings of the same God, “who executes justice for the oppressed; who gives food to the hungry.”

In the Gospel Jesus reaches the summit of the Sermon on the Mount. “You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.’” The Law of Moses commanded love of neighbour (Leviticus 19:18); nowhere did it command hatred of enemies, but many concluded that a neighbour meant only one's own people, and that enemies could be hated. Jesus breaks that boundary: “But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven.” And he gives the reason from the sky itself: the Father “makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the righteous and on the unrighteous.” In these monsoon weeks we see it with our own eyes: the rain does not ask whose field it is falling on. To love only those who love us is no more than “the tax collectors” do; to greet only our own is what everyone does. Then comes the great command: “Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect.” The Greek word, teleios, means complete, mature, whole. St Luke gives the same saying as “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (Luke 6:36). The perfection of the Father is not a cold flawlessness; it is mercy without limits. Every Christian, and not only priests and religious, is called to this holiness (Lumen Gentium 40).

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells a beautiful story about the churches of Macedonia. They were poor and suffering, yet their abundant joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity (2 Corinthians 8:2). They even begged Paul for the privilege of giving! How is that possible? Paul says: they gave themselves first to the Lord (2 Corinthians 8:5). When we belong to God, giving becomes a joy.

And Paul shows us where this generosity comes from: you know the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). Jesus left the glory of heaven, was born in a stable, and died with nothing. Everything we have of God, we have because he became poor for us.

In the Gospel Jesus asks the hardest thing of all: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (Matthew 5:44). Look at the monsoon rain. It falls on the field of the good farmer and of the lazy one, on the church and on the toddy shop. Jesus says: that is how your Father loves. He makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good. If we love only those who love us, what is special about that?

Our Church in India knows such love. Blessed Rani Maria was killed in 1995, and her family forgave her murderer and welcomed him as a brother. That is what Jesus means by: be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect (Matthew 5:48). Not a perfection of never making mistakes, but a perfection of mercy. Let us ask today for this grace: to pray, by name, for one person who has hurt us. Amen.

For children

Children, when it rains, does the rain fall only on good children's houses? No! It falls on everyone. Jesus says God loves everyone like that. And he asks us to love even people who are not nice to us. Is there someone at school who teases you? Tonight, say a little prayer for them: Jesus, bless my friend. That is a very brave and beautiful thing to do.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, though you were rich, yet for our sakes you became poor, so that by your poverty we might become rich. Give us the joy of the churches of Macedonia, who gave beyond their means because they gave themselves first to you. Father in heaven, you make your sun rise on the evil and on the good and send your rain on all; teach us to love our enemies and to pray for those who persecute us, so that we may truly be your children, merciful as you are merciful. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

St Paul holds up the poor churches of Macedonia, who gave beyond their means for the poor of Jerusalem, and points to the deepest reason for giving: Jesus Christ, “though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor.” In the Gospel Jesus asks the hardest love of all: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father, who sends sun and rain on all. Both readings ask a love that gives without counting, as God gives.

2 Corinthians 8:1–9 · Though he was rich, he became poor

Setting.

For several years Paul organised a collection among his Gentile churches for the poor Christians of Jerusalem (Romans 15:25–27; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4; Galatians 2:10). It was a sign of unity between Jewish and Gentile believers. The Corinthians had begun eagerly a year before, but had not finished. Chapters 8 and 9 are Paul’s appeal to complete it.

Macedonia was the northern province of Greece, with the churches of Philippi, Thessalonica and Beroea. They were poorer than Corinth and had suffered for the faith (1 Thessalonians 2:14).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: the example of the Macedonians: in affliction and poverty, overflowing generosity; they gave themselves first to the Lord.
  • vv. 6–8: Titus is sent to complete the work; excel in this too; not a command but a test of love.
  • v. 9: the reason: the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Key words.

  • charis, grace, generous act: Paul uses this one word many times in these chapters: for God’s grace given to the Macedonians (v. 1), for the collection itself (vv. 6–7), and for the self-giving of Christ (v. 9). Giving is grace received and grace passed on.
  • haplotēs, generosity: Literally singleness, simplicity. A generous person gives with an undivided heart, without second thoughts. The same word family appears in tomorrow’s and Friday’s readings.
  • eptōcheusen, he became poor: The verb for falling into real poverty. Paul is thinking of the whole mystery of Christ: the eternal Son who became man, lived poor and died on a cross.

Verse by verse.

“their abundant joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part.” (8:2)

Joy and poverty together produce generosity. It is often the poor who give most gladly.

“they gave themselves first to the Lord and, by the will of God, to us,” (8:5)

The gift of money came after the gift of self. Christian giving begins in prayer and self-offering.

“though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich.” (8:9)

In one sentence Paul tells the whole Gospel. Compare the hymn of Philippians 2:6–8: he emptied himself, taking the form of a slave.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse to teach that the mystery of redemption is at work in Christ’s whole life, already in his Incarnation, through which by becoming poor he enriches us with his poverty (CCC 517). St John Chrysostom, quoted in the Catechism, says that not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them (CCC 2446). The Church’s love for the poor is part of her constant tradition (CCC 2444).

Matthew 5:43–48 · Love your enemies

Setting.

This is the last of the six antitheses of the Sermon on the Mount. It gathers all the others into one command: love as the Father loves. The section ends with the call to be perfect as the heavenly Father is perfect.

Structure.

  • v. 43: “love your neighbor and hate your enemy”.
  • vv. 44–45: love your enemies and pray for persecutors, so as to be children of the Father, who gives sun and rain to all.
  • vv. 46–47: loving only those who love us is what everyone does.
  • v. 48: be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect.

Key words.

  • agapate, love: The love that wills the good of the other, even when there is no feeling of affection. It can be commanded because it is a choice and a prayer, not first an emotion.
  • echthros, enemy: Personal and public enemies alike. Jesus does not define the enemy narrowly; he leaves no one outside love.
  • teleios, perfect: Complete, whole, mature. The perfection asked is the wholeness of a love that excludes no one, like the Father’s. Luke gives the saying as “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (Luke 6:36).

Verse by verse.

“You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.” (5:43)

The first half is Leviticus 19:18. The words “hate your enemy” are not found in the law. The law itself told Israel to help an enemy’s animal (Exodus 23:4–5), and Proverbs to feed a hungry enemy (Proverbs 25:21). Jesus may be answering a narrow popular understanding of “neighbour”.

“Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,” (5:44)

Prayer is where love of enemies begins. It is hard to keep hating someone for whom we pray by name.

“for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the righteous and on the unrighteous.” (5:45)

Jesus points to creation. Every morning God’s goodness falls on all. Children show the family likeness by acting as their Father acts.

“Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect.” (5:48)

This is not a call for a few. The Second Vatican Council quotes it to teach that all the faithful are called to the fullness of the Christian life (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s teaching goes so far as to require the forgiveness of offences, and extends the commandment of love to all enemies (CCC 1933). Christian prayer extends to the forgiveness of enemies and so makes the disciple like the Master (CCC 2844). Jesus prayed from the Cross, “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34), and St Stephen prayed for those who stoned him (Acts 7:60). In many lands today Christians suffer for their faith; the Church prays for them, and for those who wrong them.

Questions people ask

Does loving my enemy mean I must trust him or pretend nothing happened?

No. Love wills the good of the other and refuses hatred and revenge. It does not require us to deny the truth or to put ourselves or others in danger. Forgiveness and trust are different: forgiveness can be given at once, by grace; trust may need time and change. The first step is the one Jesus names: pray for them.

For study and teaching

  • The Macedonians “gave themselves first to the Lord”. Before I give money or time this week, how will I first give myself to him?
  • Who is the hardest person for me to pray for? Can I pray for them by name today?
