Word for the day

“An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” Matthew 1:1

Reflection

Today the final stretch of Advent begins. The readings now follow the calendar date, and the Church sings the great “O Antiphons”. Today's: “O Wisdom, who came from the mouth of the Most High, reaching from end to end and ordering all things mightily and sweetly: come and teach us the way of prudence” (cf. Wisdom 8:1). Jacob blesses Judah: “The scepter shall not depart from Judah… until tribute comes to him; and the obedience of the peoples is his.”

Matthew opens with a genealogy: forty-two generations in three groups of fourteen, from Abraham to David to the exile to Christ. It contains saints and sinners, kings and nobodies, and, unusually, four women (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and the wife of Uriah), some foreigners, some with complicated stories. God's plan runs through real, messy family history. Then the pattern breaks: “Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born.” Jesus enters our family tree as a true man and true Son of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, from today the Church enters the last week before Christmas. At Evening Prayer she begins to sing the great O Antiphons. Tonight's is: O Wisdom, coming forth from the mouth of the Most High, reaching from end to end and ordering all things mightily and sweetly: come and teach us the way of prudence.

In the first reading the dying Jacob blesses his sons and says of Judah: the sceptre shall not depart from Judah, until tribute comes to him; and the obedience of the peoples is his (Genesis 49:10). From the tribe of Judah would come David, and from David the Messiah.

The Gospel gives us the genealogy of Jesus: Abraham was the father of Isaac, Isaac the father of Jacob, and so on through forty-two generations to Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah (Matthew 1:1–16).

We may be tempted to skip this long list of names. But it is full of meaning. It includes great saints like Abraham and David, and great sinners too. It includes four women, some of them foreigners, and one whose story involved adultery. God's plan passed through all of them.

This tells us that God can write salvation through crooked lines. Our family history may have its own sins, wounds and shames. God is not ashamed to enter it. Jesus took a real human family tree, with all its brokenness, to heal it from within.

Today, let us thank God for our ancestors who handed on the faith, and pray for those in our family line who need healing and mercy. And let us pray with the Church: O Wisdom, come and teach us the way of prudence. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus had grandparents and great-grandparents, just like you. Pray today for your whole family!

Prayer

O Wisdom, who came forth from the mouth of the Most High, reaching from end to end and ordering all things mightily and sweetly: come and teach us the way of prudence. Bless my family and all its history. Amen.
