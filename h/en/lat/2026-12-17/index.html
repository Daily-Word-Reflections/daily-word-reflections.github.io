Word for the day

“An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” Matthew 1:1

Reflection

Today the final stretch of Advent begins. The readings now follow the calendar date, and the Church sings the great “O Antiphons”. Today's: “O Wisdom, who came from the mouth of the Most High, reaching from end to end and ordering all things mightily and sweetly: come and teach us the way of prudence” (cf. Wisdom 8:1). Jacob blesses Judah: “The scepter shall not depart from Judah… until tribute comes to him; and the obedience of the peoples is his.”

Matthew opens with a genealogy: forty-two generations in three groups of fourteen, from Abraham to David to the exile to Christ. It contains saints and sinners, kings and nobodies, and, unusually, four women (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and the wife of Uriah), some foreigners, some with complicated stories. God's plan runs through real, messy family history. Then the pattern breaks: “Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born.” Jesus enters our family tree as a true man and true Son of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, from today the Church enters the last week before Christmas. At Evening Prayer she begins to sing the great O Antiphons. Tonight's is: O Wisdom, coming forth from the mouth of the Most High, reaching from end to end and ordering all things mightily and sweetly: come and teach us the way of prudence.

In the first reading the dying Jacob blesses his sons and says of Judah: the sceptre shall not depart from Judah, until tribute comes to him; and the obedience of the peoples is his (Genesis 49:10). From the tribe of Judah would come David, and from David the Messiah.

The Gospel gives us the genealogy of Jesus: Abraham was the father of Isaac, Isaac the father of Jacob, and so on through forty-two generations to Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah (Matthew 1:1–16).

We may be tempted to skip this long list of names. But it is full of meaning. It includes great saints like Abraham and David, and great sinners too. It includes four women, some of them foreigners, and one whose story involved adultery. God's plan passed through all of them.

This tells us that God can write salvation through crooked lines. Our family history may have its own sins, wounds and shames. God is not ashamed to enter it. Jesus took a real human family tree, with all its brokenness, to heal it from within.

Today, let us thank God for our ancestors who handed on the faith, and pray for those in our family line who need healing and mercy. And let us pray with the Church: O Wisdom, come and teach us the way of prudence. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

From today until Christmas Eve the Mass readings change their tone: Advent now prepares directly for the birth of Christ. The Church begins at the roots. On his deathbed Jacob promises that the scepter shall not depart from Judah, and Matthew opens his Gospel with the family tree in which that promise runs on, through saints and sinners, to Jesus the Messiah, “the son of David, the son of Abraham.” Psalm 72 sings of the king in whose time justice shall flourish.

Genesis 49:2, 8–10 · The scepter shall not depart from Judah

Setting.

Genesis is ending. Jacob, also called Israel, is dying in Egypt and gathers his twelve sons to tell them what will happen “in days to come” (49:1). The poem is called his blessing, though the first three sons, Reuben, Simeon and Levi, hear hard words (49:3–7). Judah, the fourth, is next, and he receives the leadership. Later history is read in this poem: David and his royal line came from the tribe of Judah.

Structure.

  • v. 2: Jacob calls his sons to listen.
  • v. 8: Judah praised by his brothers, victorious over his enemies.
  • v. 9: the lion.
  • v. 10: the scepter and the promise of one to come.

Key words.

  • šēbeṭ, scepter: A staff or rod, the sign of a ruler. It can also mean a tribe. In v. 10 it is paired with the “ruler's staff” held between the feet of an enthroned king.
  • gûr ’aryēh, a lion's whelp: A young lion, strong and fearless. The New Testament takes up the image: “the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered” (Revelation 5:5).
  • ‘ad kî-yābō’ šîlōh, until ... comes: The most disputed phrase of the poem; see the note under v. 10.

Verse by verse.

“Judah, your brothers shall praise you” (49:8)

A play on words. Judah (Yehudah) sounds like the verb “to praise” (yādâ), as Leah said at his birth: “This time I will praise the Lord” (29:35).

“The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler’s staff from between his feet, until tribute comes to him; and the obedience of the peoples is his.” (49:10)

A note on the text. The Hebrew phrase is obscure and has been read in several ways. It could be “until Shiloh comes”, which the Hebrew letters most directly suggest; “until tribute comes to him”, as the NRSV has it, by a small change in reading; or “until he comes to whom it belongs”, close to the Malayalam POC's “its heir”. The Latin Vulgate has “until he comes who is to be sent”. Many ancient readers felt that the verse looks forward to a ruler still to come.

How the Church reads it.

Ancient Jewish interpreters read this verse of the king to come; the Aramaic Targum Onkelos names the Messiah here. Christians read it of Jesus, who “was descended from Judah” (Hebrews 7:14). St Justin Martyr used it in his First Apology to show that the Messiah had been foretold (in substance). The last words of the verse are wider than Israel: “the obedience of the peoples”. The Church hears that fulfilled when Jesus is confessed among the nations.

Matthew 1:1–17 · The book of the origin of Jesus Christ

Setting.

Matthew wrote for a Church deeply rooted in Israel's Scriptures, and his first page is a list of names. To a modern reader it seems dull; to his first readers it was a claim. Family trees established who a person was and what he could inherit. This one says that Jesus is the heir of the promises made to Abraham (Genesis 12:3) and to David (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

Structure.

  • v. 1: the title.
  • vv. 2–6a: Abraham to David, rising to the kingship.
  • vv. 6b–11: David to the deportation to Babylon, falling to disaster.
  • vv. 12–16: the deportation to Jesus, in obscurity, with most of the names otherwise unknown.
  • v. 17: the summary: three sets of fourteen.

Key words.

  • biblos geneseōs, an account of the genealogy: The phrase of the Greek Old Testament at Genesis 2:4 and 5:1. It could also be heard as “the book of the origin”. Matthew's first words recall the first book of the Bible: something new is beginning.
  • Christos, Messiah: The Greek for the Hebrew Mashiach, the anointed one. Here it is not yet simply a surname: it is the claim that Jesus is the anointed king Israel was waiting for.
  • dekatessares, fourteen: Matthew reaches this number with some selection: three kings between Joram and Uzziah are omitted (compare 1 Chronicles 3:11–12). Many scholars suggest a reason: in Hebrew the letters of David's name, DWD, add up to fourteen. If that is correct, the whole list is signed with David's name.

Verse by verse.

“An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” (1:1)

Two names, two promises. David stands for the kingdom, Abraham for the blessing of all nations. Matthew ends where he begins: “make disciples of all nations” (28:19).

“and Judah the father of Perez and Zerah by Tamar” (1:3)

Genealogies usually name only fathers. Matthew names four women before Mary: Tamar (v. 3), Rahab and Ruth (v. 5), and “the wife of Uriah” (v. 6). Rahab was of Jericho, Ruth of Moab, and Uriah a Hittite; each of their stories is irregular or painful. God's plan passes through foreigners and through wounded histories.

“and Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah.” (1:16)

The pattern “A the father of B” breaks here. In Greek “of whom” is feminine, and refers to Mary alone. Joseph is the legal link to David, and Mary is the mother. Tomorrow's Gospel explains why.

How the Church reads it.

St Leo the Great, in a letter used in the Office of Readings today, says that to call the Lord true man would be of no value to us unless we believe he comes from the line of ancestors the Gospel sets out (in substance). The list guards the truth of the Incarnation: God entered a real family. The Catechism notes that Joseph was called so that Jesus should be born “into the messianic lineage of David” (CCC 437). The women point ahead too: the mission of many holy women prepared for Mary's, and God chooses those considered weak to show his faithfulness (CCC 489).

Questions people ask

Why do Matthew and Luke give different genealogies of Jesus?

Luke 3:23–38 has a different list between David and Joseph. Neither Gospel explains this. An early explanation, by Julius Africanus and preserved by Eusebius, appeals to levirate marriage; others suggest that one list is legal and the other natural. No solution is certain. Both Gospels agree on what matters: Jesus belongs to David's line, and his conception is the work of God.

For the Latin reader

From 17 December the Roman Rite sings the “O Antiphons”, one each evening at the Magnificat of Vespers and also used before the Gospel at Mass. Today's is O Sapientia, “O Wisdom”. Each one calls on Christ by an Old Testament title and ends with “Come”.

For study and teaching

  • Whose names would appear in my own story of faith, including the painful ones? Can I see God at work through them?
  • Matthew does not hide the sinners among Jesus' ancestors. How does that change the way I think about my own family and my parish?
