Word for the day

“And no one puts new wine into old wineskins; otherwise, the wine will burst the skins, and the wine is lost, and so are the skins; but one puts new wine into fresh wineskins.”” Mark 2:22

Reflection

Hebrews begins its great teaching on Christ the High Priest. Every high priest is chosen from among mortals to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins, and “is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is subject to weakness.” Christ did not appoint himself; the Father said: “You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.” And he learned his priesthood in suffering: “In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears… Although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered.”

In the Gospel people ask why Jesus' disciples do not fast. He answers with images of joy and newness: wedding guests do not fast while the bridegroom is with them; no one sews new cloth on an old garment; “one puts new wine into fresh wineskins.” Christ brings something new that needs new hearts. Today the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity begins: divisions among Christians are old wineskins that cannot hold the new wine of Christ's love. We pray, as Jesus did, “that they may all be one.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Hebrews shows us Christ as our High Priest. Every priest is chosen from among people and can deal gently with the weak, because he too is weak. Christ did not make himself a priest; the Father said: you are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.

In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, and although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered (Hebrews 5:7–8). Our High Priest knows our tears from inside.

In the Gospel people ask why his disciples do not fast. Jesus says the wedding guests cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them. And no one puts new wine into old wineskins; one puts new wine into fresh wineskins (Mark 2:22). Jesus brings something new that needs new hearts.

Today the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity begins. Our divisions are old wineskins. Let us pray with Jesus, that they may all be one, and let us become new wineskins ourselves. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hebrews today looks at Jesus in the days of his flesh, praying with loud cries and tears, and learning obedience through suffering. Mark shows him in the days of his flesh too, the bridegroom among his friends, and he says quietly that the day will come when he will be taken away. Joy and sorrow are not opposed here: the one who is the Church’s joy is the one who suffered for her. The key words are high priest, bridegroom and new.

Hebrews 5:1–10 · He learned obedience

Setting.

The preacher has just invited his hearers to approach the throne of grace (4:16). Now he explains what kind of priest is waiting there. First he lists what a high priest needs, as seen in the priesthood of Aaron, then he shows how Christ meets those needs in a greater way. Two texts carry the argument, Psalm 2:7 and Psalm 110:4, the psalm the Lectionary gives us today.

Melchizedek, named here for the first time in the letter, will be taken up at length in chapter 7.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the high priest: taken from among mortals, gentle because weak, and called by God.
  • vv. 5–6: Christ did not appoint himself: two psalms.
  • vv. 7–10: his prayer and his suffering: he is made perfect and becomes the source of salvation.

Key words.

  • metriopathein, to deal gently: A rare word for keeping one’s feelings in measure, neither harsh nor indulgent. The high priest shares human weakness and so can be patient with the weak.
  • emathen … epathen, he learned … he suffered: A play on words in the Greek, and a saying known among the Greeks that one learns by suffering. As Son he always loved the Father. As man he came to know obedience from the inside, in the suffering it cost him.
  • teleiōtheis, having been made perfect: This does not mean that Jesus had moral faults to overcome (4:15 excludes that). The verb means to bring to completion, and in the Greek Old Testament it is used for the consecration of priests (Exodus 29:9). Through his Passion and glory he is fully established in his office as Saviour.

Verse by verse.

“He is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is subject to weakness” (5:2)

This applies to priests of the old order, and the preacher will note where Christ differs: he has no sins of his own to offer for (v. 3; 7:27). But every Catholic priest can take the verse to heart. He offers the sacrifice as a sinner among sinners.

“In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to the one who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverent submission.” (5:7)

Most readers think of Gethsemane and the Cross, though the text does not name them. It goes further than the Gospels: “loud cries and tears”. But how was he “heard”, since he did die? He was not spared death. He was brought through it, in the Resurrection.

“Although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered; and having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him” (5:8–9)

Notice the word “obey” in both places. His obedience becomes the source of ours. The one who learned obedience now saves those who learn it from him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees all the troubles and petitions of humanity gathered up in the cry of the incarnate Word on the Cross, and sees the Father answer them, beyond all hope, by raising his Son (CCC 2606). It quotes v. 8 in explaining the prayer “Thy will be done”: how much more reason have we sinful creatures to learn obedience (CCC 2825). The Council of Trent’s teaching that Christ is the source of eternal salvation by his Passion is taken up in CCC 617. Every ministerial priesthood is understood by the Church only as a sharing in his one priesthood (CCC 1544–1545).

Mark 2:18–22 · The bridegroom is with them

Setting.

This is the third of the five controversy stories. Fasting was a serious part of Jewish piety. The Law commanded it on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29–31), and devout people added other days; Luke mentions a Pharisee who fasted twice a week (Luke 18:12). Mark says that John’s disciples and the Pharisees were fasting. The surprise is that those around Jesus, who is plainly a man of God, are not. The question is not hostile, and it is a fair one.

Structure.

  • v. 18: the question about fasting.
  • vv. 19–20: the bridegroom: now no fast; one day there will be.
  • vv. 21–22: two short sayings: the patch and the wineskins.

Key words.

  • nymphios, bridegroom: In the prophets, God himself is Israel’s husband (Hosea 2:16–20; Isaiah 54:5; 62:5). Jesus takes this image for himself, without explanation. It is a large claim made in a few words.
  • aparthē, is taken away: A strong verb for removing someone. This is the first shadow of the Passion in Mark’s Gospel, in a conversation about a wedding.
  • askos, wineskin: A leather bag for wine. New wine is still fermenting and stretching its container; an old skin has lost its give and bursts.

Verse by verse.

“The wedding guests cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them, can they?” (2:19)

A wedding in that world could last for days and was the happiest occasion a village knew. Nobody fasts at the wedding. By this image Jesus says that the time of waiting has given way to the time of fulfilment.

“The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast on that day.” (2:20)

Jesus does not abolish fasting. He places it after his Passion. The Church’s fasting looks to Christ’s absence and to his return. Some have seen in “that day” a reference to the Friday of his death. The Christian fast is not the fast of those who have no bridegroom. It is the fast of those who love him and long for him.

“but one puts new wine into fresh wineskins.” (2:22)

The sayings warn against patching and pouring: what Jesus brings cannot simply be added to life as it was. They do not say that the old cloak and the old wine were bad. Elsewhere Jesus praises the scribe who brings out of his treasure “what is new and what is old” (Matthew 13:52), and says that he has not come to abolish the law (Matthew 5:17).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points out that the Lord referred to himself as the bridegroom in this very verse, and that the Church is the bride (CCC 796). The Church keeps the days of fasting and abstinence in this spirit: the Fridays of the year and the season of Lent are days of penance (CCC 1438; Code of Canon Law, cann. 1250–1251). The Church does not read “new wine” as contempt for Israel. Jesus himself came to fulfil the Law, not to abolish it (CCC 577–582).

Questions people ask

Why should Catholics fast at all, if the bridegroom has risen and is with us?

He is with us, and yet we still wait to see him. Both are true. That is why the Church does not fast on Sundays, the days of the Resurrection, but does fast at other times. Fasting opens the heart for prayer and for the poor, and it joins us to the Lord in his Passion (CCC 540, 1434, 1438).

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews says that Jesus prayed with loud cries and tears. Do I allow myself to pray like that? What would I bring?
  • What does fasting mean in my life? Does it bring me closer to the bridegroom and to the poor, or is it only a rule?
