Word for the day

“I have come as light into the world, so that everyone who believes in me should not remain in the darkness.” John 12:46

Reflection

“The word of God continued to advance and gain adherents.” With this short line Luke closes the story of King Herod Agrippa, who had killed James and imprisoned Peter, and who then died miserably. Kings rise and fall; the word goes on. Then the scene moves to Antioch, where the Church has grown rich in gifts. Luke names five “prophets and teachers”: Barnabas from Cyprus, Simeon called Niger, which means “the black,” Lucius of Cyrene in North Africa, Manaen who had been brought up with Herod the tetrarch, and Saul of Tarsus. An African, a Cypriot, a man of the royal court, a former persecutor: already the Church is a gathering of nations and classes. And it is while they are at prayer that the Spirit speaks: “While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.’”

The first great missionary journey is born in the liturgy. The word Luke uses for “worshiping” is the word from which we get “liturgy.” The Church did not choose missionaries by vote or by talent; it fasted, prayed, listened, and then obeyed. “Then after fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off.” And Luke adds: “being sent out by the Holy Spirit.” The community lays on hands; the Spirit sends. The two go down to the port of Seleucia, sail to Cyprus, and begin to proclaim the word in the synagogues at Salamis, with young John Mark as their helper. The Psalm is the perfect prayer for this moment of mission: “Let the peoples praise you, O God; let all the peoples praise you.” The blessing given to Israel is meant to shine out to every nation, so that “your way may be known upon earth, your saving power among all nations.”

In the Gospel Jesus cries aloud, almost his last public words in John before the Last Supper: “Whoever believes in me believes not in me but in him who sent me. And whoever sees me sees him who sent me.” Jesus is not a messenger who points away from himself; to see him is to see the Father. “I have come as light into the world, so that everyone who believes in me should not remain in the darkness.” And he says that he did not come to condemn: “I came not to judge the world, but to save the world.” Yet the word he speaks is not empty. It will stand on the last day as the measure of our lives, because it is the Father's own word. Today the Church remembers St Anselm, the monk of Bec and Archbishop of Canterbury, who spent his life seeking to understand this light. His famous motto was “faith seeking understanding.” He did not believe because he had understood; he believed so that he might understand. He prayed: teach me to seek you, and show yourself to me when I seek. For Anselm, thinking about God was itself a way of praying, and prayer was the light in which thought could see.

Full homily

Dear friends, in the church at Antioch the leaders are at prayer. They are worshiping and fasting. And in that prayer the Holy Spirit speaks: set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them (Acts 13:2). The greatest missionary journey in history began in a prayer meeting.

The Church did not choose Paul's mission by a committee vote. It prayed, it fasted, it listened, and it obeyed. Then they laid hands on them and sent them off. Whenever we have important decisions, in our families, our parishes, our lives, let us learn from Antioch: pray first, then act.

Look also at who those leaders were: a Cypriot, a man called Niger, the black, a man from Cyrene in Africa, a member of Herod's court, and a former persecutor. The Church was already one family of many nations.

In the Gospel Jesus cries out: I have come as light into the world, so that everyone who believes in me should not remain in the darkness (John 12:46). He did not come to condemn but to save. Whoever sees Jesus sees the Father.

Today we remember St Anselm, a monk who became Archbishop of Canterbury. He loved to think about God, but he always thought on his knees. He said: I believe, so that I may understand. He did not stop believing when he had questions; he took his questions into prayer.

Let us ask for that grace today: to let the light of Christ fill both our hearts and our minds, and to be ready, like Barnabas and Saul, to go where the Spirit sends us. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, when the leaders at Antioch were praying, the Holy Spirit chose Barnabas and Saul to go and tell the whole world about Jesus.

When we pray, God can tell us what he wants us to do too. Jesus is the light of the world. Let us say: Jesus, shine in my heart!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, light of the world, whoever sees you sees the Father who sent you. Shine into the darkness of our minds and hearts. As your Spirit set apart Barnabas and Saul while your Church was at prayer, call and send in our time missionaries, priests and religious, and make every one of us ready to go where you send. By the prayers of St Anselm, give us a faith that seeks to understand and an understanding that bows in love, so that we may know you more truly and love you more dearly. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The word of God “continued to advance”, and in Antioch the Holy Spirit speaks while the Church is at worship: “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul”. Mission is born of liturgy and fasting. In the Gospel Jesus gives his last public summary: he has come as light, not to judge the world but to save it. Psalm 67 prays that God’s saving power be known among all nations. Today is the optional memorial of St Anselm, who wrote that he believed in order to understand.

Acts 12:24–13:5a · Set apart for the work

Setting.

Chapter 12 ends with the death of Herod Agrippa I and the growth of the word of God (12:23–24): persecutors pass, and the word remains. Barnabas and Saul had taken famine relief from Antioch to Jerusalem (11:29–30) and now return with John Mark. From here on Antioch, not Jerusalem, is the base of the mission to the nations, and Saul moves to the center of the story.

Structure.

  • 12:24–25: the word grows; Barnabas and Saul return with John Mark.
  • 13:1: five prophets and teachers.
  • 13:2–3: the Spirit speaks at worship; fasting, prayer, laying on of hands.
  • 13:4–5a: sent by the Spirit to Seleucia, Cyprus and Salamis.

Key words.

  • leitourgeō, to worship, to serve in the liturgy: The word behind our “liturgy” (13:2). The Spirit speaks while the community is at worship and fasting. The great missionary journeys begin not from a planning meeting but from prayer.
  • aphorizō, to set apart: Paul will use the same verb of himself: “set apart for the gospel of God” (Romans 1:1). To be set apart is not to be cut off from people but to be dedicated to a task for them.
  • syntrophos, member of the court, one brought up with: Manaen had been brought up with Herod Antipas, the tetrarch who beheaded John the Baptist. The list of leaders joins a Levite from Cyprus, Simeon called Niger (“black”), Lucius from Cyrene in Africa, a man of the royal court and a former persecutor. The Gospel had reached the palace and the street.

Verse by verse.

“Then after completing their mission Barnabas and Saul returned to Jerusalem” (12:25)

A known puzzle. The oldest manuscripts read “to Jerusalem”, but the story needs a return from Jerusalem to Antioch, and other manuscripts read “from Jerusalem”, as the Malayalam POC does. Some scholars read the Greek as “they returned, having completed their service in Jerusalem”.

“While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said,” (13:2)

Luke does not say how the Spirit spoke; most likely through one of the prophets named in v. 1.

“So, being sent out by the Holy Spirit,” (13:4)

The community laid hands on them and sent them (v. 3); Luke now says the Spirit sent them. This was not the first making of Saul an apostle, for his call came from the risen Lord (Galatians 1:1), but the Church’s blessing and sending for a particular work. The Spirit calls and the Church sends, and both are one act.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen in this scene the pattern of mission: the initiative of the Spirit, the discernment of a community at prayer, and the sending by the Church with the laying on of hands. Pope St Paul VI wrote that evangelizing is the grace and vocation proper to the Church, her deepest identity (Evangelii Nuntiandi 14). A parish that prays and fasts together will sooner or later hear the same voice: set someone apart for the work.

John 12:44–50 · I have come as light into the world

Setting.

These verses close the first half of John’s Gospel, which many call the Book of Signs. The evangelist has just said that many did not believe, while some leaders believed in secret (12:37–43). Then, with no place or time given, Jesus “cried aloud” a last public summary of all he has said: to believe in him is to believe in the Father; he is light; he came to save; his word will judge. From chapter 13 on he speaks only to his own.

Structure.

  • vv. 44–45: to believe in Jesus and to see him is to believe in and see the one who sent him.
  • v. 46: light in the darkness.
  • vv. 47–48: not to judge but to save; the word as judge.
  • vv. 49–50: the Father’s commandment is eternal life.

Key words.

  • ekraxen, cried aloud: Used of Jesus teaching in the Temple at the feast of Tabernacles (7:28, 37). A public, urgent cry, like a prophet’s; this is the last time Jesus addresses the crowds in John.
  • skotia, darkness: Not only the absence of light but the world closed against God (1:5). No one who believes need “remain” there: the verb suggests that all begin in the dark, and Christ comes to lead them out.
  • krinō, to judge: Jesus does not come as a judge looking for the guilty. Yet a word refused becomes a judgment: the light itself shows what a person has chosen (3:19).

Verse by verse.

“Whoever believes in me believes not in me but in him who sent me.” (12:44)

Jesus never draws people to himself apart from the Father. Faith in him is the way into the Father’s own life.

“I have come as light into the world, so that everyone who believes in me should not remain in the darkness.” (12:46)

The Easter candle, carried into the dark church at the Vigil with the chant “The Light of Christ”, proclaims this verse without words.

“I came not to judge the world, but to save the world.” (12:47)

Compare 3:17. The Father’s purpose in sending the Son is mercy.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses as Jesus’ own summary of the Gospel. The Catechism teaches that Christ has full right to judge, yet he came to save, and that by rejecting grace in this life a person already judges himself (CCC 679). So the Church preaches with urgency and gentleness together: the light is offered to all, and no one is forced into it.

For the Latin reader

St Anselm (1033–1109), born at Aosta, was a monk and abbot of Bec in Normandy and then Archbishop of Canterbury; he is a Doctor of the Church. In his Proslogion he prays: “I do not seek to understand in order to believe, but I believe in order to understand.” His watchword, faith seeking understanding, suits today’s Gospel: whoever believes does not remain in darkness, and faith becomes a light for the mind as well as the heart.

For study and teaching

  • What does it mean for my parish that mission is born from worship and fasting, as in Antioch?
  • Where in my life am I still choosing to “remain in the darkness”? What would it take to step into the light?
