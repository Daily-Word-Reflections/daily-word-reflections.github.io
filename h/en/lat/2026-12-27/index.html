Word for the day

“The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom; and the favor of God was upon him.” Luke 2:40

Reflection

On the Sunday after Christmas the Church looks at the home in which the Son of God chose to grow up. He did not come as an adult from nowhere; he came as a child into a family, with a mother, a foster father, a village, relatives and the Law of Moses. The Second Vatican Council calls the family “the domestic church” (Lumen Gentium 11), and the Holy Family of Nazareth is its model.

This year's readings put faith at the centre of family life. Abraham, childless and old, is told to look at the stars: “So shall your descendants be.” And “he believed the LORD; and the LORD reckoned it to him as righteousness.” Sarah conceives Isaac at the time God had promised. Hebrews praises their faith: Abraham set out “not knowing where he was going”, and later offered Isaac, trusting that God could raise the dead. A family is built not first on money or plans but on trust in God's promise.

In the Gospel, Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to the Temple to present him to the Lord, as the Law prescribed for the firstborn (Exodus 13:2), offering the gift of the poor, “a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons”. Simeon takes the child in his arms and blesses God; he also tells Mary that her son will be “a sign that will be opposed” and that “a sword will pierce your own soul too”. Even the Holy Family was not spared sorrow. Then they return to Nazareth, where “the child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom”. The hidden years at Nazareth sanctify ordinary family life: work, prayer, obedience and love (CCC 531–533).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, two days ago we knelt before the manger. Today the Church asks us to look around the manger: at Mary, at Joseph, at the family into which God chose to be born.

God could have come in any way he wished. He chose to come as a baby, into a family. He needed a mother to feed him, a father to protect him and teach him a trade, and a home where he could grow. That tells us how much God values the family.

Today's first reading shows Abraham and Sarah, old and childless. God tells Abraham to count the stars: so many will be your descendants. And Abraham believed. A family is not built first on property, jobs or plans, but on faith in God.

In the Gospel, Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to the Temple. They are poor; they can only offer two small birds. Simeon takes the child in his arms and praises God, but also tells Mary that a sword will pierce her heart. Even the Holy Family knew sorrow, fear and uncertainty. If your family is going through hard times, you are not far from Nazareth.

Then the Gospel says simply: they went home to Nazareth, and the child grew, filled with wisdom, and the favour of God was upon him. Thirty quiet years of work, prayer and ordinary love. That is where holiness grows for most of us: at the kitchen table, at work, in patience with each other.

So let us bring our families to the Lord today. Let us pray together at home, forgive each other quickly, respect our elders and bless our children. Jesus, Mary and Joseph, make our homes like yours.

Dear brothers and sisters, on this feast of the Holy Family let us look at the one member who never says a word in the Gospels: St Joseph. Mary speaks, Jesus speaks, angels speak, but Joseph only listens and acts.

Four times an angel speaks to Joseph in a dream, and four times he obeys at once: to take Mary as his wife, to flee to Egypt, to return to the land of Israel, and to settle in Nazareth (Matthew 1:20; 2:13, 19, 22). Each time the Gospel says simply that Joseph got up and did as he was told. In a world full of noise, Joseph teaches us to listen to God in silence and to act without delay.

Joseph was a protector. When Herod sought to kill the child, Joseph took the child and his mother by night and fled to Egypt (Matthew 2:14). Think of that journey: a refugee family, poor, in a foreign land, with a little baby. Joseph worked with his hands to feed them. Today many fathers work far from home, in the Gulf or abroad, to support their families. Joseph understands them.

The Gospel calls Joseph a just man (Matthew 1:19). When he discovered that Mary was with child, he did not want to expose her to public disgrace. His justice was full of mercy. In our families too, justice and tenderness must go together: correction without humiliation, firmness without harshness, forgiveness without weakness.

Fathers, look at Joseph: protect your families, work honestly, pray, bless your children. Mothers, look at Mary. Children, look at Jesus, who was obedient to His parents. And let every family here entrust itself to St Joseph, guardian of the Redeemer. May he who guarded the Holy Family guard ours too.

Dear brothers and sisters, the Second Vatican Council gave the Christian family a beautiful name: the domestic church (Lumen Gentium 11). On the feast of the Holy Family, let us see what this means in practice.

A church is a place where God is worshipped, His Word is heard and love is lived. The home is meant to be exactly that. Parents are the first heralds of the faith for their children (CCC 1656). Long before catechism class, children learn from their parents how to make the sign of the cross, how to fold their hands, how to forgive. Moses told the Israelites to speak of God's words at home and on the road, when lying down and when rising (Deuteronomy 6:7).

The Fourth Commandment teaches children to honour their father and mother, with respect, gratitude, obedience while they are young, and care for them when they are old (CCC 2214–2220; Sirach 3:1–14). It also gives parents duties: to educate their children in faith, in prayer and in virtue, to respect their vocation, and to give the example of a good life (CCC 2221–2226). St Paul writes: fathers, do not provoke your children, or they may lose heart (Colossians 3:21).

Families today are under great pressure: work far from home, financial stress, screens that keep everyone apart in the same room, addiction, broken marriages. The Holy Family also knew danger, poverty and exile. They survived because God was at the centre. Let our families do the same: pray together every evening, even briefly; eat at least one meal a day together without phones; come to the Holy Mass together on Sunday; forgive before sleeping.

And let the parish be a family of families, supporting those who are struggling, single parents, widows, the elderly living alone. May Jesus, Mary and Joseph bless every family in this parish, and make our homes true domestic churches.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sunday within the Octave of Christmas the Church looks at the home in which the Son of God grew up. In Year B the Lectionary offers these readings, and they begin long before Nazareth, with an old couple who have no child. Abraham and Sarah receive a son only as God's gift. Hebrews shows that Abraham's faith went further: he was ready to give that son back. The psalm sings of the God who remembers his covenant. In Luke, Mary and Joseph carry their child to the Temple, offer him to the Lord, and take him home to grow up. The key words are promise, offering and growing. In each of these households the child is first of all God's gift, not the parents' possession.

Genesis 15:1–6; 21:1–3 · Your heir shall be your own son

Setting.

God had promised Abram a land, a great name and many descendants when he called him out of Haran (Genesis 12:1–4). Years have passed. “After these things” (15:1) points back to chapter 14, where Abram has rescued his nephew Lot from the kings and been blessed by Melchizedek. Outwardly he is a man of success, but the promise on which he left everything has not begun: he has no child.

The reading then jumps over six chapters. It leaves out the covenant ceremony of 15:7–21, the story of Hagar and Ishmael, the covenant of circumcision and the visit at Mamre, and goes straight to the birth of Isaac.

Structure.

  • 15:1: God speaks first: do not fear.
  • 15:2–3: Abram's honest complaint: I am childless, and a servant of my house will inherit.
  • 15:4–5: the answer: your own son; look at the stars.
  • 15:6: Abram believes, and God counts it as righteousness.
  • 21:1–3: God keeps his word: Sarah bears Isaac.
  • ¶ The two passages are promise and fulfilment. Between them lie many years of waiting, which the Lectionary passes over in a moment, but which Abraham and Sarah lived day by day.

Key words.

  • he’ĕmîn, he believed: From the same root as “Amen”. It means to lean on someone as firm and reliable. Abram does not see anything new in v. 6. He rests his weight on the one who has spoken.
  • ṣĕdāqāh, righteousness: In the Bible, to be righteous is to stand rightly in relationship with God. God counts Abram's trust as that right standing.
  • pāqad, to deal with, to visit: The verb of 21:1, which the NRSV renders “dealt with”. It often describes God coming in person to act for his people, as in Genesis 50:24 and Exodus 3:16.
  • yiṣḥāq, Isaac: The name is linked with the Hebrew for laughter. Both Abraham and Sarah had laughed at the promise (17:17; 18:12), and Sarah now laughs for joy (21:6).

Verse by verse.

“Do not be afraid, Abram, I am your shield; your reward shall be very great.” (15:1)

This is the first time in the Bible that God says “Do not be afraid”. It is said to a man who is weary of waiting, before he has said a word.

“O Lord God, what will you give me, for I continue childless” (15:2)

Faith here is not silence. Abram tells God exactly what hurts. Many couples who long for a child, and many who have lost one, can pray with this verse.

“Look toward heaven and count the stars, if you are able to count them.” (15:5)

God brings Abram outside. Abram has been counting what he lacks, and God tells him to count something that cannot be counted.

“And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness.” (15:6)

One of the great verses of the Bible. St Paul builds on it in Romans 4 and Galatians 3, and James 2:23 quotes it too.

“Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the time of which God had spoken to him.” (21:2)

Abraham is a hundred years old (21:5). The stress falls on God's fidelity: “as he had said”, “as he had promised”, “at the time of which God had spoken”.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul reads Genesis 15:6 as proof that God's people are made righteous through faith, and calls Abraham the father of all who believe (Romans 4:11). The Catechism says that Abraham fulfils the definition of faith given in Hebrews 11:1 (CCC 146) and adds that God's promise of descendants is “the fruit of faith and of the power of the Holy Spirit” (CCC 706). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the beginnings of the Church's faith and election are already found among the patriarchs, Moses and the prophets (Nostra Aetate 4). The Church is not ashamed of these roots; she gives thanks for them. In the Roman Canon the Mass itself names “the sacrifice of Abraham, our father in faith”.

Psalm 105:1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 8–9 · He remembers his covenant for ever

Setting.

Psalm 105 is a long hymn that tells Israel's history as the story of God's faithfulness: Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, Joseph in Egypt, Moses and the Exodus, and the gift of the land. Its first verses (vv. 1–15) appear again in 1 Chronicles 16:8–22, where they are sung when David brings the ark to Jerusalem. Today's verses take only the opening call to praise and the statement of the covenant with Abraham.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: give thanks and tell the nations.
  • vv. 3–4: rejoice and seek the Lord.
  • vv. 5–6: remember, you children of Abraham and Jacob.
  • vv. 8–9: God himself remembers his covenant.
  • ¶ The people are told to remember (v. 5) because God remembers first (v. 8). Israel's memory rests on the memory of God.

Key words.

  • hôdû, give thanks: This imperative opens the psalm. The verb also means to acknowledge, to declare openly what God has done.
  • zākar, to remember: When God “remembers” in the Bible he does not recall a forgotten fact. He acts on his promise, as in Genesis 8:1 and Exodus 2:24.
  • bĕrît, covenant: The binding bond God makes with his people. Psalm 105 uses it of the promise to Abraham, the same promise as today's first reading.

Verse by verse.

“O give thanks to the Lord, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples.” (105:1)

Even in the Old Testament, the story of Israel is meant to be told to the nations. Simeon will sing of the same horizon in today's Gospel.

“Seek the Lord and his strength; seek his presence continually.” (105:4)

Literally “seek his face always”. To seek God is the work of a whole life, never something finished.

“He is mindful of his covenant forever, of the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations,” (105:8)

A thousand generations is the Bible's way of saying without end. Compare Deuteronomy 7:9.

How the Church reads it.

Zechariah and Mary sing in the same key at the beginning of Luke's Gospel. God has remembered “his holy covenant, the oath that he swore to our ancestor Abraham” (Luke 1:72–73), and Mary too ends her song with Abraham (Luke 1:54–55). St Augustine, in his work On the Trinity, returns to the words “seek his face always” as a motto for the whole search for God (in substance). On this feast the psalm tells parents that they hand on a memory: the deeds of God, told from one generation to the next.

Hebrews 11:8, 11–12, 17–19 · By faith Abraham obeyed

Setting.

Hebrews is a sermon to tired Christians, probably of Jewish background; its author is not named. Chapter 11 opens with a definition: “faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1). A long roll of witnesses follows, from Abel to the martyrs, each introduced by the same words “by faith”. Abraham has more space than anyone. The reading selects three moments: his call, the birth of a son, and the test on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22).

Structure.

  • v. 8: the call: he leaves without knowing where he is going.
  • vv. 11–12: the child: from a man as good as dead come descendants like the stars.
  • vv. 17–19: the test: he is ready to offer the son of the promise.
  • ¶ Each step asks more. First Abraham gives up his past, then he trusts God for his future, and finally he is ready to give back the future God has given him.

Key words.

  • pistei, by faith: The one word that opens each example in the chapter, like a drumbeat. It stands first in the Greek for emphasis.
  • monogenēs, only son: Used of Isaac in v. 17, although Abraham also had Ishmael. It marks Isaac as the unique child of the promise. John uses the same word of Christ (John 1:14, 18).
  • en parabolēi, figuratively speaking: Literally “in a parable”. Isaac did not die, so his return was not a true resurrection, yet it points forward to one.

Verse by verse.

“By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to set out for a place that he was to receive as an inheritance; and he set out, not knowing where he was going.” (11:8)

Abraham knew who was calling him, even though he did not know the destination. Faith does not need the whole map.

“because he considered him faithful who had promised.” (11:11)

A note on the text. The Greek of this verse is difficult, and translators differ on who is the subject. The NRSV makes Abraham the one who received power to beget; the Malayalam POC, like many older versions, makes Sarah the one who received power to conceive. Either way, the ground of faith is the same: the one who promised is faithful.

“He considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead” (11:19)

Here is the most striking comment on Genesis 22. Abraham could not see how the promise would survive, but he trusted that God could raise the dead. This is faith at its fullest.

“figuratively speaking, he did receive him back.” (11:19)

The Church has always seen here an image of Christ's death and resurrection.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism presents Abraham's sacrifice as the last stage in the purification of his faith, and says that the father of believers is thus made like the Father who will not spare his own Son (CCC 2572; compare CCC 145–147). St Irenaeus teaches that Abraham, in his faith, readily gave up his only and beloved son so that God might also give his own Son for us (in substance, Against Heresies 4.5). For parents the passage is demanding and consoling: to hand a child over to God is the highest act of faith, and God does not take what is given to him in order to destroy it.

Luke 2:22–40 (or 2:22, 39–40) · They brought him up to present him to the Lord

Setting.

Forty days after the birth of a son, a Jewish mother came to the Temple for the rite of purification. She brought a lamb and a bird, or two birds if she could not afford a lamb (Leviticus 12:2–8). Separately, every firstborn son belonged to the Lord (Exodus 13:2, 12) and was bought back with a payment (Numbers 18:16).

Luke weaves these customs into one scene. He speaks of “their” purification, and he says nothing of a payment. He shows the child being given to God, as Hannah gave Samuel at the sanctuary (1 Samuel 1:24–28). Five times in this passage Luke mentions the law (2:22, 23, 24, 27, 39). The family of Jesus is a devout Jewish family, living by the Torah. The shorter form of the Gospel keeps only the arrival and the return to Nazareth.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: Mary and Joseph bring the child and the offering of the poor.
  • vv. 25–28: Simeon, led by the Spirit, takes the child in his arms.
  • vv. 29–32: his song: a light for the Gentiles, glory for Israel.
  • vv. 33–35: the second word: a sign opposed, and a sword for Mary.
  • vv. 36–38: Anna gives thanks and speaks about the child.
  • vv. 39–40: home to Nazareth, where the child grows.

Key words.

  • parastēsai, to present: The word for bringing someone before a superior, and also for presenting an offering. Jesus enters the Temple as one who is offered.
  • paraklēsis, consolation: What Simeon awaits (v. 25). It recalls “Comfort, O comfort my people” in Isaiah 40:1. Jewish hope is met in a child.
  • rhomphaia, sword: A large sword. Simeon's word sets the shadow of the Cross over Mary's joy, in the middle of the Christmas season.

Verse by verse.

“they brought him up to Jerusalem to present him to the Lord” (2:22)

Malachi 3:1 had said that the Lord would suddenly come to his Temple. He comes, carried in his parents' arms, and hardly anyone notices.

“a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons.” (2:24)

This is the offering of those who could not afford a lamb (Leviticus 12:8). The Son of God grew up in a family of modest means. St Paul's words fit: “though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor” (2 Corinthians 8:9).

“Simeon took him in his arms and praised God” (2:28)

Mary and Joseph let a stranger take their child in his arms. A family that holds its child with open hands lets others meet him.

“a sword will pierce your own soul too.” (2:35)

Mary does not stand outside the drama of her Son. The pain of a mother is included in his mission.

“The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom; and the favor of God was upon him.” (2:40)

Luke says almost the same of John the Baptist (1:80) and of the boy Samuel (1 Samuel 2:21, 26). The eternal Son truly grows, day by day, in a home at Nazareth, among his relatives and neighbours.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in this scene the firstborn Son who belongs to the Lord, and says that Simeon's sword announces Christ's perfect oblation on the cross (CCC 529). Vatican II says that the union of the Mother with her Son in the work of salvation appears here (Lumen Gentium 57). On Nazareth, the Catechism teaches that the hidden life lets every person meet Jesus in the most ordinary events of daily life (CCC 533), and it quotes St Paul VI's address at Nazareth on the lessons of silence, family life and work. Christ chose to be born and grow up in a family, and so the Christian home is called the domestic church (CCC 1655–1657; Lumen Gentium 11). St John Chrysostom told his people to make the home a little church (in substance, Homilies on Ephesians 20).

Questions people ask

Why are there different readings for this feast?

The Lectionary gives Sirach 3 and Colossians 3 as readings that may be used in every year, and it also offers proper readings for Years A, B and C. Today's are those of Year B. A parish that hears Sirach and Colossians is keeping the same feast.

Mary was without sin. Why did she need purification?

Purification in Leviticus 12 concerns ritual purity after childbirth, not moral guilt. Mary and Joseph keep the law as faithful Jews, and her Son himself was “born under the law” (Galatians 4:4). Their obedience is an act of devotion, not an admission of sin.

Luke calls Joseph the child's father. Does that deny the virgin birth?

No. Luke has already told of the virginal conception (1:34–35). Joseph was truly the head of the household and the father who cared for Jesus, gave him his name and his place in the house of David. St John Paul II reflects on this fatherhood in Redemptoris Custos.

Was the Holy Family perfect, so that my family cannot compare?

They were holy, but their life was not easy. They knew poverty, a birth away from home, and a word about a sword. Pope Francis points to this real family in Amoris Laetitia 65–66. Their holiness lay in trust and in love, and every family can learn these.

For the Latin reader

The devotion to the Holy Family grew in the nineteenth century. Pope Leo XIII promoted it, and Pope Benedict XV extended the feast to the whole Latin Church in 1921. The Roman calendar of 1969 placed it on the Sunday within the Octave of Christmas, so that the mystery of the home at Nazareth is kept in the light of the crib. Because 27 December is a Sunday this year, the feast of St John the Apostle gives way to it. The Presentation itself has its own feast on 2 February, when candles are blessed. St Sophronius of Jerusalem preached on that day that all should run to meet Christ with lights in their hands (in substance). The whole Mass of today is an invitation for each family to bring its children, and its worries, to the altar.
