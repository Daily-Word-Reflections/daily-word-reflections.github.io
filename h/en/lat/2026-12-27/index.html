Word for the day

“The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom; and the favor of God was upon him.” Luke 2:40

Reflection

On the Sunday after Christmas the Church looks at the home in which the Son of God chose to grow up. He did not come as an adult from nowhere; he came as a child into a family, with a mother, a foster father, a village, relatives and the Law of Moses. The Second Vatican Council calls the family “the domestic church” (Lumen Gentium 11), and the Holy Family of Nazareth is its model.

This year's readings put faith at the centre of family life. Abraham, childless and old, is told to look at the stars: “So shall your descendants be.” And “he believed the LORD; and the LORD reckoned it to him as righteousness.” Sarah conceives Isaac at the time God had promised. Hebrews praises their faith: Abraham set out “not knowing where he was going”, and later offered Isaac, trusting that God could raise the dead. A family is built not first on money or plans but on trust in God's promise.

In the Gospel, Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to the Temple to present him to the Lord, as the Law prescribed for the firstborn (Exodus 13:2), offering the gift of the poor, “a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons”. Simeon takes the child in his arms and blesses God; he also tells Mary that her son will be “a sign that will be opposed” and that “a sword will pierce your own soul too”. Even the Holy Family was not spared sorrow. Then they return to Nazareth, where “the child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom”. The hidden years at Nazareth sanctify ordinary family life: work, prayer, obedience and love (CCC 531–533).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, two days ago we knelt before the manger. Today the Church asks us to look around the manger: at Mary, at Joseph, at the family into which God chose to be born.

God could have come in any way he wished. He chose to come as a baby, into a family. He needed a mother to feed him, a father to protect him and teach him a trade, and a home where he could grow. That tells us how much God values the family.

Today's first reading shows Abraham and Sarah, old and childless. God tells Abraham to count the stars: so many will be your descendants. And Abraham believed. A family is not built first on property, jobs or plans, but on faith in God.

In the Gospel, Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to the Temple. They are poor; they can only offer two small birds. Simeon takes the child in his arms and praises God, but also tells Mary that a sword will pierce her heart. Even the Holy Family knew sorrow, fear and uncertainty. If your family is going through hard times, you are not far from Nazareth.

Then the Gospel says simply: they went home to Nazareth, and the child grew, filled with wisdom, and the favour of God was upon him. Thirty quiet years of work, prayer and ordinary love. That is where holiness grows for most of us: at the kitchen table, at work, in patience with each other.

So let us bring our families to the Lord today. Let us pray together at home, forgive each other quickly, respect our elders and bless our children. Jesus, Mary and Joseph, make our homes like yours.

Dear brothers and sisters, on this feast of the Holy Family let us look at the one member who never says a word in the Gospels: St Joseph. Mary speaks, Jesus speaks, angels speak, but Joseph only listens and acts.

Four times an angel speaks to Joseph in a dream, and four times he obeys at once: to take Mary as his wife, to flee to Egypt, to return to the land of Israel, and to settle in Nazareth (Matthew 1:20; 2:13, 19, 22). Each time the Gospel says simply that Joseph got up and did as he was told. In a world full of noise, Joseph teaches us to listen to God in silence and to act without delay.

Joseph was a protector. When Herod sought to kill the child, Joseph took the child and his mother by night and fled to Egypt (Matthew 2:14). Think of that journey: a refugee family, poor, in a foreign land, with a little baby. Joseph worked with his hands to feed them. Today many fathers work far from home, in the Gulf or abroad, to support their families. Joseph understands them.

The Gospel calls Joseph a just man (Matthew 1:19). When he discovered that Mary was with child, he did not want to expose her to public disgrace. His justice was full of mercy. In our families too, justice and tenderness must go together: correction without humiliation, firmness without harshness, forgiveness without weakness.

Fathers, look at Joseph: protect your families, work honestly, pray, bless your children. Mothers, look at Mary. Children, look at Jesus, who was obedient to His parents. And let every family here entrust itself to St Joseph, guardian of the Redeemer. May he who guarded the Holy Family guard ours too.

Dear brothers and sisters, the Second Vatican Council gave the Christian family a beautiful name: the domestic church (Lumen Gentium 11). On the feast of the Holy Family, let us see what this means in practice.

A church is a place where God is worshipped, His Word is heard and love is lived. The home is meant to be exactly that. Parents are the first heralds of the faith for their children (CCC 1656). Long before catechism class, children learn from their parents how to make the sign of the cross, how to fold their hands, how to forgive. Moses told the Israelites to speak of God's words at home and on the road, when lying down and when rising (Deuteronomy 6:7).

The Fourth Commandment teaches children to honour their father and mother, with respect, gratitude, obedience while they are young, and care for them when they are old (CCC 2214–2220; Sirach 3:1–14). It also gives parents duties: to educate their children in faith, in prayer and in virtue, to respect their vocation, and to give the example of a good life (CCC 2221–2226). St Paul writes: fathers, do not provoke your children, or they may lose heart (Colossians 3:21).

Families today are under great pressure: work far from home, financial stress, screens that keep everyone apart in the same room, addiction, broken marriages. The Holy Family also knew danger, poverty and exile. They survived because God was at the centre. Let our families do the same: pray together every evening, even briefly; eat at least one meal a day together without phones; come to the Holy Mass together on Sunday; forgive before sleeping.

And let the parish be a family of families, supporting those who are struggling, single parents, widows, the elderly living alone. May Jesus, Mary and Joseph bless every family in this parish, and make our homes true domestic churches.
