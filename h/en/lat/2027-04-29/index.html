Word for the day

“As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” John 15:9

Reflection

The council in Jerusalem reaches its decision. “After there had been much debate, Peter stood up.” He reminds the assembly that God chose him to bring the Gospel to the Gentiles in the house of Cornelius, and that God “who knows the human heart, testified to them by giving them the Holy Spirit, just as he did to us; and in cleansing their hearts by faith he has made no distinction between them and us.” Then comes the sentence that sums up the whole matter: “we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will.” Salvation is not earned by keeping a list of customs; it is a gift received in faith, for Jew and Gentile alike. The assembly falls silent and listens to Barnabas and Paul tell of the signs God has worked among the nations. Then James, the leader of the church in Jerusalem, speaks. He reads the prophet Amos, “I will rebuild the dwelling of David, which has fallen,” so that “all other peoples may seek the Lord,” and gives his judgment: “we should not trouble those Gentiles who are turning to God,” asking only a few things that would let Jewish and Gentile believers share one table in peace. Peter speaks first, James confirms, the whole assembly listens: this is how the Church discerns. James also honours the Law of Moses, “read aloud every sabbath in the synagogues”; the decision does not despise Israel's heritage but opens its promises to all.

In the Gospel Jesus tells us where the grace that saves us comes from: “As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” The love between the Father and the Son is poured out on the disciples, and the commandments are simply the way to stay in it. And the goal is joy: “I have said these things to you so that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete.” Few saints show this joy on fire more clearly than St Catherine of Siena (1347 to 1380). The twenty-third or twenty-fourth child of a dyer in Siena, she joined the Dominican lay women called the Mantellate, nursed the sick through plague, made peace between feuding families and cities, and, though she had little schooling, dictated The Dialogue and hundreds of letters. She loved the Church so much that she wrote to the Pope as “sweet Christ on earth,” and she also told him plainly to return from Avignon to Rome, which Gregory XI did in 1377. She died in Rome on 29 April 1380, aged thirty-three. Paul VI named her a Doctor of the Church in 1970. She wrote to a disciple (in substance): if you are what you ought to be, you will set the whole of Italy on fire. And she prayed (in substance): eternal Trinity, you are a deep sea; the more I enter, the more I find, and the more I find, the more I seek you.

Full homily

Dear friends, after a long debate in Jerusalem, Peter stands up. He has seen the Holy Spirit fall on the Gentiles in the house of Cornelius. God made no distinction between them and us, he says, cleansing their hearts by faith. And then: we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will (Acts 15:9–11). James confirms it, and the Church opens its doors wide.

Grace means gift. We do not earn God's love by a list of achievements, customs or good works. We receive it, and then our good works flow from it like fruit. The first Christians had to learn this together, and we have to learn it again every day.

Jesus tells us where that grace comes from: as the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love (John 15:9). The love between the Father and the Son is offered to us. His commandments are not a heavy yoke; they are the walls of the house in which we abide in his love. And the purpose is joy: so that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete (John 15:11).

St Catherine of Siena lived in that love. A dyer's daughter with little schooling, she prayed for hours, nursed plague victims, made peace between enemies, and spoke the truth even to the Pope, out of love for the Church. She told her friends (in substance) that if they were what they ought to be, they would set Italy on fire. Let us ask her to pray that we may abide in Christ's love, and let that fire spread. Amen.

For children

Children, St Catherine had more than twenty brothers and sisters! When she was small she loved to pray and to tell Jesus everything. When she grew up she looked after sick people when no one else would, and she even wrote letters to the Pope. Jesus says: I love you as my Father loves me; stay in my love. When we stay close to Jesus, we are full of joy, like Catherine!

Prayer

Father, as you have loved your Son, so he has loved us; keep us in his love. You set St Catherine of Siena on fire with love for Christ crucified and for his Church: grant that, through her prayers, we may be saved by the grace of the Lord Jesus, speak the truth with courage and charity, and find in your love the joy that is complete. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

At the assembly in Jerusalem Peter speaks of grace, Barnabas and Paul tell what God has done among the Gentiles, and James, reading the prophet Amos, proposes the way forward. In the Gospel Jesus says, “Abide in my love”, and gives the reason for all his words: that his joy may be in us and our joy be complete. Psalm 96 calls on every nation to sing to the Lord. Today the Church keeps the memorial of St Catherine of Siena, virgin and Doctor of the Church.

Acts 15:7–21 · No distinction between them and us

Setting.

This is Peter’s last appearance in Acts. He recalls his own experience at the house of Cornelius, when the Holy Spirit fell on Gentiles before they were baptised (10:44–48). After Barnabas and Paul have spoken, James replies. This is James “the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), the leader of the church in Jerusalem (12:17; 21:18), respected for his faithfulness to the Law. His word therefore carries great weight with the believers from the Pharisees.

James quotes Amos 9:11–12 in the Greek form of the Old Testament, which speaks of other peoples seeking the Lord; the Hebrew text speaks of possessing the remnant of Edom. The four things the Gentiles are asked to avoid (v. 20) are, many scholars think, drawn from the rules that Leviticus 17–18 laid down for foreigners living among Israel.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–11: Peter: God gave the Gentiles the Spirit; all are saved by grace.
  • v. 12: Barnabas and Paul tell of signs and wonders.
  • vv. 13–18: James: the prophets agree.
  • vv. 19–21: James proposes not to trouble the Gentiles, but to ask four things of them.

Key words.

  • diakrinō, to make a distinction: In 11:12 the Spirit told Peter to go to Cornelius “not to make a distinction”. Now he says God himself made no distinction (v. 9).
  • charis, grace: God’s unearned favour. Peter’s conclusion (v. 11) is the heart of the matter: Jew and Gentile are saved in the same way, through the grace of the Lord Jesus.
  • skēnē Dauid, the dwelling of David: Literally David’s tent or booth, fallen into ruin. James sees it rebuilt in the risen Son of David, and the Gentiles coming to seek the Lord there. The Gentiles join the restored house of Israel; they do not replace it.

Verse by verse.

“And God, who knows the human heart, testified to them by giving them the Holy Spirit, just as he did to us;” (15:8)

Peter’s argument is not a theory but an event. God had already decided, and the Church must follow.

“and in cleansing their hearts by faith he has made no distinction between them and us.” (15:9)

The real cleanness God seeks is in the heart.

“we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will.” (15:11)

Notice the surprise: not “they will be saved as we are”, but “we will be saved as they are”. All stand before God in the same need of grace.

“we should not trouble those Gentiles who are turning to God,” (15:19)

James listens before he speaks. His proposal protects the Gentiles from burdens and also protects the table fellowship of Jewish and Gentile believers.

How the Church reads it.

On the “yoke” of v. 10: Israel loved the Law as a gift, and Jewish tradition speaks gladly of taking on the yoke of the commandments. Peter is not mocking the Law. His point is that no one is saved by keeping it perfectly, but all are saved by grace. The Catechism teaches that our justification comes from the grace of God (CCC 1996). Verse 21 shows James’s respect for the synagogue where Moses is read every sabbath. The assembly found a way to keep both truths: salvation by grace for all, and charity toward the Jewish believers.

John 15:9–11 · Abide in my love

Setting.

These three verses come straight after the vine. The image now becomes plain speech: to abide in the vine is to abide in his love. There is a chain of love: the Father loves the Son, the Son loves the disciples, and the disciples are to remain in that love.

Structure.

  • v. 9: as the Father has loved me, so I have loved you.
  • v. 10: keeping the commandments is the way of abiding.
  • v. 11: the purpose: his joy in us, our joy complete.

Key words.

  • agapē, love: The self-giving love of God. Here it flows from the Father, through the Son, to us.
  • chara, joy: Joy appears often in the farewell discourse (15:11; 16:20–24; 17:13). It is spoken on the night before the Passion, so it is a joy that suffering cannot take away (16:22).
  • plēroō, to fill, complete: “That your joy may be complete”, filled to the brim. John uses a similar phrase in his first letter (1 John 1:4).

Verse by verse.

“As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” (15:9)

We are loved with the very love that the Father has for the Son. We do not have to earn a place; we are asked to stay.

“If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love,” (15:10)

Obedience is not the price of love but the way to stay in it, as Jesus himself stays in the Father’s love by doing his will.

“so that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be complete.” (15:11)

The commandments are given for joy. A saint is a person whose joy has been made complete.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it teaches that charity, the fruit of the Spirit and fullness of the Law, keeps the commandments of God and of Christ (CCC 1824). Christian moral life is not a list of rules but remaining in a love that comes first from God.

Questions people ask

Why are Christians not bound by circumcision and the food laws of Moses?

Because the apostles, guided by the Spirit, recognised that Gentiles are saved by the grace of Christ without becoming Jews first. This does not mean that the Law was bad or that God has rejected Israel; God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). The moral teaching of the Law, summed up in the Ten Commandments, remains for all.

For the Latin reader

St Catherine of Siena (1347–1380) was one of the youngest children of a large family of a dyer in Siena. She joined the lay Dominican women known as the Mantellate, lived a life of prayer and penance in her own home, nursed the sick and the plague-stricken, and gathered disciples. Though she had little formal schooling, she dictated many letters and her book, the Dialogue. She urged Pope Gregory XI to return from Avignon to Rome, which he did in 1377, and she worked for peace and unity in a divided Church. She died in Rome on 29 April 1380, aged about thirty-three. St Paul VI declared her a Doctor of the Church in 1970, and St John Paul II named her a co-patroness of Europe in 1999. Like the assembly in Acts, she loved the Church enough to speak the truth to it, and she abided in the love of Christ, which today’s Gospel commands.

For study and teaching

  • Peter says that “we will be saved … just as they will”. Do I secretly think that I deserve God’s grace more than others?
  • St Catherine spoke the truth to the Church out of love. Is there a truth I need to say with love, and a love I need to keep while saying it?
