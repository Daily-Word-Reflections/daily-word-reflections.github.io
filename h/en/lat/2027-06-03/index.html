Word for the day

“you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.” Mark 12:30

Reflection

The Book of Tobit now reaches its love story. Young Tobias has set out on a long journey for his blind father, with a companion who calls himself Azariah but is really the angel Raphael. As they approach Ecbatana, Raphael tells him about Sarah, the daughter of their kinsman Raguel: “the girl is sensible, brave, and very beautiful, and her father is a good man.” Tobias is afraid, and with reason: seven husbands have died on their wedding night. But Raphael says: “Do not be afraid, for she was set apart for you before the world was made.” At Raguel's house there are tears of recognition and a warm welcome. Raguel is honest about the danger, yet he gives his daughter in marriage with a blessing: “May the Lord of heaven, my child, guide and prosper you both this night and grant you mercy and peace.” A marriage contract is written. Then comes the wedding night. Before anything else, Tobias rises and says to Sarah: “Sister, get up, and let us pray and implore our Lord that he grant us mercy and safety.” Their prayer is one of the most beautiful in the Bible on marriage. It begins with praise: “Blessed are you, O God of our ancestors, and blessed is your name in all generations forever.” It remembers creation: “You made Adam, and for him you made his wife Eve as a helper and support.” And it offers an honest heart: “I now am taking this kinswoman of mine, not because of lust, but with sincerity. Grant that she and I may find mercy and that we may grow old together.” And together they say: “Amen, Amen.” A marriage that begins on its knees is protected from evil. The Psalm blesses such a home: “Your wife will be like a fruitful vine within your house; your children will be like olive shoots around your table.”

In the Gospel a scribe, impressed by Jesus' answers, asks a sincere question: “Which commandment is the first of all?” Jesus answers with the Shema, the prayer every devout Jew says morning and evening: “Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one; you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.” And he joins to it a second commandment from Leviticus 19:18: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” The scribe agrees warmly and adds that this love is “much more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices.” Jesus looks at him and says: “You are not far from the kingdom of God.” Here a Jewish teacher and Jesus stand together on the foundation of Israel's faith, the love of the one God and of neighbour. Tobias and Sarah lived this double love on their wedding night: love of God first, in prayer, and then a love for each other that is sincere and pure. The martyrs of Uganda lived it to the point of fire. Charles Lwanga, head of the royal pages at the court of Kabaka Mwanga II, protected the boys in his care from the king's sinful demands and taught them the faith. When the persecution broke out, he baptised several catechumens the night before their arrest. On 3 June 1886, at Namugongo, he and many companions were burned alive, praying for their persecutors. Charles is said to have told the executioner that it was as if he were pouring water on him. Some of the pages were barely fourteen. With them died Anglican Christians too, witnesses to the same Lord. St Paul VI canonised the twenty-two Catholic martyrs in 1964, and today the Church of Africa, rich in faith, is their harvest.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's first reading is a wedding story. Young Tobias comes to the house of his kinsman Raguel and asks to marry Sarah. Everyone knows the danger: seven husbands have died on their wedding night. But the angel Raphael has told Tobias: do not be afraid, she was set apart for you before the world was made. So Raguel gives his daughter with a blessing, and a marriage contract is written.

Then comes the wedding night, and what does Tobias do first? He says to Sarah: sister, get up, and let us pray (Tobit 8:4). Before anything else, the two of them stand before God. They praise him, they remember how he made man and woman, and Tobias says: I take her not because of lust, but with sincerity; grant that she and I may grow old together (Tobit 8:7). And Sarah answers: Amen. What a beautiful beginning for a marriage! Love that begins in prayer is protected from evil.

In the Gospel Jesus tells us that the first commandment is to love God with all our heart, soul, mind and strength, and the second is to love our neighbour as ourselves (Mark 12:30–31). Tobias and Sarah did exactly that. First God, then each other. Our families too need this order. When God is first, love between husband and wife, parents and children, is pure and strong.

Today we also remember St Charles Lwanga and his companions, young Christians in Uganda. The king demanded of the palace boys what was sinful, and Charles protected them. When they refused to give up their faith, they were burned alive at Namugongo in 1886. Some of them were only fourteen. They loved God with all their strength, more than their own lives. Let us ask them to give our young people pure and brave hearts, and let us ask Tobias and Sarah to teach our families to pray together. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know the most important rule in the whole world? Jesus told us: love God with all your heart, and love your neighbour as much as you love yourself. Long ago in Uganda some boys your age loved Jesus so much that they would not do something wrong even when the king ordered them. They were very brave. Today, show your love for God by saying a prayer, and your love for others by being kind.

Prayer

O God, who have made the blood of martyrs the seed of Christians, mercifully grant that the field which is your Church, watered by the blood shed by Saints Charles Lwanga and his companions, may be fertile and always yield you an abundant harvest. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Lord, bless all married couples with the prayer of Tobias and Sarah, give our young people pure and brave hearts, and teach us to love you with all our heart and our neighbour as ourselves. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The story of Tobit reaches its wedding night. Guided by Raphael, Tobias comes to the house of Raguel, receives Sarah as his wife, and before anything else, the two of them rise and pray together. In the Gospel a scribe asks Jesus which commandment is the first of all, and Jesus answers with the love of God and the love of neighbour. Psalm 128 blesses the home of the one who fears the Lord. On the memorial of St Charles Lwanga and his companions, young martyrs of Uganda, the readings speak of a love that is pure and faithful to the end.

Tobit 6:10–11; 7:1, 9–16; 8:4–9 (Lectionary 6:10–11; 7:1bcde, 9–17; 8:4–9a) · That we may grow old together

Setting.

Tobit has sent his son Tobias to Media to recover money he left there. A travelling companion has joined him, calling himself Azariah; the reader knows he is the angel Raphael. On the road, Raphael tells Tobias about Sarah, his relative, and says that he has the right to marry her. Tobias is afraid, because seven husbands have died, but Raphael tells him what to do and promises that she was set apart for him before the world was made (6:18).

Background. Marriage within the wider family was the custom for Israel in exile, to keep the people faithful amid foreign nations (compare 4:12). The book’s verse numbers differ between translations, which is why the Lectionary and our Bible give slightly different references.

Structure.

  • 6:10–11: approaching Ecbatana, Raphael tells Tobias about Sarah.
  • 7:1, 9–12: welcome at Raguel’s house; Tobias asks for Sarah; Raguel gives her to him according to the law of Moses.
  • 7:13–16: the marriage contract; the meal; Edna prepares the room and comforts her daughter.
  • 8:4–9: Tobias and Sarah rise and pray; the prayer recalls Adam and Eve; “Amen, Amen.”

Key words.

  • adelphē, sister: Tobias calls his bride “sister” (8:4), and Raguel says “from now on you are her brother and she is your sister” (7:11). The language of the Song of Songs uses the same tender word (Song 4:9).
  • ou dia porneian, not because of lust: Tobias takes Sarah not for selfish desire but “with sincerity”, literally “in truth”. Love in marriage seeks the good of the other.
  • syngērasai, to grow old together: The whole hope of the prayer: not only a wedding night, but a life lived side by side to old age.

Verse by verse.

“Take courage, my daughter; the Lord of heaven grant you joy in place of your sorrow.” (7:16)

Edna weeps, remembering the seven deaths, but she blesses her daughter in faith. A mother’s prayer accompanies the new marriage.

“Sister, get up, and let us pray and implore our Lord that he grant us mercy and safety.” (8:4)

Before they come together, husband and wife stand before God. The marriage begins in prayer.

“You made Adam, and for him you made his wife Eve as a helper and support.” (8:6)

Tobias goes back to the beginning (Genesis 2:18). Their marriage is part of God’s plan from creation.

“Grant that she and I may find mercy and that we may grow old together.” (8:7)

A simple prayer that every couple can make their own.

How the Church reads it.

The Church loves this passage as a picture of Christian marriage. The prayer of Tobias is among the readings offered for the celebration of marriage, and the Catechism quotes it when it speaks of the fidelity and holiness of married love (CCC 2361). Marriage is a gift from God, blessed from the beginning, and protected by prayer against the forces that would destroy it. Raphael’s help shows that God is not absent from the most personal moments of human life.

Mark 12:28–34 · The first commandment of all

Setting.

After the hostile questions of the Pharisees, Herodians and Sadducees, a scribe comes with a sincere question. He has heard Jesus answer well. The teachers of the Law counted many commandments in the Torah (later tradition says 613) and discussed which were the weightiest.

Jesus answers with two texts every Jew knew. The first is the Shema, “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4–5), recited every morning and evening. The second is from Leviticus 19:18: “you shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

Structure.

  • v. 28: the scribe’s question: which commandment is first of all?
  • vv. 29–31: Jesus: the Shema and the love of neighbour; no commandment is greater.
  • vv. 32–33: the scribe agrees: this is more than all burnt offerings and sacrifices.
  • v. 34: “You are not far from the kingdom of God.”

Key words.

  • Shema, hear: The first word of Deuteronomy 6:4. To hear, in Hebrew, is also to obey. The love of God begins with listening.
  • agapēseis, you shall love: A command to love: not a passing feeling, but a choice of the whole person, heart, soul, mind and strength.
  • ho plēsion, the neighbour: Literally “the one near”. In Leviticus it means first a fellow Israelite; Jesus will show, in Luke’s parable of the Good Samaritan, that the neighbour is anyone in need.

Verse by verse.

“Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one;” (12:29)

Jesus begins with faith in the one God. Love of neighbour grows from this root.

“you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.” (12:30)

Mark’s form has four words where the Hebrew of Deuteronomy has three, adding “mind”. God asks for the whole person, including our thinking.

“You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” (12:31)

The two commandments are distinct but never separated. Jesus joins them into one answer.

“You are not far from the kingdom of God.” (12:34)

The scribe is praised. In Mark, this is a warm word to a teacher of the Law, a reminder that not all the scribes opposed Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the Ten Commandments with this Gospel: Jesus sums up the whole Law in the double commandment of love (CCC 2055). The first three commandments concern the love of God and the other seven the love of neighbour (CCC 2067). The Second Vatican Council taught that love of God and of neighbour is the first and greatest commandment, and that the two cannot be separated (Gaudium et Spes 24, in substance).

Questions people ask

The scribe says love is more than sacrifices. Does this make the Mass less important?

No. The scribe, like the prophets (Hosea 6:6), means that worship without love is empty. Jesus himself offers the perfect sacrifice of love on the Cross, made present in the Mass. That is why the Mass is the source of the love the commandment asks for, and why we are sent from it to love our neighbour.

For the Latin reader

St Charles Lwanga and his twenty-one companions were young Catholic men of the royal court of Buganda, in present-day Uganda. Under King Mwanga II they refused to give up their faith and to submit to the king’s demands against chastity. Between 1885 and 1887 they were put to death, most of them burned alive at Namugongo on 3 June 1886. Anglican Christians died with them. Pope St Paul VI canonised them in 1964 and in 1969 became the first pope to visit Africa, at Kampala. The shrine at Namugongo draws great crowds of pilgrims every year on this day.
