Word for the day

“My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish.” John 10:27–28

Reflection

After the martyrdom of Stephen a persecution broke out in Jerusalem, and many believers fled. What looked like a disaster became a sowing. Those who were scattered “traveled as far as Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Antioch,” and wherever they went they spoke the word. At first they spoke only to fellow Jews. Then some men from Cyprus and Cyrene, whose names we do not even know, did something new at Antioch: they “spoke to the Hellenists also, proclaiming the Lord Jesus.” Here the word means Greek-speaking Gentiles. And “the hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number became believers and turned to the Lord.” The first great mission to the Gentiles was not planned by a committee. It was begun by refugees who could not keep silent about Jesus.

The Church in Jerusalem sends Barnabas to see what is happening. Luke gives him one of the finest descriptions in the New Testament: “he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” When he arrives, he does not look for faults. He “saw the grace of God” and “he rejoiced.” He encourages them to stay faithful. Then he does something humble and wise: he goes to Tarsus to fetch Saul, a converted persecutor whom many still feared, and brings him to Antioch. For a whole year they teach together. And “it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called ‘Christians.’” The name means “Christ's people,” the people who belong to the Anointed One. Antioch, a great city of many nations, became the mother church of the missions. The Psalm sings of Zion as the mother of all peoples: “Glorious things are spoken of you, O city of God,” and of people from Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre and Ethiopia, it says, “This one was born there.” In the Church, every nation is born again.

The Gospel takes us to the Temple in winter, at the festival of the Dedication, the feast we know as Hanukkah, which remembers the rededication of the Temple after the Maccabees. Jesus is walking in the portico of Solomon. Some of the Temple crowd gather around him: “How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.” Jesus answers that his works already speak. And then he gives one of the most consoling promises in the Gospel: “My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one will snatch them out of my hand.” The security of the sheep does not rest on their strength but on the hand that holds them, and that hand is one with the Father's: “no one can snatch it out of the Father's hand. The Father and I are one.” At the feast that celebrated the dedication of the Temple, Jesus reveals himself as the true dwelling of God among us. The Church has always heard in these words the mystery of the Holy Trinity: the Son is one in being with the Father.

Full homily

Dear friends, after Stephen was killed, the believers ran away from Jerusalem. It looked like the end. But Luke tells us that wherever they went, they spoke about Jesus. They were scattered like seed, and the seed grew.

In Antioch some unnamed believers did something new: they told the Greeks about the Lord Jesus. And the hand of the Lord was with them (Acts 11:21). Many of us, too, have been scattered, to the Gulf, to Europe, to other states. God can use our scattering to sow his word.

Then the Church sends Barnabas. Luke says he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith. When he saw the grace of God, he rejoiced (Acts 11:23). He did not complain that things were done without his permission. He rejoiced, and he encouraged.

And he went to look for Saul, a man others were afraid of. He gave him a place. Together they taught for a whole year, and in Antioch the disciples were first called Christians. That is our name too. Do we live up to it?

In the Gospel Jesus says: my sheep hear my voice; I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one will snatch them out of my hand (John 10:27–28). Whatever storms we face, we are held. And the hand that holds us is the Father's hand, for Jesus says: the Father and I are one.

Today let us be Barnabas for someone: let us see grace and rejoice. And let us rest in the hand of the Shepherd, from which no one can snatch us. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says he holds his sheep in his hand, so tightly that no one can take them away. You are in Jesus' hand!

At Antioch the friends of Jesus were first called Christians. That is your name too. Let us say: Jesus, I belong to you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Good Shepherd, one with the Father, you know your sheep and give them eternal life, and no one can snatch them out of your hand. Make us worthy of the name Christian, which we have received from you. Give us the courage of the scattered disciples who spoke of you wherever they went, and the generous heart of Barnabas, who rejoiced to see your grace at work in others. Keep us in your hand in every danger, and bring us at last into the joy of the Father, with whom you live and reign in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church moves outward. Believers scattered by persecution reach Antioch, and some unnamed men from Cyprus and Cyrene speak of the Lord Jesus to Greeks; there the disciples are first called Christians. In the Temple at the feast of Dedication, Jesus says that no one can snatch his sheep out of his hand: “The Father and I are one.” Psalm 87 sees Zion as the mother city of all peoples, where even foreigners are counted as born.

Acts 11:19–26 · First called Christians

Setting.

Luke picks up a thread left at 8:4: the persecution after the death of Stephen scattered the believers, and they carried the word with them. Antioch on the Orontes, capital of the Roman province of Syria, was one of the largest cities of the empire, with a large Jewish community and many Gentiles drawn to the synagogue. Here, for the first time on a large scale, the Gospel is preached to Gentiles, and by ordinary believers whose names we are not told.

Structure.

  • v. 19: the scattered believers speak to Jews only.
  • vv. 20–21: men of Cyprus and Cyrene speak to Greeks; many believe.
  • vv. 22–24: Jerusalem sends Barnabas; he sees grace and rejoices.
  • vv. 25–26: Barnabas brings Saul; a year of teaching; the name “Christians”.

Key words.

  • Hellēnistas / Hellēnas, Hellenists or Greeks: A real textual question. Many manuscripts read “Hellenists”, the word used in 6:1 for Greek-speaking Jews; others read “Greeks”. The contrast with v. 19 (“no one except Jews”) shows that Luke means Gentiles, and many translations, the Malayalam POC among them, say “Greeks”.
  • parakaleō, to exhort, to encourage: Barnabas’ name was explained as “son of encouragement” (4:36), and here he does what his name says: he “exhorted” (parekalei) them all to remain faithful. The man sent to inspect finds grace and encourages it.
  • Christianoi, Christians: A name formed like those given to the followers or household of a leader (compare “Herodians”, Mark 3:6). Many scholars think outsiders coined it. What others noticed about the disciples was that their life was about Christ.

Verse by verse.

“The hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number became believers and turned to the Lord.” (11:21)

The preachers are nameless; the hand is the Lord’s. In Acts the growth of the Church is always first the Lord’s work.

“When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced,” (11:23)

A model for anyone sent to look at something new in the Church: look first for grace, and when you find it, rejoice.

“it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called “Christians.”” (11:26)

Antioch became one of the great Churches of the ancient world. St Ignatius, its bishop early in the second century, is the first writer known to us who calls the Church “catholic” (Letter to the Smyrnaeans 8).

How the Church reads it.

The Church in Antioch shows what Easter does: persecution scatters, and the scattering becomes a sowing. The Church in Jerusalem does not shut down the new work; it sends a wise and generous man to strengthen it. Barnabas then goes to look for Saul, whom many still feared (9:26–27), and puts him to work. Much of the Church’s mission depends on people like Barnabas, who notice the gifts of others.

John 10:22–30 · No one will snatch them out of my hand

Setting.

The feast of Dedication (Hanukkah) recalls the cleansing and rededication of the Temple by Judas Maccabeus in 164 BC, after Antiochus IV had profaned it (1 Maccabees 4:36–59). It falls in December, so “It was winter”. Jesus walks in the portico of Solomon, the covered colonnade on the east side of the Temple court where the first believers would later gather (Acts 5:12). At the feast of the Temple’s consecration, Jesus will speak of himself as the one “whom the Father has sanctified and sent” (10:36).

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: the setting and the demand: “tell us plainly”.
  • vv. 25–26: works that testify, and unbelief.
  • vv. 27–29: the sheep hear, are known, follow, receive eternal life, and are held.
  • v. 30: “The Father and I are one.”

Key words.

  • enkainia, Dedication, renewal: The Greek name of the feast means “renewal” or “inauguration”. The Temple renewed by the Maccabees points to the new Temple, the body of Jesus (2:21).
  • harpazō, to snatch: The wolf “snatches” the sheep (10:12); no one can snatch them out of the hand of Jesus or of the Father. The believer’s security rests not on his own grip on God but on God’s grip on him.
  • hen, one (neuter): Jesus does not say “one person” (masculine heis) but “one” in the neuter (hen): one in being and in work, while remaining Father and Son.

Verse by verse.

“How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.” (10:24)

“The Jews” here are the Temple authorities and those who sided with them, as often in John (compare 9:22); Jesus and his disciples were Jews too. Jesus answers that his works have already spoken.

“My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me.” (10:27)

Three short sentences make a whole spiritual life: listening, being known, following.

“The Father and I are one.” (10:30)

St Augustine notes that “one” answers Arius, who made the Son less than the Father, and “are” answers Sabellius, who made Father and Son a single person (in substance). The Church’s faith holds both: one God, and distinct Persons (CCC 253–254).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this Gospel in the Easter season because the risen Shepherd keeps his promise: no one can snatch his own from his hand, not even death. This assurance is not presumption; it is hope resting on the faithfulness of God (CCC 1821). The verse “The Father and I are one” became a key text in the fourth-century defense of the true divinity of the Son, whom the Creed confesses as “consubstantial with the Father”.

Questions people ask

Does “No one will snatch them out of my hand” mean that a believer can never be lost?

No power outside us can snatch us from Christ. What remains is our own ability to stop listening and following. So the Church speaks of final perseverance as a grace we hope for and ask for, trusting in God’s faithfulness (CCC 2016).

For study and teaching

  • Like Barnabas, do I look first for the grace of God in what is new and unfamiliar in the Church? Whose gifts could I encourage?
  • Which of the three, hearing his voice, being known, following, is weakest in me now?
