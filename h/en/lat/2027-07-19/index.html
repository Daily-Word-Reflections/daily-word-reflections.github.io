Word for the day

“But Moses said to the people, “Do not be afraid, stand firm, and see the deliverance that the Lord will accomplish for you today; for the Egyptians whom you see today you shall never see again. The Lord will fight for you, and you have only to keep still.”” Exodus 14:13–14

Reflection

Israel has walked out of slavery on the night of the Passover, and for a few days it seems that the story has ended happily. Then comes the news in Pharaoh's palace: the people “had fled,” and “the minds of Pharaoh and his officials were changed.” “What have we done, letting Israel leave our service?” The king takes “six hundred picked chariots and all the other chariots of Egypt,” the most advanced army of the age, and goes after a crowd of ex-slaves, women, children and flocks. The Israelites are camped by the sea, at Pi-hahiroth, in front of Baal-zephon, and when they look back they see the dust of the chariots. “In great fear the Israelites cried out to the Lord.” That is a good prayer. But the next words are not: they turn on Moses. “Was it because there were no graves in Egypt that you have taken us away to die in the wilderness?” Fear can make a person forget that slavery was slavery; “it would have been better for us to serve the Egyptians,” they say. Moses answers with three short commands: “Do not be afraid, stand firm, and see the deliverance that the Lord will accomplish for you today.” Not only the Lord's strength but his timing is announced: “today.” “The Lord will fight for you, and you have only to keep still.” To keep still does not mean to do nothing. Immediately the Lord says to Moses, “Why do you cry out to me? Tell the Israelites to go forward.” There is a time to stand and trust and a time to move; faith is knowing which is which, and trusting that the God who opens a way will also tell us when to step on it. The Lord adds that he will “gain glory for myself over Pharaoh,” so that “the Egyptians shall know that I am the Lord.” The reading stops before the sea opens; the psalm gives us the answer in advance, the song Israel sang on the far shore: “I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea.” Jewish tradition keeps a gentle comment on this song: an old rabbinic story says that when the angels wished to sing as the Egyptians drowned, God said, “My creatures are drowning in the sea, and you would sing?” Even in victory, the God of Israel does not rejoice in the death of any human being. The Church sings this canticle at the Easter Vigil, because the sea is also a picture of baptism, and St Paul says that our ancestors “were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:2).

In the Gospel, some scribes and Pharisees say to Jesus, “Teacher, we wish to see a sign from you.” They have just watched him heal a man who could not see or speak (Matthew 12:22), and they have heard his teaching on the kingdom. A sign for them means a proof they can examine on their own terms. Jesus answers sharply: “An evil and adulterous generation asks for a sign.” In the prophets, “adulterous” is the word used for a people that is unfaithful to the covenant, as a spouse is unfaithful to a spouse; it is not an insult against a race. Jesus is himself a son of Israel, and he speaks as the prophets spoke to their own people, with grief and love. The Church has always taught that what was done in his Passion cannot be charged to all the Jews of that time, still less to the Jews of our own day (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 597). The only sign that will be given is “the sign of the prophet Jonah.” “For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth.” Jesus points to his own death and rising, the sign above all signs. He names two figures from outside the circle of those who asked. The people of Nineveh, a pagan city, “repented at the proclamation of Jonah”; the queen of the South, a foreign monarch, “came from the ends of the earth to listen to the wisdom of Solomon.” Each of them recognised God's word when it came in a modest and costly way. “And see, something greater than Jonah is here! … something greater than Solomon is here!” In the first reading Moses stood by the sea and said, “Stand firm”; here the One greater than Moses stands among the people and gives himself as the sign. At the Red Sea God opened a path through the waters of death for Israel; in the Resurrection of Jesus he opens a path through death itself for the whole human race.

Full homily

Dear friends, picture the scene. Israel has just left Egypt. They are camped by the sea, and then the dust cloud appears behind them: Pharaoh's six hundred chariots. In front of them the water, behind them the army. They cry out to the Lord, and then they turn on Moses: was it because there were no graves in Egypt? How quickly fear makes us forget that slavery was slavery. Have we not also spoken that way when things get difficult, wishing that we were back in the old life, even the old sin?

Moses says three things: do not be afraid, stand firm, and see the deliverance that the Lord will accomplish for you today (Exodus 14:13). The Lord will fight for you, and you have only to keep still. But look what the Lord says next: why do you cry out to me? Tell the Israelites to go forward. There is a time for stillness, a time for prayer, and then a time to walk into the water. God does not do for us what we can do with his help. He made a way through the sea, but the people had to put their feet on it.

In the Gospel some people ask Jesus for a sign: show us something so that we can believe. Jesus has healed and taught, and still they ask for more. He answers that only one sign will be given, the sign of Jonah: as Jonah spent three days in the belly of the sea monster, so the Son of Man will spend three days in the heart of the earth. Jesus is saying: my death and my Resurrection are the sign. Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching; the queen of the South travelled the world to hear Solomon. And something greater than Jonah, something greater than Solomon, is here.

Let us not keep asking for more proof while the Lord himself stands among us. At every Mass he gives himself to us, risen and alive. Today let us name our sea, whatever frightens us, and say with Moses: the Lord will fight for me. And then let us take one step forward. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago God's people were running away from a cruel king. They came to a big sea, and the king's soldiers were behind them! The people were very scared. But Moses said: do not be afraid! God will help us. And God opened the sea, and the people walked right through on dry ground! When you are scared, remember: God can make a path where there is no path. Say a little prayer, and then do the brave thing.

Prayer

Lord God, you led your people out of Egypt with a mighty hand and made a path for them through the sea. When we are afraid, give us Moses' faith: to stand firm, to keep still before you, and to go forward when you say go. Lord Jesus, you gave us the sign of Jonah, your death and Resurrection; through the waters of baptism lead us out of every slavery into your freedom. Make us people who sing your praise even when the way is not yet open. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

These weeks the first reading follows the Exodus. Today Pharaoh changes his mind and chases the people to the edge of the sea. Trapped between the water and the chariots, they cry out in fear, and Moses answers: “Do not be afraid, stand firm, and see the deliverance that the Lord will accomplish for you today.” In the Gospel some scribes and Pharisees ask Jesus for a sign, and he points them to Jonah, and to “something greater than Jonah”. Both readings speak of God who saves when every road seems closed. The responsorial psalm is the song of Moses that follows the crossing (Exodus 15).

Exodus 14:5–18 · Stand firm and see the Lord’s deliverance

Setting.

Israel has left Egypt after the death of the firstborn, led by the pillar of cloud and fire (13:17–22). God has told Moses to turn back and camp by the sea, so that Pharaoh will think the people are lost in the wilderness (14:1–4). The reading begins when Pharaoh hears that they have gone and regrets it. The places named, Pi-hahiroth and Baal-zephon, cannot be located with certainty today. What the story wants the reader to feel is plain: the people are cornered, with the sea in front and the army behind.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–9: Pharaoh’s change of heart; he gathers six hundred picked chariots and overtakes the camp.
  • vv. 10–12: the people cry out to the Lord, then turn on Moses and say it was better in Egypt.
  • vv. 13–14: Moses answers: do not be afraid; the Lord will fight for you.
  • vv. 15–18: the Lord tells Moses to go forward, to lift his staff over the sea, and promises glory over Pharaoh.

Key words.

  • ḥāzaq, to harden, to make strong: In v. 8 the Lord “hardened” Pharaoh’s heart. The Hebrew verb means to make firm or strong, and in the Exodus story Pharaoh’s own stubbornness and God’s hand are both spoken of (compare 8:15). The Church reads it as a mystery of human responsibility, not as God forcing a person to sin.
  • yĕšûʿâ, deliverance, salvation: From the same root as the name Jesus (Yeshua). Moses promises that the people will “see” it. Here the word means rescue from an enemy; the New Testament will give it its widest meaning.
  • ḥāraš, to keep still: In v. 14 the people are told they “have only to keep still”: not to do nothing, but to stop panicking and let God act, while v. 15 at once tells them to go forward.

Verse by verse.

“Was it because there were no graves in Egypt that you have taken us away to die in the wilderness?” (14:11)

Fear speaks bitterly. The people forget the slavery they cried out against and remember only that Egypt was familiar. The same temptation returns again and again in the wilderness.

“Do not be afraid, stand firm, and see the deliverance that the Lord will accomplish for you today;” (14:13)

Moses gives three commands in a row: do not fear, stand firm, look. Faith here is trust before any proof appears.

“The Lord will fight for you, and you have only to keep still.” (14:14)

Israel is not asked to defeat Pharaoh. The victory belongs to the Lord, and a saying from Israel’s tradition of the Lord’s battles becomes here a saying of trust.

“Why do you cry out to me? Tell the Israelites to go forward.” (14:15)

Prayer is not enough on its own at this moment. God asks the people to take the step, and then he opens the way. Trust and action go together.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the crossing of the sea in the light of Baptism. The Catechism teaches that the passage of the Red Sea, which was literally Israel’s liberation from the slavery of Egypt, announces the liberation wrought by Baptism (CCC 1221). St Paul already says that our ancestors “were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:1–2). For this reason the Exodus crossing is always read at the Easter Vigil. The text also speaks to every believer who stands between a danger and a sea: God does not remove the sea, but he opens a way through it.

Matthew 12:38–42 · The sign of Jonah

Setting.

Jesus has just healed a man who was blind and mute, and some Pharisees have said he drives out demons by the prince of demons (12:22–37). Now some scribes and Pharisees (not all of them, as Matthew writes “some”) ask for a sign. Jesus has done many signs already. What they want is one that settles the question on their terms, and he refuses it.

Matthew’s Gospel is addressed first to a community of Jewish followers of Jesus, and its sharp words against his opponents belong to a family argument inside Israel. They are not a verdict on the Jewish people, then or now (CCC 597).

Structure.

  • vv. 38–39: the request for a sign; Jesus calls this generation “evil and adulterous” and promises only the sign of Jonah.
  • v. 40: Jonah’s three days and nights, and the Son of Man in the heart of the earth.
  • v. 41: the people of Nineveh repented; something greater than Jonah is here.
  • v. 42: the queen of the South came to hear Solomon; something greater than Solomon is here.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: In Matthew a sign is not a wonder that forces belief but a pointer that asks for faith. The sign Jesus gives is his own death and rising.
  • moichalis, adulterous: In the prophets, such as Hosea, Israel’s unfaithfulness to the Lord is called adultery, because Israel was bound to him by covenant as a bride. Jesus uses that language of unfaithful hearts; it is not about the sin of any particular person.
  • pleion, something greater: The Greek is neuter: not “someone” but “something” greater. Jesus claims more than a prophet and more than a king: he himself is the place where God’s word and wisdom are present.

Verse by verse.

“An evil and adulterous generation asks for a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of the prophet Jonah.” (12:39)

The sign is Jonah’s deliverance from the sea monster, which Jesus applies to his own death and rising. It is a sign given by God, not at the demand of the questioners.

“so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth.” (12:40)

“Three days and three nights” is a way of speaking of a short time counted inclusively, as in Esther 4:16 and 5:1, and agrees with the Church’s teaching that Christ rose on the third day.

“because they repented at the proclamation of Jonah, and see, something greater than Jonah is here!” (12:41)

The Gentiles of Nineveh listened to a reluctant prophet. How much more should God’s own people listen to the one who is more than a prophet.

“because she came from the ends of the earth to listen to the wisdom of Solomon,” (12:42)

The queen of the South is the Queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10:1–10). She travelled a long way for wisdom. Jesus says that the Wisdom of God stands here, and the people can hear him without any journey.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers saw in Jonah’s three days in the fish a figure of the burial and resurrection of Christ, and Jesus himself offers this reading here. The Church carries the same pattern into Baptism: going down into the water and rising to new life, as the Catechism says of the sacrament (CCC 1214, 1227). At every Mass the Church also proclaims the death and rising of the Lord as the one sign given to the world. The sharp words of this Gospel are a warning to every believer who asks for proof instead of trust, not a condemnation of a people.

Questions people ask

Why did God harden Pharaoh’s heart, and was Pharaoh responsible for his own choice?

The book of Exodus speaks in two ways: Pharaoh hardens his own heart, and the Lord hardens it. Many commentators read this as the Bible’s way of saying that God is sovereign even over human refusal, while the refusal remains Pharaoh’s own. The Church teaches that God never causes sin and that every person is responsible for his or her own choices. The story’s main point is not Pharaoh’s fate but that the Lord, not Egypt’s army, decides Israel’s future.

For study and teaching

  • Moses told the frightened people, “Do not be afraid, stand firm.” Where in my life do I need to stand firm and also to take the next step?
  • Jesus is the “something greater” than Jonah and Solomon. What do I still look for before I am ready to trust him?
