Word for the day

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” John 6:40

Reflection

After celebrating the saints in glory, the Church turns to all the faithful departed who are still being purified. Today is a day of prayer, not of fear. We pray for our dead because we believe that love does not end at the grave, and that the communion of saints joins the living and the dead in one body.

The Church teaches that those who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are assured of their eternal salvation, but undergo purification after death to attain the holiness needed to enter the joy of heaven (CCC 1030). This final purification the Church calls Purgatory. It is not a second chance and not a lesser hell; it is the fire of God's love completing what was unfinished.

Scripture already shows the practice of praying for the dead. Judas Maccabeus made atonement for the fallen soldiers, “so that they might be delivered from their sin” (2 Maccabees 12:45). From the beginning the Church has honoured the memory of the dead and offered prayers, above all the Eucharistic sacrifice, for them, as well as almsgiving, indulgences and works of penance (CCC 1032).

The readings ground all this in hope. “The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God” (Wisdom 3:1). “Hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts” (Romans 5:5). And Jesus himself says that it is the Father's will that he should lose nothing of all that has been given to him. Our grief is real, but it is the grief of those who have hope.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, yesterday we celebrated the saints in heaven. Today we pray for all who have died, especially our own families: parents, grandparents, brothers and sisters, friends.

Death is painful. Even Jesus wept at the grave of Lazarus. The Church does not ask us to hide our grief. But she gives us a hope that changes it. The Book of Wisdom says: “The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God.” Our dead are not lost; they are held.

Why do we pray for them? Because many of us die not yet fully ready to see God face to face. The Church teaches that there is a final purification, Purgatory. It is not a place of despair but of hope; those there are certain of heaven. It is like gold refined in the fire. And our love can help them: our prayers, our sacrifices, and above all the Holy Mass.

St Monica, dying far from home, said to her son Augustine: “Lay this body anywhere. Only this I ask: remember me at the altar of the Lord.” That is what we do today. We bring our dead to the altar.

And Jesus gives us his promise: “This is the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” Our faith is not only that the soul lives on. We believe in the resurrection of the body. The graves we visit are resting places, waiting for that morning.

So this week, visit the cemetery, light a candle, pray the rosary for the dead, and have a Mass offered. And let us also live in such a way that we are ready when our time comes. Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.

Remembering, praying, preparing: three ways love goes on.

Our cemeteries are not places of despair but of waiting.

Take-home resolution: This month I will pray for my dead every day and make my own peace with God.

Dear brothers and sisters, today our cemeteries are full of flowers and candles. Families come to clean the graves, to pray and to remember. The whole month of November is dedicated to the holy souls. Today let us think about three things we do for our dead: we remember, we pray and we prepare.

First, we remember with gratitude. Our parents, grandparents, brothers and sisters, friends and priests who have died gave us so much: life itself, the faith, love, sacrifices we may never know. St Paul wrote that he thanked God every time he remembered his friends (Philippians 1:3). Gratitude heals grief. Tell their stories to your children, so that the next generation knows where its faith came from.

Second, we pray with hope. The Book of Wisdom says that the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God (Wisdom 3:1). Jesus said that it is the will of the Father that He should lose nothing of all that has been given Him, but raise it up on the last day (John 6:39). Those who have died in God's friendship but are not yet fully purified can be helped by our prayers, especially by the Holy Mass. So have the Holy Mass offered for your dead, pray the Rosary for them, give alms in their name.

Third, we prepare our own departure. Every grave preaches a silent sermon: one day it will be my turn. The psalm prays: teach us to count our days, that we may gain a wise heart (Psalm 90:12). Do not wait to make peace with God and with others. Make a good confession this month. Forgive the person you have not forgiven.

Then our cemeteries will not be places of despair, but places of waiting, gardens where seeds have been sown for the resurrection. Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.

Homily option 3 · Teaching homily: purgatory, indulgences and prayer for the dead

Prayer for the dead is an ancient practice of the Church and an act of mercy.

The Church's teaching is full of hope: God's love purifies and saves.

Take-home resolution: This week I will go to confession and gain an indulgence for one of my dead.

Dear brothers and sisters, on All Souls' Day it is good to understand clearly what the Church teaches, because there is much confusion about purgatory and indulgences.

The Catechism says that all who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven. The Church gives the name purgatory to this final purification (CCC 1030–1031). It is not a second chance for those who rejected God, and it is not hell. It is the completion of love, the fire of God's love burning away what is still selfish in us. St Paul speaks of a person being saved, but only as through fire (1 Corinthians 3:15).

From the beginning the Church has honoured the memory of the dead and offered prayers for them. Judas Maccabeus made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin (2 Maccabees 12:46). Above all the Church offers the Eucharistic sacrifice for them, and also commends almsgiving, indulgences and works of penance (CCC 1032).

What is an indulgence? When our sins are forgiven in confession, the guilt is taken away, but there can remain what the Church calls temporal punishment, the wounds and disorder that sin leaves. An indulgence is a remission of that temporal punishment, granted through the Church from the treasury of the merits of Christ and the saints (CCC 1471, 1476–1477). Indulgences can be applied to the dead (CCC 1479). The usual conditions are sacramental confession, Holy Communion, prayer for the intentions of the Pope, and detachment from all sin. In the first days of November the Church grants a plenary indulgence, applicable to the dead, to those who visit a cemetery and pray for the departed.

So this is not a dry doctrine; it is a work of mercy. Our dead cannot help themselves, but we can help them. Go to confession this week, receive Communion, visit the cemetery and pray for your dead. And may the souls of the faithful departed, through the mercy of God, rest in peace.
