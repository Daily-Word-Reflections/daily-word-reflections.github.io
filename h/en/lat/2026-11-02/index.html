Word for the day

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” John 6:40

Reflection

After celebrating the saints in glory, the Church turns to all the faithful departed who are still being purified. Today is a day of prayer, not of fear. We pray for our dead because we believe that love does not end at the grave, and that the communion of saints joins the living and the dead in one body.

The Church teaches that those who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are assured of their eternal salvation, but undergo purification after death to attain the holiness needed to enter the joy of heaven (CCC 1030). This final purification the Church calls Purgatory. It is not a second chance and not a lesser hell; it is the fire of God's love completing what was unfinished.

Scripture already shows the practice of praying for the dead. Judas Maccabeus made atonement for the fallen soldiers, “so that they might be delivered from their sin” (2 Maccabees 12:45). From the beginning the Church has honoured the memory of the dead and offered prayers, above all the Eucharistic sacrifice, for them, as well as almsgiving, indulgences and works of penance (CCC 1032).

The readings ground all this in hope. “The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God” (Wisdom 3:1). “Hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts” (Romans 5:5). And Jesus himself says that it is the Father's will that he should lose nothing of all that has been given to him. Our grief is real, but it is the grief of those who have hope.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, yesterday we celebrated the saints in heaven. Today we pray for all who have died, especially our own families: parents, grandparents, brothers and sisters, friends.

Death is painful. Even Jesus wept at the grave of Lazarus. The Church does not ask us to hide our grief. But she gives us a hope that changes it. The Book of Wisdom says: “The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God.” Our dead are not lost; they are held.

Why do we pray for them? Because many of us die not yet fully ready to see God face to face. The Church teaches that there is a final purification, Purgatory. It is not a place of despair but of hope; those there are certain of heaven. It is like gold refined in the fire. And our love can help them: our prayers, our sacrifices, and above all the Holy Mass.

St Monica, dying far from home, said to her son Augustine: “Lay this body anywhere. Only this I ask: remember me at the altar of the Lord.” That is what we do today. We bring our dead to the altar.

And Jesus gives us his promise: “This is the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” Our faith is not only that the soul lives on. We believe in the resurrection of the body. The graves we visit are resting places, waiting for that morning.

So this week, visit the cemetery, light a candle, pray the rosary for the dead, and have a Mass offered. And let us also live in such a way that we are ready when our time comes. Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.

Remembering, praying, preparing: three ways love goes on.

Our cemeteries are not places of despair but of waiting.

Take-home resolution: This month I will pray for my dead every day and make my own peace with God.

Dear brothers and sisters, today our cemeteries are full of flowers and candles. Families come to clean the graves, to pray and to remember. The whole month of November is dedicated to the holy souls. Today let us think about three things we do for our dead: we remember, we pray and we prepare.

First, we remember with gratitude. Our parents, grandparents, brothers and sisters, friends and priests who have died gave us so much: life itself, the faith, love, sacrifices we may never know. St Paul wrote that he thanked God every time he remembered his friends (Philippians 1:3). Gratitude heals grief. Tell their stories to your children, so that the next generation knows where its faith came from.

Second, we pray with hope. The Book of Wisdom says that the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God (Wisdom 3:1). Jesus said that it is the will of the Father that He should lose nothing of all that has been given Him, but raise it up on the last day (John 6:39). Those who have died in God's friendship but are not yet fully purified can be helped by our prayers, especially by the Holy Mass. So have the Holy Mass offered for your dead, pray the Rosary for them, give alms in their name.

Third, we prepare our own departure. Every grave preaches a silent sermon: one day it will be my turn. The psalm prays: teach us to count our days, that we may gain a wise heart (Psalm 90:12). Do not wait to make peace with God and with others. Make a good confession this month. Forgive the person you have not forgiven.

Then our cemeteries will not be places of despair, but places of waiting, gardens where seeds have been sown for the resurrection. Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.

Prayer for the dead is an ancient practice of the Church and an act of mercy.

The Church's teaching is full of hope: God's love purifies and saves.

Take-home resolution: This week I will go to confession and gain an indulgence for one of my dead.

Dear brothers and sisters, on All Souls' Day it is good to understand clearly what the Church teaches, because there is much confusion about purgatory and indulgences.

The Catechism says that all who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven. The Church gives the name purgatory to this final purification (CCC 1030–1031). It is not a second chance for those who rejected God, and it is not hell. It is the completion of love, the fire of God's love burning away what is still selfish in us. St Paul speaks of a person being saved, but only as through fire (1 Corinthians 3:15).

From the beginning the Church has honoured the memory of the dead and offered prayers for them. Judas Maccabeus made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin (2 Maccabees 12:46). Above all the Church offers the Eucharistic sacrifice for them, and also commends almsgiving, indulgences and works of penance (CCC 1032).

What is an indulgence? When our sins are forgiven in confession, the guilt is taken away, but there can remain what the Church calls temporal punishment, the wounds and disorder that sin leaves. An indulgence is a remission of that temporal punishment, granted through the Church from the treasury of the merits of Christ and the saints (CCC 1471, 1476–1477). Indulgences can be applied to the dead (CCC 1479). The usual conditions are sacramental confession, Holy Communion, prayer for the intentions of the Pope, and detachment from all sin. In the first days of November the Church grants a plenary indulgence, applicable to the dead, to those who visit a cemetery and pray for the departed.

So this is not a dry doctrine; it is a work of mercy. Our dead cannot help themselves, but we can help them. Go to confession this week, receive Communion, visit the cemetery and pray for your dead. And may the souls of the faithful departed, through the mercy of God, rest in peace.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On All Souls the Church prays for all who have died in Christ and are still being purified. The readings give the reasons for this prayer and for our hope. Wisdom says that the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, even when their death looks like defeat. Psalm 23 walks with the believer through the darkest valley to a table and a house. Romans grounds our hope in the love God has already shown: Christ died for us while we were still sinners. In John, Jesus says that the Father’s will is that he should lose nothing of all that has been given him, but raise it up on the last day.

The line runs from the hand of God, to the Shepherd’s staff, to the poured-out love of the Spirit, to the promise of resurrection. The key words are hand, hope and raising up.

Wisdom 3:1–9 · The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God

Setting.

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, probably in Alexandria in Egypt, in the century before Christ. Its author, a devout Jewish teacher, speaks in the name of Solomon, the model of wisdom. He writes for Jews living in a Greek city, some of whom were tempted to give up their faith. The book is part of the Catholic canon of Scripture (one of the deuterocanonical books).

Chapters 1–5 contrast the fate of the righteous and the ungodly. In chapter 2 the ungodly say that life is short and death is the end, so “let us enjoy” and let us oppress the righteous man and test him with a shameful death (2:1–20). Chapter 3 answers them: what looked like the end of the righteous was not the end.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: how things look and how they are: “they seemed to have died… but they are at peace”.
  • vv. 5–6: the meaning of their suffering: God tested them like gold in a furnace and accepted them like a sacrifice.
  • vv. 7–8: their future glory: they will shine and share in God’s rule.
  • v. 9: those who trust understand the truth; grace and mercy are on God’s holy ones.

Key words.

  • athanasia, immortality: Wisdom is one of the first books of the Bible to use this Greek word. For the author, immortality is not a natural property we hold by right but life with God, given to the righteous (1:15: “righteousness is immortal”).
  • dokimazō, to test: The word used for testing metal. Suffering does not prove God’s absence; it can be the fire that shows what is precious.
  • holokarpōma, burnt offering: A sacrifice wholly given to God. The death of the righteous is seen as an offering that God accepts.
  • episkopē, visitation: The time when God comes to look upon his people, to judge and to save. The English word bishop (episkopos) comes from the same root.

Verse by verse.

“But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them.” (3:1)

The hand of God means power, care and protection (compare Psalm 31:5, “Into your hand I commit my spirit”, the prayer Jesus spoke on the cross in Luke 23:46).

“In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died, and their departure was thought to be a disaster,” (3:2)

“The foolish” are the ungodly of chapter 2, who judge only by what they see. Faith looks further.

“For though in the sight of others they were punished, their hope is full of immortality.” (3:4)
“like gold in the furnace he tried them, and like a sacrificial burnt offering he accepted them.” (3:6)

The passage speaks first of the trials of life, especially persecution. The image of fire that purifies gold has also helped the Church to speak of the final purification of those who die in God’s friendship but are not yet wholly purified (compare 1 Peter 1:7; 1 Corinthians 3:15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage at funerals and on All Souls as a word of comfort: the dead who belong to God are safe in his hand. The Catechism teaches that those who die in God’s grace and friendship but are still imperfectly purified are assured of salvation, and undergo purification after death so as to enter the joy of heaven; the Church calls this purification purgatory (CCC 1030–1031). From the beginning the Church has honoured the memory of the dead and offered prayers, above all the Eucharistic sacrifice, for them (CCC 1032, citing 2 Maccabees 12:46).

Psalm 23:1–6 · The Lord is my shepherd

Setting.

Psalm 23 is a psalm of trust, headed “A Psalm of David”. David was a shepherd before he was king, and in the ancient Near East kings were often called shepherds of their people. The psalm uses two pictures: the shepherd with his flock (vv. 1–4) and the host who welcomes a guest (vv. 5–6).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the shepherd provides pasture, water, rest and right paths.
  • v. 4: the dark valley; the speaker now speaks to God directly: “you are with me”.
  • v. 5: the host prepares a table, anoints the head, fills the cup.
  • v. 6: goodness and mercy follow; the guest will dwell in the Lord’s house.
  • ¶ The change from “he” to “you” in v. 4 comes exactly in the darkest place. In the valley the psalmist stops talking about God and starts talking to him.

Key words.

  • rō‘î, my shepherd: A personal word: not only the shepherd of Israel, but mine.
  • ṣalmāwet, darkest valley: The traditional reading is “the shadow of death”, which the Greek and Latin versions follow and the Malayalam POC keeps. Many scholars read the word as “deep darkness”. Both fit the psalm, and on All Souls the old rendering speaks with special force.
  • ḥesed, mercy, steadfast love: God’s faithful covenant love. In v. 6 goodness and mercy do not merely accompany; the Hebrew verb rādap means to pursue. God’s love runs after us.

Verse by verse.

“The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want.” (23:1)
“Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I fear no evil; for you are with me;” (23:4)

The psalm does not promise that we will avoid the valley, but that we will not walk it alone. The shepherd’s rod defended the sheep; the staff guided them.

“You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; you anoint my head with oil; my cup overflows.” (23:5)
“and I shall dwell in the house of the Lord my whole life long.” (23:6)

The Greek and Latin versions read “for length of days”, which Christian readers have long understood of the eternal house of the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Good Shepherd is one of the most common images in the Roman catacombs, painted on the tombs of the first Christians. In the ancient Church, St Ambrose and St Cyril of Jerusalem explained Psalm 23 to the newly baptized: the water, the anointing, the table and the overflowing cup were signs of baptism, chrism and the Eucharist (in substance). This psalm is often sung at Catholic funerals, as the Church accompanies her children through the last valley to the Father’s house. Jesus calls himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11).

Romans 5:5–11 · Hope does not disappoint us

Setting.

St Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome around AD 57, probably from Corinth, before he had visited them. In chapters 1–4 he shows that all, Jews and Gentiles, are justified by God’s grace through faith in Christ. Chapter 5 begins to draw out what follows: “we have peace with God” (5:1), and we boast in the hope of sharing God’s glory, even in our sufferings (5:2–4).

Structure.

  • v. 5: hope does not disappoint, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts by the Spirit.
  • vv. 6–8: the proof of that love: Christ died for the ungodly, while we were still sinners.
  • vv. 9–10: two “much more” arguments: if God did the harder thing for enemies, he will surely complete our salvation.
  • v. 11: we boast in God, through Christ, through whom we have received reconciliation.

Key words.

  • ou kataischynei, does not disappoint: Literally “does not put to shame”. In the psalms, those who trust in God “are not put to shame” (Psalm 22:5). Christian hope will never be exposed as an illusion.
  • ekkechytai, has been poured: A perfect tense: poured once and still overflowing. The language recalls the promise of the Spirit poured out on all flesh (Joel 2:28, quoted in Acts 2:17).
  • katallagē, reconciliation: A word for restoring friendship between enemies. It is God who reconciles; we receive it.
  • pollō mallon, much more: Paul argues from the greater to the lesser: the cross was the hard part; our final salvation is the sure result.

Verse by verse.

“and hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” (5:5)

“God’s love” can mean God’s love for us or our love for God. Here it is first God’s love for us, which, given by the Spirit, becomes the love with which we love him.

“But God proves his love for us in that while we still were sinners Christ died for us.” (5:8)

Human love usually responds to something lovable (v. 7). God’s love goes first and creates what it loves.

“For if while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more surely, having been reconciled, will we be saved by his life.” (5:10)

“By his life” means the risen life of Christ. This verse is the ground of our hope for the dead: those joined to his death are joined to his life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Romans 5:5 when it teaches that love is the first gift of the Spirit, the gift that contains all others (CCC 733), and Romans 5:8 to show that God’s love for us comes before any merit of ours (CCC 604). St Augustine returned to Romans 5:5 again and again to teach that charity is not our own achievement but God’s gift through the Holy Spirit (in substance). On All Souls this passage tells us why our prayer for the dead is full of hope and not of fear.

John 6:37–40 · I will raise them up on the last day

Setting.

These verses belong to the Bread of Life discourse, spoken in the synagogue at Capernaum (6:59) the day after the feeding of the five thousand, near the time of Passover (6:4). The crowd has asked for a sign like the manna. Jesus has just said, “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry” (6:35).

Structure.

  • v. 37: all that the Father gives will come; Jesus will never drive away anyone who comes.
  • v. 38: he came down from heaven to do the Father’s will.
  • v. 39: that will, first stated: to lose nothing and raise it up on the last day.
  • v. 40: that will, stated again: all who see the Son and believe have eternal life, and he will raise them up.
  • ¶ The phrase “I will raise them up on the last day” is a refrain in this discourse (6:39, 40, 44, 54).

Key words.

  • thelēma, will: The word occurs three times in three verses. Our salvation rests not on our grip on God but on the Father’s will and the Son’s obedience.
  • ou mē ekbalō exō, I will never drive away: A double negative in Greek, the strongest possible denial: under no circumstances.
  • anastēsō, I will raise up: The resurrection of the body on the last day. Eternal life begins now (v. 40, “may have”) and is completed then.

Verse by verse.

“Everything that the Father gives me will come to me, and anyone who comes to me I will never drive away;” (6:37)

“Everything” is neuter in Greek: the whole company given by the Father, seen as one gift. Then Jesus speaks of each person: “anyone who comes”.

“that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day.” (6:39)

The Good Shepherd loses none of his sheep (10:28). This is the deepest comfort of All Souls.

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” (6:40)

To “see” here is more than to look: it is to recognise the Son with faith.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites these verses when it teaches that the resurrection of all the dead will take place “on the last day” (CCC 1001). Later in this chapter Jesus links the promise with the Eucharist: “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood have eternal life, and I will raise them up on the last day” (6:54). St Ignatius of Antioch called the Eucharist “the medicine of immortality” (Letter to the Ephesians 20; CCC 1405). That is why, from the earliest times, the Church has offered the Mass for her dead.

Questions people ask

Is purgatory in the Bible?

The word is not, but the reality is taught. Judas Maccabeus made atonement for the dead “so that they might be delivered from their sin” (2 Maccabees 12:46). St Paul speaks of one who “will be saved, but only as through fire” (1 Corinthians 3:15). The Church formulated this teaching especially at the Councils of Florence and Trent (CCC 1030–1032).

If Christ has saved them, why do they need our prayers?

Purgatory is not a second chance or a place of revenge. It is the final purification of those already saved, so that they can enter fully into God’s holy presence. Our prayers, alms and above all the Mass help them through the communion of saints (CCC 1032, 1475).

Wisdom says “they seemed to have died”. Do the dead really die?

Yes, death is real, and the Church grieves. But for the righteous, death is not destruction; their life is in God’s hand, and their bodies will be raised on the last day (John 6:40; CCC 1005).

For the Latin reader

The commemoration of all the faithful departed on 2 November goes back to St Odilo, abbot of Cluny, who around 998 ordered his monasteries to pray for all the dead on the day after All Saints; the custom spread through the Latin Church. In 1915 Pope Benedict XV allowed every priest to celebrate three Masses on this day. The faithful may gain a plenary indulgence, applicable only to the souls in purgatory, by devoutly visiting a cemetery and praying for the dead on any day from 1 to 8 November, under the usual conditions. St Augustine records the words of his dying mother, St Monica: lay this body anywhere; only remember me at the altar of the Lord (in substance, Confessions 9.11).
