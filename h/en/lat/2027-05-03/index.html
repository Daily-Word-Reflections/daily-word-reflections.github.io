Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, ‘Show us the Father’?”” John 14:9

Reflection

Today the Church keeps the feast of two of the Twelve whom the Gospels mention only briefly: Philip, and James the son of Alphaeus, often called James the Less to distinguish him from James the brother of John. The first reading was chosen because of one line in it. St Paul is reminding the Corinthians of the Gospel he handed on to them, the core of the faith that he “in turn had received”: “that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve.” Then come the other witnesses: more than five hundred at once, “most of whom are still alive”; “then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me.” This is one of the oldest texts in the whole New Testament. Paul wrote it around the year 54, but he is quoting a summary of the faith he had received years earlier, very soon after the Resurrection. The apostles' faith is not a pious idea that grew slowly; it is the testimony of people who saw the risen Lord. The James to whom the Lord appeared is, in Paul's letters, “James the Lord's brother” (Galatians 1:19), the leader of the Church in Jerusalem. The Latin tradition has long identified him with the apostle James the son of Alphaeus, whom we honour today. Scholars discuss whether these are one man or two, and the Church has not settled the question; what is sure is that the liturgy wants us to see in today's James a witness of the Resurrection.

The Psalm gives the reason the apostles still speak to us: “There is no speech, nor are there words; their voice is not heard; yet their voice goes out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world.” The psalmist was speaking of the sun, the moon and the stars, which praise God without words. St Paul applies the verse to the preachers of the Gospel (Romans 10:18). Two quiet men from Galilee, of whom we know very little, have a voice that has reached the ends of the earth, as far as India.

The Gospel gives us Philip at the Last Supper. Philip came from Bethsaida, the town of Andrew and Peter, on the shore of the Lake of Galilee. He was among the very first to be called: Jesus “found Philip and said to him, ‘Follow me’” (John 1:43). At once Philip found his friend Nathanael and told him, “We have found him about whom Moses in the law and also the prophets wrote.” When Nathanael scoffed, Philip did not argue; he said, “Come and see.” At the feeding of the five thousand, Jesus tested Philip: “Where are we to buy bread for these people to eat?” And Philip, practical as ever, did the arithmetic: “Six months' wages would not buy enough bread.” Later, some Greeks came to Philip, perhaps because of his Greek name: “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” Now, at the supper, Jesus has said, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me. If you know me, you will know my Father also.” And Philip, honest and a little puzzled, speaks for every seeker: “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied.” The answer of Jesus is gentle and astonishing: “Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” Philip has been looking past Jesus for God, when God was looking at him through the face of Jesus. The Son is not a messenger who points to a distant Father; he is in the Father and the Father is in him.

Then Jesus makes a promise to all who believe: “the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these, because I am going to the Father.” Greater works, because after the Ascension the Gospel will spread through the whole world by the apostles, by Philip and James and all the rest. And “I will do whatever you ask in my name.” Tradition says that Philip preached in Asia Minor and died at Hierapolis in Phrygia; in 2011 archaeologists working there reported finding a tomb they believe to be his. James, according to the Jewish historian Josephus, was stoned in Jerusalem around the year 62 on the orders of the high priest; the Christian writer Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, tells of his prayer for his killers. The letter of James, which tradition ascribes to the Lord's brother, gives us a James who is utterly practical: “be doers of the word, and not merely hearers.” Philip who brought others to Jesus, James who lived what he believed: two faces of the one apostolic faith that has come down to us.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate two apostles about whom the Gospels say very little: Philip and James. They were not the famous ones like Peter or John. But through them, and through the Twelve, the faith has come down to us. St Paul says: I handed on to you what I in turn had received, that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Cephas, to the Twelve, and then to James (1 Corinthians 15:3–7).

This is the oldest creed of the Church. It is not a beautiful story invented later. It is the testimony of people who saw the risen Lord with their own eyes, and James was one of them. When we say the Creed on Sunday, we are standing with Philip and James and repeating what they saw.

Look at Philip in the Gospel. He was a practical man. When Jesus asked how to feed five thousand people, Philip calculated: six months' wages would not be enough. And at the Last Supper he asks a very honest question: Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied (John 14:8). Many people today ask the same: show me God, and I will believe.

Jesus answers with tenderness and a little sadness: have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father (John 14:9). Philip was looking past Jesus for God. But God was there, at the table, in the face of Jesus. If you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus: touching the leper, forgiving the sinful woman, washing feet, dying on the cross, rising again.

And Philip teaches us how to share this. When he found Jesus, he did not give his friend Nathanael a lecture. He said: come and see (John 1:46). That is still the best way. Bring a friend to Mass, to adoration, to a prayer meeting. Let them see Jesus.

St James, the leader of the Church in Jerusalem, reminds us that faith must be lived: be doers of the word, and not merely hearers (James 1:22). Tradition tells us he died praying for those who killed him. Today let us thank God for the apostles, pray for our bishops, their successors, and ask Philip and James to make us witnesses: people who say, come and see, and people whose lives show what they believe. Amen.

For children

Children, Philip asked Jesus: show us God! And Jesus said: when you see me, you see God the Father. So if you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus: he loves children, he helps the sick, he forgives.

Philip brought his friend to Jesus by saying: come and see! Who can you bring to Jesus? Maybe you can invite a friend to come to church or to say a prayer with you.

Prayer

God our Father, you gladden us each year with the feast of the Apostles Philip and James. Through their prayers, grant us a share in the passion and resurrection of your only-begotten Son, so that we may come to see you for ever. Lord Jesus, way, truth and life, you said to Philip, whoever has seen me has seen the Father: open our eyes to see the Father in your face, and make us, like James, doers of the word and not hearers only. As Philip brought others to you, let us say to our friends, come and see; and through the faith handed on to us by the apostles, keep us witnesses of your Resurrection until you come again. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church keeps the Feast of Saints Philip and James, Apostles. The first reading gives the oldest summary of the Gospel in the New Testament: Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared; and one of those to whom he appeared was James. The Gospel shows Philip at the Last Supper, asking what every human heart asks: “Lord, show us the Father.” Psalm 19 says that the voice of the heavens goes out through all the earth; the voice of the apostles went with it.

Why the two together? According to tradition, their relics were placed in the Church of the Holy Apostles in Rome, dedicated in the sixth century, and the shared feast grew from that dedication. Philip of Bethsaida and James son of Alphaeus are among the less known of the Twelve, yet the Church is built on them too.

1 Corinthians 15:1–8 · Then he appeared to James

Setting.

Paul writes to Corinth in the mid-50s. Some there were saying that there is no resurrection of the dead (15:12). Before answering them, Paul reminds them of the Gospel they received, on which they stand. These verses contain an early formula of faith which Paul himself had received. Many scholars think it goes back to the first years after the Resurrection.

Background. The James of v. 7 is James the brother of the Lord, leader of the church in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19; Acts 15:13). In the Bible “brother” can mean a close relative, and the Church understands it so here (CCC 500). The Latin tradition, following St Jerome, identified this James with the apostle James son of Alphaeus, and that is why this reading is chosen for today. Many scholars distinguish the two, and the Eastern tradition generally keeps them apart; the Church has not settled the question by definition.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the Gospel you received, in which you stand and through which you are being saved.
  • vv. 3–5: the formula: died, buried, raised, appeared to Cephas and to the Twelve.
  • vv. 6–7: more than five hundred at once; James; all the apostles.
  • v. 8: last of all, to Paul.

Key words.

  • paradidōmi / paralambanō, to hand on / to receive: The technical words for passing on a tradition (v. 3; the same pair introduces the Lord’s Supper in 11:23). Paul does not invent the Gospel; he received it and hands it on. This is what the Church calls apostolic Tradition.
  • kata tas graphas, in accordance with the scriptures: Said twice (vv. 3, 4). The death and Resurrection of Christ are not accidents of history but the fulfilment of God’s plan foretold in the Scriptures of Israel.
  • ōphthē, he appeared, he was seen: Four times in these verses (vv. 5, 6, 7, 8). The Risen One lets himself be seen. The witnesses do not produce the experience; they receive it.
  • ektrōma, one untimely born: A child born out of time (v. 8). Paul uses a hard word of himself: he came to faith late, and as a persecutor.

Verse by verse.

“For I handed on to you as of first importance what I in turn had received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures,” (15:3)

The same words of handing on and receiving introduce the account of the Lord’s Supper (11:23). Faith in the Resurrection and the Eucharist reach us by the same road: the living Tradition of the Church.

“and that he was buried,” (15:4)

A short phrase with weight. The burial confirms that the death was real, and it stands behind the Gospel accounts of the empty tomb.

“Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers and sisters at one time, most of whom are still alive,” (15:6)

Almost an invitation to check: the witnesses could still be asked. Paul is not repeating a legend from long ago.

“Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles.” (15:7)

The Gospels do not narrate this appearance. During the ministry the brothers of the Lord did not believe in him (John 7:5); after the Resurrection they are found praying with the apostles and with Mary (Acts 1:14). An appearance of the Risen Lord to James may lie behind that change.

“Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me.” (15:8)

Paul counts his meeting with Christ on the road to Damascus among the appearances, though it came later than the others.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes vv. 3–4 to show how early the faith in the Resurrection was handed on (CCC 639). It also notes that Peter and the Twelve are the first witnesses of the Resurrection, but not the only ones: Paul speaks of more than five hundred, of James, and of all the apostles (CCC 642). The Second Vatican Council teaches that what the apostles handed on grows in understanding in the Church, with the help of the Holy Spirit, without ceasing to be the same faith (Dei Verbum 8).

John 14:6–14 · Lord, show us the Father

Setting.

At the Last Supper Jesus has said that he is going to prepare a place for his disciples and that they know the way (14:1–4). Thomas objects that they do not even know where he is going (14:5). Jesus answers with one of his great “I am” sayings, and then Philip speaks.

Background. Philip came from Bethsaida, the town of Andrew and Peter (1:44). Jesus called him with the words “Follow me”, and Philip at once went to bring Nathanael (1:43–46). Before the feeding of the five thousand Jesus tested him by asking where they could buy bread (6:5–7), and it was to Philip that some Greeks came, wishing to see Jesus (12:20–22). In John, Philip is the disciple of plain questions and of bringing others to Jesus.

Structure.

  • v. 6: I am the way, and the truth, and the life.
  • v. 7: to know me is to know the Father.
  • vv. 8–11: Philip’s request, and the answer: whoever has seen me has seen the Father.
  • vv. 12–14: greater works, and prayer in his name.

Key words.

  • hodos, way: A road, and also a way of life. The first Christians were called those who belonged to “the Way” (Acts 9:2). Jesus does not only show the way; he is the way.
  • alētheia, truth: In John, truth is the faithful reality of God made known. Jesus does not only speak true things; he is the truth in person (v. 6).
  • en tō onomati mou, in my name: To ask in his name (vv. 13–14) is not to add a formula, but to ask as one united with him, for what he wills. That is why the prayers of the Mass end “through our Lord Jesus Christ”.

Verse by verse.

“I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” (14:6)

Three words, one person. St Augustine taught that Christ is our homeland as God and our way as man: we go through him to him (in substance).

“Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied.” (14:8)

Philip’s request is the longing of the whole Bible. Moses once asked to see the glory of God (Exodus 33:18). Philip asks well; what he has not yet grasped is that the answer is standing in front of him.

“Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” (14:9)

Less a rebuke than a sigh of love. Philip had been with him for years, and there was still more to know. So it is with us.

“the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these, because I am going to the Father.” (14:12)

Not greater in power than the raising of Lazarus, but greater in reach: after Pentecost the Gospel goes through the apostles to the nations. The reason is “because I am going to the Father”: the works of the Church are his works, done through the Spirit he sends.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes v. 9 to teach that Jesus completes and perfects revelation: to see him is to see the Father (Dei Verbum 4). The Catechism says the same of his whole earthly life, his words and deeds, his silences and sufferings: all of it reveals the Father (CCC 516). Ancient tradition honours St James, identified in the West with the Lord’s brother, as the first bishop of Jerusalem, and remembers St Philip as having preached in Asia Minor and died at Hierapolis in Phrygia.

Questions people ask

Does “No one comes to the Father except through me” mean that only baptised Christians can be saved?

Christ is the one way to the Father; there is no other name by which we are saved (Acts 4:12). But the Church also teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ and his Church, yet seek God with a sincere heart and, moved by grace, try to do his will, can attain salvation (CCC 847; Lumen Gentium 16). Whoever is saved is saved through Christ, even without yet knowing his name. That is why the Church still has the duty to proclaim him to all (CCC 848).

Which James is this? There are several in the New Testament.

There is James son of Zebedee, brother of John (feast 25 July); James son of Alphaeus, one of the Twelve; and James the brother of the Lord, leader of the church in Jerusalem, a close relative of Jesus and not a son of Mary (CCC 500). The Latin tradition, following St Jerome, identified the second and the third, and honours him today with Philip. He is often called James the Less, from Mark’s “James the younger” (Mark 15:40). Many scholars, and the Eastern Churches, distinguish them. The Letter of James is traditionally linked with the brother of the Lord.

For the Latin reader

Both apostles are named among the saints in the first Eucharistic Prayer, the Roman Canon. Their feast has its own Gloria and the Preface of the Apostles; the Creed is not said, since it is a feast and not a solemnity. Since 1969 the feast has been kept on 3 May. Earlier it was kept on 1 May, and from 1955 on 11 May, when 1 May was given to St Joseph the Worker.

For study and teaching

  • Philip asked a plain question and received a deep answer. What plain question would I ask Jesus today?
  • Where do I see the Father in the face and deeds of Jesus in the Gospels?
  • James was once among the relatives who did not believe. What does his story say to families where some do not yet believe?
