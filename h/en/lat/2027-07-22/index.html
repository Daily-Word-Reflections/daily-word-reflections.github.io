Word for the day

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”; and she told them that he had said these things to her.” John 20:18

Reflection

The Church gives us today two of the most loving texts in the Bible. In the first, from the Song of Songs, a woman is awake in the night, searching for the one she loves. “Upon my bed at night I sought him whom my soul loves; I sought him, but found him not; I called him, but he gave no answer.” She does not stay in bed with her longing; she says, “I will rise now and go about the city, in the streets and in the squares; I will seek him whom my soul loves.” The watchmen on their rounds find her, and she asks them at once, “Have you seen him whom my soul loves?” Then, “Scarcely had I passed them, when I found him whom my soul loves. I held him, and would not let him go.” Since early times the Church has read this poem as the story of God and the human soul, and the Christian tradition has heard in it the voice of Mary Magdalene at the tomb. The third-century writer Hippolytus of Rome, in one of the oldest Christian commentaries on the Song of Songs, already compares the women who went to the tomb with the bride who seeks her beloved. The setting matches: the Song's bride seeks him at night in a garden city; John says that Mary came “while it was still dark,” and that the tomb was in a garden (John 19:41). The psalm is the prayer of the same seeker: “O God, you are my God, I seek you, my soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you, as in a dry and weary land where there is no water.” And it knows the answer: “Because your steadfast love is better than life, my lips will praise you.” If the alternative reading is used, St Paul gives us the other side of the same love: “The love of Christ urges us on,” and “if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (2 Corinthians 5:14, 17). Mary of Magdala is the first person who sees what that new creation looks like.

Magdala was a fishing town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, and excavations there have uncovered a synagogue of the time of Jesus. Mary of Magdala is named in all four Gospels. Luke tells us that among the women who travelled with Jesus and supported him and the Twelve “out of their resources” was “Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out” (Luke 8:2). The Gospels say nothing more about the nature of her illness. She remained with Jesus to the end: she stands near the cross with his mother (John 19:25), she sees where he is laid (Mark 15:47), and on Sunday morning she comes back. Here we need to be careful. A tradition in the Western Church, which became widespread after a homily of Pope St Gregory the Great in the sixth century, joined her with the unnamed sinful woman who anointed Jesus' feet in Luke 7:36–50 and also with Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha. The Gospels never say so. Today the Church keeps these figures distinct, and reads on this feast only what Scripture tells us about Mary of Magdala: a woman healed by Jesus, a faithful disciple, a witness of the cross and the empty tomb, and the first to see the risen Lord. John's account is among the most moving in the Gospels. “Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark,” she sees that the stone has been removed, and runs to Simon Peter and the beloved disciple: “They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.” The reading omits the visit of Peter and John to the tomb. The two men see the burial cloths and go home. “But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb.” She bends down and sees two angels in white, who ask, “Woman, why are you weeping?” and she says, “They have taken away my Lord”: it is my Lord, not only the Lord. Then she turns round and sees Jesus standing there but does not recognise him, taking him for the gardener, and says to him, “Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.” Her love will carry the body of Jesus herself if she must. And then the whole scene turns on one word: “Jesus said to her, ‘Mary!’” The Good Shepherd “calls his own sheep by name” (John 10:3). “She turned and said to him in Hebrew, ‘Rabbouni!’ (which means Teacher).” She turned a second time: the first was her body, the second her heart. It is the moment of faith.

Then Jesus says something strange: “Do not hold on to me, because I have not yet ascended to the Father.” The Greek is a command to stop clinging, as if she were taking hold of him to keep him as before. Jesus is not rejecting her; he is telling her that the old way of being with him is over and a new one begins. He will not be held in the garden; he will be with his disciples always through the Spirit, in the Church. He sends her: “But go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.’” For the first time he calls the disciples his brothers, and he gives them, through her, the whole meaning of Easter: his Father is now our Father. Mary Magdalene “went and announced to the disciples, ‘I have seen the Lord.’” In these few words the Church's whole message is already present, and she is the first to say it. That is why the Church, since the early centuries, has called her the “apostle of the apostles”: apostle means “one who is sent,” and the risen Lord sent her to the apostles. St Thomas Aquinas, commenting on this passage, uses exactly this title. The Catechism puts it plainly: Mary Magdalene and the holy women “were the first to encounter the Risen One. Thus the women were the first messengers of Christ's Resurrection for the apostles themselves” (Catechism 641). The apostles, at first, did not believe the women's words (Luke 24:11), as people sometimes do not believe the testimony of those who are overlooked; but the Church, which rests on the testimony of Peter and the Twelve, rests also on this first testimony. In 2016 the Church raised her day to the rank of a feast, so that all the faithful, and women in particular, may remember the place of this witness. This is also a day for those who weep. In the monsoon weeks, when for days together the sky itself seems to weep, we remember that Mary's tears were seen by someone: the Lord was already there, and he was calling her by name.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church keeps the feast of a woman who stood at the cross and went to the tomb: Mary of Magdala. We know a few things about her from the Gospels. Jesus drove seven demons out of her. She followed him with other women and helped to support him. She stood near the cross. And on Sunday morning, while it was still dark, she went to the tomb. We have to be careful not to add what the Gospels do not say. The Gospels do not name her as the sinful woman of Luke's seventh chapter, and the Church keeps them apart. What we know is already enough: a healed woman, a faithful disciple, and the first witness of the Resurrection.

John tells the story slowly. Mary sees the stone rolled away and runs to Peter and the other disciple. They run to the tomb, see the cloths and go home. But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb (John 20:11). Everyone else had gone home; she stays. Two angels ask: woman, why are you weeping? She says: they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him. Notice: my Lord. Then she turns and sees a man standing there; she thinks he is the gardener. Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away. She is ready to carry a dead body on her own shoulders. That is love.

And then one word changes everything: Mary! (John 20:16). He calls her by name. The Good Shepherd calls his own sheep by name. And she turns, and says: Rabbouni! My Teacher! It was not a stranger's voice. It is the voice that called her out of darkness, and now it calls her out of grief. Do you hear your name when the Lord calls you? In prayer, in the Scriptures, in the Eucharist, in the cry of a poor person: he calls you by name.

Then Jesus says: do not hold on to me. She wants to keep him as before, and he says: that way is over, now I am going to the Father. But go to my brothers and say to them: I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God. And Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples: I have seen the Lord (John 20:18). This is why the Church calls her the apostle of the apostles. The first to see the Lord alive was the first to tell. Peter and the Twelve would announce it to the world, but a woman announced it to them.

Brothers and sisters, how do we honour this feast? First, by staying: with those who weep, with those everybody has given up on. Second, by letting the Lord call us by name. Third, by not clinging to the past, and by going. Fourth, by saying out loud what Mary said: I have seen the Lord. In this rainy season, when the sky itself seems to weep, let us remember that no tear is unseen, because the Lord was already there, behind Mary, calling her by name. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know who was the very first person to see Jesus alive on Easter morning? A friend of Jesus named Mary Magdalene! She went to the tomb very early and cried because Jesus was not there. Then a man said: Mary! She knew his voice at once. It was Jesus, alive! He said: go and tell my friends. And she ran and said: I have seen the Lord!

Jesus knows your name too, and he loves you. Today, tell somebody the happiest news in the world: Jesus is alive! And you can say, like Mary: I have seen the Lord!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you called Mary of Magdala by her name and sent her to announce your Resurrection to the apostles. Make us seekers like her, who rise before dawn, do not stop weeping before the empty tomb, and know your voice when you call us by name. Teach us not to cling to what is past, but to go to our brothers and sisters and say: I have seen the Lord. Through the prayers of St Mary Magdalene, the apostle of the apostles, give your Church the faith that proclaims you without fear, and bring us all to the joy of your presence, where you live and reign for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church keeps the feast of St Mary Magdalene, the first witness of the Resurrection. The two readings sing one longing. In the Song of Songs a woman searches the city by night for the one her soul loves, asks the watchmen, and at last finds him and holds him. In John’s Gospel Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb in the dark, weeps outside it, does not recognise Jesus until he speaks her name, and is sent to tell the disciples, “I have seen the Lord.” Seeking in love, being found by name, and being sent: these are the three movements of today’s feast. Psalm 63, “My soul is thirsting for you, O Lord my God,” gives the same longing a prayer.

Song of Songs 3:1–4a · I sought him whom my soul loves

Setting.

The Song of Songs is a collection of love poems, in which a woman and a man speak and sing of one another, with a chorus of friends, the “daughters of Jerusalem”. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture and has always read it on two levels: as a celebration of faithful human love, and as a picture of the love between God and his people, between Christ and the Church, and between God and each soul. The Mass reads it on this feast because Mary Magdalene’s search at the tomb recalls the lover’s search in the night.

This passage is the first of the Song’s two searches (the second is in 5:2–8). It is partly a dream or a night-time scene, told in the lover’s own voice: the empty bed, the empty streets, the watchmen who meet her on their rounds. The Mass reading stops at the finding in v. 4a; the text goes on to say that she holds him until she brings him to her mother’s house.

Structure.

  • v. 1: by night in her bed she seeks him; she does not find him; she calls and he gives no answer.
  • v. 2: she resolves to rise and search the city, in the streets and the squares.
  • v. 3: the sentinels find her, and she asks if they have seen him.
  • v. 4a: scarcely has she passed them when she finds him; she holds him and will not let him go.

Key words.

  • nap̄šî, my soul, my whole self: Nefesh means breath, life, the whole person with its desires, more than the “soul” as we now say. The lover loves with her whole self and seeks with her whole self.
  • biqqaštî, I sought: The verb “seek” comes four times in vv. 1–2. In the Bible, seeking God is a great theme (Psalm 63:1; Isaiah 55:6; Jeremiah 29:13). Here it is the language of love.
  • ʾăḥaztîw, I held him: The verb means to grasp, to take hold. It meets its answer in John 20:17, where Jesus says to Mary, “Do not hold on to me”; the same pattern of finding and holding returns, but now love must learn to let the Lord ascend.

Verse by verse.

“Upon my bed at night I sought him whom my soul loves; I sought him, but found him not; I called him, but he gave no answer.” (3:1)

Love’s first experience here is absence. Christian spiritual writers have long seen in it the “night” of the soul that seeks God and does not yet feel him near.

““I will rise now and go about the city, in the streets and in the squares; I will seek him whom my soul loves.”” (3:2)

Longing will not stay in bed. She rises and goes into the dark streets. Love moves.

“The sentinels found me, as they went about in the city. “Have you seen him whom my soul loves?”” (3:3)

She asks the watchmen of the city. They give no answer in the text, and she finds him soon after, as if the asking itself had brought her close.

“Scarcely had I passed them, when I found him whom my soul loves. I held him, and would not let him go” (3:4)

Here is the end of the search. The joy is in the finding, and in the clinging, and in the wish to bring him home.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers of the Church read the Song in the light of Christ and the soul. St Gregory the Great, in a homily on the Gospel for this feast, quotes this Song when speaking of Mary Magdalene: she seeks the Lord who is gone, and her love will not let her rest (in substance). Hippolytus of Rome, in one of the oldest commentaries on the Song, already links the women at the tomb with the Bride of the Song, and calls them apostles to the apostles (in substance). Today’s pairing follows them. The Church, too, is the Bride who searches for her Lord in the night of the world; and each Christian is invited to seek him with the whole heart (compare CCC 2560–2565 on prayer as thirst for God, in substance).

John 20:1–2, 11–18 · I have seen the Lord

Setting.

John’s Resurrection chapter begins in darkness. All four Gospels name Mary Magdalene among the women who went to the tomb on the morning after the Sabbath; John tells only of her, and in his account she is the one who finds the stone removed and runs to tell Peter and the disciple whom Jesus loved. The reading skips vv. 3–10, the race of the two disciples to the tomb and the first faith of the beloved disciple. It then returns to Mary, who has come back, or has stayed, weeping at the entrance.

Who is she? The Gospels introduce her as Mary “called Magdalene”, that is, of Magdala, a town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Luke 8:2 says seven demons had gone out of her, and that she followed Jesus and supported his ministry with other women. She stood at the cross with Jesus’ mother (John 19:25). The Gospels do not say she was a prostitute. She is not the sinful woman who anoints Jesus’ feet in Luke 7:36–50, and she is not Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus. These identifications came from later centuries and have no support in the Gospel texts.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the stone is removed; Mary runs to Peter and the beloved disciple: “They have taken the Lord.”
  • vv. 11–13: Mary weeps outside; she sees two angels; they ask why she weeps.
  • vv. 14–15: she turns and sees Jesus, but does not recognise him; he asks why she weeps and whom she seeks; she thinks he is the gardener.
  • v. 16: he says her name, “Mary!”, and she answers, “Rabbouni!”
  • vv. 17–18: “Do not hold on to me.” He sends her to his brothers; she announces, “I have seen the Lord.”

Key words.

  • Rabbouni, my Teacher: An Aramaic word, a more personal form of “Rabbi”: “my master”. John says the language was “Hebrew”, as he uses the word for the Jewish language of Jesus’ day. She recognises his voice before his face.
  • mē mou haptou, do not hold on to me: The Greek present imperative can mean “stop clinging to me”. Mary’s embrace (compare Matthew 28:9, where the women take hold of his feet) is not forbidden as sinful. Jesus is telling her that the relationship has changed: she must not try to keep him as he was, because he is ascending to the Father, and will be present in a new way, in the Spirit and in the Church.
  • angellousa, announcing, bringing news: The verb used of Mary in v. 18 is the verb for an announcement from a messenger. She is the first to bring the news of the Resurrection to the disciples. Over centuries, Christians have called her the “apostle to the apostles”, since she was sent to the apostles with the news.

Verse by verse.

“Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb” (20:1)

The “first day of the week” is the Day of the Lord, the day of new creation. Darkness is both the hour and the state of her heart.

““They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.”” (20:2)

“We” suggests she was not alone, as the other Gospels say. Her first thought is not of resurrection but of theft: love will not accept that the body is lost.

““Woman, why are you weeping?” She said to them, “They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him.”” (20:13)

The angels ask what Jesus will ask. Her tears show love which has not yet understood. Notice “my Lord”: the love is personal.

“Jesus said to her, “Mary!” She turned and said to him in Hebrew, “Rabbouni!”” (20:16)

The whole scene turns on one word. Jesus had said in the Good Shepherd discourse that the sheep hear his voice and he calls his own by name (John 10:3–4). She is found by name.

““Do not hold on to me, because I have not yet ascended to the Father.” (20:17)

Easter is not a return to the earlier life. The Risen Lord is going to the Father, and the disciples will have to learn to be with him in faith.

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”;” (20:18)

The first words of the Church’s preaching are: “I have seen the Lord.” They are spoken by a woman, and the disciples hear them from her.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Mary Magdalene and the holy women, who had come to finish anointing the body of Jesus, were the first to encounter the Risen One, and so women were the first messengers of Christ’s Resurrection for the apostles themselves (CCC 641). Jesus’ words “my Father and your Father” show that he is the Son of God in a way no disciple is, and also that he makes his disciples sharers in his filial life (CCC 443, in substance). St Thomas Aquinas, commenting on John, calls Mary the apostle to the apostles (in substance), and Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, sees in her tears the love that seeks and does not give up (in substance). On 3 June 2016, the Congregation for Divine Worship, with the approval of Pope Francis, raised her memorial to the rank of feast in the Roman calendar, so that she may be honoured as the apostle of the apostles.

Questions people ask

Was Mary Magdalene a sinful woman? Was she Jesus’ wife?

The Gospels say she was a woman from whom seven demons had gone out (Luke 8:2), a disciple and a witness of the cross and the empty tomb. They never say she was a prostitute, and they do not identify her with the woman of Luke 7. That identification goes back to a sermon of Pope Gregory the Great in the sixth century and shaped art and preaching for a long time, but the Church’s own liturgy no longer makes it. As for the claim that she was Jesus’ wife, no Gospel, and no ancient Christian tradition of the first centuries, says so. It comes from much later writings and from modern fiction, and has no support in the Scriptures the Church receives.

Why does Jesus say “Do not hold on to me”, when he later lets Thomas touch his wounds?

Both are true to the Gospel of John. The words to Mary are not a rebuke but a change of relation: she is not to cling to the earlier way of being with him, for he is ascending to the Father. The invitation to Thomas is a different moment, for a different need, to bring a doubting disciple to faith (John 20:27). Jesus is the same in both, risen with a true, glorified body (CCC 645). In both cases the answer is the same: believe and go and tell.

Is it right to call a woman an “apostle”? Does it change the apostolic ministry of the Twelve?

In the early Church “apostle” has more than one use. In its strictest meaning it names the Twelve and Paul, chosen by Christ to be witnesses and the foundation of the Church’s ministry, and bishops are their successors. In a wider sense it means “one who is sent”, and Mary Magdalene was sent by the Risen Lord himself to the disciples. The title “apostle of the apostles”, used by Hippolytus and Aquinas, honours that mission without changing the Church’s teaching on the apostolic ministry.

For the Latin reader

Until 2016 the Roman calendar kept 22 July as an obligatory memorial. By decree of the Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments, on 3 June 2016, it became a feast, in the same rank as the feasts of the other apostles. The Mass, with the Gloria, has its own Preface, which praises her as the one sent by the Risen Christ as apostle to the apostles. The Mass readings are the Song of Songs 3 (or 2 Corinthians 5:14–17) and John 20. In Latin art she is often shown with a jar of ointment, as one of the women who came to anoint the Lord. Her name, “of Magdala”, reminds us that in Galilee, a town beside the lake was the home of the first witness of the Resurrection.

For study and teaching

  • Mary kept looking for the Lord even when she found only an empty tomb. When has my search for God felt like an empty place, and what kept me going?
  • Jesus called her by name, and she knew him. Where do I hear God calling me by name: in prayer, in Scripture, in a person who needs me?
  • “Do not hold on to me”: what in my faith do I try to keep unchanged, when the Lord is leading me on?
  • Mary became the first to say, “I have seen the Lord.” To whom am I being sent this week with good news?
