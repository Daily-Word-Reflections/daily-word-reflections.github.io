Word for the day

“And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father's only son, full of grace and truth.” John 1:14

Reflection

Christmas has three Masses, and together they tell the whole mystery. At night, Luke's Gospel: the census, Bethlehem, the manger, the angels' “Glory to God in the highest heaven, and on earth peace among those whom he favors.” At dawn, the shepherds who go with haste and find the child, and Mary who “treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart”. In the day, John's Prologue lifts our eyes from the manger to eternity: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God… And the Word became flesh and lived among us.”

The child in the manger is not a man who became God, nor God pretending to be human. He is the eternal Son, true God from true God, who “for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven, and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man”. The Church teaches that he is one Person in two natures, divine and human, without confusion or division (Council of Chalcedon, 451; CCC 464–469). He “emptied himself” (Philippians 2:7) without ceasing to be God.

Why did he come? Hebrews says: God, who once spoke “in many and various ways by the prophets”, has now spoken “to us by a Son”. Christmas is God's final word, and the word is a child. He came to save us from sin, to make us “partakers of the divine nature” (2 Peter 1:4; CCC 460), to show us the Father, and to give us a model of holiness. “To all who received him… he gave power to become children of God.” St Leo the Great's Christmas sermon gives the response: “Christian, remember your dignity.”

Isaiah sings of the messenger's beautiful feet on the mountains announcing peace, and of watchmen shouting for joy. Tonight the shepherds are those watchmen, and we are invited to join them. The manger points to the altar: born in Bethlehem, “House of Bread”, laid in a feeding trough, the Child will give himself as the Bread of Life.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, a happy and holy Christmas to you all!

Tonight and today the Church tells the story three ways. At night, Luke tells us of the census, the journey to Bethlehem, no room in the inn, the manger, the shepherds and the angels singing, “Glory to God in the highest.” In the day, John lifts our eyes to eternity: “In the beginning was the Word… and the Word became flesh and lived among us.”

Who is this child? Not just a good man, not an angel, but God himself. The eternal Son, through whom everything was made, now lies in a manger, needing his mother's milk. St Augustine marvelled: the Bread was hungry, the Fountain was thirsty, the Light slept. God became small so that we would not be afraid to come close.

Why did he come? The letter to the Hebrews says: long ago God spoke through the prophets, but now he has spoken to us by a Son. Christmas is God's final word to us, and that word is love in the form of a child. He came to forgive our sins, to show us the Father, and to make us God's own children. St John says: “To all who received him, he gave power to become children of God.”

But there was no room in the inn. Is there room in ours? In our busy celebrations, our shopping, our food and decorations, is there room for the Child? And is there room for the poor, the lonely, the migrant, those for whom this Christmas is sad?

Bethlehem means “House of Bread”. The Child was laid in a manger, where animals eat. From the beginning he was preparing to give himself as our food. Today at this altar he comes to us again. Let us receive him with joy, and, as St Leo said, “Christian, remember your dignity.” A blessed Christmas to you and your families!

Dear brothers and sisters, a joyful and blessed Christmas to all of you! Tonight let us go in spirit to a small town in Judea. Its name was Bethlehem. In Hebrew, Bethlehem means house of bread. It was there, and nowhere else, that God chose to be born.

The prophet Micah had foretold it: from you, Bethlehem, small among the clans of Judah, shall come forth one who is to rule in Israel (Micah 5:2). God plans with care. The One who would say I am the bread of life (John 6:35) was born in the house of bread.

And where did Mary lay Him? In a manger (Luke 2:7). We have made the crib beautiful with lights and decorations, but a manger was a feeding trough, the place where animals came to eat. The prophet Isaiah had said that the ox knows its owner and the donkey its master's crib, but Israel does not understand (Isaiah 1:3). The Son of God was placed where food is placed. From His first night on earth He was saying: I have come to be your food.

That is why Christmas leads us to the altar. In a few moments, on this altar, the same Christ born in Bethlehem will become present under the appearance of bread. The shepherds knelt at the manger; we kneel before the Lord in the Holy Mass. They saw a child; we see bread. In both cases faith sees God hidden in humility. Receive Him tonight with the faith of the shepherds.

And the shepherds did not keep the good news to themselves. They told everyone what they had heard (Luke 2:17). Bread is meant to be shared. This Christmas, share your bread with someone who is hungry, lonely or forgotten: a meal, a visit, a phone call. Then Christmas will not end tonight. It will continue in every house where Christ, the Bread of Life, is received and shared.

📖 2 Peter 1:4

Apply: Thank God that He came to make you His child.

Take-home resolution: This Christmas I will bow at the words of the Creed and thank God for becoming man.

Dear brothers and sisters, a blessed Christmas to you all! We have heard the familiar story again: the journey, the manger, the shepherds. But behind the story is the most astonishing truth ever proclaimed: God became man. Tonight let us look at this mystery as the Church teaches it.

St John writes: in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; and the Word became flesh and lived among us (John 1:1, 14). The Child in the manger is not a good man whom God later adopted, nor a god who only seemed human. He is the eternal Son of God who took to Himself a full human nature, body and soul, in the womb of the Virgin Mary. That is why, in the Creed, at the words and became man, we bow; on Christmas day, we kneel.

The early Church had to defend this truth against many errors. The Councils of Nicaea, Ephesus and Chalcedon taught that Jesus Christ is one divine Person in two natures, divine and human, without confusion and without separation (CCC 464–469). Because the Child is God, the Council of Ephesus proclaimed Mary Theotokos, Mother of God (CCC 495). This is not an exaggeration about Mary; it is a protection of the truth about Jesus.

Why did God become man? The Catechism gives four reasons. To save us by reconciling us with God. So that we might know God's love. To be our model of holiness. And to make us partakers of the divine nature (CCC 457–460; 2 Peter 1:4). The Fathers of the Church summed it up: the Son of God became a son of man so that we might become children of God.

So Christmas is not only for children; it is the foundation of our whole faith. At this Holy Mass the same Word made flesh comes to us. Let us receive Him with wonder, and let His love, so humble and so close, change the way we live.

Take-home resolution: This Christmas I will make peace with one person, and pray daily for one nation at war.

Dear friends, on the night of the Nativity the shepherds heard the angels sing: glory to God in the highest heaven, and on earth peace among those whom he favours (Luke 2:14). Glory to God and peace on earth go together. When God is given his glory, peace follows.

Isaiah had called the Child the Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6). Strange prince: born in a stable, laid in a manger, threatened by a king. Yet he brings what no army can bring. St Paul says: he is our peace; in his flesh he has broken down the dividing wall of hostility (Ephesians 2:14).

The world longs for peace. Tonight we pray for the land where Jesus was born, for all places torn by war, and for our own country wherever communities are divided. Christmas is not a holiday from the world's pain; it is God entering it.

But peace begins closer. In many families tonight there is an empty chair, not because of death, but because of a quarrel. Brothers who do not speak, sisters divided over property, a son estranged from his father. The Child in the manger asks: will you let me bring peace into your home?

Jacob, after twenty years of fear, ran to meet his brother Esau, and Esau embraced him (Genesis 33:4). Jesus taught: if your brother has something against you, go first and be reconciled (Matthew 5:24). Christmas is the best time to take that first step: a phone call, a visit, a slice of cake carried across the road.

At this Holy Mass, when we offer one another the sign of peace, let it be real. And let us go home as peacemakers, for blessed are the peacemakers; they will be called children of God (Matthew 5:9). A blessed and peaceful Christmas to all. Amen.

Dear friends, on that first Christmas night, while Bethlehem slept, shepherds were keeping watch over their flocks in the fields. To them, not to the powerful in Jerusalem or Rome, an angel appeared and said: do not be afraid; I am bringing you good news of great joy for all the people (Luke 2:10).

Shepherds in those days were poor and often looked down upon. Their work kept them away from the Temple and from respectable company. Yet God chose them. The prophet Ezekiel had promised that God himself would shepherd his people, seek the lost and bind up the injured (Ezekiel 34:11–16). The Good Shepherd announces himself first to shepherds.

The sign they were given was not a palace or a crown, but a child wrapped in bands of cloth and lying in a manger (Luke 2:12). The poor recognised him because he was poor like them. God did not come to the world from above, but from below.

Think of the shepherds of our time: the migrant worker from another state who builds our houses, the nurse on night duty in a hospital abroad, the fisherman at sea on Christmas night, the security guard at the gate, the family in a rented room. Christmas is first their feast.

The shepherds went with haste, found Mary and Joseph and the child, and then made known what had been told them. They returned glorifying and praising God (Luke 2:16–20). The poor became the first evangelists. They had nothing to give but their joy, and it was enough.

At this Holy Mass, Christ comes to us as he came to the shepherds: simply, humbly. Let us receive him, and then, like them, go out with haste: to share our Christmas food, to visit someone alone, to bring good news to the margins. A joyful Christmas to all. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Mass during the Day does not retell the story of Bethlehem. Luke's shepherds and manger belong to the Masses of the night and of dawn. Now, in full daylight, the Church asks who this child is.

The three readings give one answer in three voices. Isaiah sees a messenger running over the mountains with the news that God has come back to his ruined city. Hebrews says that the God who spoke in fragments through the prophets has now spoken by a Son, the very imprint of his being. John goes back behind creation itself: “In the beginning was the Word”, and ends with the sentence on which all Christian faith rests: “the Word became flesh”. The key words are messenger, Son and Word.

Isaiah 52:7–10 · Your God reigns

Setting.

This passage belongs to the Book of Consolation (Isaiah 40–55), which speaks to the Jews in exile in Babylon in the years before Cyrus allowed them to return (539 BC). Jerusalem lies in ruins. Chapter 52 opens with a call to the city to wake up and rise from the dust (52:1–2).

The poet imagines the day of return as a scene. Watchmen stand on the broken walls. A runner appears on the mountains. Before anyone can see the returning exiles, they hear the news that the Lord himself is coming home at their head. Directly after this passage comes the song of the Suffering Servant (52:13–53:12), which asks, “to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?” (53:1).

Structure.

  • v. 7: the messenger on the mountains.
  • v. 8: the sentinels see the Lord's return.
  • v. 9: the ruins themselves are told to sing.
  • v. 10: the Lord's arm, and salvation for all the earth.

Key words.

  • mĕbaśśēr, messenger of good news: One who brings good news, especially news of victory. The Greek Old Testament uses a verb from the same family as euangelion, “gospel”. This is one of the roots of the Christian word.
  • mālak ’ĕlōhāyik, your God reigns: The whole message in two Hebrew words. It is the Lord, not Babylon, who is king. The Gospel opens with the same news: “the kingdom of God has come near” (Mark 1:15).
  • zĕrôa‘, arm: God's arm is his saving power, the arm that brought Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 6:6). To bare it is to roll back the sleeve and go to work.
  • yĕšû‘āh, salvation: From the same root as the name Yeshua, Jesus. On this day the word has a new sound.

Verse by verse.

“How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, who brings good news, who announces salvation, who says to Zion, “Your God reigns.”” (52:7)

Feet are not usually called beautiful, least of all feet that have run over mountain paths. They are beautiful because of what they bring. St Paul quotes this verse of those who preach Christ (Romans 10:15).

“Listen! Your sentinels lift up their voices, together they sing for joy; for in plain sight they see the return of the Lord to Zion.” (52:8)

“In plain sight” is literally “eye to eye”. The glory that left the Temple (Ezekiel 10) returns, and it can be seen. John will say, “we have seen his glory”.

“Break forth together into singing, you ruins of Jerusalem;” (52:9)

It is the ruins who are told to sing, before any stone has been rebuilt. Comfort has come already, because God has come.

“The Lord has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations; and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God.” (52:10)

The news is not only for Israel. The psalm today answers in the past tense: all the ends of the earth have seen it (Psalm 98:3).

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in this messenger first of all Christ, who “came and proclaimed peace” (Ephesians 2:17), and then everyone who carries him to others. St Paul VI taught that evangelizing is the grace and vocation proper to the Church, her deepest identity (Evangelii Nuntiandi 14). So the messenger's feet are the feet of every catechist and parent who hands on this news.

Hebrews 1:1–6 · God has spoken by a Son

Setting.

Hebrews calls itself “a word of exhortation” (13:22). It is a sermon to Christians who were growing weary. Its author is not named. These opening verses are among the finest Greek of the New Testament, and they state the theme of the whole work: Christ is God's final word and the one true high priest.

Our reading ends at v. 6, in the middle of a chain of Old Testament quotations (1:5–14) showing that the Son is greater than the angels. Many Jews of the time held that the Law had been delivered through angels (compare Galatians 3:19; Hebrews 2:2), so the comparison is a way of saying that the Son stands above every messenger who has gone before.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2a: two ways of God's speaking: long ago by the prophets, now by a Son.
  • vv. 2b–3: seven statements about the Son: heir, agent of creation, reflection of glory, imprint of being, sustainer of all things, purifier from sin, seated at the right hand.
  • v. 4: superior to the angels.
  • vv. 5–6: the proof from Scripture: Psalm 2:7, 2 Samuel 7:14, and a text calling the angels to worship.

Key words.

  • polymerōs kai polytropōs, in many and various ways: Literally in many parts and many manners. Each prophet gave a part, and truly. The Son is the whole.
  • apaugasma, reflection, radiance: The light that shines out from a source. The Book of Wisdom uses this word of Wisdom (Wisdom 7:26). The radiance is not a second light; it has the same nature as its source.
  • charaktēr, exact imprint: The mark a seal or die leaves on wax or a coin, the exact likeness of the original. Our word “character” comes from it.
  • hypostasis, very being: God's own reality. Later theology used this word for the divine Persons, but here it has the older sense of essential being.
  • prōtotokos, firstborn: A title of rank and inheritance (Psalm 89:27), not a claim that the Son was created. It is the same Psalm 89 heard at yesterday's Mass.

Verse by verse.

“Long ago God spoke to our ancestors in many and various ways by the prophets,” (1:1)

The old revelation was true and came from God. The author does not despise it; he calls it God's speech. It was unfinished, not false.

“but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son,” (1:2)

The Greek has no article, literally “in Son”: the point is the kind of messenger. God has no further word to add to this one.

“He is the reflection of God’s glory and the exact imprint of God’s very being,” (1:3)

Two images side by side, because each alone could mislead. Light and its radiance show that they cannot be separated; a seal and its imprint show that they are distinct.

“When he had made purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high,” (1:3)

Already, in the Christmas reading, the Cross and the Ascension. The priests of old stood at their work; this one sits, because the work is finished (10:11–12). The words recall Psalm 110:1.

““Let all God’s angels worship him.”” (1:6)

The wording is close to the Greek Old Testament of Deuteronomy 32:43 (compare Psalm 97:7); the standard Hebrew of Deuteronomy does not have this line. “When he brings the firstborn into the world” has been read of the Incarnation and also of Christ's return in glory. On this day the angels' song at Bethlehem (Luke 2:13–14) comes to mind.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses to teach that Christ is God's one and final Word, and it adds the words of St John of the Cross: in giving us his Son, his only Word, God spoke everything to us at once, and he has no more to say (CCC 65). There will be no new public revelation before the Lord's return in glory (CCC 66). The Catechism also cites Hebrews 1:3 among the texts confessing the divinity of the Son (CCC 241).

St Athanasius used “reflection of God's glory” against the Arians: since a light is never without its radiance, the Father has never been without the Son (in substance). It is the thought behind “Light from Light” in the Creed.

John 1:1–18 (or 1:1–5, 9–14) · The Word became flesh

Setting.

The Prologue stands over the whole Fourth Gospel like a title. It opens with the same words as the Greek Book of Genesis: en archē, “in the beginning”. Mark begins with John at the Jordan, Matthew and Luke with the birth; John begins in eternity.

Behind the term “Word” are two lines of the Old Testament: the word of God that creates (“God said”, Genesis 1) and speaks through the prophets, and Wisdom, who is with God from the beginning and pitches her tent in Israel (Proverbs 8:22–31; Sirach 24:8). Greek readers also knew logos as the reason behind the universe. John takes up all of this and says something none of it had said: this Word has a human name.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: the Word with God, in creation, and as life and light.
  • vv. 6–8: John, a witness to the light.
  • vv. 9–13: the light comes into the world; some refuse, some receive and become children of God.
  • v. 14: the Word became flesh.
  • v. 15: John's witness again.
  • vv. 16–18: grace upon grace; the only Son has made God known.
  • ¶ The shorter form leaves out the verses on the Baptist and the last verses. The movement of the whole is a descent and a return: from the Father's side (v. 1) to our flesh (v. 14), and back to “the Father's heart” (v. 18).

Key words.

  • logos, Word: Speech, reason, meaning. In Hebrew thought dābār is a word that does what it says. The Word is God's self-expression, a Person (“he”), not a mere message.
  • pros ton theon, with God: The Greek preposition suggests being turned toward God, a relationship. From the first verse the Word is distinct from the Father and yet God.
  • sarx, flesh: Not only a body, but human nature in its weakness and mortality. John chooses the strongest word. In his day some could not accept that God would touch flesh (compare 1 John 4:2).
  • eskēnōsen, lived among us: Literally “pitched his tent”. The word recalls the tent in the desert, where the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34), and Wisdom's tent (Sirach 24:8).
  • charis kai alētheia, grace and truth: These likely echo the great pair of Exodus 34:6, God's ḥesed and ’ĕmet, “steadfast love and faithfulness”, the self-description of God given to Moses.

Verse by verse.

“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” (1:1)

“Was”, three times: not “came to be”. Nothing here has a start. The last clause has no article before “God” in Greek, so it does not say that the Word is the Father, but that the Word is what God is.

“in him was life, and the life was the light of all people.” (1:4)

A note on the text: the old manuscripts have no punctuation, so “what has come into being” may close v. 3 or open v. 4. NRSV takes the second course, POC the first. Both are ancient.

“The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not overcome it.” (1:5)

The verb can mean both “overcome” and “grasp”: the darkness neither understood the light nor put it out. The tense of “shines” is the present. This is still happening.

“He came to what was his own, and his own people did not accept him.” (1:11)

This is not a verdict on the Jewish people: the first who accepted him, Mary, Joseph, John and the apostles, were Jews, and the Church rejects any blame laid on the Jews as a people (Nostra Aetate 4). It describes every heart that closes its door, ours included.

“But to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave power to become children of God,” (1:12)

The Son of God by nature makes children of God by grace. A note on v. 13: all Greek manuscripts have the plural “who were born”. A few early Latin witnesses, with Irenaeus and Tertullian, have a singular “who was born”, which would refer to Christ.

“And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father’s only son, full of grace and truth.” (1:14)

Not “seemed to be” and not “was changed into”. He remained God and became man. In the desert the glory filled a tent; now it is seen in a man's face. “We have seen”: this is the voice of witnesses.

“From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace.” (1:16)
“No one has ever seen God. It is God the only Son, who is close to the Father’s heart, who has made him known.” (1:18)

A note on the text: the oldest manuscripts read “only God” or “God the only one”, others “the only Son”. NRSV combines them, and POC also says that the only-begotten is God himself. “Made him known” (Greek exēgēsato) gives us our word “exegesis”: Jesus is the Father's own exegesis.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Word became flesh to save us, so that we might know God's love, to be our model of holiness, and to make us “partakers of the divine nature” (CCC 456–460). It cites St Athanasius: the Son of God became man so that we might become God (CCC 460). The Incarnation does not mean that Jesus is partly God and partly man, nor a mixture: he is truly God and truly man (CCC 464).

St Augustine says in his Confessions that he found in the books of the Platonists something like “the Word was God”, but not “the Word became flesh” (in substance). St Leo the Great, in a Christmas sermon, tells the Christian to recognize his dignity, now that he shares in God's own nature (CCC 1691). The Church turns this verse into daily prayer in the Angelus.

Questions people ask

Was Jesus really born on 25 December?

The Gospels do not give the date. The Church's oldest clear witness to the feast on this day is a Roman calendar in a collection of the year 354. Scholars still debate why this day was chosen: some link it with a Roman festival of the sun, others with an old calculation from 25 March. What the Church celebrates is the fact of the birth, not an exact date in the calendar. On the mystery of Christmas, see CCC 525–526.

“The Word was God”: some translate it “a god”. Is that right?

No. In Greek, a noun placed before the verb without an article commonly describes what someone is, here God's own nature. The Prologue also says that all things were made through him (1:3), which excludes his being a creature. The Council of Nicaea (325) confessed him “true God from true God”. And since the Word is “with God”, he is not the Father: the divine Persons are really distinct (CCC 253–254).

If God has said everything in his Son, why do we still need the Church's teaching?

Revelation is complete, but “it has not been made completely explicit; it remains for Christian faith gradually to grasp its full significance over the course of the centuries” (CCC 66). The Church does not add to it; she goes on understanding and handing it on.

For the Latin reader

The Latin Church has four Masses of Christmas with their own readings: the Vigil, the Mass during the Night, at Dawn, and during the Day. These are the readings of the last, and they are the same every year. At the Creed today all genuflect at the words “and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man”, as also on the Annunciation (General Instruction of the Roman Missal 137). On other days we bow.

For centuries before the reform after the Council, the Prologue was read at the end of nearly every Roman Mass, and all genuflected at “the Word became flesh”. Today the Church hears it on this day above all. The liturgy calls this feast a wonderful exchange: the Creator becomes man, and we are made sharers in his divinity (CCC 526).

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah tells the ruins to sing before they have been rebuilt. What ruin in my life or family could begin to sing this Christmas?
  • Hebrews says God has no further word to add to his Son. Do I look for God's word in him first, or elsewhere?
  • John says, “his own people did not accept him”. Where do I keep my own door shut against him?
  • “Grace upon grace”: how can a family tell its children that the crib and the altar hold the same Lord?
