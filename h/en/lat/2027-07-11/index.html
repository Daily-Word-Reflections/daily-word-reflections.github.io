Word for the day

“So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent. They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” Mark 6:12–13

Reflection

The first reading is one of the great scenes of the prophets. Amos was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees from Tekoa, a village in Judah, south of Jerusalem. About the middle of the eighth century before Christ, in the prosperous days of King Jeroboam II, God sent him north into the kingdom of Israel to speak against the luxury of the rich and the oppression of the poor (Amos 2:6–7; 5:24). He came to Bethel, the great shrine of the northern kingdom, and preached there. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, reported him to the king and then told Amos to his face: “O seer, go, flee away to the land of Judah, earn your bread there, and prophesy there; but never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom.” The priest takes it for granted that a prophet is a paid professional who can be sent to find work elsewhere, and that the shrine belongs to the king. Amos does not argue about credentials. “I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son; but I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees, and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, ‘Go, prophesy to my people Israel.’” He belongs to no guild of prophets; he has no career, no title and no salary. He has a call. The sycamore fig of Palestine bears the poor man's fruit, and it needs tending to ripen; so the man who looked after sheep and fig trees was taken by the Lord and sent to speak to a nation. Those whom God sends are seldom those whom the establishment would have chosen, and the word they carry belongs neither to the shrine nor to the king. Notice, too, to whom he is sent: “my people Israel.” The hard word of Amos is the word of a Father who wants his people to live justly and be saved. The Psalm gives the answer of those who listen: “Let me hear what God the Lord will speak, for he will speak peace to his people, to his faithful, to those who turn to him in their hearts,” and “Righteousness will go before him, and will make a path for his steps.”

The second reading is one of the most magnificent passages of the New Testament. In the Greek it is a single long sentence of praise, a hymn that sweeps from before the creation of the world to the final gathering of all things in Christ. Paul blesses God because God has first blessed us: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places.” Then he unfolds the story of salvation in three movements, one for each Person of the Trinity. The Father chose us: “just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love. He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will.” Before there was a world, God had you in mind, in Christ; your life is not an accident. The Son redeems us: “In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace that he lavished on us.” And he makes known the hidden plan, “the mystery of his will,” “as a plan for the fullness of time, to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” The Greek verb means to sum up under one head; St Irenaeus called it recapitulation. Christ, the new Adam, takes the whole of creation and human history into himself and sets it right. The Spirit seals us. Paul speaks first of “we,” the Jewish believers who were the first to hope in Christ, then of “you also,” the Gentiles; one Church is formed of two peoples. “In him you also, when you had heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and had believed in him, were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit; this is the pledge of our inheritance toward redemption as God's own people, to the praise of his glory.” The seal is the mark of belonging received in baptism and confirmation. The pledge is a first instalment, like the deposit that guarantees a purchase: we already have the Spirit, and are promised the fullness.

The Gospel follows directly on the sad scene of Nazareth, where Jesus was rejected in his hometown and “was amazed at their unbelief.” He does not stop. “He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” They go in pairs: because two witnesses make a testimony valid (Deuteronomy 19:15), because they support one another on the road, and because the Gospel is announced by a community and not by lone individuals. St Gregory the Great said (in substance) that the Lord sends them two by two because charity cannot exist with fewer than two. Their equipment is almost nothing: “He ordered them to take nothing for their journey except a staff; no bread, no bag, no money in their belts; but to wear sandals and not to put on two tunics.” A staff and sandals are exactly what the Israelites had in their hands and on their feet when they ate the Passover, ready to leave Egypt (Exodus 12:11). The missionaries are a people on the road of a new exodus, unburdened, and their security is the word they carry and the welcome they receive. “Wherever you enter a house, stay there until you leave the place”: they are not to shop around for a better table. And when they are rejected: “If any place will not welcome you and they refuse to hear you, as you leave, shake off the dust that is on your feet as a testimony against them.” It is a sign that they have done their part, not a curse. “So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent.” The message is the same as that of Jesus (Mark 1:15). “They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them”: the Church sees in this anointing a root of the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick (Catechism 1510). The three readings meet in one word: calling. Amos is taken from the flock, the Christian is chosen before the foundation of the world, and the Twelve are called and sent. We are chosen to be sent.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the priest Amaziah tells the prophet Amos: go away. Go back to Judah, earn your bread there, and do not prophesy at Bethel. And Amos answers with one of the simplest and bravest sentences in the Bible: I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son; but I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees, and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, Go, prophesy to my people Israel (Amos 7:14–15). He has no title. He has no training. But he has a call, and a call does not need the permission of a king.

Brothers and sisters, God still calls like that. He calls fishermen and tax collectors, farmers and teachers, nurses and drivers, grandmothers who pray the rosary every evening. He does not ask for credentials. He asks: Will you go? And St Paul tells us in the second reading that this call is older than our own lives: he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love (Ephesians 1:4). Before there was a world, God had you in his mind and in his heart. You are not an accident. You are wanted.

In the Gospel Jesus sends out the Twelve. Two by two. Why two? Because the Gospel is not a private opinion; it is a shared life. Because when one is tired, the other holds him up; when one falls, the other lifts him. Think of the families, the couples, the pairs of friends who have carried the Gospel in our own land. And what do they take? A staff and sandals. No bread, no bag, no money. They are dressed like Israel on the night of the Passover, ready to go. They can go anywhere, because they carry nothing but the word.

What will they say? That all should repent. And what will they do? Cast out demons, and anoint with oil many who were sick and heal them. The Church continues this mission. In the sacrament of Anointing of the Sick, in confession, in every Mass, the apostolic work goes on. And you and I are asked to join it in our own small ways: a word, a visit, a prayer, a kindness to someone who is carrying a heavy load.

So this week, let us ask ourselves: what is the Lord sending me to do? What is the staff and sandals I need? And whom will I take with me? Let us go, trusting that the One who sends us is the One who blessed us with every spiritual blessing in Christ. Amen.

For children

Children, the Gospel today tells us that Jesus had twelve special friends, the apostles. He sent them out to tell people about God, and he sent them two by two, so that nobody would be alone. They took only a walking stick and sandals. They did not need to carry anything else, because God would look after them. Jesus wants to send you too! You can bring his love to your school, your friends and your family. You can help a child who is lonely. You can say a prayer for someone who is sick. And it is much more fun when you do it together with a friend!

Prayer

Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The three readings of this Fifteenth Sunday of Year B are about being chosen and sent. In the first, Amos is a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees whom the Lord took “from following the flock” and sent to prophesy to Israel; the priest of the king’s sanctuary orders him home, and he answers that he did not choose this work, God did. In the Gospel Jesus calls the Twelve, sends them out two by two with authority over unclean spirits, tells them to take almost nothing for the road, and they go out to proclaim repentance, to cast out demons and to anoint the sick with oil. Between the two, the opening of the Letter to the Ephesians tells us why: before the foundation of the world God chose us in Christ and destined us to be his children, so that the whole plan of his will might gather all things in Christ.

Psalm 85, the Sunday’s psalm, is the prayer of the listener: “Let me hear what God the Lord will speak, for he will speak peace to his people”, and “Steadfast love and faithfulness will meet; righteousness and peace will kiss each other.” The word that sends the prophet and the apostles is a word of peace. The memorial of St Benedict, abbot and patron of Europe, falls on this Sunday and is not kept this year; the Mass is of the Sunday.

Amos 7:12–15 · The Lord took me from following the flock

Setting.

Amos prophesied in the northern kingdom of Israel around the middle of the eighth century before Christ, in the time of King Jeroboam II (about 790–750). It was a time of peace and prosperity, and of injustice: the rich sold the poor for a pair of sandals (2:6; 8:6). Amos was not from the north. He came from Tekoa, a village in Judah, south of Bethlehem (1:1), and he spoke harshly against the sins of the powerful. “Let justice roll down like waters” is his best known word (5:24).

Background. Bethel was one of the two royal sanctuaries of the northern kingdom (1 Kings 12:29). Its priest Amaziah heard Amos announce that Jeroboam would die and Israel would go into exile, and he reported it to the king (vv. 10–11, left out of the reading). The reading is Amaziah’s order and Amos’s answer. Amaziah speaks like a state official: go home and earn your bread there. This is one of the earliest accounts we have of a prophet explaining where his authority comes from.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: Amaziah tells Amos, the “seer”, to flee to Judah, earn his bread there and never again prophesy at Bethel, because it is the king’s sanctuary.
  • v. 14: Amos answers that he is no prophet by profession, but a herdsman and dresser of sycamore trees.
  • v. 15: the Lord took him from following the flock and sent him to prophesy to his people Israel.

Key words.

  • ḥōzeh, seer: Amaziah’s word for Amos. Seers could be paid for their service at sanctuaries; he tells Amos to take his gift elsewhere and “earn his bread”.
  • nābîʾ, prophet: One who speaks for God. Amos distances himself from the professional bands of prophets (“prophet’s son” means a member of such a guild). The Hebrew has no verb, so it can be read “I am no prophet” or “I was no prophet”; either way, Amos says his authority is not from his profession but from God’s call.
  • lāqaḥ, to take: “The Lord took me” (v. 15). The same verb is used when God takes a person for his purposes, as he took Abraham (Joshua 24:3) and David (2 Samuel 7:8). It is God who begins, not Amos.

Verse by verse.

“O seer, go, flee away to the land of Judah, earn your bread there, and prophesy there;” (7:12)

Amaziah does not deny that Amos has a message; he says it belongs in a place where no one will be disturbed by it. Religion tied to the king’s favour becomes unable to hear a word of God.

“but never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king’s sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom.” (7:13)

“The king’s sanctuary”: worship at Bethel had become part of the royal order. The prophet’s word is a challenge to the idea that God belongs to the state.

“I am no prophet, nor a prophet’s son; but I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees,” (7:14)

Amos names his ordinary work. A sycamore fig tree gave cheap food for the poor, and the dresser nipped the fruit so that it would ripen. He was a working man of Judah, not a member of a religious class.

“and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, ‘Go, prophesy to my people Israel.’” (7:15)

The call came in the midst of work, as it did for David (2 Samuel 7:8), and for the fishermen of Galilee (Mark 1:17). Notice also “my people Israel”: the one who sends him from Judah calls the northern kingdom his people.

How the Church reads it.

The Church confesses in the Creed that the Holy Spirit “has spoken through the prophets” (CCC 702). Amos stands for the prophet who does not choose his mission and cannot be silenced by those in power, and for the link between worship and justice that runs through the prophets: God does not accept sacrifices from a people who trample on the poor (Amos 5:21–24). The Church teaches that all the baptized share in Christ’s prophetic office, to bear witness to the truth (Lumen Gentium 12 and 35). The scene also invites us to remember that all who are sent, in every age, are sent by God and not by the favour of the powerful.

Ephesians 1:3–14 (or shorter: 1:3–10) · He chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter in the name of Paul, written from prison (3:1; 4:1; 6:20). Many scholars think it was written by a disciple of Paul who developed his thought, others hold that Paul himself wrote it; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture in the apostolic tradition either way. Some early manuscripts omit “in Ephesus” in the first verse, and some think it was a circular letter for the churches of Asia Minor. Its first part (chapters 1–3) praises the plan of God, and the second (chapters 4–6) draws out the life of the Church.

Background. In the Greek, vv. 3–14 are a single sentence, a hymn of blessing in the manner of the Jewish prayer “Blessed be God”. It moves through the work of the Trinity: the Father who chooses and destines (vv. 3–6), the Son in whom we have redemption (vv. 7–12), and the Holy Spirit who seals the believers (vv. 13–14). The refrain “to the praise of his glory” closes each part (vv. 6, 12, 14). The shorter form ends at v. 10.

Structure.

  • v. 3: blessing of the Father, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing.
  • vv. 4–6: the Father chose us in Christ before the world, to be holy, and destined us for adoption as his children.
  • vv. 7–10: in Christ we have redemption and the forgiveness of sins; the mystery of his will: to gather up all things in Christ.
  • vv. 11–14 (full form): the inheritance; those who first hoped in Christ, and “you also”; the seal of the Holy Spirit, the pledge of our inheritance.

Key words.

  • eulogētos, blessed: The Greek translation of the Jewish blessing bārûk. We bless God because he first blessed us (v. 3). The word begins the letter’s long hymn.
  • hyiothesia, adoption as children: God’s plan is not only to forgive but to make us children, by sharing in the Son (Galatians 4:5–6; Romans 8:15). The word is from the Roman legal practice of adoption.
  • anakephalaiōsasthai, to gather up, to sum up: Literally to bring under one head. All things in heaven and on earth are to be united under Christ as head (compare 1:22). St Irenaeus of Lyons, in the second century, built his teaching on this idea that Christ recapitulates, or sums up, all things in himself (in substance).
  • arrabōn, pledge, first instalment: A commercial word for a down payment that guarantees the rest. The Holy Spirit is God’s first gift, the guarantee that the full inheritance will follow.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places,” (1:3)

The letter begins with praise, not a request. The blessing is already given, “in Christ”: this phrase and its cousins appear again and again in vv. 3–14.

“just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” (1:4)

God’s choice comes before the world was made. It is a choice for holiness, for a life lived in love. The Church reads it as God’s call to all in Christ, not as a choice of some for hell.

“He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ,” (1:5)

The goal is a family. We are not servants only, but sons and daughters in the Son.

“In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace” (1:7)

Redemption was paid for by Christ’s blood, and the measure is the riches of grace. The Church sees here the offering of Christ on the Cross, made present in the Mass.

“as a plan for the fullness of time, to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” (1:10)

The great vision of the letter: the division between heaven and earth, between Jew and Gentile, and the brokenness of creation are all to be gathered up in Christ.

“were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit;” (1:13)

A seal marks ownership and security. Baptism and the gift of the Spirit mark the believer as belonging to Christ.

“this is the pledge of our inheritance toward redemption as God’s own people,” (1:14)

The Spirit is the guarantee. Our life now is a beginning, and the inheritance is to come.

How the Church reads it.

The Church places Ephesians 1:3–6 at the head of her teaching on the liturgy: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ” is the opening of the Catechism’s section on the blessing of the Father (CCC 1077). Christ’s whole life is a mystery of recapitulation, by which all things are summed up in him (CCC 518). On predestination the Church teaches that God’s eternal plan includes each person’s answer to his grace (CCC 600), and that God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). The reading also has a place for Israel: “we who were the first to set our hope on Christ” (v. 12) is generally understood as the Jewish believers, and “you also” (v. 13) as the Gentiles who join them. The Gentiles are brought into a hope that began with Israel.

Mark 6:7–13 · Sent out two by two

Setting.

This passage follows the rejection at Nazareth (6:1–6), when Jesus “was amazed at their unbelief” and went about the villages teaching. Mark then shows him sending out the Twelve, whom he had called to be “with him” and “to be sent out” (3:14). The mission of the Twelve in Mark is shorter than in Matthew 10, and tells what the disciples did rather than a long discourse.

Background. The disciples are sent “two by two”, which made their witness valid under the Jewish law (Deuteronomy 19:15) and gave them company. Mark allows a staff and sandals, which Matthew 10:10 does not, and the difference is a good example of how the evangelists adapt the tradition. The point in all of them is the same: dependence on God and on the hospitality of those who receive them. After the mission, the Twelve return and report all they did and taught (6:30).

Structure.

  • v. 7: Jesus calls the Twelve, sends them out two by two and gives them authority over unclean spirits.
  • vv. 8–9: they take only a staff and sandals: no bread, bag, money or second tunic.
  • vv. 10–11: stay in the house where they are received; if a place will not welcome them, shake off the dust as a testimony.
  • vv. 12–13: they proclaim repentance, cast out demons and anoint many sick people with oil.

Key words.

  • apostellein, to send out: The same root as apostolos, apostle. In 3:14 Jesus called them to be with him and to be sent: first the company, then the mission.
  • metanoēsōsin, that they should repent: From metanoia, a change of mind and heart, a turning to God. The message of the disciples is that of Jesus himself in 1:15.
  • aleiphein elaiō, to anoint with oil: In the ancient world oil was used as medicine (Luke 10:34) and as a sign of the Spirit. Here it is joined with the proclamation and the casting out of demons as a sign of the coming of the kingdom.

Verse by verse.

“He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” (6:7)

Jesus does not send them alone, and he does not send them empty. The authority is his, shared with them.

“He ordered them to take nothing for their journey except a staff; no bread, no bag, no money in their belts;” (6:8)

The disciples must look like pilgrims. They carry the message and nothing else, and learn to depend on the Father.

“but to wear sandals and not to put on two tunics.” (6:9)

Mark records even what they wore. The rule is plain: a traveller’s simplicity.

“shake off the dust that is on your feet as a testimony against them.” (6:11)

The gesture is a sign that the disciples have done their duty and that the responsibility passes to the one who refuses. It is a sad warning, not an act of anger.

“So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent.” (6:12)

The first mission of the Twelve is a mission of the word. The call to repent is a call to turn toward the God who is near.

“They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” (6:13)

The only mention in the Gospels of anointing the sick with oil in the ministry of the disciples; it is the root of the Church’s practice in James 5:14–15.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this scene both the mission of the whole Church and the roots of a sacrament. The Church has recognized in the anointing of the sick in Mark 6:13 and James 5:14–15 a rite instituted by the Lord (CCC 1510). The whole Church is sent: the Church is missionary by her very nature, because she is sent by the Father through the Son and the Spirit (Ad Gentes 2, CCC 850). The pairs recall the communal character of witness. Jesus shows that mission is not heroism but obedience: the disciples take what he gives and go where he sends. The Church also keeps the Gospel’s gentleness: when a place refuses the Gospel, the disciples go on, and the Church prays for those who refuse.

Questions people ask

Does Ephesians say that God chose some people for heaven and others for hell?

No. The Church teaches that God desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4) and predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). In Ephesians 1 the choice is “in Christ”, for holiness and adoption, and Paul speaks to a community of believers, giving thanks for their calling. The mystery of how God’s eternal plan and human freedom work together is held in faith, with the Catechism’s assurance that God’s plan includes each person’s response (CCC 600).

Why does Mark allow a staff and sandals when Matthew does not?

The Gospels have the same core instruction: travel light and trust God. The differences in detail show that each evangelist wrote for his own community and handed down the words of Jesus with some freedom of expression, while keeping their sense. The Church reads the four Gospels together and does not need to make them agree in every detail to hear the single message of simplicity.

For the Latin reader

On this Sunday the Latin Church would keep the memorial of St Benedict, abbot, patron of Europe, but a Sunday in Ordinary Time takes precedence, so the Mass is of the Sunday. The Gloria and Creed are said. The anointing of the sick, which is rooted in the Gospel of today, is celebrated in parishes and at the bedside, and in some parishes also in a communal celebration for the elderly. In the monsoon season it is fitting, especially in Kerala, to remember in this Sunday’s prayers the farmers and the families affected by the rains, and the Church prays also for all who suffer from floods and storms in many lands.

For study and teaching

  • Amos did not choose to be a prophet, but he did not refuse. What is the ordinary work in which God may be calling me?
  • Ephesians says God chose us “before the foundation of the world”. How does this change the way I think of my worth and my vocation?
  • Jesus sent the disciples two by two and with few things. Who is my companion in the mission, and what do I need to put down in order to travel lighter?
  • When the Gospel is not welcomed, how can I “shake off the dust” without anger and still pray for the people?
