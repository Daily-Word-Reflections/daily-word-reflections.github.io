Word for the day

“So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent. They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” Mark 6:12–13

Reflection

The first reading is one of the great scenes of the prophets. Amos was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees from Tekoa, a village in Judah, south of Jerusalem. About the middle of the eighth century before Christ, in the prosperous days of King Jeroboam II, God sent him north into the kingdom of Israel to speak against the luxury of the rich and the oppression of the poor (Amos 2:6–7; 5:24). He came to Bethel, the great shrine of the northern kingdom, and preached there. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, reported him to the king and then told Amos to his face: “O seer, go, flee away to the land of Judah, earn your bread there, and prophesy there; but never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom.” The priest takes it for granted that a prophet is a paid professional who can be sent to find work elsewhere, and that the shrine belongs to the king. Amos does not argue about credentials. “I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son; but I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees, and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, ‘Go, prophesy to my people Israel.’” He belongs to no guild of prophets; he has no career, no title and no salary. He has a call. The sycamore fig of Palestine bears the poor man's fruit, and it needs tending to ripen; so the man who looked after sheep and fig trees was taken by the Lord and sent to speak to a nation. Those whom God sends are seldom those whom the establishment would have chosen, and the word they carry belongs neither to the shrine nor to the king. Notice, too, to whom he is sent: “my people Israel.” The hard word of Amos is the word of a Father who wants his people to live justly and be saved. The Psalm gives the answer of those who listen: “Let me hear what God the Lord will speak, for he will speak peace to his people, to his faithful, to those who turn to him in their hearts,” and “Righteousness will go before him, and will make a path for his steps.”

The second reading is one of the most magnificent passages of the New Testament. In the Greek it is a single long sentence of praise, a hymn that sweeps from before the creation of the world to the final gathering of all things in Christ. Paul blesses God because God has first blessed us: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places.” Then he unfolds the story of salvation in three movements, one for each Person of the Trinity. The Father chose us: “just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love. He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will.” Before there was a world, God had you in mind, in Christ; your life is not an accident. The Son redeems us: “In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace that he lavished on us.” And he makes known the hidden plan, “the mystery of his will,” “as a plan for the fullness of time, to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” The Greek verb means to sum up under one head; St Irenaeus called it recapitulation. Christ, the new Adam, takes the whole of creation and human history into himself and sets it right. The Spirit seals us. Paul speaks first of “we,” the Jewish believers who were the first to hope in Christ, then of “you also,” the Gentiles; one Church is formed of two peoples. “In him you also, when you had heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and had believed in him, were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit; this is the pledge of our inheritance toward redemption as God's own people, to the praise of his glory.” The seal is the mark of belonging received in baptism and confirmation. The pledge is a first instalment, like the deposit that guarantees a purchase: we already have the Spirit, and are promised the fullness.

The Gospel follows directly on the sad scene of Nazareth, where Jesus was rejected in his hometown and “was amazed at their unbelief.” He does not stop. “He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” They go in pairs: because two witnesses make a testimony valid (Deuteronomy 19:15), because they support one another on the road, and because the Gospel is announced by a community and not by lone individuals. St Gregory the Great said (in substance) that the Lord sends them two by two because charity cannot exist with fewer than two. Their equipment is almost nothing: “He ordered them to take nothing for their journey except a staff; no bread, no bag, no money in their belts; but to wear sandals and not to put on two tunics.” A staff and sandals are exactly what the Israelites had in their hands and on their feet when they ate the Passover, ready to leave Egypt (Exodus 12:11). The missionaries are a people on the road of a new exodus, unburdened, and their security is the word they carry and the welcome they receive. “Wherever you enter a house, stay there until you leave the place”: they are not to shop around for a better table. And when they are rejected: “If any place will not welcome you and they refuse to hear you, as you leave, shake off the dust that is on your feet as a testimony against them.” It is a sign that they have done their part, not a curse. “So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent.” The message is the same as that of Jesus (Mark 1:15). “They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them”: the Church sees in this anointing a root of the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick (Catechism 1510). The three readings meet in one word: calling. Amos is taken from the flock, the Christian is chosen before the foundation of the world, and the Twelve are called and sent. We are chosen to be sent.

Homily outline

Theme: “Go, prophesy to my people.”

A shepherd is taken from his flock, all of us are chosen before the foundation of the world, and the Twelve are sent two by two with a staff and sandals.

1. The Lord took me from following the flock

  • No title, no salary.
  • The king's sanctuary and the word of God.
  • A call that does not wait for credentials.
“and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, ‘Go, prophesy to my people Israel.’” Amos 7:15
“While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.”” Acts 13:2

Apply: Ask the Lord this week what he is calling you to do, and take one concrete step.

2. He chose us in Christ

  • Blessed, chosen, redeemed, sealed.
  • Before the foundation of the world.
  • A plan to gather up all things.
“Let me hear what God the Lord will speak, for he will speak peace to his people, to his faithful, to those who turn to him in their hearts.” Psalm 85:8
“just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love. He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will,” Ephesians 1:4–5

Apply: Write down ten blessings you have received in Christ, and read them at supper with your family.

3. Two by two

  • Two witnesses.
  • One lifts up the other.
  • The Gospel is a shared life.
“Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil. For if they fall, one will lift up the other;” Ecclesiastes 4:9–10
“He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” Mark 6:7

Apply: Ask someone to join you in one act of service this week: a visit, a prayer, a gift.

4. Take nothing but a staff

  • Dressed for the Passover.
  • Welcome in ordinary homes.
  • Repent, heal, anoint.
“This is how you shall eat it: your loins girded, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and you shall eat it hurriedly. It is the passover of the Lord.” Exodus 12:11
“So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent. They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” Mark 6:12–13

Apply: Set aside one thing that weighs you down this week, and use the time for God or for a neighbour.

God calls whom he wishes, a shepherd, a fisherman, a tax collector, and chose each of us in Christ before the foundation of the world.

The Twelve are sent two by two with almost nothing, to proclaim repentance and heal; the Church continues that mission.

Take-home resolution: Do one thing this week that the Lord is asking of you, and do it with someone else.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the priest Amaziah tells the prophet Amos: go away. Go back to Judah, earn your bread there, and do not prophesy at Bethel. And Amos answers with one of the simplest and bravest sentences in the Bible: I am no prophet, nor a prophet's son; but I am a herdsman, and a dresser of sycamore trees, and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said to me, Go, prophesy to my people Israel (Amos 7:14–15). He has no title. He has no training. But he has a call, and a call does not need the permission of a king.

Brothers and sisters, God still calls like that. He calls fishermen and tax collectors, farmers and teachers, nurses and drivers, grandmothers who pray the rosary every evening. He does not ask for credentials. He asks: Will you go? And St Paul tells us in the second reading that this call is older than our own lives: he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love (Ephesians 1:4). Before there was a world, God had you in his mind and in his heart. You are not an accident. You are wanted.

In the Gospel Jesus sends out the Twelve. Two by two. Why two? Because the Gospel is not a private opinion; it is a shared life. Because when one is tired, the other holds him up; when one falls, the other lifts him. Think of the families, the couples, the pairs of friends who have carried the Gospel in our own land. And what do they take? A staff and sandals. No bread, no bag, no money. They are dressed like Israel on the night of the Passover, ready to go. They can go anywhere, because they carry nothing but the word.

What will they say? That all should repent. And what will they do? Cast out demons, and anoint with oil many who were sick and heal them. The Church continues this mission. In the sacrament of Anointing of the Sick, in confession, in every Mass, the apostolic work goes on. And you and I are asked to join it in our own small ways: a word, a visit, a prayer, a kindness to someone who is carrying a heavy load.

So this week, let us ask ourselves: what is the Lord sending me to do? What is the staff and sandals I need? And whom will I take with me? Let us go, trusting that the One who sends us is the One who blessed us with every spiritual blessing in Christ. Amen.

For children

Children, the Gospel today tells us that Jesus had twelve special friends, the apostles. He sent them out to tell people about God, and he sent them two by two, so that nobody would be alone. They took only a walking stick and sandals. They did not need to carry anything else, because God would look after them. Jesus wants to send you too! You can bring his love to your school, your friends and your family. You can help a child who is lonely. You can say a prayer for someone who is sick. And it is much more fun when you do it together with a friend!

Prayer

O God, who show the light of your truth to those who go astray, so that they may return to the right path, give all who for the faith they profess are accounted Christians the grace to reject whatever is contrary to the name of Christ and to strive after all that does it honour. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Father, you chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world; you took Amos from his flock and the Twelve from their nets, and you call us by name. Give us the lightness of those who carry only a staff and sandals, trusting in you. Make us a blessing, as you have blessed us; send us out two by two with the Gospel and the oil of healing; and when we are rejected, keep us from bitterness. Seal us with your Spirit until we enter into our inheritance. Amen.
