Word for the day

“Pray then in this way: Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.” Matthew 6:9–10

Reflection

In the last chapters of the Second Letter to the Corinthians St Paul defends his ministry against some visiting preachers whom he calls, with irony, “super-apostles.” They were eloquent, they demanded payment, and they belittled Paul. He hates to boast, so he asks the Corinthians to put up with “a little foolishness.” Then he opens his heart: “I feel a divine jealousy for you, for I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” In the customs of the time, a friend of the family arranged a betrothal and kept the bride safe until the wedding. Paul sees himself as that friend: he brought the Corinthians to Christ, and he wants to present them to their Bridegroom with an undivided heart. His fear is that “as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning, your thoughts will be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ.” The danger is “another Jesus,” “a different spirit,” “a different gospel.” The Church has always loved this image of herself as the virgin bride of Christ, and the Fathers saw its perfect fulfilment in the Blessed Virgin Mary, whose heart was wholly given to God (Lumen Gentium 63–64). Paul also explains why he never took money from the Corinthians: he “proclaimed God's good news” to them without charge, supported by the brothers from Macedonia, so that no one could say he preached for profit. And why? “Because I do not love you? God knows I do!”

In the Gospel Jesus teaches his disciples how to pray. First he warns against a mistake: “When you are praying, do not heap up empty phrases as the Gentiles do; for they think that they will be heard because of their many words.” Prayer is not a machine in which more words produce more results. “Your Father knows what you need before you ask him.” Then why pray at all? Because prayer is not informing God; it is being with him as children with a Father, and letting our hearts be formed by his. So Jesus gives the prayer that has been on Christian lips ever since: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.” The first three petitions are about God: his name, his kingdom, his will. Only then come our needs: “Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And do not bring us to the time of trial, but rescue us from the evil one.” Tertullian called this prayer “the summary of the whole Gospel” (Catechism 2761), and the early Christians prayed it three times a day (Didache 8). Jesus adds a comment on one petition only, the one about forgiveness: “For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you; but if you do not forgive others, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.” We cannot pray “Our Father” with a closed heart towards our brothers and sisters.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul speaks today like a loving father of the bride: I feel a divine jealousy for you, for I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2). The Church is the bride of Christ. Each of us, by Baptism, belongs to him. Paul's fear is that our hearts may be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:3), by another gospel, by false teachers, by the attractions of the world.

How do we keep our hearts for Christ? Above all, by prayer. And in the Gospel Jesus teaches us how to pray. He says: do not heap up empty phrases, for your Father knows what you need before you ask him (Matthew 6:7–8). Sometimes we think that the longer our prayer, the more God will listen. But God is not tired by our words or impressed by them. He is our Father. He wants our hearts.

Then Jesus gives us the Our Father. Notice the order. First: hallowed be your name, your kingdom come, your will be done. Only after that: give us bread, forgive us, protect us. When we put God first, our own needs find their right place. And notice that we say our Father, not my Father. We never pray alone; we pray as one family.

Finally, Jesus explains only one line: the line about forgiveness. If you do not forgive others, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses (Matthew 6:15). Every time we pray the Our Father, we are asking God to forgive us in the same way we forgive. Today, let us pray it slowly, and let us mean it, especially those difficult words: as we forgive those who trespass against us. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus taught us a very special prayer: the Our Father. Do you know it by heart? Jesus said we do not need to use lots and lots of words, because God already knows what we need. In the Our Father we call God our Daddy in heaven! We ask him for food, we say sorry, and we promise to forgive others. Tonight, pray it slowly with your family, and think about each word.

Prayer

Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name; your kingdom come; your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Keep us, your Church, a chaste bride for your Son, faithful to the one Jesus, the one Spirit and the one Gospel we have received. Give us today the bread we need, the bread of the earth and the Bread of life. Forgive us as we forgive those who have wronged us, and do not bring us to the time of trial, but rescue us from the evil one. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the last chapters of the Second Letter to the Corinthians, Paul defends his ministry against visiting preachers he calls “super-apostles”. He speaks as a father who has promised his church to Christ as a chaste bride, and fears she may be led astray. In the Gospel Jesus teaches his disciples the Our Father. A sincere and pure devotion to Christ, which Paul longs for, is taught and nourished in the prayer Jesus gave us.

2 Corinthians 11:1–11 · A chaste virgin for Christ

Setting.

Chapters 10–13 of the letter are sharper in tone than what comes before. Some scholars think they were once a separate letter; others read the whole as one letter written in stages. In either case Paul is answering preachers who came to Corinth, boasted of their gifts and eloquence, accepted payment, and belittled him.

Paul, who dislikes boasting, decides to “boast a little” in order to win the Corinthians back. He calls it “a little foolishness”.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: “bear with me”: Paul’s divine jealousy; the church betrothed to Christ; the fear of the serpent’s deceit.
  • vv. 4–6: another Jesus, another spirit, another gospel; Paul not inferior to the super-apostles.
  • vv. 7–11: why Paul preached without payment in Corinth: out of love.

Key words.

  • zēlos theou, divine jealousy: Literally “the jealousy of God”: the zeal of the God of the covenant for his people, his bride (Hosea 2:19–20). Paul shares God’s own zeal for the Corinthians.
  • hērmosamēn, I promised you in marriage: A word for arranging a betrothal. Paul is like the friend or father who betroths the bride and must present her pure on the wedding day.
  • hyperlian apostoloi, super-apostles: An ironic name: literally “the very-extra apostles”. Paul uses it for the visiting preachers, probably not for the Twelve.

Verse by verse.

“I feel a divine jealousy for you, for I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” (11:2)

The image of God’s people as a bride runs from the prophets to Revelation (Isaiah 62:5; Revelation 21:2). The whole Church, and each soul, is betrothed to Christ.

“as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning,” (11:3)

Paul recalls Genesis 3. The danger is not open hostility but a clever voice that draws the heart away from simple devotion.

“And why? Because I do not love you? God knows I do!” (11:11)

Paul’s refusal of payment was not coldness but love. He did not want anything to stand between the Corinthians and the Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council calls the Church the spotless spouse of the spotless Lamb (Lumen Gentium 6). The Church sees in this verse the meaning of consecrated virginity: virgins consecrated to God are a sign of the Church as the Bride of Christ, and of the world to come (CCC 922–923, 1619). But every Christian, married or single, is called to “a sincere and pure devotion to Christ”.

Matthew 6:7–15 · Pray then in this way

Setting.

In the middle of his teaching on almsgiving, prayer and fasting, Jesus pauses to give his disciples a model of prayer. Matthew’s form of the Our Father, with seven petitions, is the one the Church prays. Luke gives a shorter form, taught when a disciple asks, “Lord, teach us to pray” (Luke 11:1–4).

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: do not heap up empty phrases; the Father knows what you need.
  • v. 9: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name.”
  • v. 10: your kingdom come, your will be done.
  • vv. 11–13: bread, forgiveness, protection in trial and from the evil one.
  • vv. 14–15: forgive others, and the Father will forgive you.

Key words.

  • battalogeō, heap up empty phrases: A rare word, perhaps meaning to babble. Jesus does not forbid long prayer or repetition (he himself prayed the same words three times in Gethsemane, Matthew 26:44); he forbids thinking we will be heard because of many words.
  • epiousios, daily: A rare Greek word, found almost nowhere outside the Lord’s Prayer. It can mean “for this day”, “for tomorrow”, or “super-substantial”. The Fathers heard in it both our daily food and the Bread of Life, the Eucharist (CCC 2837).
  • opheilēmata, debts: Sin is pictured as a debt owed to God. Luke’s form says “sins” (Luke 11:4). In v. 14 Matthew uses “trespasses”.

Verse by verse.

“for your Father knows what you need before you ask him.” (6:8)

We do not pray to inform God, but to open our hearts to him as children.

“Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name.” (6:9)

Jesus lets us call God what he calls him: Father. “Our”, not “my”: we never pray alone.

“Give us this day our daily bread.” (6:11)

A prayer for each day, like the manna in the desert, given day by day (Exodus 16:4).

“And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.” (6:12)

The only petition Jesus comments on afterwards (vv. 14–15). Our forgiving does not earn God’s mercy, but a heart closed to others cannot receive it.

“And do not bring us to the time of trial, but rescue us from the evil one.” (6:13)

The Greek ponēros can mean evil or the evil one. The Church understands it of Satan, the one who opposes God (CCC 2851).

How the Church reads it.

Tertullian called the Lord’s Prayer a summary of the whole Gospel, and St Augustine said that if we run through all the prayers of Scripture we will find nothing that is not contained in it (CCC 2761–2762, in substance). The Didache told Christians to pray it three times a day (CCC 2767). In the Mass it is prayed before Communion, and the priest’s prayer and the people’s doxology “For the kingdom, the power and the glory are yours” follow it. That doxology is not in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew, but it is very ancient, and a shorter form of it is found in the Didache. The Catechism teaches that the mercy of God cannot penetrate our hearts as long as we have not forgiven those who have trespassed against us (CCC 2840).

Questions people ask

Why do some Christians end the Our Father with “For thine is the kingdom”, while Catholics do not say it at the end?

The doxology is an ancient addition found in some manuscripts and in the Didache, but not in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew. Catholics do pray it at Mass, after the priest’s prayer that follows the Our Father. So it is not a difference of faith, only of where the words are placed.

For study and teaching

  • Pray the Our Father slowly, pausing after each line. Which petition is hardest for me to mean?
  • Is there someone I must forgive before I can pray “as we also have forgiven our debtors” honestly?
