Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!” Jesus said to him, “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.”” John 20:28–29

Reflection

Eight days ago the Church sang, “This is the day that the LORD has made.” Today, on the eighth day, the Octave Day of Easter, she sings the same psalm again, and the refrain falls like a drumbeat of mercy: “Let Israel say, ‘His steadfast love endures forever.’ Let the house of Aaron say, ‘His steadfast love endures forever.’ Let those who fear the LORD say, ‘His steadfast love endures forever.’” The first reading shows what that mercy does when it takes hold of a community. “Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common.” Luke places at the centre of this picture the preaching of the Resurrection: “With great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all.” And the fruit is very concrete: “There was not a needy person among them.” Easter faith is not only a conviction in the mind; it opens hands and purses. Mercy received becomes mercy shared.

St John's First Letter tells us where this new life comes from. “Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the parent loves the child.” To love God is to love his children, and to keep his commandments, which “are not burdensome.” John then gives one of the great Easter sentences of the New Testament: “this is the victory that conquers the world, our faith.” Not our cleverness, not our strength, but faith in the Son of God. Finally John speaks of Jesus as “the one who came by water and blood, Jesus Christ, not with the water only but with the water and the blood.” He is thinking of the Jordan, where Jesus was baptized, and of Calvary, where his side was pierced and “at once blood and water came out” (John 19:34). The Fathers saw in that water and blood the sacraments of Baptism and the Eucharist, flowing from the Heart of Christ to give birth to the Church. On this Sunday the newly baptized of Easter night come to the end of their first week in Christ, washed in that water and fed with that Blood.

The Gospel is read on this Sunday every year. It is the evening of Easter Day. The disciples are behind locked doors, “for fear of the Jews,” as John puts it; the disciples were Jews themselves, and John means the Temple authorities who had handed Jesus over, not the Jewish people as such. Into that locked room of fear the Risen Lord comes: “Peace be with you.” He shows them his hands and his side, so that they may know the one who stands before them is the one who was crucified. “Then the disciples rejoiced when they saw the Lord.” And at once he sends them: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” He breathes on them, as God breathed the breath of life into Adam at the creation, and says: “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.” On the very first Easter night the Risen Lord gives his Church the power to forgive sins in his name. The Council of Trent taught that in these words Christ instituted the sacrament of Penance. Every confessional is a little upper room where the wounded hands of Christ are raised in absolution and the same words are heard: peace be with you.

Thomas was not there. When the others say, “We have seen the Lord,” he answers with hard words: “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” A week later, on the next first day of the week, the disciples are together again and Thomas is with them. Jesus comes, says “Peace be with you,” and turns to Thomas with his own words: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” The Gospel never says that Thomas touched him. It says only that he cried out the greatest confession of faith in all the Gospels: “My Lord and my God!” The doubter becomes the one who names Jesus God. Then the Lord speaks to us, who were not there: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” In Kerala this Sunday is widely called Puthu Njayar, New Sunday, and many pilgrims climb the hill of Malayattoor, where tradition says St Thomas prayed. And this is why the Church calls it the Sunday of Divine Mercy. The wounds that Thomas was invited to touch are not closed; they have become the open door of mercy. St Faustina saw the Lord with two rays streaming from his Heart, pale and red, the water and the blood, and beneath him the prayer every sinner may make: Jesus, I trust in you. John tells us why he wrote: “so that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and that through believing you may have life in his name.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, it is evening on the first Easter day, and the disciples are behind locked doors, afraid. Then Jesus comes and stands among them and says: peace be with you (John 20:19). No reproach, no anger, only peace. And he shows them his hands and his side.

Many of us live behind locked doors too: doors of fear, of guilt, of old hurts that we will not let anyone see. The Risen Lord is not stopped by those doors. He comes in, and the first thing he shows us is his wounds, the proof of how much he has loved us.

Then he breathes on them and says: receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them (John 20:22–23). On the very first Easter night Jesus gives his Church the sacrament of mercy. When we go to confession, it is the Risen Lord himself who says through the priest: your sins are forgiven; go in peace.

Thomas was not there, and he would not believe. A week later Jesus comes again, just for him: put your finger here and see my hands. And Thomas cries out: my Lord and my God! (John 20:28). The one who doubted most makes the greatest act of faith. The Lord is patient with our doubts too.

This is why today is called the Sunday of Divine Mercy. Jesus showed St Faustina his Heart, with rays of water and blood flowing out, and asked her to teach the world a simple prayer: Jesus, I trust in you. It is enough to begin there. And mercy, once received, must be shared. The first Christians were of one heart and soul, and there was not a needy person among them (Acts 4:32, 34).

So let us leave this Mass with three things: a heart at peace, because we are forgiven; the words of Thomas on our lips, my Lord and my God; and open hands for the poor. Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe. That blessing is for us. Jesus, I trust in you. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Thomas said: I will not believe unless I touch Jesus' wounds. Then Jesus came and said: Thomas, come and see! And Thomas said: my Lord and my God!

We cannot see Jesus with our eyes, but we believe in him, and Jesus says we are blessed. Let us say together: Jesus, I trust in you!

Prayer

God of everlasting mercy, who in the very recurrence of the paschal feast kindle the faith of the people you have made your own, increase, we pray, the grace you have bestowed, that all may grasp and rightly understand in what font they have been washed, by whose Spirit they have been reborn, by whose Blood they have been redeemed. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Merciful Jesus, you came through the locked doors of our fear and showed us your wounds. Breathe your Spirit upon us, forgive our sins, heal our doubts, and make us one heart and one soul in your Church. With Thomas we confess you: my Lord and my God. Jesus, I trust in you. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The eighth day after Easter brings us back to the upper room, “a week later”, and the Gospel is the same in all three years of the Lectionary: the risen Lord breathes the Holy Spirit on the apostles and gives them power to forgive sins, and then he meets Thomas. Everything else today gathers around that Gospel. Acts shows the community that the Resurrection has made: one heart and soul, nobody in need. Psalm 118, the great Easter psalm, sings that his steadfast love endures forever. The First Letter of John tells the believer that faith is the victory that conquers the world, and points to the one who came by water and blood.

Since the year 2000, by decision of St John Paul II, this Sunday is also called the Sunday of Divine Mercy. The readings fit the title well: mercy in the forgiveness of sins (John), mercy in the sharing of goods (Acts), mercy that endures forever (the psalm), mercy flowing as water and blood (1 John). The optional Sequence of Easter may still be sung today. The key words are peace, mercy and believe.

Acts 4:32–35 · One heart and soul

Setting.

This is the second of Luke's summaries of life in the first Jerusalem community (the first is 2:42–47; the third, 5:12–16). Each describes what the Church looked like when the Spirit had just been given. This one comes straight after the community has prayed for boldness and been shaken by the Spirit (4:23–31). Luke goes on at once to two contrasting examples: Barnabas, who gives generously (4:36–37), and Ananias and Sapphira, who pretend to (5:1–11).

Structure.

  • v. 32: one heart and soul; no one claims anything as his own; all held in common.
  • v. 33: great power in the apostles' testimony to the Resurrection; great grace on all.
  • vv. 34–35: no one in need; owners sell and lay the price at the apostles' feet; each receives as he needs.

Key words.

  • kardia kai psychē mia, one heart and soul: A Greek proverb said that friends have one soul. Luke goes further: the whole community, not only a pair of friends, is one. The unity is the work of the Spirit, not of agreement alone.
  • koina, in common: The sharing was a willing act of love, not a law: Peter will say to Ananias that the land and the money remained his own to dispose of (5:4). What mattered was that nobody held on to his goods against a brother in need.
  • endeēs, needy: The word recalls Deuteronomy 15:4 in the Greek Old Testament: “there will be no one in need among you”. The promise of the law is fulfilled in the community of the Risen One.

Verse by verse.

“Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common.” (4:32)

Unity of heart comes first, then the sharing of goods. The second grows out of the first.

“With great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all.” (4:33)

This verse stands in the middle of a passage about money, and it explains it. A community that believes Christ is risen can hold its possessions loosely.

“There was not a needy person among them,” (4:34)

This is the test Luke applies. The measure of a Christian community is not only how well it prays, but whether anyone among them is left in need.

“They laid it at the apostles’ feet, and it was distributed to each as any had need.” (4:35)

To lay something at someone's feet was a sign of handing it over to his authority. The gifts are entrusted to the apostles, and from the start the Church's care for the poor is organised and ordered.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of the communion of saints, quotes this verse: everything the true Christian has is to be regarded as a good possessed in common with everyone else, and Christians should be ready to come to the help of the needy (CCC 952). The Church has never made common ownership a law for all; she defends the right to private property and also teaches the universal destination of goods (CCC 2402–2404). Religious communities, which hold their goods in common, have always looked to this passage, and St Augustine's Rule opens with it (in substance).

Psalm 118:2–4, 13–15, 22–24 · His steadfast love endures forever

Setting.

Psalm 118 is the last of the Hallel psalms (113–118), sung at the great feasts of Israel and at the Passover meal. It was very likely the hymn Jesus and the disciples sang before going out to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26). It is a song of thanksgiving by someone rescued from death, entering the Temple gates in procession, and the whole people joins in. The Church sings it throughout the Easter Octave.

Structure.

  • vv. 2–4: a three-part call: Israel, the house of Aaron, those who fear the Lord: his steadfast love endures forever.
  • vv. 13–15: “I was pushed hard, so that I was falling, but the Lord helped me”; songs of victory.
  • vv. 22–24: the rejected stone; this is the Lord's doing; this is the day the Lord has made.

Key words.

  • ḥesed, steadfast love: God's faithful covenant love, the love that does not give up. The Latin version renders it misericordia, mercy, and so this psalm stands well on the Sunday of Divine Mercy.
  • ’eben mā’ăsû habbônîm, the stone the builders rejected: In the psalm, the one rejected and then rescued; in the New Testament, Christ rejected and raised (Matthew 21:42; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“Let Israel say, “His steadfast love endures forever.”” (118:2)

The refrain was a response sung by the people. The Church sings it with Israel, not instead of Israel.

“I was pushed hard, so that I was falling, but the Lord helped me.” (118:13)

On Easter lips this is the voice of Christ, who was pushed all the way into death and was raised up.

“This is the day that the Lord has made; let us rejoice and be glad in it.” (118:24)

The Roman liturgy sings this verse throughout the Easter Octave. Each day of the eight is counted as one great day.

How the Church reads it.

Jesus himself applies verse 22 to his rejection (Mark 12:10–11), and the apostles proclaim it of his Resurrection (Acts 4:11). The Church has made verse 24 the song of Easter, and in the Roman Rite it is sung in the Gradual or the responsory at Easter and through the Octave. On this Sunday the refrain “his steadfast love endures forever” becomes the Church's thanks for Divine Mercy.

1 John 5:1–6 · This is the victory that conquers the world

Setting.

The First Letter of John was written in the circle of the Fourth Gospel, toward the end of the first century, to communities troubled by people who had left them (2:19). Some of those who left seem to have denied that Jesus truly came in the flesh (4:2–3). The letter keeps returning to two tests of true faith: to believe rightly about Jesus, and to love one's brothers and sisters. In these verses the two tests come together. Throughout the Easter season, Year B reads from this letter on its Sundays.

Structure.

  • v. 1: whoever believes that Jesus is the Christ is born of God; whoever loves the parent loves the child.
  • vv. 2–3: love of God shows itself in keeping his commandments, which are not burdensome.
  • vv. 4–5: what is born of God conquers the world; our faith is the victory.
  • v. 6: the one who came by water and blood; the Spirit testifies.

Key words.

  • gegennētai ek tou theou, born of God: A theme of John's Gospel and letters (John 1:13; 3:3–5). Faith is not only an opinion. It is a new birth, the life of a child of God.
  • nikē, victory: The only time this noun occurs in the New Testament. It echoes Jesus' words at the Last Supper: “take courage; I have conquered the world” (John 16:33).
  • di’ hydatos kai haimatos, by water and blood: Many scholars think John means Jesus' baptism in the Jordan and his death on the Cross: he did not only come by water, as some taught, but truly suffered and died. Many Fathers also heard an echo of the water and blood from his pierced side (John 19:34).

Verse by verse.

“Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the parent loves the child.” (5:1)

A family logic: if you love the Father, you love his children. Love of God and love of neighbour are not two loves but one.

“For the love of God is this, that we obey his commandments. And his commandments are not burdensome,” (5:3)

Not because they are easy, but because the one who is born of God has received the strength to keep them. Love makes the yoke light (Matthew 11:30).

“for whatever is born of God conquers the world. And this is the victory that conquers the world, our faith.” (5:4)

The “world” here means the power that opposes God. The victory has already been won by Christ, and faith is how we share in it.

“This is the one who came by water and blood, Jesus Christ, not with the water only but with the water and the blood.” (5:6)

John insists on the blood: the Christ who saves us truly died. A Christianity without the Cross is not the faith of the apostles.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the blood and water that flowed from the pierced side of the crucified Jesus are types of Baptism and the Eucharist, the sacraments of new life (CCC 1225). Many Fathers saw the Church herself born from the side of Christ (CCC 766). The image of Divine Mercy given to St Faustina shows two rays, pale and red, streaming from the heart of Jesus; she understood them as the water and blood, which make souls righteous and give them life.

John 20:19–31 · My Lord and my God!

Setting.

It is the evening of the first Easter day. The disciples are behind locked doors “for fear of the Jews”. In John's Gospel this phrase usually means the Temple authorities who had handed Jesus over, not the Jewish people as such; the disciples themselves were Jews. Jesus comes through the locked doors, as the Risen One who is no longer bound by space.

The passage has two scenes a week apart, both on the first day of the week, and an ending (vv. 30–31) that tells us why the whole Gospel was written. Thomas, absent the first time, sets his own conditions for believing. The second Sunday answers him.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: evening of the first day; locked doors; “Peace be with you”; hands and side; joy.
  • vv. 21–23: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you”; he breathes on them; the power to forgive and retain sins.
  • vv. 24–25: Thomas absent: “Unless I see … I will not believe.”
  • vv. 26–29: a week later; “Put your finger here”; “My Lord and my God!”; “Blessed are those who have not seen.”
  • vv. 30–31: why this book was written: that you may believe and have life.

Key words.

  • eirēnē hymin, peace be with you: The ordinary Jewish greeting, shalom. On the lips of the risen Lord it becomes the gift he promised at the Supper (14:27), and it is said to men who had abandoned him. It is the first word of mercy.
  • enephysēsen, he breathed on them: The same rare verb the Greek Old Testament uses when God breathes the breath of life into the first man (Genesis 2:7). The risen Christ begins a new creation.
  • meth’ hēmeras oktō, a week later (literally, after eight days): Counted in the ancient way, this is again the first day of the week. From the start the risen Lord meets his Church on Sunday.

Verse by verse.

“When it was evening on that day, the first day of the week, and the doors of the house where the disciples had met were locked for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you.”” (20:19)

Fear locks doors, and Jesus comes through them. He does not reproach the disciples for running away. His first word is peace.

“When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them, “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.”” (20:22–23)

The first gift of the Risen One to his Church is the power to forgive. Mercy is not left to feelings. It is entrusted to the apostles as a ministry.

“Then he said to Thomas, “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.”” (20:27)

Jesus takes up Thomas's own conditions, almost word for word. He had heard. He meets the doubter where he is, and offers him the wounds.

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” (20:28)

The highest confession of faith in the Gospels. John's Gospel began, “the Word was God” (1:1), and it ends its main part on the lips of a doubter who now believes.

“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

The last beatitude of the Gospels, and it is spoken for us. Every generation after the apostles believes through their word (17:20).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the risen Lord gave the apostles his own power to forgive sins when he breathed on them, and it cites this text as the institution of the sacrament of Penance (CCC 976, 1461, 1485). The Council of Trent taught the same. It takes Thomas's words as a confession of Jesus' divinity (CCC 448), and notes the apostles' doubt as evidence that their faith was not their own invention (CCC 644). St Gregory the Great preached that the unbelief of Thomas did more for our faith than the faith of the believing disciples, because as he touched the wounds he healed the wound of our doubt (in substance).

Questions people ask

Why is this called the Sunday of Divine Mercy?

St Faustina Kowalska, a Polish sister who died in 1938, recorded in her diary that Jesus asked for a feast of mercy on the Sunday after Easter. When St John Paul II canonized her on 30 April 2000, he declared that this Sunday would be called Divine Mercy Sunday throughout the Church, and the Congregation for Divine Worship decreed it that year. The name adds to the Sunday; it does not replace it. Its Mass and readings remain those of the Second Sunday of Easter, and they are already a feast of mercy.

Is “For fear of the Jews” anti-Jewish?

No. Jesus and all his disciples were Jews. In John's Gospel “the Jews” often means the Temple authorities who opposed Jesus, and the disciples feared that they would be arrested next. The Church teaches that the Jewish people as a whole are not to be blamed for Christ's death, and must never be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

Does John 20:23 really mean confession to a priest?

The Church has always understood these words as giving the apostles and their successors the power to forgive and to retain sins (CCC 1461). To forgive or retain, they must know what the sins are, so the sacrament includes confession by the penitent, and the bishops and priests exercise this ministry (CCC 1456, 1461). The Council of Trent taught that this is the meaning of the text.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman Rite this Sunday ends the Octave of Easter, and it was once called Dominica in albis, “Sunday in white”, when the newly baptized laid aside their white robes. Many parishes add devotions of Divine Mercy after Mass or at three in the afternoon: the image with the words “Jesus, I trust in you”, the Chaplet of Divine Mercy, and confession. A plenary indulgence was granted for this day in 2002 on the usual conditions (sacramental confession, Communion, prayer for the Pope's intentions, and detachment from all sin). The heart of the feast is in the Gospel itself: go to confession, and receive the mercy that Christ entrusted to his Church on the first Easter evening.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus' first word to the disciples who had abandoned him was “Peace”. Whom do I need to greet with peace rather than reproach?
  • When did I last receive the sacrament of Penance? What keeps me from it?
  • “There was not a needy person among them.” What does that ask of my parish and my family?
  • Thomas believed when he saw the wounds. Where do I meet the wounds of Christ today, in the poor, the sick, the suffering?
