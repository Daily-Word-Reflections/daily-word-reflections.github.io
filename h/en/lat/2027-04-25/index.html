Readings

  • First Reading Acts 9:26–31 · Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles
  • Responsorial Psalm Psalm 22:25–26, 27 and 29, 30–31 · I will praise you, Lord, in the assembly of your people (or: Alleluia)
  • Second Reading 1 John 3:18–24 · Let us love, not in word or speech, but in truth and action
  • Gospel John 15:1–8 · I am the vine, you are the branches

Word for the day

“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” John 15:5

Reflection

The first reading shows us Saul of Tarsus after his conversion, trying to join the disciples in Jerusalem. It does not go well. “They were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple.” And who could blame them? Only a short time before he had been dragging Christians to prison. Saul has met the risen Lord, but the Church does not yet trust him. Then one man changes everything: “Barnabas took him, brought him to the apostles, and described for them how on the road he had seen the Lord, who had spoken to him, and how in Damascus he had spoken boldly in the name of Jesus.” Barnabas, the “son of encouragement,” takes a risk on a man everyone else feared. Without Barnabas, perhaps there would have been no St Paul as we know him. Soon Saul is preaching boldly in Jerusalem, his life is threatened again, and the believers send him off to Tarsus to keep him safe. Luke ends with a beautiful summary: “the church throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria had peace and was built up. Living in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, it increased in numbers.”

The Psalm is the second half of Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus prayed on the cross: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” On Palm Sunday of the Passion we sang from its first half, the cry of anguish. In the Easter season the Church sings its second half, the song of thanksgiving of one who has been rescued: “From you comes my praise in the great congregation.” “The poor shall eat and be satisfied.” “All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the Lord.” The one who was forsaken now praises God in the assembly, and his deliverance will be told “to a people yet unborn.” That is the Resurrection, and that is the Church, the great congregation where the risen Christ praises the Father with us.

The second reading, from the First Letter of John, is short and very practical: “Little children, let us love, not in word or speech, but in truth and action.” Christian love is not a feeling or a speech; it is shown in deeds. Then John speaks tenderly to troubled consciences: “whenever our hearts condemn us; for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything.” When we feel unworthy, our feelings do not have the last word; God does. And he sums up the whole of Christian life in one commandment with two parts: “that we should believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another.” Faith and love are not two separate things; they are one life. “All who obey his commandments abide in him, and he abides in them. And by this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit that he has given us.”

The Gospel gives us one of the richest images of the whole Bible. At the Last Supper Jesus says: “I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinegrower.” In the Old Testament Israel was God's vine, planted with love and tended with care (Isaiah 5; Psalm 80). Now Jesus says that he himself is the true vine, and we are the branches. The life of the branch is the life of the vine; cut off, it withers. “Abide in me as I abide in you.” The word “abide” appears again and again: to stay, to remain, to make one's home. And the Father prunes: “Every branch that bears fruit he prunes to make it bear more fruit.” Pruning hurts, but it is not punishment; it is the vinegrower's care, so that the branch may bear more. “Apart from me you can do nothing.” We cannot love as 1 John asks by our own effort; we can only love if the life of Christ flows in us. The Barnabas of the first reading is such a branch: his encouragement of Paul was the fruit of Christ's own life in him. Today would ordinarily be the feast of St Mark the Evangelist, the same John Mark who was Barnabas's cousin. This year the Sunday takes its place, but we may thank God for him, who wrote the Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God, so that it could bear fruit in every age.

Full homily

Dear friends, if you cut a branch from a mango tree, for a day or two it stays green. It looks alive. But it has no future. In a few days its leaves are dry. Jesus says today: I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing (John 15:5).

Look at the first reading. Saul, the former persecutor, comes to Jerusalem, but everyone is afraid of him. Nobody trusts him. Then Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles (Acts 9:27). Barnabas trusted when others feared. He gave Paul a place in the Church.

Where did Barnabas get that courage and that generosity? From the Vine. He was a branch joined to Christ, and the fruit of Christ's life in him was encouragement. Is there someone in our parish, perhaps someone with a bad past, a broken family, a different background, who needs a Barnabas? Will you be that person?

St John tells us in the second reading: little children, let us love, not in word or speech, but in truth and action (1 John 3:18). Our love must be seen. And if our hearts accuse us, if we feel we are not good enough, John says: God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything. Do not let guilt cut you off from the Vine; bring it to him in confession.

Jesus also speaks of pruning. The Father prunes every fruitful branch to make it bear more fruit. Sometimes life cuts us: illness, loss, failure. It hurts. But in the Father's hands, pruning is not punishment; it is love preparing a greater harvest.

How do we abide in Christ? In prayer every day. In the word of God. Above all in the Eucharist: whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him. Today is also the day we would have kept the feast of St Mark, who wrote the Gospel. Let us thank God for him, and for the Blessed Virgin Mary, whose month begins this week. Let us stay joined to the Vine, and bear much fruit. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a branch on a plant gets its food from the plant. If you break it off, it dries up. Jesus says: I am the vine, you are the branches!

When we pray, go to Mass and love others, we stay joined to Jesus, and we grow good fruit like kindness and joy. Let us say: Jesus, keep me close to you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, true vine, your Father is the vinegrower and we are the branches. Keep us abiding in you, for apart from you we can do nothing. Nourish us with your word and with your Body and Blood, so that your life may flow in us and bear fruit that lasts. When the Father prunes us, give us trust in his loving hands. Make us like Barnabas, quick to welcome and to encourage; teach us to love, not in word or speech, but in truth and action; and when our hearts condemn us, remind us that God is greater than our hearts. With St Mark, who wrote your Gospel, and with the Blessed Virgin Mary, the fruitful branch who bore you, we praise you in the assembly of your people, now and for ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Fifth Sunday of Easter turns from the appearances of the Risen Lord to the life of his body, the Church. Saul, the persecutor who has met the Lord, cannot get in through the door of the Jerusalem community until Barnabas takes him by the hand. John’s letter says that love is shown in deeds, and that whoever keeps the commandment abides in God. In the Gospel Jesus gives the image that holds it all together: the vine and the branches.

The word that runs through the letter and the Gospel is abide. The psalm is the last part of Psalm 22, the psalm whose opening cry Jesus prayed on the Cross. Here it ends in praise in the great assembly, with the ends of the earth turning to the Lord. The Church of Acts, growing in Judea, Galilee and Samaria, is the beginning of that answer.

Acts 9:26–31 · Barnabas took him

Setting.

Saul has met the Risen Lord on the road to Damascus (9:1–19), has preached in the synagogues there that Jesus is the Son of God (9:20), and has escaped a plot by being let down the city wall in a basket (9:23–25). Luke now brings him to Jerusalem. Paul’s own account in Galatians 1:17–19 adds that he first went away into Arabia, and that it was after three years that he went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas, staying fifteen days and seeing James the Lord’s brother. Luke tells it more briefly and keeps his eye on one thing: how the former persecutor was received.

Background. Barnabas first appears in 4:36–37: a Levite from Cyprus named Joseph, who sold a field and laid the money at the apostles’ feet. The apostles called him Barnabas, which Luke explains as “son of encouragement”. The Hellenists were Greek-speaking Jews in Jerusalem. Stephen had argued with members of their synagogues (6:9), and Saul had stood by at his death (7:58; 8:1). Now Saul takes Stephen’s place in the same debate and meets the same danger.

Structure.

  • v. 26: Saul tries to join the disciples, and they are afraid of him.
  • v. 27: Barnabas brings him to the apostles and speaks for him.
  • vv. 28–29: Saul speaks boldly in Jerusalem and argues with the Hellenists, who try to kill him.
  • v. 30: the believers bring him down to Caesarea and send him to Tarsus.
  • v. 31: the Church has peace, is built up and grows.
  • ¶ Verse 31 is one of Luke’s short summaries, like 2:42–47, 4:32–35 and 6:7. They are pauses in the story that show the whole Church at a glance.

Key words.

  • parrēsiazomai, to speak boldly: Used twice here (vv. 27, 28). The noun parrēsia means plain, fearless speech, the courage of the apostles before the council (4:13, 29, 31). The same noun is the “boldness before God” of today’s second reading (1 John 3:21).
  • paraklēsis, comfort, encouragement: The Church grows “in the comfort of the Holy Spirit” (v. 31). Luke explains the name Barnabas as “son of paraklēsis” (4:36). The word belongs to the same family as Paraclete, the name Jesus gives the Spirit in John. Barnabas does on earth what the Spirit does in the whole Church.
  • ekklēsia, church: In the best manuscripts the word is singular: “the church throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria”. Later manuscripts have the plural, “churches”. The singular shows one Church spread over several regions.

Verse by verse.

“they were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple.” (9:26)

Their fear was reasonable. He had dragged men and women off to prison (8:3). A conversion can be real and still need time before others can trust it.

“But Barnabas took him, brought him to the apostles,” (9:27)

Barnabas risks his own good name. He listens to Saul’s story and then tells it for him: the road, the Lord who spoke, the bold preaching in Damascus. Every parish needs someone who will take the newcomer by the hand.

“they brought him down to Caesarea and sent him off to Tarsus.” (9:30)

Caesarea was the Roman port on the coast. Tarsus in Cilicia was Saul’s home city (21:39). He disappears from the story for some years, until Barnabas goes to Tarsus to look for him and brings him to Antioch (11:25–26). Barnabas opens the door a second time.

“Living in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, it increased in numbers.” (9:31)

Two things that seem opposed stand together: fear of the Lord, which is reverence, and comfort. The Church’s growth here comes from the Spirit, not from strategy.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of faith in Jesus as the Son of God, recalls Paul’s encounter on the road to Damascus and his first preaching that Jesus is the Son of God (CCC 442). The Church honours Barnabas, whom Acts calls an apostle (14:14), with a memorial on 11 June. In him she sees the ministry of welcome and encouragement: the one who believed in another’s conversion before the others did. Saul’s story also shows that the Church is not a club of the deserving. It is the place where an enemy can become a brother.

1 John 3:18–24 · In truth and action

Setting.

The First Letter of John comes from the tradition of the beloved disciple and was probably written near the end of the first century. Ancient tradition ascribes it to the apostle John; the letter does not name its writer, and many scholars think it comes from a disciple within the circle of John. It speaks to communities shaken after some members had left them (2:19). Its two great concerns are true faith in Jesus who has come in the flesh (4:2) and real love for brothers and sisters.

Just before our passage, John says that we know love because Christ laid down his life for us, and asks how God’s love can abide in anyone who sees a brother or sister in need and refuses help (3:16–17). Today’s verses follow from that.

Structure.

  • v. 18: love in deeds, not only in words.
  • vv. 19–20: a troubled heart, and God who is greater than our hearts.
  • vv. 21–22: boldness before God and prayer that is heard.
  • v. 23: one commandment with two sides: believe in the name of the Son, and love one another.
  • v. 24: abiding in God, and the Spirit as the sign.

Key words.

  • alētheia, truth: For John, truth is not only correct words. It is what is real, and it comes from God. To love “in truth” is to love in a way that is real, and to be “from the truth” (v. 19) is to belong to God.
  • parrēsia, boldness: Confidence before God, the openness of a child who can speak to a father without fear (v. 21). The same word describes the apostles’ bold speech in Acts.
  • menō, to abide, remain: One of John’s favourite words, here in v. 24 and again and again in today’s Gospel passage. It means to stay, to dwell, to make one’s home. Christian life is not a visit to God but a remaining with him.

Verse by verse.

“Little children, let us love, not in word or speech, but in truth and action.” (3:18)

“Little children” is the tender address of an old teacher. The verse does not despise words; it asks that they be true, and true words become deeds.

“whenever our hearts condemn us; for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything.” (3:20)

The verse is a comfort. Our own heart can accuse us too much, or too little. God’s knowledge is larger than our self-condemnation, and his mercy is larger still. The Catechism quotes these words when it speaks of God’s holiness and mercy (CCC 208), and again when it speaks of conscience (CCC 1781).

“And this is his commandment, that we should believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another,” (3:23)

The commandment is singular, yet it has two sides. Faith and love are not two separate tasks. Faith in the Son who loved us becomes love for one another.

“And by this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit that he has given us.” (3:24)

The first mention of the Spirit in the letter. The sign that God dwells in us is not a feeling of our own making but a gift we have received.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine preached ten homilies on this letter during Easter time. In one of them he gives the famous saying “Love, and do what you will”, meaning that if the root within is love, nothing but good can come from it (in substance). He did not mean that anything goes. He meant that love is the root from which every good act must grow, which is exactly the teaching of v. 18. The Church reads this letter in the Easter season because it shows what the new life of Easter looks like day by day: faith in the Son, love that acts, and the Spirit abiding within.

John 15:1–8 · I am the vine, you are the branches

Setting.

We are at the Last Supper, in the long farewell discourse of John 13–17. At the end of chapter 14 Jesus says, “Rise, let us be on our way” (14:31), and yet the discourse continues. Many scholars think chapters 15–17 were added to the first discourse as the Gospel took shape; others think the words were spoken on the way. Either way, the Church receives them as the Lord’s farewell teaching.

Background. In the Old Testament Israel is God’s vine, planted with love and tended with care, yet often disappointing: Isaiah 5:1–7, Psalm 80:8–16, Jeremiah 2:21. The Jewish historian Josephus describes a great golden vine over the entrance of the Temple sanctuary. When Jesus says “I am the true vine”, he presents himself as the faithful Israel in person, and his disciples belong to God’s people by belonging to him. This is the last of the seven “I am” sayings in John with an image: bread, light, gate, shepherd, resurrection, way, and vine.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the vine, the vinegrower and the pruning; the disciples already cleansed by the word.
  • vv. 4–5: “Abide in me”: the branch lives only on the vine.
  • v. 6: the branch that does not abide.
  • vv. 7–8: prayer that is answered, much fruit, and the Father glorified.

Key words.

  • ampelos alēthinē, the true vine: “True” in John means real, the genuine thing of which others were signs, as in “the true light” (1:9) and “the true bread from heaven” (6:32).
  • kathairō / katharos, to prune / clean: A play on words in the Greek. The Father “prunes” (kathairei, v. 2) the fruitful branch, and the disciples are already “cleansed” (katharoi, v. 3) by the word. Pruning and cleansing are the same action of love.
  • menō, to abide: The key word of the passage, repeated again and again in vv. 4–7. It is not an effort to cling but a staying put: in his word, in his love, in his sacraments, in his Church.

Verse by verse.

“I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinegrower.” (15:1)

The Father is not far away. He is the one with his hands in the soil, looking after every branch.

“Every branch that bears fruit he prunes to make it bear more fruit.” (15:2)

Any farmer in Kerala knows that a plant cut back grows stronger. Pruning hurts, but it is a sign that the vinegrower expects more fruit, not a sign of his anger.

“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” (15:5)

St Augustine notes that the Lord did not say “without me you can do little”, but “nothing” (in substance). The branch does not produce the sap; it receives it.

“Whoever does not abide in me is thrown away like a branch and withers;” (15:6)

Vine wood is useless for building; Ezekiel says that no peg can be made of it (Ezekiel 15:2–5). Augustine says that the branch has only two places to be, either in the vine or in the fire (in substance).

“My Father is glorified by this, that you bear much fruit and become my disciples.” (15:8)

The fruit is not success in the world’s sense. It is the life of a disciple. Even at the end of the Gospel, being a disciple is still something we “become”.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council lists the vine among the images of the Church: Christ is the true vine who gives life and fruitfulness to the branches, that is, to us, who through the Church abide in him (Lumen Gentium 6). The Catechism quotes vv. 4–5 to describe the intimate communion between Christ and those who follow him (CCC 787), and it explains that the fruit spoken of here is the holiness of a life made fruitful by union with Christ (CCC 2074). The Church has always heard in the vine a hint of the Eucharist too: the cup of the fruit of the vine which becomes his blood is the sap that runs through the branches.

Questions people ask

Does “apart from me you can do nothing” mean that our efforts do not matter?

No. It means that even our good efforts are a gift. The Catechism teaches that God’s grace comes first and prepares us, and that God’s initiative calls for our own real response (CCC 2001–2002). The branch truly bears the fruit, but only because the sap of the vine is running in it.

Is verse 6 about hell?

It is a serious warning. To cut oneself off from Christ ends in loss, and the Church teaches that a person who dies refusing God’s love by mortal sin remains separated from him for ever (CCC 1033). But the verse names no one. It is spoken to disciples so that they may abide. While we live, the way back to the vine is always open through repentance and the sacrament of reconciliation.

Why were the disciples so slow to receive Saul?

Because they had suffered from him. Fear after real harm is not a sin. What Acts shows is the way through it: someone who knew both sides, Barnabas, took the risk of trust and spoke for him. Communities today face the same task when someone who once did harm comes back changed.

For the Latin reader

In the Easter season the Paschal candle stands near the ambo or the altar until Pentecost, a sign that the Risen Lord is present among us. At the preparation of the gifts the priest blesses God for the wine, “fruit of the vine and work of human hands”, which will become our spiritual drink: the vine of today’s Gospel comes to the altar. In Year B every Sunday of Easter except the Third takes its Gospel from John, and on Wednesday this week the Mass will read the same passage of the vine again. At home, the Regina Caeli replaces the Angelus until Pentecost.

For study and teaching

  • Who is the Saul in my parish or family, the person others are slow to trust? Could I be a Barnabas for him or her?
  • John asks for love “in truth and action”. Which kind words of mine need to become a deed this week?
  • What pruning has God done in my life, and what fruit has come of it?
  • How do I abide in the vine: in prayer, in the Word, in the Eucharist, in the community? Which of these is weakest in me now?
