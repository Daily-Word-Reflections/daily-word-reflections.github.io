Word for the day

“Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father in heaven may also forgive you your trespasses.” Mark 11:25

Reflection

Ben Sira now begins the longest section of his book, a hymn in praise of the ancestors of Israel, from Enoch and Noah to the high priest Simon of his own day. He opens with a famous line: “Let us now sing the praises of famous men, our ancestors in their generations.” He names kings, counsellors, prophets, musicians and wise teachers. But then he does something tender. He remembers those whom nobody remembers: “But of others there is no memory; they have perished as though they had never existed; they have become as though they had never been born.” And he insists that they too count before God: “But these also were godly men, whose righteous deeds have not been forgotten; their wealth will remain with their descendants, and their inheritance with their children's children.” Their goodness lives on in their families: “Their descendants stand by the covenants; their children also, for their sake. Their offspring will continue forever, and their glory will never be blotted out.” Every family in our parishes has such people: a grandmother who taught the rosary, a father who worked hard and never cheated, a teacher who lived the faith quietly. No book records their names, but their goodness is not forgotten, and we are living on their inheritance. The Psalm sings with joy: “the LORD takes pleasure in his people; he adorns the humble with victory.”

The Gospel is one of the most dramatic in St Mark. Jesus enters Jerusalem and the Temple, looks around at everything, and leaves for Bethany. The next morning he sees a fig tree “in leaf,” goes to look for fruit and finds “nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs.” He says, “May no one ever eat fruit from you again.” Then he enters the Temple and drives out the sellers and buyers, overturns the tables of the money changers and quotes two prophets: “Is it not written, ‘My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations’? But you have made it a den of robbers.” The next morning the disciples see the fig tree “withered away to its roots.” Mark places the cleansing of the Temple between the two halves of the fig tree story, like a filling in a sandwich, so that each explains the other. Jesus is acting like the prophets of old, who used signs to wake people up. A tree full of leaves but without fruit is a picture of worship full of activity but without prayer, justice and faith. This is not a judgment on the Jewish people; Jesus loved the Temple, called it “my house,” and the Church teaches that he honoured it (Catechism 584). It is a warning to every place of worship and every heart, including our own parishes. Then Jesus tells the disciples what real fruit looks like: “Have faith in God.” Faith that moves mountains, prayer that trusts, and above all forgiveness: “Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father in heaven may also forgive you your trespasses.” A house of prayer is a house of forgiveness.

Full homily

Dear friends, Ben Sira praises the great heroes of Israel's history. But he also remembers the forgotten ones: of others there is no memory. And he says: these also were godly men, whose righteous deeds have not been forgotten (Sirach 44:9–10). God does not forget. The grandmother who prayed the rosary every evening, the father who worked honestly all his life: they are not in the history books, but they are in God's book, and their goodness lives in us.

In the Gospel Jesus is hungry and sees a fig tree full of leaves. From a distance it looks wonderful. But when he comes close he finds nothing but leaves (Mark 11:13). Then he goes into the Temple and finds it busy with buying and selling. He drives out the traders and says: my house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations (Mark 11:17). The fig tree is a picture: much activity, many leaves, but no fruit.

This is a warning for us, not for others. A parish can be very busy, with feasts, programmes, collections and meetings, and still have little real prayer. A Christian can say many prayers and still have a hard heart. Jesus is looking for fruit. And he tells us what fruit he wants: have faith in God (Mark 11:22). Trust that he hears. Pray with confidence.

And then he says the most important thing: whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father in heaven may also forgive you (Mark 11:25). The best fruit of prayer is a forgiving heart. Today let us thank God for the forgotten saints in our families, and let us bring to him one person we need to forgive. Then our hearts will be a house of prayer. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus once saw a tree with lots of green leaves, but when he looked for fruit, there was none! Then he went to God's house, the Temple, and people were busy buying and selling instead of praying. Jesus said: God's house is for praying! He also said: when you pray, forgive anyone who has hurt you. Tonight before you pray, forgive your brother, your sister or your friend. That is a beautiful fruit for Jesus!

Prayer

Lord God, you take delight in your people and you do not forget the righteous deeds of those whom the world forgets. We thank you for the godly men and women who handed on the faith to us. Lord Jesus, you came to your Father's house looking for fruit; do not find our hearts full of leaves only. Make our churches houses of prayer for all the nations, strengthen our faith in God, and teach us to forgive whenever we stand to pray, so that our Father in heaven may forgive us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ben Sira begins his praise of the ancestors: some left a name, others are forgotten, but the godly ones live on in the covenant and in their children. In the Gospel Jesus enters the Temple and looks around, and the next day curses a barren fig tree and drives out the traders. Then he teaches faith that can move mountains and prayer that forgives. Psalm 149 sings that the Lord takes delight in his people, the faithful whose lives bear fruit.

Sirach 44:1, 9–13 · Their righteous deeds have not been forgotten

Setting.

Chapters 44–50 are the “Praise of the Ancestors”. Ben Sira walks through Israel’s history from Enoch and Noah to Abraham, Moses, David, the prophets, and finally to the high priest Simon son of Onias, whom he had seen serving at the altar (50:1–21). Few passages in the Old Testament offer so long a gallery of the faithful; the Letter to the Hebrews will do something similar in chapter 11.

Our verses are taken from the introduction. Ben Sira first lists many kinds of famous people (vv. 2–8), then admits that others are forgotten (v. 9), and then says what lasts: the godly, whose goodness lives on.

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Let us now sing the praises of famous men.”
  • v. 9: those of whom there is no memory.
  • vv. 10–13: the godly, whose righteous deeds are not forgotten; their descendants stand by the covenant.

Key words.

  • eleos, mercy, godliness: The Greek calls them “men of mercy” (v. 10); the Hebrew word behind it is very likely ḥesed, faithful love. They were good not only for themselves; their love was faithful to God and to others.
  • diathēkē, covenant: Their descendants “stand by the covenants” (v. 12). What keeps a family’s goodness alive is not wealth alone but fidelity to God’s covenant.
  • mnēmosynon, memory, remembrance: Some are forgotten by people, but the question in this passage is who is remembered by God. The godly live on in his memory and in the people they formed.

Verse by verse.

“Let us now sing the praises of famous men, our ancestors in their generations.” (44:1)

To praise the ancestors is to praise God who worked through them. Ben Sira’s list will be honest about their faults too.

“But of others there is no memory; they have perished as though they had never existed;” (44:9)

A sober verse. Many lives leave no trace in history. Ben Sira does not pretend otherwise.

“But these also were godly men, whose righteous deeds have not been forgotten;” (44:10)

The goodness of the faithful does not vanish. It is carried on in their children and in God’s memory.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sings these verses on the feasts of saints, especially of holy founders and pastors. For Christians, the memory that matters most is God’s memory, and the communion of saints means that the godly are not simply remembered: they live in Christ and pray for us (CCC 956). The countless saints whose names no history records are honoured together on All Saints’ Day. Ben Sira also reminds parents and teachers that the greatest legacy is a family that keeps the covenant.

Mark 11:11–26 · A house of prayer for all the nations

Setting.

Jesus has entered Jerusalem to the crowd’s acclaim (11:1–10). Mark then sets the cleansing of the Temple between the two halves of the fig tree story: the cursing (vv. 12–14) and the withered tree the next morning (vv. 20–21). This “sandwich” is a favourite way for Mark to let two stories explain each other.

The traders sold animals for sacrifice, and the money changers changed coins into the Tyrian silver used for the Temple tax. This trade was not wicked in itself; pilgrims needed it. Many scholars think it was set up in the great outer court, the only part of the Temple where Gentiles could pray.

Structure.

  • v. 11: Jesus looks around the Temple and goes out to Bethany.
  • vv. 12–14: the fig tree with leaves and no fruit.
  • vv. 15–19: the cleansing of the Temple; the authorities seek to destroy him.
  • vv. 20–26: the withered fig tree; faith, prayer and forgiveness.

Key words.

  • sykē, fig tree: The prophets used the fig tree and its fruit as an image of Israel and its faithfulness (Jeremiah 8:13; Hosea 9:10; Micah 7:1). The leaves promise fruit, but there is none. The act is a prophetic sign, not an outburst of temper.
  • oikos proseuchēs, house of prayer: Jesus quotes Isaiah 56:7, where the Lord promises that foreigners who hold to his covenant will be joyful in his house of prayer. Only Mark keeps the words “for all the nations”.
  • spēlaion lēstōn, den of robbers: From Jeremiah 7:11. A den is where robbers hide, not where they rob. Jeremiah warned that people could not sin and then shelter in the Temple as if it would protect them.

Verse by verse.

“he found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs.” (11:13)

Mark’s remark shows that the story is not about a tree at all. Jesus seeks fruit out of season, as God seeks fruit from his people at every hour.

“My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations” (11:17)

Jesus defends the Temple’s true purpose, prayer, and its wide welcome, the nations. He acts as the prophets did, from within Israel, out of love for the house of his Father.

“Have faith in God.” (11:22)

The withered tree becomes a lesson on prayer. Faith moves what seems immovable, because it trusts God, not itself.

“Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone;” (11:25)

Prayer that moves mountains must also forgive. Verse 26 is printed in some Bibles, but it is missing from the best ancient manuscripts and was probably added from Matthew 6:15.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus venerated the Temple, went up to it for the feasts, and that his zeal for his Father’s house shows his love, not hostility (CCC 583–584). His action is a prophetic sign of the new Temple, his own body (John 2:21). It must never be read as a rejection of the Jewish people. The Catechism also quotes verse 24 when it speaks of the boldness of a child’s prayer (CCC 2610), and teaches that God’s mercy cannot reach a heart that refuses to forgive (CCC 2840).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus curse a tree for having no figs when it was not the season?

Because the act is a sign, like the signs of the prophets, who broke jars or wore yokes to make a point. Mark frames the Temple scene with it to show what Jesus seeks: not leaves, the outward show of worship, but fruit, a life of prayer, faith and forgiveness. It is a warning to every believer and every community, the Church included.

For study and teaching

  • If Jesus came looking for fruit in my life today, what would he find among the leaves?
  • Is there someone I need to forgive before I can pray with an open heart? What first step can I take?
