Word for the day

“The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.” John 10:10

Reflection

Peter comes back to Jerusalem from Caesarea and finds himself in trouble. The believers of Jewish birth have heard that he went into the house of Cornelius, a Roman centurion, and ate with uncircumcised men. To them this looks like a betrayal of everything God gave Israel: the Law, the food laws, the holy separation of God's people. Peter does not shout them down. He does not pull rank as the chief of the apostles. The text says he “began to explain it to them, step by step.” He tells them about the vision at Joppa: a large sheet let down from heaven, full of animals the Law called unclean, and a voice saying, “Get up, Peter; kill and eat.” He tells them his own reply, the reply of a faithful Jew: “By no means, Lord; for nothing profane or unclean has ever entered my mouth.” And he tells them what the voice said a second time: “What God has made clean, you must not call profane.”

Then Peter gives the decisive fact. While he was still speaking in Cornelius's house, “the Holy Spirit fell upon them just as it had upon us at the beginning.” It was a little Pentecost in a Gentile house. Peter remembered the word of the Lord: “John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit.” And he ends with a question that silences the room: “If then God gave them the same gift that he gave us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was I that I could hinder God?” The objectors fall silent, and then they do something beautiful: they praise God. “Then God has given even to the Gentiles the repentance that leads to life.” This is a turning point in the history of the Church. The Gospel is not the property of one people or one culture. The door is open to all. And it was opened not by argument alone but by listening together to what God was doing. The Psalm puts the longing of every human heart into words: “My soul thirsts for God, for the living God.”

In the Gospel Jesus uses a picture every villager understood. At night the shepherds of a village brought their flocks into one walled fold with a single gate, and a gatekeeper watched it. In the morning each shepherd came to the gate and called, and his own sheep knew his voice and came out after him. Jesus says: “He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.” Then he changes the image and says something even more astonishing: “I am the gate.” There is one way in to safety and pasture, and it is Jesus himself. “Whoever enters by me will be saved, and will come in and go out and find pasture.” Thieves come to take; Jesus comes to give. “I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.” The first reading and the Gospel belong together. The gate that Jesus is cannot be locked against anyone whom the Father calls. Peter learned in Cornelius's house that the gate stands open to the nations. The Church is the fold; Christ is its one gate; and the risen Shepherd still calls each of his sheep by name.

Full homily

Dear friends, Peter is in trouble with his own community. He has eaten with Gentiles. And the Christians in Jerusalem, good and faithful Jews, are upset. How does the chief of the apostles answer? Not with anger. He explains, step by step, what God has done.

He tells them about the vision: a sheet full of unclean animals and a voice saying, what God has made clean, you must not call profane (Acts 11:9). Then he tells them that the Holy Spirit fell on Cornelius and his family, just as on the apostles at Pentecost.

And he asks: who was I that I could hinder God? (Acts 11:17). That question is for us too. How often do we stand at the gate and decide who may come in? How often do we hinder God by our prejudice, our caste thinking, our coldness to strangers?

Notice what the listeners do. They fall silent, and then they praise God. That is the sign of a living Church: when the Spirit shows us something new, we stop arguing and we praise.

In the Gospel Jesus says: I am the gate. There is one way into life, and it is Jesus. And he is a shepherd who knows each sheep by name. He does not come like a thief to take from us. He says: I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly (John 10:10).

Today, let us listen for that voice. And let us keep the gate open, in our homes and our parishes, for everyone the Shepherd is calling. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a shepherd calls his sheep by name, and they come running because they know his voice. Jesus knows your name too!

Jesus says: I am the gate. When we come to Jesus, we are safe, and he gives us a happy, full life. Let us say: Jesus, I am listening!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd and gate of the sheep, you call each of us by name and you came that we may have life in abundance. Open our ears to your voice among all the voices of the world. Give your Church the courage of Peter, who would not hinder the work of the Holy Spirit, and the humility of those who listened to him and praised God. Keep the gate of our hearts open to every person you are calling, and lead us at last to the pasture of eternal life. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about a door. In Jerusalem Peter explains why he went through the door of a Gentile house, and the believers end by praising God: “Then God has given even to the Gentiles the repentance that leads to life.” In John, Jesus calls himself the gate through which the sheep come in and go out and find pasture. Sunday’s Gospel heard the Good Shepherd lay down his life (John 10:11–18); today the Church goes back to the opening of the same discourse. Psalms 42–43 sing the thirst for the living God that only this gate satisfies.

Acts 11:1–18 · Who was I that I could hinder God?

Setting.

News travels faster than Peter. Before he is back in Jerusalem, the believers in Judea have heard that the Gentiles have accepted the word of God (10:1–48). Some Jewish believers are troubled, not about the baptism itself but because Peter ate with uncircumcised men (11:3). Table fellowship was where the Law’s distinction between clean and unclean was most visible. Peter does not argue from theory; he tells what happened, “step by step”. Luke tells the story of Cornelius a second time here, and recalls it again in 15:7–9, a sign of how much it matters to him.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the news and the complaint.
  • vv. 4–10: the vision at Joppa, three times over.
  • vv. 11–14: the three men, the Spirit’s command, the angel’s message to Cornelius.
  • vv. 15–17: the Spirit falls “just as it had upon us”.
  • v. 18: silence, then praise.

Key words.

  • diakrinō, to make a distinction, to take issue: Luke plays on one verb. In v. 2 the believers “criticized” Peter (diekrinonto); in v. 12 the Spirit tells him to go with the men “not to make a distinction” (mēden diakrinanta). Those who were dividing are taught by the Spirit not to divide. Some manuscripts have a related form meaning “without hesitating”, which is how the Malayalam POC renders the verse.
  • kōluō, to hinder, to prevent: The same verb sounds at the baptism of the Ethiopian (8:36) and in Cornelius’ house (10:47). Some scholars hear in it an echo of an early baptismal question. Here Peter’s answer is final: no one may hinder what God has given.
  • metanoia, repentance, change of mind: Literally a change of mind and heart. The Jerusalem believers call it a gift: God “has given” it. Conversion is first God’s work in a person, and then that person’s answer.

Verse by verse.

“What God has made clean, you must not call profane.” (11:9)

The voice is not about food alone. In Cornelius’ house Peter himself drew the conclusion: God had shown him that he should not call anyone profane or unclean (10:28).

“the Holy Spirit fell upon them just as it had upon us at the beginning.” (11:15)

“At the beginning” points to Pentecost. Peter sees in Cornelius’ house a Pentecost of the Gentiles: the same Spirit, the same gift, and no second class of believers.

“who was I that I could hinder God?” (11:17)

Peter’s authority shows itself here as obedience. He does not open the door by his own decision; he recognizes a door God has already opened.

“Then God has given even to the Gentiles the repentance that leads to life.” (11:18)

The word “even” carries all the surprise. The Gentiles are not taking Israel’s place; they are welcomed into the blessing promised to Abraham for all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:3).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this chapter as the moment when the mission to all nations was accepted in Jerusalem, a step the Council of Jerusalem would confirm (Acts 15). The point is not that the Law was bad, but that in Christ God gathers Jew and Gentile into one people (Ephesians 2:14). Every parish has people some would rather not sit at table with. Peter’s question still stands: who are we to hinder God?

John 10:1–10 · I am the gate

Setting.

Jesus is still speaking to the Pharisees who had questioned the man born blind and driven him out (9:34, 40–41). That man heard the voice of Jesus and followed him; those who should have been his shepherds cast him out. The background is Ezekiel 34, where God condemns shepherds who feed themselves and promises to shepherd his flock himself. In Palestine the sheep of several flocks were often kept at night in one walled fold with a gatekeeper; in the morning each shepherd called his own sheep out by his voice.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: the figure: thief and shepherd, gate and voice.
  • v. 6: they do not understand.
  • vv. 7–10: “I am the gate”: salvation, pasture, abundant life.

Key words.

  • paroimia, figure of speech: Not the usual word for parable (parabolē), which John never uses. It means a veiled saying whose sense opens to those who come to believe (compare 16:25, 29).
  • thyra, gate, door: Jesus is both the gate (v. 7) and, a few verses later, the shepherd (v. 11). As the gate he is the only right way into the flock, for shepherds as well as sheep; as the shepherd he leads them.
  • perissos, abundantly, overflowing: The thief takes; Jesus gives more than enough, as at Cana and at the feeding of the five thousand, where more was left over than was needed (6:12–13).

Verse by verse.

“He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.” (10:3)

Faith in John is personal: Mary Magdalene will know the risen Lord when he calls her by name (20:16). “Leads them out” recalls the man born blind: driven out of the synagogue, he is found by Jesus (9:35).

“I am the gate. Whoever enters by me will be saved, and will come in and go out and find pasture.” (10:9)

“Come in and go out” is a biblical way of describing a whole life lived in safety (compare Deuteronomy 28:6; Psalm 121:8). Christ is not a gate that shuts the sheep in, but one that gives them a home and wide pasture.

“I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.” (10:10)

The purpose of the whole Gospel in one line (compare 20:31).

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council begins its images of the Church with this passage: the Church is a sheepfold whose one and necessary door is Christ (Lumen Gentium 6; CCC 754). The image is both comfort and charge. For the faithful it means safety: no one who comes in by Christ is a stranger. For pastors it means that every ministry must enter by the same gate, serving a flock that belongs to Christ and not owning it.

Questions people ask

Who are the “thieves and bandits” who came before Jesus?

Not Moses and the prophets, whom Jesus honors (5:46). Most commentators think of false leaders and would-be messiahs who used the people for their own ends, and, in this context, of those who had just cast out the healed man. The warning applies in every age to anyone who leads God’s people without entering by Christ.

For study and teaching

  • Whom do I find it hard to sit at table with? What would Peter’s question, “who was I that I could hinder God?”, mean for me?
  • Do I hear Christ calling me by name? Where is he leading me out to “find pasture” this week?
