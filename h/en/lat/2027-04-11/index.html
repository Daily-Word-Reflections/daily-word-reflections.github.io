Word for the day

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” Luke 24:39

Reflection

On the Third Sunday of Easter the Church sits us down in Jerusalem on the evening of the first Easter day. The two disciples have just run back from Emmaus and are telling “how he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.” Before St Luke shows us the risen Lord again, the first reading lets us hear what the apostles did with that experience. In Solomon's Portico, after the healing of the lame man, Peter speaks to the crowd: “The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, the God of our ancestors has glorified his servant Jesus.” He names the tragedy plainly: “you rejected the Holy and Righteous One and asked to have a murderer given to you, and you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. To this we are witnesses.” Then his tone changes completely: “And now, friends, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” These are not words of condemnation but of a brother; they echo Jesus' own prayer from the cross, “Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing.” Peter, who denied the Lord three times, is in no position to accuse anyone. The Church has always taught that the sins of all of us brought Christ to the cross and that the Jewish people may not be held collectively responsible for his death (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 597–598). Peter's purpose is the same as ours today: “Repent therefore, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out.” The Psalm is the prayer of one who has turned back: “Let the light of your face shine on us, O LORD!”

The second reading, from the First Letter of St John, is tender and strong. “My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin.” The Easter life is a life without sin, and John does not lower the standard. But he knows his children: “But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous; and he is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world.” The Greek word for advocate is parakletos, the same word Jesus uses for the Holy Spirit. Jesus is our first advocate, standing before the Father with the wounds of his love; and the word for atoning sacrifice, hilasmos, recalls the mercy seat of the Ark, where the blood of atonement was sprinkled. He is not only the lawyer who pleads for us; he is himself the offering that reconciles. Then John gives a simple test for a living faith: “Now by this we may be sure that we know him, if we obey his commandments.” Knowing Christ is not a feeling but a life: “whoever obeys his word, truly in this person the love of God has reached perfection.”

Then the Gospel. “While they were talking about this, Jesus himself stood among them and said to them, ‘Peace be with you.’” They are “startled and terrified,” thinking they see a ghost. Jesus' answer is one of the most important texts in the Bible for our faith in the bodily Resurrection: “Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” He shows them his wounds. And while “in their joy they were disbelieving and still wondering,” he asks a homely question: “Have you anything here to eat?” They give him a piece of broiled fish, “and he took it and ate in their presence.” The risen Christ is not a memory, not an idea, not a spirit only: he is the same Jesus, body and soul, now glorified, never to die again. Then he opens their minds to understand the Scriptures: “everything written about me in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms must be fulfilled.” The whole Old Testament, all three parts, points to him. And he gives them their mission: “repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things.” This Sunday has the same shape as every Mass: the Lord stands among us and says Peace; he opens the Scriptures; he gives himself as food; and he sends us out as witnesses.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the disciples are gathered in Jerusalem on the evening of Easter. The two from Emmaus are telling their story. And suddenly Jesus himself stands among them and says: Peace be with you. They are terrified. They think they are seeing a ghost.

Jesus does not scold them. He says: look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have (Luke 24:39). And then he asks: have you anything here to eat? He eats a piece of fish in front of them. Our faith is not in a ghost or a memory. Jesus rose in his real body, the same body that was crucified, now glorified for ever.

Why does this matter for us? Because our bodies matter. The body that works in the field, the body that is sick in the hospital, the body that grows old, is not thrown away. We believe in the resurrection of the body. And because the risen Lord has a body, he can give it to us. At this altar he gives us not a symbol but his own risen Body and Blood.

In the first reading Peter speaks to the crowd in Jerusalem: you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. But then he says: I know that you acted in ignorance. Repent, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out (Acts 3:17, 19). Peter had denied Jesus three times. He knows what forgiveness is. He does not preach blame; he preaches mercy. And the Church teaches that all of us, by our sins, share in the Passion.

St John says the same in the second reading: if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous (1 John 2:1). When we fall, we are not alone before the Father. Jesus stands beside us, showing his wounds, pleading for us. That is why the sacrament of confession is an Easter sacrament: the risen Lord breathes peace on our sins.

Finally Jesus opens their minds to understand the Scriptures and says: you are witnesses of these things (Luke 24:48). We too have been with him today. We have heard his word, we will receive his Body. Let us go out as witnesses: with peace in our hearts, mercy on our lips, and a table open to those who eat alone. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Third Sunday of Easter all three readings speak of witness and forgiveness. In Acts Peter tells the crowd in the temple that they killed the Author of life, whom God raised, and at once offers them repentance and the wiping out of sins. In 1 John the Risen One is our advocate with the Father and the atoning sacrifice for the sins of the whole world. In Luke Jesus stands among the disciples, shows his hands and feet, eats before them, opens their minds to the Scriptures and sends them out: “You are witnesses of these things.” Psalm 4 prays, “Lord, let your face shine on us”. The thread is this: the Risen Lord is real, he forgives, and he makes witnesses.

Acts 3:13–15, 17–19 · The Author of life, whom God raised

Setting.

Peter and John have healed a man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate of the temple (3:1–10). The crowd runs together in Solomon’s Portico, a colonnade along the eastern side of the temple court, and Peter explains what they have seen. This is his second great speech in Acts, after Pentecost.

The Lectionary leaves out verse 16, where Peter says that faith in the name of Jesus healed the man. The verses chosen keep the heart of the speech: what was done to Jesus, what God did, and what the hearers can do now.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–15: a contrast: you handed him over and rejected him; God glorified and raised him.
  • vv. 17–18: you acted in ignorance; God fulfilled what the prophets foretold.
  • v. 19: the call: repent and turn, so that your sins may be wiped out.

Key words.

  • pais, servant: The Greek can mean servant or child. “God has glorified his servant” echoes the Greek of Isaiah 52:13, where, in the Greek, the Lord’s servant will be lifted up and glorified. Peter reads the suffering and glory of Jesus through the Servant Songs.
  • archēgos tēs zōēs, the Author of life: Leader, founder, the one who opens the way to life. Peter sets a sharp paradox: they asked for a murderer, a taker of life, and killed the giver of life.
  • agnoia, ignorance: Not innocence, but not full knowledge either. It echoes Jesus’ prayer on the cross: “Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing” (Luke 23:34).

Verse by verse.

“has glorified his servant Jesus,” (3:13)

Peter begins with “the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob”. He speaks as a Jew to fellow Jews, of the God of their fathers, who has kept his promise in Jesus.

“you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. To this we are witnesses.” (3:15)

The sentence turns on its middle: you killed, God raised. Human sin does not have the last word. “Witnesses” is the apostles’ title throughout Acts (1:8).

“I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” (3:17)

Peter, who had himself denied Jesus, speaks with mercy. He calls them “friends” (literally “brothers”) and opens a door rather than closing one.

“Repent therefore, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out,” (3:19)

“Wiped out” is the image of erasing writing from a tablet. The debt is not just covered; it is taken away.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that the Passion of Christ cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4). The Catechism adds that sinners were the authors of Christ’s Passion, and that Christians, who know him and still sin, bear the graver responsibility (CCC 598). Peter’s “you” is therefore also our “we”. And his speech shows the right response: not blame, but repentance and the joy of forgiveness.

1 John 2:1–5a · An advocate with the Father

Setting.

The First Letter of John reads more like a homily than a letter. Tradition links it with the apostle John, and it shares the language of the Fourth Gospel; many scholars place it among the communities that treasured John’s Gospel, toward the end of the first century. A group had left the community (2:19), and some apparently claimed to be without sin (1:8–10).

John steers between two errors: saying that sin does not matter, and despairing because of sin. The answer to both is Christ.

Structure.

  • v. 1a: the purpose: so that you may not sin.
  • vv. 1b–2: if anyone sins, Christ is our advocate and atoning sacrifice.
  • vv. 3–5a: the test of knowing God: keeping his commandments, his word.

Key words.

  • teknia, little children: A tender word, used often in this letter. John writes as an old pastor to people he loves.
  • paraklētos, advocate: One called to stand beside another, especially in court. In the Gospel Jesus calls the Holy Spirit “another Advocate” (John 14:16); here Jesus himself is the Advocate before the Father.
  • hilasmos, atoning sacrifice, expiation: The means by which sin is taken away and fellowship with God restored. In 4:10 John says that God himself sent his Son for this. It is not man appeasing an angry God, but God’s love providing the remedy.

Verse by verse.

“My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin.” (2:1)

The goal is holiness, not mere forgiveness. Yet the next words admit at once that Christians do sin.

“we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous;” (2:1)

St Augustine notes that John does not say “you have” but “we have”: the apostle counts himself among sinners who need an advocate (in substance, Homilies on the First Letter of John).

“and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world.” (2:2)

No one is outside the reach of the cross. The Catechism, citing this verse, teaches that Christ died for all without exception (CCC 605).

“but whoever obeys his word, truly in this person the love of God has reached perfection.” (2:5)

“Love of God” can mean our love for God or God’s love in us; both come to completion in obedience. Knowledge of God is proved by a life, not only by words.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that Jesus himself is the first Paraclete, and that he promised the Spirit as “another” Paraclete (CCC 692). The Church receives the forgiveness promised here above all in Baptism and in the sacrament of Penance; John has just written that if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us (1:9; CCC 1847).

Luke 24:35–48 · Touch me and see

Setting.

It is still Easter evening. The two disciples have run back from Emmaus to Jerusalem and found the Eleven saying, “The Lord has risen indeed, and he has appeared to Simon!” (24:34). As they tell their story, Jesus himself appears. This is Luke’s last scene before the Ascension (24:50–53).

In Year B of the Lectionary this Gospel continues the Emmaus story. Luke is writing for readers who might think the risen Jesus was a vision or a ghost; he insists on the reality of the risen body.

Structure.

  • v. 35: the Emmaus disciples tell how he was known in the breaking of the bread.
  • vv. 36–43: Jesus appears, shows his hands and feet, and eats.
  • vv. 44–47: he opens their minds to the Scriptures: suffering, resurrection, repentance for all nations.
  • v. 48: “You are witnesses.”

Key words.

  • pneuma, ghost, spirit: Here a disembodied spirit or apparition. Jesus denies that he is one: he has flesh and bones.
  • dianoigein, to open: A key word of Luke 24: the disciples’ eyes are opened (v. 31), the Scriptures are opened (v. 32), and their minds are opened (v. 45). The Risen One opens what was shut.
  • metanoia, repentance: A change of mind and heart, a turning back to God. It is the first content of the mission, joined to the forgiveness of sins (v. 47).

Verse by verse.

“how he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.” (24:35)

“The breaking of the bread” is the name Luke uses in Acts for the Eucharist (Acts 2:42). The Catechism sees in the Emmaus journey the shape of the Mass: the word explained, then the bread broken (CCC 1347).

“Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” (24:39)

St Ignatius of Antioch, writing about AD 110, records a saying close to this, that the disciples should handle him and see that he was not a bodiless spirit, and stresses that the Lord was in the flesh after the Resurrection (in substance, Letter to the Smyrnaeans 3). The Catechism teaches that the risen body is the same body that was crucified, now glorified (CCC 645; CCC 999).

“While in their joy they were disbelieving and still wondering,” (24:41)

An honest and tender note: the news is too good to believe. Joy itself makes them hesitate.

“Then he opened their minds to understand the scriptures,” (24:45)

“The law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms” (v. 44) names the three parts of the Jewish Scriptures. The Resurrection fulfils the whole Old Testament (CCC 652).

“You are witnesses of these things.” (24:48)

Not spectators but witnesses. In Acts 1:8 this becomes the program of the whole book: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria and the ends of the earth.

How the Church reads it.

The Church holds that the Resurrection was a real event in history, with manifestations that could be verified, and also more than a return to earthly life: the risen body is glorified and no longer bound by space and time (CCC 639; CCC 645–646). Luke 24 also shows how the risen Lord is met today: in the Scriptures opened and in the breaking of the bread, which is why every Mass has a Liturgy of the Word and a Liturgy of the Eucharist.

Questions people ask

Did the risen Jesus need to eat?

No. His glorified body did not need food. He ate to show that he was not a ghost but truly risen in the body. Peter later speaks of witnesses “who ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead” (Acts 10:41). The meal was a gift to their faith.

“You killed the Author of life”: does the Bible blame the Jewish people for the death of Jesus?

No. Peter speaks to a particular crowd in Jerusalem, as one of their own, calling them “friends” and offering forgiveness. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4), and the Catechism says that we sinners bear the graver responsibility (CCC 598).

What does “atoning sacrifice” mean? Was God angry and needing to be paid?

The Greek hilasmos means the means by which sin is taken away and communion restored. John says that the initiative is God’s: “he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins” (1 John 4:10). The Catechism cites that verse to say that the Word became flesh to save us by reconciling us with God (CCC 457). The cross is God’s love reaching us, not a price wrung from him.

For the Latin reader

This Sunday the Lectionary is in Year B, and the Gospel continues the Emmaus story with the appearance in Jerusalem. Through the Easter season the first reading comes from the Acts of the Apostles instead of the Old Testament, so that we watch the risen Lord at work in his Church. The Easter candle stands by the ambo or by the altar until Pentecost, and the Regina Caeli replaces the Angelus. Luke’s pairing of word and broken bread is the shape of every Mass, so this Sunday is a fitting one to teach the people why the Mass has two tables, the word and the altar.

For study and teaching

  • Peter offers forgiveness to those who wronged his Lord. Whom do I need to forgive, or ask forgiveness of, this Easter?
  • 1 John says we have an advocate. How does that change the way I go to confession?
  • Where do I most often meet the risen Lord: in the Scriptures, in the breaking of the bread, in others?
  • What is one way I can be a witness this week, in word or in deed?
