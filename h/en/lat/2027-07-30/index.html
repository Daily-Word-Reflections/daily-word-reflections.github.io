Word for the day

“And they took offense at him. But Jesus said to them, “Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house.” And he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” Matthew 13:57–58

Reflection

The Book of Leviticus opens a calendar. “These are the appointed festivals of the Lord, the holy convocations, which you shall celebrate at the time appointed for them.” Time itself becomes holy. The year of Israel is not a mere wheel of seasons, but a series of meetings with God at fixed hours. First comes the Passover, “on the fourteenth day of the month, at twilight,” followed by seven days of unleavened bread, the memorial of the night of the Exodus. Then the first fruits: when the harvest begins, the people bring “the sheaf of the first fruits” to the priest, who raises it before the Lord “on the day after the sabbath.” From that day they “count off seven weeks... fifty days,” and then bring an offering of new grain, the feast of Weeks, which Greek-speaking Jews called Pentecost, “the fiftieth.” Then the Day of Atonement, “you shall deny yourselves,” when the people confessed their sins, and the Feast of Booths, seven days of living in huts of branches in memory of the wilderness. These festivals are God's gifts to Israel, and the Jewish people still keep them with faithfulness; the Church honours them as such and teaches that God's covenant with Israel has never been revoked (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 839). Christians also see in them a pattern that was fulfilled in Christ. He died at Passover; he rose on “the day after the sabbath,” which is the day of the first fruits, and St Paul calls him “the first fruits of those who have died” (1 Corinthians 15:20); fifty days later the Holy Spirit came at Pentecost. The Psalm is the song for such feasts: “Sing aloud to God our strength; shout for joy to the God of Jacob. Raise a song, sound the tambourine, the sweet lyre with the harp. Blow the trumpet at the new moon, at the full moon, on our festal day. For it is a statute for Israel.”

In the Gospel Jesus comes “to his hometown” of Nazareth and teaches in the synagogue. The people are astounded, but their astonishment does not turn into faith. “Where did this man get this wisdom and these deeds of power? Is not this the carpenter's son? Is not his mother called Mary? And are not his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas? And are not all his sisters with us?” They know everything about him except who he is. The “brothers” and “sisters” are, in the language of the Bible, close relatives; the Church has always taught that Mary remained a virgin and that Jesus was her only child (Catechism 499–500). “They took offense at him”: the Greek word means that he became a stumbling block to them. Familiarity can hide holiness; they could not believe that God could be so near, so ordinary, so well known. Jesus answers with a proverb: “Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house.” Then St Matthew says something sobering: “he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” God does not force his gifts on anyone; where faith is closed, there is little the Lord can do. The two readings belong together. In Leviticus God comes near on the appointed days, and the people have to be ready to meet him. In Nazareth he came near on an ordinary day, in an ordinary man, and they missed him. We should be careful that we do not miss him either, in the Mass we know so well, in the neighbour we think we know, in the daily bread of our own home. St Peter Chrysologus, whom the Church remembers today, was a bishop who knew how to make the familiar words of the Gospel sound new.

Full homily

Dear friends, Leviticus gives Israel a calendar of meetings with God: the Passover, the first fruits, the fifty days, the Day of Atonement, the Feast of Booths. These are the appointed festivals of the Lord (Leviticus 23:4). Time itself becomes holy. The Church too has a calendar: Sunday, Easter, Pentecost, the feasts of the saints. Why? Because we forget. We need fixed hours and seasons to remind us that God is near.

And God is faithful to those hours. At Passover the Lord rescued his people. The sheaf of first fruits is raised before him on the day after the sabbath, and we know what rose on that day: Christ, the first fruits of those who have died (1 Corinthians 15:20). Fifty days later came Pentecost. The festivals of Israel are God's gifts to the Jewish people, who keep them to this day, and in them we Christians see a pattern fulfilled in Christ. The Psalm says: Sing aloud to God our strength. Joy is the proper response.

But now look at the Gospel. Jesus goes to his hometown, and in the synagogue he teaches. People are astonished. Where did this man get this wisdom? Then comes the great but: is not this the carpenter's son? Is not his mother called Mary? They took offense at him (Matthew 13:57). They knew all about him, and they did not know him. And Matthew says: he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.

How easily this happens to us. We have heard the Gospel a hundred times. We know the Mass by heart. We have seen the crucifix on the wall since we were children. And so we can sit in church and see nothing. Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house. Let us pray today for fresh eyes. St Peter Chrysologus, the golden-worded bishop, used to preach short sermons about the oldest passages of the Gospel and people would listen as if they had never heard them before. May the Lord give us that same freshness at the next Mass, when he will be in front of us again, on the altar, risen, the first fruits of our harvest. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago God told the people of Israel to have special feast days, when they would stop work and sing and thank him. We have feast days too, like Sundays and Christmas and Easter. In the Gospel Jesus went back to the village where he grew up, and the people said: he is only the carpenter's son! They did not see how special he was, so Jesus did not do many miracles there. Do not forget how special your family and friends are. And remember how special Jesus is every Sunday at Mass!

Prayer

O God, who made Saint Peter Chrysologus an outstanding preacher of your incarnate Word, grant, we pray, that we may always ponder in our hearts the mystery of your salvation, which he so wonderfully expounded, and faithfully express it in the way we live. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord, you come near to us on the appointed days and on the plainest days. Open our eyes, so that we may recognise you in the Mass, in the Word and in our neighbour, and keep us from the unbelief of those who knew you too well to know you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Leviticus now gives the calendar of Israel’s year: “These are the appointed festivals of the Lord, the holy convocations.” Time itself is made holy: Sabbath, Passover, the first fruits, the fifty days to the festival of Weeks, the Day of Atonement, and the festival of Booths. Each is a meeting with God at an appointed time. In the Gospel Jesus comes to his own hometown, Nazareth, and teaches in the synagogue. The people are astounded, then offended: they know his family and cannot see beyond it. One reading is about time that God makes holy; the other is about a familiar person in whom God was present, and was not recognised. The memorial of St Peter Chrysologus, bishop and Doctor of the Church, may be kept today.

Leviticus 23:1, 4–11, 15–16, 27, 34b–37 · These are the appointed festivals of the Lord

Setting.

Chapter 23 belongs to the Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26), laws that call Israel to be holy because the Lord is holy. After laws on sacrifice and purity, it turns to time. The chapter lists the weekly Sabbath and then the annual festivals, each with its date and its actions. Our reading takes some of the high points; today and tomorrow the Lectionary reads a few selected passages of the book.

The festivals are rooted in the agricultural year, with the barley harvest in spring, the wheat harvest in early summer, and the fruit harvest in autumn. They are also rooted in Israel’s history: Passover recalls the night of the Exodus, and Booths recalls the years in the wilderness (23:43). In this way the seasons of nature and the story of salvation are joined.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the Lord introduces the appointed festivals, the “holy convocations”.
  • vv. 5–8: Passover on the fourteenth day, followed by seven days of unleavened bread.
  • vv. 9–11: the first sheaf of the harvest is brought to the priest and waved before the Lord.
  • vv. 15–16: seven weeks are counted, fifty days, to a new offering of grain.
  • v. 27: the Day of Atonement on the tenth day of the seventh month.
  • vv. 34–37: the festival of Booths, seven days, with a solemn assembly on the eighth.

Key words.

  • môʿēd, appointed time, festival: From a root “to meet” or “to appoint”. A festival is an appointment with God. The “tent of meeting” has the same word.
  • miqrāʾ qōdeš, holy convocation: A holy gathering, “called together”. God calls his people to assemble, and the people come.
  • ʿōmer, sheaf: The first bundle of barley cut at the start of the harvest and brought to the priest. It is waved before the Lord as a sign that the whole harvest belongs to him.

Verse by verse.

“These are the appointed festivals of the Lord, the holy convocations, which you shall celebrate at the time appointed for them.” (23:4)

The Lord, not the people, appoints the times. Israel does not choose when to meet God; God gives the times, and the community answers.

“you shall bring the sheaf of the first fruits of your harvest to the priest.” (23:10)

Before the people eat any of the new harvest they give the first of it to God. The first fruits are a thanksgiving and a confession that the land is a gift.

“You shall count until the day after the seventh sabbath, fifty days;” (23:16)

The fifty days between the sheaf and the new grain offering give the festival its later Greek name, Pentecost, “the fiftieth”. It is a time of counting, expectation and thanks.

“you shall deny yourselves” (23:27)

The Day of Atonement is a day of fasting and repentance, the one day of the year when the whole people stands before God as sinners and asks forgiveness.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in these festivals the shadow of what Christ fulfils (Colossians 2:16–17), without denying their worth or rejecting the Jewish people who keep them still. The Catechism teaches that Jesus chose the time of Passover to institute the Eucharist (CCC 1340), and that Christian liturgy has roots in Jewish worship, for the Church’s feasts are connected to the same story of salvation (CCC 1096). St Paul calls Christ “the first fruits of those who have died” (1 Corinthians 15:20), and the fiftieth day, Pentecost, is when the Holy Spirit is poured out on the Church (Acts 2:1; CCC 731). The Church’s own year, with its appointed seasons, is the continuation of this idea: time is made holy by God’s works and by our meeting with him.

Matthew 13:54–58 · Prophets are not without honor except in their own country

Setting.

After the parables of chapter 13, Jesus comes “to his hometown”, Nazareth, and teaches in the synagogue there. The people’s first reaction is astonishment at his wisdom and his deeds of power, which they have heard of from elsewhere.

The astonishment turns to offence when they think of his family. They know him as the carpenter’s son, and they know his mother and his relatives. Familiarity becomes a barrier: because they know where he came from, they cannot imagine who he is.

Structure.

  • v. 54: Jesus teaches in the synagogue; the people are astounded.
  • vv. 55–56: “Where did this man get all this?” They name his family.
  • v. 57: “they took offense at him”; Jesus answers with a proverb about prophets.
  • v. 58: he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.

Key words.

  • tektōn, carpenter: A craftsman who worked in wood and also in stone or metal. Jesus and Joseph were ordinary working people. The people of Nazareth see only the ordinary.
  • eskandalizonto, took offense: From skandalon, a stumbling block. The people stumble at the contrast between what they see and what he teaches. Faith needs openness to the unexpected.
  • apistia, unbelief: The lack of trust that closes the door to God’s action. Jesus’ power is not lessened; it is the people’s closed hearts that limit what they receive.

Verse by verse.

“Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary?” (13:55)

The questions are ordinary, and they are true: Jesus is the son of a carpenter’s household and of Mary. The Church holds that this ordinary humanity is real, and also that there is more to him than they see.

“Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house.” (13:57)

The proverb is true in the story of Israel too, for Jeremiah and other prophets were rejected by their own. Jesus says it with sadness; he takes it as the lot of a prophet.

“And he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” (13:58)

Mark says even more strongly that he “could do no deed of power there” (Mark 6:5). Faith does not force God’s hand, but where there is no faith, there is no opening for the gift.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that during the greater part of his life Jesus shared the condition of most human beings: a daily life without evident greatness, a life of manual labour (CCC 531). That the people of Nazareth found this hard to reconcile with his teaching is a warning to everyone who has grown used to him. The “brothers and sisters” named here are, in the Catholic understanding, near relatives of Jesus and not children of Mary, who remained a virgin; the Greek and Hebrew words cover cousins and other relatives (CCC 500). The Church also teaches that faith is a gift that must be welcomed willingly, and that one can close the door by refusing to listen.

Questions people ask

Who are the “brothers and sisters” of Jesus?

The Gospels use the word adelphoi, which in the Greek of the Septuagint, and in the Hebrew word behind it, can mean brothers but also cousins and other close relatives. The Catholic Church holds that Mary remained a virgin all her life and that these were near kinsmen of Jesus (CCC 500). Mark and Matthew also name a different Mary at the cross as mother of James and Joseph (Matthew 27:56), which suggests that at least some of these were children of another woman.

For the Latin reader

St Peter Chrysologus (about 380–450) was bishop of Ravenna, then the capital of the Western Roman Empire. His name, “golden-worded”, was given later in recognition of his preaching. His sermons are short, vivid and practical; many speak of the Gospel, of prayer and fasting, and of Mary. He was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1729. His memorial may be kept today, and the Gospel suits him: he preached to ordinary people in their own language and in familiar images.

For study and teaching

  • Israel kept holy times: Sabbath, Passover, Booths. What are the holy times in my own week and year, and do I keep them or let them be crowded out?
  • The people of Nazareth could not see past what they knew. Whom do I take for granted, in my family, in the parish or in Christ himself, and how might I look again?
