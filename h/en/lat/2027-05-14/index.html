Word for the day

“You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last,” John 15:16

Reflection

Of all the apostles, Matthias is the one we know least, and yet his feast falls at exactly the right moment. The Lord has ascended; the disciples have gone back to Jerusalem and are praying in the upper room “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus.” In those days of waiting, Peter stands up among about a hundred and twenty believers. There is a wound in the Twelve. Judas, who “was numbered among us and was allotted his share in this ministry,” has gone “to his own place.” Peter reads the tragedy in the light of the Psalms: “Let his homestead become desolate,” and “Let another take his position of overseer.” The number twelve is not a detail. Jesus chose twelve to stand for the twelve tribes of Israel, the whole People of God gathered anew, and he promised that they would sit on thrones judging those tribes (Luke 22:30). Before the Spirit comes and the Church goes out to the nations, the circle must be complete.

What kind of man must fill the empty place? Peter names the condition: “one of the men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us.” Above all, “one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” An apostle is not chosen for his talents or his eloquence, but because he was with Jesus and has seen him risen. Two names are put forward: “Joseph called Barsabbas, who was also known as Justus, and Matthias.” Then the whole community prays: “Lord, you know everyone's heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen.” They cast lots, an ancient way in Israel of leaving the decision to God (“The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is the Lord's alone,” Proverbs 16:33), and “the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” It is the last time lots are used in the New Testament. After Pentecost the Church will discern by the Holy Spirit, by prayer and by the laying on of hands.

The Psalm sings what happened to Matthias: “He raises the poor from the dust, and lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes, with the princes of his people.” An unknown disciple, who had followed Jesus quietly from the Jordan to the Mount of Olives without ever being named, is suddenly placed among the foundations of the Church. The book of Revelation will say that the wall of the holy city “has twelve foundations, and on them are the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb.” Matthias's name is written there. Joseph Barsabbas was not chosen, and we hear no complaint from him; he too is part of the story, a reminder that God's choice is never a rejection of the one who serves elsewhere.

The Gospel tells us where every apostolic call begins: “You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last.” Matthias did not campaign for the place; the Lord, who knows every heart, chose him. And the choice is an act of friendship: “I do not call you servants any longer … but I have called you friends.” The heart of the apostle's work is the commandment of love: “love one another as I have loved you. No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” Of Matthias's later life we know almost nothing for certain. Eusebius records a tradition that he was one of the Seventy whom Jesus sent out (Luke 10:1). Ancient accounts send him to preach in Judea, in Cappadocia, or on the shores of the Black Sea, and say he died a martyr; the Abbey of St Matthias at Trier in Germany has honoured relics believed to be his since the Middle Ages. What is certain is enough: he was with Jesus, he saw him risen, he was chosen by the Lord, and he received the Holy Spirit with the others at Pentecost. His name means “gift of the Lord.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate an apostle who is mentioned by name only twice in the whole Bible, both times in one chapter of the Acts of the Apostles. We know almost nothing of his words or his travels. And yet Matthias is one of the twelve foundations of the Church.

The apostles had returned from the Mount of Olives and were praying in the upper room with Mary and the other women. There was an empty place. Judas, who had been one of them, had gone to his own place. Jesus had chosen twelve, for the twelve tribes of Israel. Before the Holy Spirit came, the circle had to be complete.

Peter says what kind of person is needed: one who had been with Jesus from the baptism of John to the Ascension, and who could become a witness with us to his resurrection (Acts 1:22). Not the cleverest, not the richest, not the most popular. The one who had stayed with Jesus, and had seen him alive.

Then the community did something we often forget to do: they prayed before they decided. Lord, you know everyone's heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen (Acts 1:24). They did not want their own choice; they wanted the Lord's. And the lot fell on Matthias. How many of our family quarrels and wrong decisions would be avoided if we prayed like this first?

In the Gospel Jesus tells us that this is how every vocation works. You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last (John 15:16). Our faith is first of all the Lord's choice of us. And he chooses us as friends: I do not call you servants any longer, but I have called you friends. The mark of his friends is love: love one another as I have loved you.

Perhaps you feel like Matthias before that day: following Jesus quietly, never noticed, never named. Do not be discouraged. The Lord sees you. He raises the poor from the dust to make them sit with princes. Stay with him, be a witness of his resurrection where you are, and when he calls, be ready. St Matthias, pray for us, and with Mary and the apostles, let us pray: Come, Holy Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, Matthias followed Jesus for a long, long time, but nobody wrote his name in the Gospels. He was a quiet friend of Jesus. When the apostles needed one more, they prayed, and God chose Matthias!

Jesus says to you: I chose you, and you are my friend. Even when nobody notices you, Jesus sees you. Say: Thank you, Jesus, for choosing me!

Prayer

O God, who assigned Saint Matthias a place in the college of Apostles, grant us, through his intercession, that, rejoicing at your love for us, we may merit to be numbered among the elect. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you did not wait for us to choose you; you chose us and called us your friends. As you chose Matthias to be a witness of your resurrection, make us witnesses in our homes and workplaces. Teach us to pray before we decide, to serve without seeking a name, and to love one another as you have loved us. With Mary and the apostles we wait for your Spirit: come, Holy Spirit, and fill the empty places in our hearts and in your Church. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today is the Feast of Saint Matthias, Apostle. The first reading tells how he was chosen, in the days between the Ascension and Pentecost, to take the place that Judas had left, so that the Twelve were complete when the Spirit came. The Gospel gives the words that stand behind every apostolic call: “You did not choose me but I chose you.” The choice is Christ’s, and it is a choice of friendship and of fruit that will last.

Psalm 113 sings of the Lord who “raises the poor from the dust”, to make them sit with the princes of his people. Matthias is not mentioned in the Gospels by name and is never mentioned again after this chapter. He is a humble disciple raised by God to sit among the Twelve. In India this year the feast falls between the Ascension (kept last Sunday) and Pentecost, exactly where the reading places it.

Acts 1:15–17, 20–26 · The lot fell on Matthias

Setting.

After the Ascension the apostles returned to Jerusalem and gathered in the upper room, devoting themselves to prayer with the women, with Mary the mother of Jesus, and with his brothers (1:12–14). Before the Spirit comes, Peter rises to act. This is his first act of leadership in Acts. The Lectionary leaves out vv. 18–19, Luke’s account of the death of Judas, which differs in detail from Matthew’s (Matthew 27:3–10); both tell of a field linked with blood.

Background. The Twelve stand for the twelve tribes of Israel. Jesus had promised that they would sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes (Luke 22:30). For the renewed Israel to be complete at Pentecost, the circle of Twelve had to be whole. Casting lots was an accepted way in Israel of leaving a decision to God: “The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is the Lord’s alone” (Proverbs 16:33). Zechariah was chosen by lot to offer incense in the Temple (Luke 1:9). After Pentecost Acts never again tells of a choice made by lot.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: Peter stands up among about one hundred and twenty; Judas was one of us.
  • v. 20: two verses of the Psalms: his house desolate; another must take his office.
  • vv. 21–22: the condition: a companion from John’s baptism to the Ascension, a witness to the resurrection.
  • v. 23: two are proposed, Joseph Barsabbas (Justus) and Matthias.
  • vv. 24–25: the prayer to the Lord who knows every heart.
  • v. 26: the lot falls on Matthias; he is added to the eleven.
  • ¶ The scene moves from Scripture (vv. 16–20), to discernment by the community (vv. 21–23), to prayer (vv. 24–25), and only then to the choice (v. 26).

Key words.

  • klēros, lot, share: Judas “was allotted his share” (v. 17, literally “received the lot”) in this ministry, and in v. 26 they “cast lots”. The same word: a share in ministry is always a gift received, never a possession. From klēros comes our word “clergy”.
  • episkopē, position of overseer: From Psalm 109:8 in the Greek Old Testament: “Let another take his position of overseer.” The word is related to episkopos, “overseer”, which later became the word for bishop.
  • martys tēs anastaseōs, witness to his resurrection: The heart of an apostle’s task (v. 22). An apostle is one who was with Jesus, saw him risen, and is sent to tell it.
  • kardiognōstēs, knower of hearts: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart” (v. 24). Luke uses the same word of God in 15:8. The community does its part, and then asks the Lord who sees what they cannot.

Verse by verse.

“In those days Peter stood up among the believers (together the crowd numbered about one hundred twenty persons) and said,” (1:15)

Peter, who denied the Lord three times, now leads his brothers, as Jesus had asked: “strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). St John Chrysostom notes that Peter acts first, as the one entrusted with the flock, yet he does nothing without the whole company (in substance).

“for he was numbered among us and was allotted his share in this ministry.” (1:17)

Judas was truly called and truly one of the Twelve. A call from Christ does not take away human liberty. The verse is sobering, and it is also a reason to pray for all who have been called.

“‘Let another take his position of overseer.’” (1:20)

Peter reads two psalms of the suffering just man (Psalm 69:25 and Psalm 109:8) in the light of what happened. The office of an apostle does not die with the one who abandons it; another must take it.

“one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” (1:22)

The one chosen must have walked with Jesus “beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us”. The witness of the apostles is not to an idea but to a person they knew, crucified and risen.

““Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen” (1:24)

They do not ask God to choose; they ask him to show whom he has already chosen. The prayer is addressed to “Lord”, probably the risen Jesus, who chose the Twelve in the first place (1:2).

“And they cast lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” (1:26)

The lot is not chance here but trust: after Scripture, discernment and prayer, the final word is left to God. Joseph Barsabbas, not chosen, remains a disciple. Not being chosen for an office is not being rejected by God.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this scene the first sign of apostolic succession: the apostolic office is meant to continue. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the apostles took care to appoint successors, so that the mission entrusted to them might go on after their death, and that the bishops by divine institution have succeeded to the place of the apostles (Lumen Gentium 20; CCC 861–862). St Clement of Rome, writing to the Corinthians about AD 96, taught that the apostles appointed overseers and deacons and gave instruction that other approved men should succeed them (in substance). Matthias was not one of the first Twelve, but he received the same office and the same witness. The Church today does not choose bishops by lot, but the pattern remains: prayer, discernment, and the conviction that it is the Lord who chooses.

John 15:9–17 · You did not choose me but I chose you

Setting.

These words come from the farewell discourse at the Last Supper, just after the image of the vine and the branches (15:1–8). Jesus has washed the disciples’ feet (13:1–20) and given them the new commandment (13:34). Now he tells them what kind of bond unites them to him.

Background. In the Old Testament Abraham is called the friend of God (Isaiah 41:8; 2 Chronicles 20:7), and the Lord spoke to Moses face to face, “as one speaks to a friend” (Exodus 33:11). In the courts of the ancient world the “friend of the king” was one who shared the king’s plans. Jesus gives this title to his disciples.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–11: abide in my love, as I abide in the Father’s; my joy in you.
  • vv. 12–14: my commandment: love as I have loved; the greatest love is to lay down one’s life.
  • v. 15: no longer servants but friends.
  • v. 16: I chose you and appointed you to bear lasting fruit.
  • v. 17: love one another.

Key words.

  • menō, abide: To stay, remain, make one’s home. It runs through the vine passage (15:4–10). Love is not a feeling that comes and goes but a place where we live.
  • philoi, friends: The word is linked to philein, to love. Jesus’ friends are those for whom he lays down his life (v. 13) and to whom he makes known all that he heard from the Father (v. 15).
  • exelexamēn, I chose: The same verb as in Luke 6:13, where Jesus chose the Twelve, and in Acts 1:2 and 1:24. In Jesus’ time a disciple usually chose his teacher; here the Teacher chooses his disciples.
  • ethēka, I appointed: Literally “I set, I placed”. The call has a purpose: to go and bear fruit.

Verse by verse.

“As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” (15:9)

The love of the Father for the Son is the source; Jesus’ love for us flows from it. We are not asked to produce this love, but to stay in it.

“No one has greater love than this, to lay down one’s life for one’s friends.” (15:13)

Jesus speaks hours before the Cross. Paul goes further: Christ died for us “while we were enemies” (Romans 5:10). Jesus lays down his life for those he makes his friends.

“I do not call you servants any longer, because the servant does not know what the master is doing; but I have called you friends,” (15:15)

Being a servant of God is an honour in the Bible (Moses, David, the Servant of Isaiah). Jesus does not cancel service; he lifts it into friendship and shares his heart with those who serve.

“You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last,” (15:16)

On the feast of Matthias these words are clear: he was chosen, not self-chosen. Every vocation, to the apostolate, the priesthood, religious life, marriage or any service, begins in Christ’s choice.

“I am giving you these commands so that you may love one another.” (15:17)

The passage ends where it began: in love. All the commands come down to one.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus makes charity the new commandment, quoting “As the Father has loved me, so have I loved you; abide in my love” and v. 12 (CCC 1823). It also teaches that the New Law is a law of love that makes us pass from the condition of a servant to that of a friend of Christ, quoting v. 15 (CCC 1972). The Preface of the Apostles praises God who governs his Church through those whom he set over it as shepherds. On this feast the Church thanks God for the apostles, the friends chosen by Christ, and for those who continue their service.

Questions people ask

Was choosing Matthias by lot a kind of gambling with God?

No. The apostles did not use the lot to avoid thinking. They first searched the Scriptures, set out the conditions, proposed two worthy men, and prayed. Only then did they cast lots, as Israel had done, trusting that “the decision is the Lord’s alone” (Proverbs 16:33). After the Spirit came at Pentecost, the Church discerned through prayer and the laying on of hands (Acts 6:6; 13:2–3), not by lot.

Why did the Twelve need to be completed, when later no one replaced James after his martyrdom?

Before Pentecost the Twelve had to be whole, as a sign of the renewed Israel with its twelve tribes (Luke 22:30). When James was killed (Acts 12:2), he had remained faithful, and no one was chosen to take his place: the Twelve as a group remain the foundation, as the Book of Revelation shows with the twelve names of the apostles on the foundations of the holy city (Revelation 21:14). Their mission of leading the Church continues through the bishops.

What do we know about Matthias after his election?

The New Testament says nothing more about him. Later traditions differ about where he preached; some name Judea, others regions further away. According to a tradition linked with St Helena, relics believed to be his are venerated at the Abbey of St Matthias in Trier, Germany. What Scripture gives is enough: he was a faithful disciple from the beginning, chosen by the Lord to be a witness of the resurrection.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman calendar the feast of St Matthias used to be kept on 24 February. In the reform of the calendar after the Second Vatican Council it was moved to 14 May, so that it falls in Eastertide, close to the days between the Ascension and Pentecost when he was chosen. As a feast, the Mass has the Gloria and the Preface of the Apostles, and the vestments are red, the colour of the apostles’ witness. The Collect asks that, through his prayer, we too may be counted among the chosen.

For study and teaching

  • Matthias was faithful for years without being noticed, and then was chosen. Where am I asked to be faithful in hidden ways?
  • The apostles moved from Scripture, to discernment, to prayer, to decision. How do I make important decisions in my life and family?
  • Jesus says, “I have called you friends.” What would change in my prayer if I spoke to him as a friend?
  • Joseph Barsabbas was not chosen. How do I respond when I am passed over?
