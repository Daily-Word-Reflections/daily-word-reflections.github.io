Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!” Jesus said to him, “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.”” John 20:28–29

Reflection

The first reading is a few lines of St Paul about what the Church is. Gentile Christians in Ephesus, who once had no part in Israel's promises, are told: “So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God.” Notice the three pictures. A city, with its citizens. A family, with its household. And a building, the strangest and most beautiful: “built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.” In the ancient world the cornerstone was the first block laid, from which the lines of the whole building were measured. Christ is that stone; the apostles, those whom he sent, are the foundation on which each generation of believers is set as a living stone. The Book of Revelation sees the same wall of the new Jerusalem with “twelve foundations, and on them are the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb.” This is what we mean when we say in the Creed that the Church is apostolic: she is built on their witness, taught by their successors, and sent as they were sent (Catechism 857). Today's feast honours one of those foundation stones. The psalm, the shortest in the Psalter, sets the mission to music: “Praise the Lord, all you nations! Extol him, all you peoples!” St Paul himself quotes it in the Letter to the Romans for the Gentiles who praise God for his mercy.

The Gospel is the best-known story about Thomas, but in John's Gospel Thomas appears three times, and each time shows a different side of him. His name means “twin,” in Aramaic Te'oma and in Greek Didymus, and the Gospel does not tell us whose twin he was. First, when Jesus is going back to Judea, to Bethany near Jerusalem, where he has enemies, the other disciples hang back. “Thomas, who was called the Twin, said to his fellow disciples, ‘Let us also go, that we may die with him.’” This is the Thomas of courage, ready to die with his Master. Second, at the Last Supper Jesus says, “You know the way to the place where I am going,” and Thomas says what the others are thinking: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” His honest question brought out one of the greatest sentences in the Gospel: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.” Third comes today's scene. On Easter evening Thomas was not with them, and we are not told why. The others tell him, “We have seen the Lord.” He answers with a demand that sounds hard and is really very human: “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” Thomas does not ask for anything that the others have not already been given: Jesus had shown them his hands and his side the first evening (John 20:20). Eight days later, on the first day of the week again, “although the doors were shut, Jesus came and stood among them and said, ‘Peace be with you.’” Notice that the Lord does not scold him. He offers exactly what Thomas asked for: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” The Gospel does not say that Thomas actually touched. What follows is the highest confession of faith in the whole Gospel of John: “My Lord and my God!” In four words, Thomas says what the Prologue said at the beginning of the book, that the Word was God. And the risen body bears the wounds: they are not erased but glorified (Catechism 645). Then comes a blessing for all of us who came later: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” The Evangelist ends this chapter by saying that all this was written “so that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and that through believing you may have life in his name.”

According to tradition, Thomas did not stay in Jerusalem. The ancient Acts of Thomas, written in the third century, probably in Syriac, tells how he travelled to India, and the Christians of Kerala hold that he landed at Muziris, near modern Kodungallur, in the year 52, preached, baptised and founded seven communities, and was martyred by a lance at Mylapore, near Chennai, in the year 72. Historians discuss the details, and the early records are few; but the Church of India has held this memory dear for many centuries, and the ancient Christian communities of the Malabar coast, called the Thomas Christians or Nazranis, are a living witness that the faith came here very early. The tomb at Mylapore, over which stands the Basilica of San Thome, is venerated as the tomb of the Apostle; an ancient tradition says that some of his relics were taken to Edessa and later to Ortona in Italy. The Catholic Churches of India, Latin and Eastern, as well as the Orthodox and Mar Thoma Churches, all look to Thomas as their father in faith, and today we rejoice with all of them. India received the faith, we may say, not from a conqueror or a trader but from a man who first doubted, then adored, and then carried the risen Lord across the sea.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today is the feast of St Thomas, and for us in India it is a very special feast. According to the tradition that our fathers have kept for nearly two thousand years, this apostle crossed the sea, came to our coast, preached, baptised, and gave his life for Christ. We are the heirs of that story. Many of our families can trace their faith back to him. Today let us give thanks.

But we know Thomas best from the Gospel we have just heard, and I want to say first that the Church does not remember him as a failure. In John's Gospel he appears three times. The first time he says: Let us also go, that we may die with him (John 11:16). Thomas is the one who is ready to follow Jesus to death, when others hesitate. The second time he says: Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way? (John 14:5). He asks the honest question, and because of it we have the answer: I am the way, and the truth, and the life. And the third time is the scene we heard today.

On Easter evening Thomas was not with the others. Perhaps he could not bear to be in that room; perhaps he was grieving by himself. When they tell him, we have seen the Lord, he says: Unless I see the mark of the nails... I will not believe (John 20:25). We are very hard on Thomas. But he asks for nothing that the others had not already been given. They too had been shown his hands and his side. Thomas is asking to be on the same footing as his friends.

And what does Jesus do? He does not scold. A week later, on the same day of the week, the first day, the day we gather for Mass, he comes through closed doors and says: Peace be with you. Then he turns to Thomas, the doubter, and gives him exactly what he asked for: Put your finger here and see my hands (John 20:27). Dear friends, how gentle is our Lord with honest doubt! If you have questions about your faith, do not hide them. Bring them to him. Bring them to a priest or a catechist. The Lord is not afraid of them.

And Thomas answers with the highest confession of faith in the whole Gospel: My Lord and my God! (John 20:28). Then Jesus looks past Thomas and speaks to us: Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe (John 20:29). That means every one of us in this church. We have not seen his wounds, but we have seen his love in the saints, in our parents, in the sacraments. Every time we kneel at the consecration, we say the words of Thomas: My Lord and my God. Let us say them today with all our hearts, and let us ask St Thomas to keep India faithful to the Lord whom he loved and for whom he died.

For children

Children, today is the feast of St Thomas, one of the twelve friends of Jesus. After Jesus rose from the dead, Thomas was not there when Jesus came to see his friends. They told him, but he said: I need to see it myself! A week later Jesus came again and said to Thomas: look at my hands. And Thomas said: My Lord and my God! He believed. Jesus said: happy are those who believe even when they cannot see. That is us! A long time ago, according to tradition, Thomas travelled all the way to India, and the Christians of Kerala say he told our country about Jesus. Let us say thank you to St Thomas, and let us say his prayer: My Lord and my God!

Prayer

Almighty ever-living God, who gave blessed Thomas the Apostle grace to believe more through his doubting, grant that we too may believe in your Son, and so may have life in his name. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, who showed your wounds to Thomas, we have not seen you, yet we love you. Strengthen our faith, answer our doubts with your peace, and make us living stones in the house built on your apostles. Bless the Church in India, which looks to Thomas as her father in faith, and make us faithful witnesses wherever you send us. My Lord and my God. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of St Thomas the Church reads two texts about foundations. Ephesians says that the household of God is “built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone”, and the Gospel shows one of those apostles at the moment his faith is laid. Thomas, who refused to believe a report, meets the risen Lord and speaks the highest confession in the Gospel of John: “My Lord and my God!” Psalm 117, with its refrain “Go out to all the world and tell the Good News” (Mark 16:15), turns the apostle’s faith into a mission to all nations. According to tradition, that mission carried Thomas as far as India.

Ephesians 2:19–22 · Built upon the foundation of the apostles

Setting.

Ephesians is a letter about the Church as one body of Jews and Gentiles in Christ. Just before our passage Paul, or the author writing in his name, reminds Gentile believers that they were once “aliens from the commonwealth of Israel” and “strangers to the covenants of promise” (2:12), and that in Christ the dividing wall has been broken down (2:14). They are now brought near, to share in what God gave to Israel.

Authorship. The letter claims to be Paul’s, written from prison (3:1; 4:1), and the Catholic tradition receives it as his. Many scholars think it was written by a disciple in Paul’s name soon after his death. In either case the Church reads it as Scripture. Some early manuscripts omit “in Ephesus” in 1:1, which suggests that it may have been a circular letter for several churches.

Structure.

  • v. 19: from outsiders to citizens: “no longer strangers and aliens”.
  • v. 20: the foundation: the apostles and prophets, and Christ as cornerstone.
  • vv. 21–22: the building grows into a holy temple, a dwelling for God in the Spirit.

Key words.

  • paroikoi, aliens, resident foreigners: People living in a place without full rights. Paul says that the Gentile believers are no longer in that condition; they now belong to the household.
  • akrogōniaios, cornerstone: The stone at the corner that fixes the line of the walls. The image comes from Isaiah 28:16 and Psalm 118:22, which the New Testament applies to Christ (1 Peter 2:6–7; Mark 12:10).
  • naos hagios, holy temple: The word naos means the sanctuary itself, where God dwells. The Church, built of living people, becomes his dwelling.

Verse by verse.

“you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God” (2:19)

Gentile believers are not guests in someone else’s home. They are members of God’s household, the same household that includes Abraham, Moses and the prophets.

“built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.” (2:20)

The apostles are witnesses to the Resurrection, and their testimony is the foundation. Most scholars take “prophets” here to be the prophets of the New Testament Church (compare 3:5; 4:11); others include the prophets of Israel. The cornerstone, Christ, holds the whole together.

“In him the whole structure is joined together and grows into a holy temple in the Lord” (2:21)

A living building: it “grows”. The Church is not finished, and each generation is another course of stones.

“you also are built together spiritually into a dwelling place for God.” (2:22)

“Together” and “spiritually”: the Spirit makes a community, not a collection of private believers, into God’s dwelling place.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage when it calls the Church God’s building: the Lord compared himself to the stone which the builders rejected, and on this foundation the Church is built by the apostles (CCC 756). The Church is also apostolic because she is founded on the apostles, in three ways: built on their witness, kept by the Holy Spirit in their teaching, and guided by their successors (CCC 857). Thomas, on this feast, stands for all the apostles whose faith became the foundation of the faith of others.

John 20:24–29 · My Lord and my God!

Setting.

It is the evening of Easter Sunday, and the risen Jesus has appeared to the disciples behind locked doors, showed them his hands and side, breathed on them the Holy Spirit and given them power to forgive sins (20:19–23). Thomas, “one of the twelve”, was not there. John calls him Didymos, the Greek for “twin” (the Aramaic is te’oma). We have met him twice before in this Gospel: when Lazarus died he said, “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (11:16), and at the Last Supper he asked, “How can we know the way?” (14:5).

Background. A week later, which is the Sunday after Easter, the disciples are again together. From earliest times the Church has seen in this the beginning of her gathering for the Lord’s Day. The passage is the last of John’s appearance stories; verses 30–31 that follow give the purpose of the whole Gospel: “that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God”.

Structure.

  • vv. 24–25: Thomas is absent; he hears the news and refuses to believe on report.
  • v. 26: a week later Jesus comes through closed doors: “Peace be with you.”
  • v. 27: Jesus invites Thomas to touch his hands and side.
  • v. 28: Thomas’ confession.
  • v. 29: the blessing on those who believe without seeing.

Key words.

  • didymos, twin: Both the Aramaic name Thomas and the Greek Didymos mean “twin”. The Gospel does not tell us whose twin he was. Tradition gives him the name Judas Thomas.
  • apistos … pistos, unbelieving … believing: The Greek says, literally, “do not become unbelieving but believing”. It is not a rebuke only; it is a call to become a believer. The verb “believe” in the Gospel’s purpose statement (20:31) comes from the same root.
  • ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, my Lord and my God: Kyrios, Lord, is the word used for the God of Israel in the Greek Old Testament, and theos is God. The Gospel which began with “the Word was God” (1:1) ends its stories with a disciple saying it of Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” (20:25)

Thomas wants no more than the others were given (20:20). He asks to see the Crucified. He is not the sceptic who asks for proof of just anything; he wants the wounds.

“Although the doors were shut, Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you.”” (20:26)

The same greeting as on Easter evening. The risen body is the same body that was crucified, and it is no longer bound by walls.

“Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” (20:27)

Jesus answers Thomas’ words one by one. He knows what Thomas said in his absence. John does not say that Thomas actually touched him; many early readers supposed he did, but the text shows him moved to confession at the sight of the Lord.

“My Lord and my God!” (20:28)

The highest confession in the Gospel, and Thomas is the one who says it. He does not say “you are like God”, but “my God”. The words are personal: my Lord, my God.

“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

The blessing looks beyond the apostles to every later believer. It does not scold Thomas for asking; it names the faith of all who rely on the apostles’ witness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the disciples’ doubt before the risen Jesus is part of the Gospel record: Thomas himself went through the test of doubt, so the idea that the Resurrection was a product of the apostles’ credulity is untenable (CCC 644). The risen body is the same body that was crucified, and it still bears the marks of the Passion, yet it already shares in the glory of God (CCC 645). St Augustine says that Thomas saw and touched the man and confessed the God whom he could neither see nor touch (in substance, Tractates on John 121). St Gregory the Great taught that the Lord allowed Thomas’ doubt so that the believers who came after him would be strengthened (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 26). Many Catholics say Thomas’ words silently as the Host and the chalice are raised at Mass.

Questions people ask

Was Thomas wrong to doubt?

Thomas wanted to meet the risen Lord for himself, as the other apostles had (20:20). He was not condemned: Jesus came back for him a week later and spoke to him with gentleness. The title “Doubting Thomas” is too narrow. The Gospel shows him earlier as loyal to the point of death (11:16) and here as the one who makes the most complete confession of faith in the Gospel.

Did Thomas really come to India?

Thomas’ journey to India is not told in the New Testament. It rests on a strong and ancient tradition. The Christians of India have always held that he preached on the Malabar coast, and an early third-century non-canonical work, the Acts of Thomas, places him in India at the court of King Gondophares. Historians discuss the details, but the Church honours the tradition, and the Christians of India of several Churches honour him as the apostle who brought them the Gospel.

What does it mean that the Church is built on the apostles, if Christ is the cornerstone?

There is no rivalry. Christ is the cornerstone who holds everything together; the apostles are the foundation because they were chosen and sent by him, and they are witnesses of his Resurrection. Their authority is his, given to them (compare Matthew 28:18–20; CCC 857).

For the Latin reader

St Thomas is listed among the Twelve in all the Gospels, and the Gospel of John gives him the most memorable lines. The feast was kept on 21 December in the Roman calendar until the reform of 1969, when it was moved to 3 July, the day on which, according to tradition, his relics were brought to Edessa (present-day Urfa in Turkey). Tradition also holds that Thomas reached the Malabar coast in the year 52, founded communities there, and was martyred at Mylapore, near present-day Chennai, about the year 72, where his tomb is venerated. For Catholics in India the feast is of special joy, and Christians of several Churches there honour him. His words, “My Lord and my God”, are among the shortest and fullest prayers of the Church.

For study and teaching

  • Thomas asked to see the wounds of the Crucified. What in my own faith needs to meet the real Jesus, wounds and all, and not an idea of him?
  • Thomas was away when Jesus came. How can I make sure I am present when my community gathers on the Lord’s Day?
  • “Blessed are those who have not seen.” Who passed the faith to me, and how can I pass it on?
  • Ephesians says the Church is still growing. What small stone can I add to it this week?
