Word for the day

“A voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more.” Matthew 2:18

Reflection

Within the joy of Christmas the Church remembers the small boys of Bethlehem killed by King Herod. Herod, afraid of losing his throne to the newborn “king of the Jews”, sent soldiers to kill all the children of two years and under in and around Bethlehem. Bethlehem was a small village, so the number of children was probably small; but each one was a person loved by God.

The Church has honoured these children as martyrs since early times. They could not yet speak or choose, yet they died in place of Christ and because of him. The Church teaches that those who die for the faith before receiving Baptism are saved by this “baptism of blood” (CCC 1258). An ancient sermon read today in the Office of Readings says of them that they confess Christ not by speaking but by dying.

Matthew sees in the scene the fulfilment of Jeremiah: “Rachel weeping for her children”. Rachel, mother of the tribes, weeps over her lost children; and every mother who has lost a child can find her grief named in Scripture. Yet Jeremiah's next words are of hope: “there is hope for your future” (Jeremiah 31:17). The child Jesus escapes to Egypt, as Israel once went down to Egypt, and later the Lord will call his Son out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1).

The first reading reminds us that “God is light and in him there is no darkness at all”, and that Jesus “is the atoning sacrifice for our sins”. Herod's darkness does not have the last word. Today the Church prays for all innocent children who suffer: unborn children, children killed in war, abused or abandoned children, and their grieving parents (CCC 2270–2274).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, Christmas is a feast of joy, yet today the Church remembers something terrible: the killing of the little children of Bethlehem.

King Herod heard from the wise men that a new king had been born. He was afraid of losing his power. He tried to trick the wise men and, when that failed, he sent soldiers to kill every boy of two years and under in Bethlehem.

But God protected his Son. An angel warned Joseph in a dream: get up, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt. And Joseph got up that very night and went. Silent, obedient Joseph saved the life of the Saviour.

Matthew recalls the prophet Jeremiah: a voice in Ramah, Rachel weeping for her children, refusing to be comforted. Scripture does not hide the grief of mothers. God sees every tear. And the Church honours these children as the first martyrs: they could not speak, but they confessed Christ by dying for him.

Today Herod has many faces. Children are still killed before birth, in wars, through hunger and abuse. The Holy Innocents ask us to stand with the weakest, to protect life from its beginning, and to comfort parents who grieve.

Jesus said: “Let the little children come to me, and do not stop them.” Let us bring all the children of the world to him today, and let us be like Joseph, ready to rise and act to protect them.

Dear brothers and sisters, three days after Christmas the Church remembers a terrible event. King Herod, afraid that the newborn King of the Jews would take his throne, ordered the killing of all the boys in and around Bethlehem who were two years old or under (Matthew 2:16). The Church honours these children as martyrs: they died in place of Christ.

Herod is a picture of power that feels threatened. He had palaces and soldiers, yet he was terrified of a baby. Fear makes people cruel. There are Herods in every age: those who sacrifice the weak to protect their comfort, position or wealth. And there is a little Herod in each of us when fear makes us harsh.

Matthew quotes the prophet: a voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more (Matthew 2:18; Jeremiah 31:15). God hears that cry. Today let us pray for all parents who have lost a child, through illness, accident, violence or miscarriage. Their children are in God's hands.

And let us ask: where are the innocents in danger today? The unborn child whose life is not protected. Children who are abused, even, to our great shame, in some places within the Church. Children forced to work, children trafficked, children in refugee camps, children growing up without love. Jesus said that whoever welcomes a child in His name welcomes Him, and warned terribly against those who cause little ones to stumble (Matthew 18:5–6).

So this feast calls each of us to be a Joseph, not a Herod: to rise and protect the child. Make our homes and parishes safe places. Support works that care for children in need. Defend life from its beginning. At this Holy Mass let us offer all the innocent victims of our world to the Child of Bethlehem, who came to save them.

Dear brothers and sisters, the Holy Innocents were killed before they could speak a word, yet the Church has honoured them as martyrs since ancient times. Their feast invites us to recall the Church's teaching on the dignity of the child.

The Catechism teaches that human life is sacred because from its beginning it involves the creative action of God; God alone is the Lord of life from beginning to end; no one can claim the right directly to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258). Human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception (CCC 2270). The prophet heard God say: before I formed you in the womb I knew you (Jeremiah 1:5). And the child John leaped in Elizabeth's womb at the presence of the unborn Christ (Luke 1:41).

Children are the supreme gift of marriage (CCC 2378). They are not property and not a burden, but persons entrusted to their parents. Parents have the first right and duty to educate them; society and the State have the duty to protect them (CCC 2211, 2221). Jesus took the children in His arms and blessed them, and said that the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14–16).

The Church also has a grave duty to protect minors in her own life. Every parish, school and institution must be a safe place. Where children have been harmed, the truth must be faced, victims must be heard and helped, and justice done.

At the same time, the Church proclaims mercy. If someone here carries the wound of abortion, in their own life or by assisting another, do not despair. The sin is grave, but God's mercy is greater. Come to confession; the Father runs to embrace the returning child (Luke 15:20). Let us offer this Holy Mass for all innocent victims, for mothers in difficulty, and for a culture that welcomes every child.
