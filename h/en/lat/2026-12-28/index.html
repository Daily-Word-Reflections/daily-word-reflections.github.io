Word for the day

“A voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more.” Matthew 2:18

Reflection

Within the joy of Christmas the Church remembers the small boys of Bethlehem killed by King Herod. Herod, afraid of losing his throne to the newborn “king of the Jews”, sent soldiers to kill all the children of two years and under in and around Bethlehem. Bethlehem was a small village, so the number of children was probably small; but each one was a person loved by God.

The Church has honoured these children as martyrs since early times. They could not yet speak or choose, yet they died in place of Christ and because of him. The Church teaches that those who die for the faith before receiving Baptism are saved by this “baptism of blood” (CCC 1258). An ancient sermon read today in the Office of Readings says of them that they confess Christ not by speaking but by dying.

Matthew sees in the scene the fulfilment of Jeremiah: “Rachel weeping for her children”. Rachel, mother of the tribes, weeps over her lost children; and every mother who has lost a child can find her grief named in Scripture. Yet Jeremiah's next words are of hope: “there is hope for your future” (Jeremiah 31:17). The child Jesus escapes to Egypt, as Israel once went down to Egypt, and later the Lord will call his Son out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1).

The first reading reminds us that “God is light and in him there is no darkness at all”, and that Jesus “is the atoning sacrifice for our sins”. Herod's darkness does not have the last word. Today the Church prays for all innocent children who suffer: unborn children, children killed in war, abused or abandoned children, and their grieving parents (CCC 2270–2274).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, Christmas is a feast of joy, yet today the Church remembers something terrible: the killing of the little children of Bethlehem.

King Herod heard from the wise men that a new king had been born. He was afraid of losing his power. He tried to trick the wise men and, when that failed, he sent soldiers to kill every boy of two years and under in Bethlehem.

But God protected his Son. An angel warned Joseph in a dream: get up, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt. And Joseph got up that very night and went. Silent, obedient Joseph saved the life of the Saviour.

Matthew recalls the prophet Jeremiah: a voice in Ramah, Rachel weeping for her children, refusing to be comforted. Scripture does not hide the grief of mothers. God sees every tear. And the Church honours these children as the first martyrs: they could not speak, but they confessed Christ by dying for him.

Today Herod has many faces. Children are still killed before birth, in wars, through hunger and abuse. The Holy Innocents ask us to stand with the weakest, to protect life from its beginning, and to comfort parents who grieve.

Jesus said: “Let the little children come to me, and do not stop them.” Let us bring all the children of the world to him today, and let us be like Joseph, ready to rise and act to protect them.

Dear brothers and sisters, three days after Christmas the Church remembers a terrible event. King Herod, afraid that the newborn King of the Jews would take his throne, ordered the killing of all the boys in and around Bethlehem who were two years old or under (Matthew 2:16). The Church honours these children as martyrs: they died in place of Christ.

Herod is a picture of power that feels threatened. He had palaces and soldiers, yet he was terrified of a baby. Fear makes people cruel. There are Herods in every age: those who sacrifice the weak to protect their comfort, position or wealth. And there is a little Herod in each of us when fear makes us harsh.

Matthew quotes the prophet: a voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more (Matthew 2:18; Jeremiah 31:15). God hears that cry. Today let us pray for all parents who have lost a child, through illness, accident, violence or miscarriage. Their children are in God's hands.

And let us ask: where are the innocents in danger today? The unborn child whose life is not protected. Children who are abused, even, to our great shame, in some places within the Church. Children forced to work, children trafficked, children in refugee camps, children growing up without love. Jesus said that whoever welcomes a child in His name welcomes Him, and warned terribly against those who cause little ones to stumble (Matthew 18:5–6).

So this feast calls each of us to be a Joseph, not a Herod: to rise and protect the child. Make our homes and parishes safe places. Support works that care for children in need. Defend life from its beginning. At this Holy Mass let us offer all the innocent victims of our world to the Child of Bethlehem, who came to save them.

Dear brothers and sisters, the Holy Innocents were killed before they could speak a word, yet the Church has honoured them as martyrs since ancient times. Their feast invites us to recall the Church's teaching on the dignity of the child.

The Catechism teaches that human life is sacred because from its beginning it involves the creative action of God; God alone is the Lord of life from beginning to end; no one can claim the right directly to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258). Human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception (CCC 2270). The prophet heard God say: before I formed you in the womb I knew you (Jeremiah 1:5). And the child John leaped in Elizabeth's womb at the presence of the unborn Christ (Luke 1:41).

Children are the supreme gift of marriage (CCC 2378). They are not property and not a burden, but persons entrusted to their parents. Parents have the first right and duty to educate them; society and the State have the duty to protect them (CCC 2211, 2221). Jesus took the children in His arms and blessed them, and said that the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14–16).

The Church also has a grave duty to protect minors in her own life. Every parish, school and institution must be a safe place. Where children have been harmed, the truth must be faced, victims must be heard and helped, and justice done.

At the same time, the Church proclaims mercy. If someone here carries the wound of abortion, in their own life or by assisting another, do not despair. The sin is grave, but God's mercy is greater. Come to confession; the Father runs to embrace the returning child (Luke 15:20). Let us offer this Holy Mass for all innocent victims, for mothers in difficulty, and for a culture that welcomes every child.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Three days after Christmas the Church wears red and remembers the children of Bethlehem. The readings do not soften the contrast. John's letter proclaims that “God is light” and that the blood of Jesus cleanses from all sin, yet it knows that darkness is real and that people walk in it. The psalm is the song of those who have escaped by God's help alone. Matthew tells of a king who, out of fear for his throne, kills children, while Joseph carries the child away by night and Rachel weeps. The key words are light, blood and weeping. The Church does not explain away this grief. She sets it beside the crib and the Cross.

1 John 1:5 – 2:2 · God is light, and the blood of Jesus cleanses us

Setting.

The First Letter of John does not name its author; tradition links it with St John and the community formed by his Gospel. It is usually dated toward the end of the first century. A division has torn that community: some have gone out (2:19) and seem to claim knowledge of God while denying that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:2) and making light of sin and of love for others.

The Lectionary reads this letter through the days of Christmas. It suits the season: the same voice that says “the Word became flesh” here says what that means for daily life. Today's passage follows straight after the opening verses (1:1–4).

Structure.

  • 1:5: the message: God is light.
  • 1:6–7: first false claim, and the true way: walking in the light.
  • 1:8–9: second false claim: “we have no sin”; the true way: confession.
  • 1:10: third false claim: “we have not sinned”.
  • 2:1–2: the remedy: Jesus Christ, advocate and atoning sacrifice.
  • ¶ The pattern “If we say… ” repeats. John measures words against life. He writes neither to excuse sin nor to crush the sinner.

Key words.

  • koinōnia, fellowship: A real sharing of life. In John it runs in two directions at once: with God and “with one another” (1:7).
  • paraklētos, advocate: One called alongside to speak for another, as a defender in court. John's Gospel uses it of the Holy Spirit (John 14:16); here it is used of Jesus.
  • hilasmos, atoning sacrifice: The offering by which sin is taken away and the relationship restored. The word occurs in the New Testament only in this letter (2:2; 4:10), where the source is God's own love.

Verse by verse.

“the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin.” (1:7)

The verb is in the present tense: it cleanses, again and again. Today of all days the words sound strong. The blood of the Innocents is shed because of Christ; the blood of Christ is shed for them.

“If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” (1:8)

Christians can still sin, and denying it is its own kind of darkness. St Augustine often quoted this verse against those who taught that the baptized could live without any sin (in substance).

“If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness.” (1:9)

God is “just” here because he keeps his promise to forgive. To confess is to say the same as God says about my sin.

“My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin.” (2:1)

John's tone is that of an old pastor. His first goal is that they not sin; the second half of the verse then gives hope to those who do.

“and he is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world.” (2:2)

The horizon is the whole world. John's little community may not think of Christ as its private possession.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses 1:5 when it teaches that God is truth (CCC 214), quotes 1:8–9 on conversion and the mercy of God (CCC 1425, 1847), and quotes 2:2 for the Church's teaching that Christ died for all without exception (CCC 605). St Augustine, who preached a series of homilies on this letter, says that its chief message is love (in substance). In the Mass we confess our sins at the start, and we hear the Lamb “who takes away the sins of the world” before Communion: the same two movements as this passage.

Psalm 124:2–3, 4–5, 7b–8 · The snare is broken, and we have escaped

Setting.

Psalm 124 is one of the Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134), sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem. Its title links it with David. It is a thanksgiving of the whole people after a danger that would have destroyed them. No particular event is named, so it can be sung in any age.

Structure.

  • vv. 2–3: if the Lord had not been with us, they would have swallowed us alive.
  • vv. 4–5: the flood would have swept over us.
  • vv. 7b–8: the snare is broken; our help is in the Lord's name.
  • ¶ Three images of danger, a beast with open jaws, a flood and a hunter's trap, are each answered by the one fact that God was on their side.

Key words.

  • ṣippôr, bird: A small, helpless creature. Israel sees itself in such a bird: it has no strength of its own.
  • paḥ, snare: A trap for birds. The psalm does not say that the bird broke it. The snare “is broken”, by another.
  • ‘ezrēnû, our help: The same language as Psalm 121:2. The maker of heaven and earth is the one who helps.

Verse by verse.

“if it had not been the Lord who was on our side, when our enemies attacked us,” (124:2)

The whole psalm hangs on this “if”. The singer looks back and sees how near the end was.

“then the flood would have swept us away, the torrent would have gone over us;” (124:4)

In the Bible, great waters are the image of chaos and death, as with Jonah 2:3 and Psalm 69:1–2.

“the snare is broken, and we have escaped.” (124:7)
“Our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth.” (124:8)

One of the best-known verses in Christian prayer.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sings this psalm today on behalf of children who did not escape from Herod's sword. So it has to be heard at a deeper level. Their escape is of another kind, for the Church holds that they have been taken into Christ's victory, beyond the reach of every snare. The Mass of the day has long applied the image of the bird to them. V. 8 has entered the liturgy: “Our help is in the name of the Lord” is the versicle with which many blessings in the Roman Rite begin.

Matthew 2:13–18 · Rachel weeping for her children

Setting.

In Matthew's first two chapters, each scene is built around a passage of the prophets, and he repeats a formula of fulfilment. Today there are two such quotations, Hosea and Jeremiah. The Magi have just left by another road. Joseph, who is silent throughout the Gospels, receives the second of his dreams (1:20; 2:13, 19, 22).

Herod the Great ruled Judea under Rome and died in 4 BC. The Jewish historian Josephus records his brutality toward any rival, including the killing of a wife and of his own sons. Josephus does not mention Bethlehem, but the deed fits what he tells of Herod. Bethlehem was a small place, and commentators often estimate that the children killed were perhaps a dozen or two. Matthew is not reporting a statistic; every one of them was somebody's child. Egypt was a natural place of refuge, with large Jewish communities at that time.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: the dream, and the night journey.
  • v. 15: first fulfilment: “Out of Egypt I have called my son”.
  • v. 16: Herod's rage and the killing of the children.
  • vv. 17–18: second fulfilment: Rachel weeps at Ramah.
  • ¶ The whole passage retells the story of Moses. A cruel king kills male children (Exodus 1:22), and one child is saved, flees, and returns when those who sought his life are dead (Exodus 4:19; Matthew 2:20).

Key words.

  • paralabe, take: Joseph is repeatedly told to take “the child and his mother” (2:13, 20). The phrase shows his place: he has charge of them, and the two are always named together.
  • empaizō, to trick, to make a fool of: Herod feels that the Magi have mocked him. The later Passion uses the same verb of the soldiers mocking Jesus (27:29). Pride wounded is the root of Herod's violence.
  • plēroō, to fulfil: Not merely a prediction coming true. God's earlier works with Israel, in Egypt and in exile, reach a fullness in Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“Get up, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt” (2:13)

The Son of God begins his life on earth as a refugee, and the Church's concern for migrants has a root here.

“Then Joseph got up, took the child and his mother by night, and went to Egypt,” (2:14)

No word is spoken, and no question is asked. Joseph obeys at once, in the dark.

“Out of Egypt I have called my son.” (2:15)

Hosea 11:1 speaks of Israel coming out of Egypt. Matthew sees Jesus living through his people's history in his own person. Jesus is God's Son in a unique sense.

“he sent and killed all the children in and around Bethlehem who were two years old or under” (2:16)

The evil here belongs to one man who feared for his power. Matthew does not blame the people of Judea, who are the victims. It is a warning about what power will do when it feels itself threatened.

“A voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children;” (2:18)

Jeremiah 31:15 pictures Rachel, mother of the northern tribes, weeping as the exiles pass on the way to Babylon; Ramah was a gathering place for the exiles (Jeremiah 40:1). Another tradition placed her tomb on the way to Bethlehem (Genesis 35:19). The next verses in Jeremiah offer comfort: “there is hope for your future” (31:17). Matthew leaves the promise unspoken here. It will come at Easter.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the flight into Egypt and the massacre of the innocents show the opposition of darkness to the light, that Christ's whole life was lived under the sign of persecution, and that his own share it with him (CCC 530). These children did not know Christ and were not baptized with water, yet the Church has honoured them since ancient times as martyrs, because they died for his sake (compare CCC 1258, on the baptism of blood). St Irenaeus says that the Lord sent them ahead into his kingdom (in substance, Against Heresies 3.16). St Quodvultdeus, in the sermon in today's Office of Readings, marvels that children who cannot yet speak bear witness to Christ (in substance), and the poet Prudentius greets them as “flowers of the martyrs”. The Church teaches that all innocent human life is sacred from conception (CCC 2270), and today she remembers all children who suffer from war, neglect and violence.

Questions people ask

Did this really happen, since no other ancient writer mentions it?

Matthew is the only source. Josephus does not mention it, but the killing of a few village children may have seemed small beside Herod's other crimes, and it matches what is known of his character. The Church has kept the feast as a real event, while Matthew's main concern is the meaning of the event in God's plan.

Why did God warn Joseph but not the other parents?

Scripture does not answer this, and we should not invent an answer. The passage gives no explanation. It gives Rachel's weeping, which is a prayer, and it shows God's Son himself entering a world where such things happen, on his way to a Cross where he too will be killed unjustly. The answer to innocent suffering in Christian faith is less an argument than the crucified and risen Lord.

How can the unbaptized children be saints?

The Church has long regarded those who die for Christ before baptism as baptized by their death (CCC 1258). The Innocents are the first example honoured in the liturgy. Scripture says nothing of their number or their names, and the Church honours them with one feast.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman calendar the Innocents stand close behind Christmas with St Stephen on 26 December and St John on 27 December. An old way of speaking, found in St Bernard, explains the three together: Stephen was a martyr in will and in deed, John in will, and the Innocents in deed (in substance). Red vestments mark the feast as a feast of martyrs. It is a fitting day to pray for parents who have lost a child, for children who are in danger, and for the protection of every unborn child, and to bring those names to the altar at Mass.

For study and teaching

  • John says, “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves.” Where do I find it hardest to tell the truth about myself before God?
  • Herod killed because he feared losing his place. What am I most afraid of losing, and how does that fear affect the people around me?
  • Joseph obeyed by night without knowing the road. What does trust look like when I cannot see the outcome?
  • How can our parish or family stand by a mother or father who, like Rachel, refuses to be consoled?
