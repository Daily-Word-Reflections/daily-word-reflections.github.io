Word for the day

“Master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace, according to your word; for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel.” Luke 2:29–32

Reflection

Old Simeon, “righteous and devout, looking forward to the consolation of Israel”, had been promised by the Holy Spirit that he would not die before seeing the Lord's Messiah. Guided by the Spirit he comes into the Temple just as Mary and Joseph bring in the child. He takes Jesus in his arms and sings the Nunc Dimittis, which the Church prays every night at Compline: now I can die in peace, for I have seen your salvation.

Simeon's song is universal: the child is “a light for revelation to the Gentiles” as well as the glory of Israel. But he also speaks of contradiction: the child will be “a sign that will be opposed”, and a sword will pierce Mary's soul. The first reading draws the practical lesson: “whoever says, ‘I abide in him,’ ought to walk just as he walked”; whoever loves a brother or sister lives in the light. St Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury, killed in his cathedral on this day in 1170, gave his life defending the freedom of the Church.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this fifth day of the Christmas Octave St John writes: by this we may be sure that we know him, if we obey his commandments. Whoever says, I abide in him, ought to walk just as he walked (1 John 2:3, 6). Knowing Christ is not only an idea; it is a way of walking.

John continues: whoever says, I am in the light, while hating a brother or sister, is still in the darkness. Whoever loves a brother or sister lives in the light (1 John 2:9–10). Christmas light is tested by love.

In the Gospel Mary and Joseph bring the infant Jesus to the Temple. There the old man Simeon, righteous and devout, who had been waiting for the consolation of Israel, takes the child in his arms and praises God: Master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace, for my eyes have seen your salvation, a light for revelation to the Gentiles (Luke 2:25–32).

Simeon waited his whole life, and his waiting was fulfilled in holding a baby. The Church prays his song every night at Compline, before sleep. It is the prayer of one who is at peace because he has seen Christ.

But Simeon also tells Mary: this child is destined for the falling and the rising of many, and a sword will pierce your own soul too (Luke 2:34–35). The light of Christmas already points to the Cross. Love costs.

Let us ask for Simeon's patience and peace. Let us walk as Jesus walked, loving our brothers and sisters, so that we truly live in the light. And tonight, before sleep, let us pray: now, Lord, let your servant go in peace. Amen.

For children

Children, old Simeon held baby Jesus and was so happy! We hold Jesus in our hearts when we receive Him with love.

Prayer

Lord, now let your servant go in peace; your word has been fulfilled. My eyes have seen the salvation you prepared for all peoples. Let me walk in your light and love my brothers and sisters. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today speak of light that has already begun to shine. John says that “the true light is already shining”, and tests the claim by a simple question: do you love your brother or sister? Simeon holds the child and calls him “a light for revelation to the Gentiles”. The light of Christmas is real, and it asks to be lived.

1 John 2:3–11 · Whoever loves lives in the light

Setting.

John continues to test the claims of those who say that they know God. Knowing God, for him, cannot be separated from obeying him and from loving others. He gives three tests: keeping the commandments, walking as Jesus walked, and love of one's brother or sister.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–6: to know him is to keep his word and to walk as he walked.
  • vv. 7–8: the commandment is both old and new.
  • vv. 9–11: love is light; hatred is darkness and blindness.

Key words.

  • ginōskō, to know: In the Bible, knowing God is a lived relationship, not just having information. John uses the word many times in this letter.
  • entolē, commandment: Old, because they had it from the beginning; new, because Christ gave it a new measure (John 13:34): “as I have loved you”.
  • skandalon, cause for stumbling: Literally a trap or an obstacle on the path. One who loves can see the road, and does not trip himself or others.

Verse by verse.

“whoever says, “I abide in him,” ought to walk just as he walked.” (2:6)

The test is plain. On the fifth day of Christmas it asks what we have seen at the crib: humility, poverty and self-gift are the way he walked.

“because the darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining.” (2:8)

The world still has darkness in it, but the change has begun, as with the first light before sunrise.

“Whoever loves a brother or sister lives in the light, and in such a person there is no cause for stumbling.” (2:10)

The Greek says “brother”. The NRSV rightly makes clear that this takes in everyone in the community, men and women alike.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, in his homilies on this letter, says that love alone distinguishes the children of God from the children of the devil (in substance, Homilies on the First Letter of John 5). The Catechism says that the law of the People of God is the new commandment to love as Christ loved us (CCC 782).

Luke 2:22–35 · My eyes have seen your salvation

Setting.

This is the first part of the scene in the Temple, read in full on the feast of the Holy Family. Today the attention falls on Simeon. Luke tells us nothing about his office and does not say that he was old, although v. 29 suggests it. Three times Luke mentions the Holy Spirit in connection with him (vv. 25, 26, 27), and the Spirit, who led the prophets, is at work again in Israel.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: the parents fulfil the law.
  • vv. 25–28: Simeon is led to the child.
  • vv. 29–32: the song known as the Nunc Dimittis.
  • vv. 33–35: the sign and the sword.

Key words.

  • despota, Master: The word a slave uses to his owner. Simeon speaks to God as a servant to his Master.
  • apolyeis, you are dismissing: The word for releasing a servant from duty. Simeon's watch is over, and he may go off duty at peace.
  • antilegomenon, opposed, spoken against: A sign that people will argue over and reject. Jesus forces a decision, and hearts are shown for what they are.

Verse by verse.

“he would not see death before he had seen the Lord’s Messiah.” (2:26)

A whole life was shaped by this one promise, and it was kept quietly, one day after another.

“Master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace, according to your word;” (2:29)

The Church prays this song every night at Night Prayer. It is the prayer of someone who can lay the day down, because God has kept his word.

“for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel.” (2:30–32)

The words echo Isaiah 42:6, 49:6 and 52:10. Christ is light for the nations, and he remains the glory of Israel. Neither takes the place of the other.

“This child is destined for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and to be a sign that will be opposed” (2:34)

Compare Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16, the stone on which some stumble and others build. This is a word about every human heart, spoken in Israel first because the Gospel comes to Israel first.

How the Church reads it.

Vatican II opens its great Constitution on the Church with the same title of Christ: “Christ is the Light of nations” (Lumen Gentium 1). The Catechism notes that with Simeon and Anna all Israel awaits its encounter with the Savior (CCC 529). Origen tells his hearers that if they want to hold Jesus in their arms, they must let the Spirit lead them, as Simeon did, into God's temple (in substance, Homilies on Luke 15).

Questions people ask

Does “the falling and the rising of many in Israel” mean that God has rejected the Jewish people?

No. The Church firmly rejects that view (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). Simeon, Anna, Mary, Joseph and the first disciples are all from Israel, and the same song calls the child Israel's glory. The words are about the decision that Christ asks of every person, and they apply no less to Christians.

For the Latin reader

Today the Roman calendar allows an optional memorial of St Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury, who was killed in his cathedral on 29 December 1170 after a long dispute with King Henry II over the rights of the Church. Like Simeon's child, he became a sign that was opposed. He is remembered in the Octave of Christmas, near St Stephen and the Innocents.

For study and teaching

  • John's test is love of the brother or sister I actually have. Who is the person in my life who shows up how much light I really walk in?
  • Simeon says, “now you are dismissing your servant in peace.” What would I need to put right to be able to pray this at the end of today?
