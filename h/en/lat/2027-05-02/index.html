Word for the day

“This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” John 15:12

Reflection

The first reading takes us to Caesarea, the Roman port city on the coast of Judea, and to the house of a Roman army officer. Cornelius is a centurion, “a devout man who feared God with all his household,” who gave alms and prayed constantly. An angel has told him to send for Peter; Peter, meanwhile, has seen a strange vision on a rooftop in Joppa and has heard a voice: “What God has made clean, you must not call profane.” Now Peter enters the house of a Gentile, something a devout Jew of the time would not normally do. Cornelius falls at his feet to worship him, “But Peter made him get up, saying, ‘Stand up; I am only a mortal.’” Then Peter speaks words that open a new chapter in the history of the Church: “I truly understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him.” And before Peter has even finished, God himself settles the question: “the Holy Spirit fell upon all who heard the word.” The Jewish believers who came with Peter are astounded “that the gift of the Holy Spirit had been poured out even on the Gentiles,” for they hear them “speaking in tongues and extolling God.” It is a Pentecost in a Gentile home. Peter can only draw the conclusion: “Can anyone withhold the water for baptizing these people who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?” And he orders them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ.

The Psalm sings what Caesarea shows: “O sing to the Lord a new song, for he has done marvelous things.” “The Lord has made known his victory; he has revealed his vindication in the sight of the nations.” The God of Israel “has remembered his steadfast love and faithfulness to the house of Israel,” and now “all the ends of the earth have seen the victory of our God.” The faithfulness of God to his own people does not shut the door to the nations; it is the door through which they come in.

The second reading gives us the shortest and perhaps the deepest definition of God in the whole Bible: “God is love.” St John does not say that God has love, or that God sometimes loves, but that love is what God is. That is why “everyone who loves is born of God and knows God,” and “whoever does not love does not know God.” Then John tells us what kind of love he means, so that we do not confuse it with a feeling: “In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins.” Love begins with God, not with us. It is first received, then given. Our love for one another is not the starting point of religion but its fruit.

The Gospel continues the Last Supper discourse, just after the image of the vine heard last Sunday. Jesus now speaks of the source of the vine's life: “As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” There is a chain of love: from the Father to the Son, from the Son to the disciples, and from the disciples to one another. “This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” The measure is not “as you love yourself” but “as I have loved you,” and within hours he will show what that means: “No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” Then comes one of the most moving sentences Jesus ever spoke: “I do not call you servants any longer, because the servant does not know what the master is doing; but I have called you friends.” God's own Son calls us his friends, and he shares with us all that he has heard from the Father. And it is his choice before it is ours: “You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last.” At Caesarea the friendship of Jesus reached people whom many thought could never be his friends. Today, as the Ascension and Pentecost draw near, the same Spirit reminds the Church that no one is outside the reach of that love.

Full homily

Dear friends, a few weeks ago we heard Jesus say, I am the vine, you are the branches. Today he tells us what flows through that vine: love. As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love (John 15:9). The Father loves the Son; the Son loves us; and we are asked to love one another with that same love.

In the first reading Peter does something new. He enters the house of Cornelius, a Roman officer, a Gentile. Cornelius falls at his feet, and Peter says: stand up; I am only a mortal (Acts 10:26). Then Peter says: I truly understand that God shows no partiality (Acts 10:34). And while he is still speaking, the Holy Spirit falls on everyone in that house. God did not wait for the Church's committee to decide. He poured out his love first.

St John says it in three words: God is love (1 John 4:8). Not that God has some love, but that love is what God is. And this love began with him: not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son (1 John 4:10). We do not earn God's love by being good. We become good because we are loved.

Then Jesus gives the measure: love one another as I have loved you (John 15:12). Not as much as is convenient, not only those who love us back. As I have loved you. And how did he love? No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends (John 15:13). Every mother who sits up at night with a sick child, every husband who forgives, every young person who stands by a friend in trouble, lives a small part of this love.

And then the most beautiful word: I do not call you servants any longer; I have called you friends (John 15:15). We are not servants waiting for orders. We are friends to whom Jesus has told everything. You did not choose me but I chose you. He chose you. Today. Here.

In this month of May, let us stay close to Mary, who knew this love better than anyone. Next Sunday, in India, we will celebrate the Ascension; then we will wait with Mary for the Holy Spirit. Let us open our hearts, our homes and our parish to everyone, as Peter opened his to Cornelius, and let our love be the fruit that lasts. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, who is your best friend? Jesus says: you are my friends! Not my servants, my friends. He tells us everything his Father told him, and he loves us so much that he gave his life for us on the cross.

And Jesus asks us: love one another as I have loved you. That means being kind even to children who are different from us, who speak another language or who have no friends. Let us say: Jesus, my friend, help me to love like you!

Prayer

Father, you are love, and you loved us first: you sent your Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and in him you show no partiality, but welcome everyone who fears you and does what is right. Lord Jesus, you call us no longer servants but friends; keep us in your love as you abide in the Father's love, and teach us to love one another as you have loved us, even to laying down our lives. Pour out your Holy Spirit on us, as on the household of Cornelius, so that we may sing a new song and bear fruit that will last. With the Blessed Virgin Mary, whom we honour this month, we ask this in your name, who live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Sixth Sunday of Easter one word runs through all three readings: love. In the house of Cornelius, Peter learns that God shows no partiality, and the Holy Spirit falls on Gentiles before anyone has baptised them. John’s letter gives the shortest description of God in the Bible: God is love. In the Gospel Jesus shows how far that love goes: to laying down one’s life for one’s friends.

The order matters. Love begins with God, not with us (1 John 4:10). It flows from the Father to the Son, from the Son to the disciples (John 15:9), and from them outward, even to a Roman officer’s household in Caesarea. Psalm 98 sings that the Lord has made known his victory in the sight of the nations; Acts shows it happening.

Acts 10:25–26, 34–35, 44–48 · God shows no partiality

Setting.

Cornelius was a centurion of the Italian Cohort at Caesarea, a devout man who feared God with all his household, gave alms generously and prayed constantly (10:1–2). An angel told him to send for Peter at Joppa. Meanwhile Peter, praying on a rooftop, saw a sheet let down from heaven full of creatures the Law counted unclean, and heard a voice telling him not to call profane what God has made clean (10:15). Then the messengers of Cornelius arrived at the door.

Background. Jewish law and custom kept a careful separation from Gentile homes and tables, as Peter himself says (10:28). It was meant to guard Israel’s holiness as God’s own people. Now Peter learns that God is also calling the Gentiles. The reading leaves out Peter’s summary of the Gospel (vv. 36–43), one of the great sermons of Acts, and moves to the moment when the Spirit interrupts him.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–26: Cornelius falls at Peter’s feet; Peter raises him up.
  • vv. 34–35: God shows no partiality.
  • vv. 44–46: the Spirit falls on the Gentiles, and they speak in tongues and praise God.
  • vv. 47–48: Peter orders them to be baptised.
  • ¶ Luke tells this story three times: in full in chapter 10, again in Peter’s report (11:1–18), and briefly at the meeting in Jerusalem (15:7–9). Repetition is his way of saying: this is a turning point.

Key words.

  • prosōpolēmptēs, one who shows partiality: Literally one who “receives the face”, who judges by appearance, rank or race (v. 34). The Old Testament already says that God is not partial (Deuteronomy 10:17). Peter now sees what this means for the nations.
  • dōrea, gift: “The gift of the Holy Spirit” (v. 45) is the same phrase Peter used at Pentecost (2:38). The Gentiles receive the very same gift, not a lesser one, as Peter later insists (11:17).
  • ekcheō, to pour out: The verb of Joel’s prophecy quoted at Pentecost: “I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh” (2:17). Here “all flesh” begins to include the Gentiles. The scene is often called the Pentecost of the Gentiles.

Verse by verse.

“But Peter made him get up, saying, “Stand up; I am only a mortal.”” (10:26)

Cornelius meant deep reverence, but Peter will not accept what belongs to God alone. The first of the apostles begins by lifting a man to his feet.

“I truly understand that God shows no partiality,” (10:34)

Peter speaks as one still learning. Even the first of the apostles had to be taught by the Spirit, through a vision and through the faith of a Gentile.

“but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him.” (10:35)

This does not mean that Christ is unnecessary: Peter at once goes on to preach Christ, and the passage ends in baptism. It means that God was already at work in Cornelius before Peter arrived, preparing him for the Gospel.

“While Peter was still speaking, the Holy Spirit fell upon all who heard the word.” (10:44)

God does not wait for Peter to finish. Elsewhere in Acts the Spirit comes with or after baptism (2:38; 8:14–17). Here he comes first, so that no one can doubt that God has accepted these Gentiles.

““Can anyone withhold the water for baptizing these people who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?”” (10:47)

The Spirit’s coming does not make baptism unnecessary; it is the reason Peter cannot refuse it. The gift within is joined to the sacrament of the Church.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council opens its chapter on the People of God with v. 35: at all times and in every nation, anyone who fears God and does what is right is acceptable to him; yet God willed to make people holy and to save them not as individuals without any bond between them, but by making them into a single people (Lumen Gentium 9, in substance). That is the whole movement of this chapter of Acts: God at work in Cornelius, and then Cornelius brought into the one Church through baptism. The Council also teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel, yet seek God with a sincere heart and, moved by grace, try to do his will, can attain salvation (Lumen Gentium 16).

1 John 4:7–10 · God is love

Setting.

John has just told his readers to test the spirits, because not every spirit is from God (4:1–6). Now he turns to the sign that does not deceive: love. This passage is the heart of the letter, and the words “God is love” return a few verses later (4:16).

Ancient tradition ascribes the letter to the apostle John. The letter does not name its writer, and many scholars think it comes from a disciple within the circle of John. It was written for communities wounded by division, near the end of the first century.

Structure.

  • v. 7: let us love one another, because love is from God.
  • v. 8: whoever does not love does not know God, for God is love.
  • v. 9: how God’s love was revealed: he sent his only Son.
  • v. 10: what love is: not our love for God, but his love for us.

Key words.

  • agapē, love: A word rare in earlier Greek writing, which the Greek Old Testament and the New Testament made their own for the love that gives itself. In English, “love”, “loves” and “loved” occur nine times in these four verses.
  • monogenēs, only, only-begotten: God sent “his only Son” (v. 9). The word means the one and only of his kind. John uses it of the Son in his Gospel too (1:14, 18; 3:16, 18). The gift is measured by what it cost.
  • hilasmos, atoning sacrifice: The means by which sin is taken away and communion with God restored (v. 10; also 2:2). It recalls the Day of Atonement. John does not picture an angry God being won over: it is God who loves first and who himself sends the Son.

Verse by verse.

“Beloved, let us love one another, because love is from God;” (4:7)

John does not first say “love, because it is commanded”, but “love, because love comes from God”. To love is to let God’s own life pass through us.

“Whoever does not love does not know God, for God is love.” (4:8)

Knowing God here is not having information about him but sharing his life. John does not say that love is God; he says that God is love. The one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, is in his very being love.

“God sent his only Son into the world so that we might live through him.” (4:9)

Love is revealed not in a feeling but in a sending, and the purpose is life.

“In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us” (4:10)

Every Christian life begins by being loved. We do not earn God’s love by our love; our love is the answer to his.

How the Church reads it.

Pope Benedict XVI began his first encyclical, Deus Caritas Est (2005), with 1 John 4:16, which repeats today’s v. 8, and said that these words express with remarkable clarity the heart of the Christian faith (in substance). The Catechism teaches that in saying “God is love” John affirms that God’s very being is love, and that by sending his Son and the Spirit, God has revealed his innermost secret: he himself is an eternal exchange of love, Father, Son and Holy Spirit (CCC 221). It quotes v. 10 to show that the Father’s giving up of his Son for our sins comes from love that goes before any merit of ours (CCC 604).

John 15:9–17 · I have called you friends

Setting.

The Last Supper, just after the image of the vine and the branches (15:1–8). Jesus now says plainly what the sap of the vine is: his love, which he has received from the Father. The passage moves from love received (v. 9), to love kept (v. 10), to love given away (vv. 12–13), and ends where it began, with the command to love (v. 17).

He speaks on the night before he dies. When he says that no one has greater love than to lay down one’s life for one’s friends (v. 13), he is describing what he will do the next day.

Structure.

  • v. 9: as the Father has loved me; abide in my love.
  • vv. 10–11: keeping the commandments, and joy made complete.
  • vv. 12–13: love one another as I have loved you; the greatest love.
  • vv. 14–15: no longer servants but friends.
  • vv. 16–17: chosen and appointed to bear fruit that will last; love one another.

Key words.

  • philos, friend: Not an equal in power, but one with whom a person shares his heart (v. 15). In the Old Testament Abraham is called God’s friend (Isaiah 41:8; see James 2:23), and the Lord spoke with Moses as one speaks to a friend (Exodus 33:11). What was given to a few is now given to all the disciples.
  • menō, to abide: To remain, to make one’s home (vv. 9–10). Love here is not a passing moment but a dwelling place.
  • chara, joy: Joy is the purpose of his words (v. 11). It is his own joy, which does not depend on circumstances, and which, in the same discourse, he says no one will take from them (16:22).

Verse by verse.

“As the Father has loved me, so I have loved you; abide in my love.” (15:9)

The love the disciples receive is the same love that flows between the Father and the Son. Nothing less is offered.

“This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” (15:12)

The “as” is both the measure and the source. We cannot love as he did by our own strength; we can love because he has first loved us.

“No one has greater love than this, to lay down one’s life for one’s friends.” (15:13)

St Paul goes further still: Christ died for us while we were still sinners (Romans 5:8). The friends for whom Jesus dies include those who will deny and abandon him that very night.

“I do not call you servants any longer,” (15:15)

Being a servant of God is no disgrace; Paul proudly calls himself a servant of Christ Jesus (Romans 1:1). But a servant is told what to do, while a friend is told why. Jesus has shared with them all that he has heard from the Father.

“You did not choose me but I chose you.” (15:16)

Usually a disciple chose his teacher. Here the Teacher chooses. A vocation begins with being chosen, and so it rests more on his faithfulness than on ours.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes vv. 9 and 12 when it teaches that Jesus makes charity the new commandment: by loving one another the disciples imitate the love of Jesus which they themselves receive (CCC 1823). St Thomas Aquinas, appealing to v. 15, teaches that charity is a friendship of man with God (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 23, a. 1, in substance). The Church sees the fullest form of v. 13 in the martyrs, and also in every hidden life spent for others.

Questions people ask

Does “God shows no partiality” mean that all religions are the same?

No. Peter says it and then preaches Christ and baptises. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions and sees in them, often, a ray of the Truth that enlightens all people; at the same time she proclaims Christ, the way, the truth and the life, in whom people find the fullness of religious life (Nostra Aetate 2). God’s welcome to every nation is the reason for the mission, not a reason to give it up.

If “God is love”, how can there be judgment?

Love is not indifference. A love that did not care whether we lived or destroyed ourselves would not be love. The same passage says that God sent his Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins (4:10): God takes sin seriously because he loves. The Church teaches that only one who finally and of his own will refuses that merciful love remains separated from him (CCC 1033).

Jesus says “I chose you”. Did he not choose everyone?

At the Supper he speaks first to the Twelve, whom he chose and appointed to go and bear fruit. But every Christian is chosen in Christ (Ephesians 1:4), and the call is offered to all. To be chosen is not a privilege to keep for oneself, but a sending: “to go and bear fruit”.

For the Latin reader

In India the Solemnity of the Ascension is kept next Sunday, 9 May. The days between the Ascension and Pentecost recall the apostles devoted to prayer together with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14), the prayer from which the Pentecost novena takes its pattern. Peter’s question about the water of baptism makes this a good Sunday for the rite of blessing and sprinkling of water, which the Roman Missal allows in place of the penitential act on Sundays, especially in Easter Time. On Thursday and Friday of last week the weekday Mass read the two halves of today’s Gospel (John 15:9–11 and 15:12–17); today they are heard together, with John’s letter as their commentary.

For study and teaching

  • Where have I seen God at work in someone before they knew him, as he was in Cornelius?
  • John says that love begins with God’s love for us. When did I last simply let myself be loved by God, in prayer or in confession?
  • Jesus calls me his friend, not his servant. How would my prayer change if I spoke to him as a friend?
  • For whom am I being asked to lay down a little of my life this week: my time, my pride, my comfort?
