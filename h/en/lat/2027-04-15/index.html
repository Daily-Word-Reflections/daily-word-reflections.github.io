Word for the day

“I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” John 6:51

Reflection

Philip has just seen a whole city rejoice. Now an angel sends him somewhere very different: “Get up and go toward the south to the road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza.” Luke adds: “This is a wilderness road.” God sends the evangelist away from the crowd to one man. On the road is an Ethiopian, a eunuch, a court official of the Candace, the queen of the Ethiopians, in charge of her whole treasury. In the Bible “Ethiopia” means the ancient kingdom of Kush, south of Egypt, in today's Sudan; for the people of Jerusalem it was the edge of the known world. This powerful man has travelled well over a thousand kilometres to worship in Jerusalem, and now, seated in his chariot, he is reading the prophet Isaiah aloud, as people did in the ancient world. The Spirit says: “Go over to this chariot and join it.” Philip runs, hears the words, and asks the simple question that begins so many conversions: “Do you understand what you are reading?” The answer is humble and honest: “How can I, unless someone guides me?” The passage is Isaiah's song of the Servant: “Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter, and like a lamb silent before its shearer.” “About whom, may I ask you, does the prophet say this?” And Philip, “starting with this scripture,” proclaims to him the good news of Jesus. They come to water, and the man cries out: “Look, here is water! What is to prevent me from being baptized?” By the old law a eunuch could not enter the assembly of the Lord (Deuteronomy 23:1), but Isaiah had promised that God would give faithful eunuchs “a name better than sons and daughters” (Isaiah 56:3–5). On a desert road that promise is kept. Philip baptizes him, the Spirit snatches Philip away, and the Ethiopian “went on his way rejoicing.” Ancient tradition, cherished in Ethiopia, honours him as one of the first to carry the Gospel to Africa, and the whole Church sees in this story the pattern of Christian initiation: the Scriptures opened, Christ proclaimed, faith, Baptism, and joy.

In the Gospel Jesus says something that should make us humble: “No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father who sent me.” Faith is never simply our achievement. St Augustine says, in substance, that the Father draws us not by force but by delight, as a branch held out draws a sheep, or as a child is drawn by the sight of what it loves. The Father drew the Ethiopian through his love of the Scriptures; he draws us too, often through someone who sits beside us and explains. Jesus quotes Isaiah: “And they shall all be taught by God.” Then he speaks in the present tense: “whoever believes has eternal life.” Eternal life begins now. And then the discourse turns a corner. “Your ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died.” “I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” The word “flesh” is the same word St John used for the Incarnation: “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14). The bread Jesus will give is not a symbol of his flesh; it is his flesh, given “for the life of the world,” that is, offered on the cross. The Eucharist makes present that one sacrifice of Calvary (Catechism 1366–1367). The Ethiopian was reading of a lamb led to the slaughter; at every Mass we receive that Lamb, the living bread who was slain and lives for ever.

Full homily

Dear friends, Philip was busy with a great mission in Samaria, and God sent him to a desert road for one man. The Lord does not only care for crowds. He will send an evangelist across a desert for a single seeking heart.

That man was reading Isaiah and could not understand it. When Philip asked: do you understand what you are reading?, he answered: how can I, unless someone guides me? (Acts 8:31). Many people today are like him: they have a Bible, or a memory of the faith, but no one to sit beside them. You could be that Philip for someone.

Philip started from the passage about the Lamb led to the slaughter and told him about Jesus. Then they came to water, and the man said: look, here is water! What is to prevent me from being baptized? (Acts 8:36). The man the old law had kept outside was welcomed into God's family. He went on his way rejoicing.

In the Gospel Jesus says: I am the living bread that came down from heaven; the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh (John 6:51). The Lamb the Ethiopian read about is the Bread we receive. Let us come to him with faith, and then go out like that man from Africa, on our way rejoicing. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a man was reading the Bible in his chariot, but he did not understand it. God sent Philip to help him. Philip told him about Jesus, and the man was baptized and was so happy! Whenever you do not understand something about Jesus, ask your parents, your teacher or Father. God loves it when we ask!

Prayer

Father, you sent Philip down the desert road to one seeking heart, and you draw us to your Son by the delight of your love. Open the Scriptures to us, renew in us the joy of our Baptism, and feed us with the living bread, the flesh of Christ given for the life of the world, who lives and reigns with you for ever and ever. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two readings about how people come to Christ. On the desert road to Gaza a royal official from the far south is reading Isaiah without understanding, and the Spirit sends Philip to run beside his chariot. In John, Jesus says that no one can come to him unless drawn by the Father, and then the discourse takes its decisive turn: “the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh”. Psalm 66 sounds like the official’s own voice on his way home: “Come and hear, all you who fear God, and I will tell what he has done for me.”

Acts 8:26–40 · Do you understand what you are reading?

Setting.

Philip has been preaching in Samaria. An angel now sends him south, to the desert road from Jerusalem to Gaza, the old route toward Egypt. There he meets a man Luke calls an Ethiopian. In the Bible “Ethiopia” (Hebrew Kush) means the land south of Egypt on the upper Nile, the kingdom of Meroë in today’s Sudan, rather than modern Ethiopia. “Candace” was the title of its ruling queens, not a personal name. For Greeks and Romans this was the edge of the known world.

The man is a eunuch and a high official, and he has been to Jerusalem to worship. The law of Deuteronomy 23:1 kept such a man out of the assembly, but Isaiah 56:3–5 promised eunuchs “a monument and a name better than sons and daughters”. Luke does not say whether he was a convert or a God-fearing Gentile; the story is told before the baptism of Cornelius (Acts 10).

Structure.

  • vv. 26–29: the angel and the Spirit send Philip to the chariot.
  • vv. 30–35: “Do you understand?” Isaiah 53 and the good news about Jesus.
  • vv. 36–38: “Here is water!” The baptism.
  • vv. 39–40: Philip is taken away; the eunuch goes on rejoicing; Philip preaches as far as Caesarea.

Key words.

  • hodēgēsei, will guide: “How can I, unless someone guides me?” The same verb is used of the Spirit who “will guide you into all the truth” (John 16:13). Scripture is read best with a guide, within the Church.
  • euēngelisato, proclaimed the good news: Starting from Isaiah, Philip “proclaimed to him the good news about Jesus”. As on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:27), the risen Christ is the key to the Scriptures.
  • ti kōlyei, what is to prevent?: The same verb returns when Peter asks whether anyone can withhold the water of baptism from Cornelius’ household (10:47), and when he says he could not hinder God (11:17). Some scholars think it echoes a question asked at early baptisms.

Verse by verse.

“So Philip ran up to it and heard him reading the prophet Isaiah. He asked, “Do you understand what you are reading?”” (8:30)

In the ancient world people usually read aloud, so Philip can hear him. The Greek has a play on words: ginōskeis ha anaginōskeis, “do you know what you are reading?”

““Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter, and like a lamb silent before its shearer, so he does not open his mouth.” (8:32)

Isaiah 53:7–8, in the Greek translation the official would have been reading. It is the fourth Servant Song, read every Good Friday.

“The eunuch asked Philip, “About whom, may I ask you, does the prophet say this, about himself or about someone else?”” (8:34)

The question is still asked today. Christians answer with Philip: the Servant’s silent suffering is fulfilled in Jesus.

““Look, here is water! What is to prevent me from being baptized?”” (8:36)

Many later manuscripts add a verse 37 in which Philip asks for faith and the eunuch replies, “I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.” The best ancient manuscripts lack it, so the NRSV passes from verse 36 to verse 38. The addition shows how early the Church joined baptism to a profession of faith.

“the eunuch saw him no more, and went on his way rejoicing.” (8:39)

Philip disappears; the joy remains.

How the Church reads it.

St Irenaeus in the second century says that this man was sent into the regions of Ethiopia to preach what he himself had believed (in substance), and many Christians of Africa look to him as a forerunner of their faith. St Jerome, writing about the study of Scripture, points to him: he held the book but did not understand it until a guide came (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that Scripture must be read in the same Spirit in which it was written, with attention to the living Tradition of the whole Church (Dei Verbum 12; CCC 111–113). The Catechism also notes that from the day of Pentecost the Church has celebrated and administered holy Baptism (CCC 1226).

John 6:44–51 · The living bread that came down from heaven

Setting.

The hearers, whom John here calls “the Jews”, complain because Jesus, whose father and mother they think they know, says that he has come down from heaven (6:41–42). In John 6 the term refers to the Galilean listeners in the synagogue at Capernaum (6:59), not to the Jewish people as such. Their murmuring recalls Israel’s murmuring over food in the desert (Exodus 16:2). Jesus answers that faith is a gift of the Father, and then speaks of a bread that is his flesh.

Structure.

  • vv. 44–46: no one comes unless drawn by the Father; “they shall all be taught by God”.
  • v. 47: whoever believes has eternal life.
  • vv. 48–50: the bread of life and the manna: the ancestors died, this bread gives life.
  • v. 51: the living bread; the bread is “my flesh”, given for the life of the world.

Key words.

  • helkysē, draws: A strong verb, used of hauling in a net (21:6, 11). Jesus uses it again: “I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself” (12:32). The Father’s drawing does not force; it attracts.
  • didaktoi theou, taught by God: From Isaiah 54:13, a promise for the restored Jerusalem. Faith is more than learning from human teachers: God himself teaches the heart.
  • sarx, flesh: The word of the Prologue: “the Word became flesh” (1:14). In verse 51 Jesus no longer speaks only of bread from heaven but of his own flesh, given “for the life of the world”, words that point to the cross and to the Eucharist.

Verse by verse.

“No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father who sent me; and I will raise that person up on the last day.” (6:44)

This does not mean that God refuses some. It means that faith is first God’s work in us; no one reaches Christ by human power alone (CCC 153).

“Your ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died.” (6:49)

The manna was a true gift, but it fed a mortal life. The bread Jesus gives feeds a life that death does not end.

“I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.”” (6:51)

“I will give” is future, and “for” (hyper) is the word used of Christ’s sacrifice (compare Luke 22:19, “given for you”). The gift will be made on the cross and given at the table.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, commenting on “unless drawn by the Father”, says that we are drawn not by force but by delight, as a sheep is drawn by the green branch held out to it; he even quotes the poet Virgil, that each one is drawn by his own delight (in substance). The Catechism teaches that faith is a gift of God and at the same time a truly human act (CCC 153–155). In verse 51 the Church hears the Eucharist announced: the bread is the flesh of the Lord given for the life of the world (CCC 1338, 1406).

Questions people ask

If no one can come unless the Father draws him, are some people left out?

No. Jesus also says that, lifted up on the cross, he will draw all people to himself (12:32), and God desires everyone to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). God predestines no one to be lost (CCC 1037). His drawing is offered to all, and it respects our freedom, which can accept or refuse it (CCC 1730).

For study and teaching

  • Who has been a “Philip” for me, helping me understand the Scriptures? For whom could I be one?
  • What draws me to Christ today, and what in my life might make it harder for others to be drawn to him?
