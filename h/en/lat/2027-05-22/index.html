Word for the day

“Let the little children come to me; do not stop them; for it is to such as these that the kingdom of God belongs.” Mark 10:14

Reflection

Ben Sira today meditates on the first pages of Genesis, and he holds together two truths about us that are easy to separate. The first is humility: “The Lord created human beings out of earth, and makes them return to it again. He gave them a fixed number of days.” We are dust, and our days are counted. The second is dignity: he “granted them authority over everything on the earth. He endowed them with strength like his own, and made them in his own image.” God gave us “discretion and tongue and eyes, ears and a mind for thinking,” filled us “with knowledge and understanding,” and “showed them good and evil.” Why? So that we might see his works and praise him: “they will praise his holy name, to proclaim the grandeur of his works.” Then Ben Sira moves from creation to covenant: “He established with them an eternal covenant, and revealed to them his decrees. Their eyes saw his glorious majesty,” a memory of Sinai, and he gave each one “commandment … concerning the neighbor.” To be made in God's image is to be made for praise and for love of neighbour. The Psalm adds the tenderness that makes this bearable: “As a father has compassion for his children, so the Lord has compassion for those who fear him. For he knows how we were made; he remembers that we are dust.”

The Gospel shows us that compassion in action. “People were bringing little children to him in order that he might touch them; and the disciples spoke sternly to them.” Perhaps they thought the Master had more important work than blessing babies. Mark uses a strong word for Jesus' reaction: “he was indignant.” Only here in the Gospels is Jesus said to be indignant with his own disciples. “Let the little children come to me; do not stop them; for it is to such as these that the kingdom of God belongs. Truly I tell you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God as a little child will never enter it.” A child does not earn a gift; a child receives it with open hands. That is how the Kingdom is entered: not by achievement but by trust. “And he took them up in his arms, laid his hands on them, and blessed them.” The Church has always seen in this scene a reason to bring even infants to Baptism (CCC 1250–1252). Today we may honour St Rita of Cascia (1381–1457), wife, mother, widow and Augustinian nun, whom many in Kerala and across India call upon in desperate situations. She lived through a hard marriage, the murder of her husband and the death of her two sons, and she answered violence with forgiveness and prayer. Like a child, she kept receiving everything from God's hands, even when those hands seemed to hold only thorns.

Full homily

Dear friends, Ben Sira reminds us of two truths today. First, we are dust: the Lord created human beings out of earth, and makes them return to it again (Sirach 17:1). Second, we are precious: he made them in his own image. He gave us eyes, ears, a tongue and a mind, so that we might praise him and love our neighbour.

If we forget that we are dust, we become proud. If we forget that we are made in his image, we despair. The Psalm holds both together: as a father has compassion for his children, so the Lord has compassion for those who fear him. He knows how we were made; he remembers that we are dust (Psalm 103:13–14). Our weakness does not surprise God.

In the Gospel mothers bring their little ones to Jesus, and the disciples try to send them away. Jesus is indignant. Let the little children come to me; do not stop them; for it is to such as these that the kingdom of God belongs (Mark 10:14). Children do not earn gifts; they receive them. This is how we must come to God: not with a list of our achievements, but with open hands.

Today many of us remember St Rita of Cascia, a wife and mother who suffered a hard marriage and the loss of her husband and sons, and who answered violence with forgiveness. She is called the saint of impossible causes because she trusted God like a child even when everything seemed lost. Tomorrow we celebrate the Most Holy Trinity, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, who has made us his children. Let us come to him as children. Amen.

For children

Children, once mothers brought their babies and little children to Jesus, but the disciples said: go away! Jesus was not happy with the disciples. He said: let the children come to me! Then he hugged them and blessed them. Jesus loves you very much and always has time for you. You can talk to him anytime!

Prayer

Lord God, you formed us from the dust of the earth and made us in your image; you remember that we are dust and you have compassion on us as a father on his children. Give us the heart of a little child, to receive your Kingdom with open hands. Through the prayers of St Rita, who answered violence with forgiveness and trusted you in every sorrow, bring peace to our homes, and grant us what seems impossible, if it is your will. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ben Sira looks at the human being as God made us: taken from the earth and returning to it, yet made in God’s image, given eyes, ears and a mind, and taught the law of life. In the Gospel Jesus is indignant when his disciples turn children away, and says that the kingdom belongs to such as these. Psalm 103 holds both truths together: we are dust and our days are like grass, but the Lord’s kindness is everlasting to those who fear him.

Sirach 17:1–15 · He made them in his own image

Setting.

This is Ben Sira’s meditation on Genesis 1–3 and on Sinai. He moves from creation (vv. 1–10) to covenant and law (vv. 11–14), and ends with God, who sees all our ways (v. 15).

Textual note. Sirach has come down in a shorter and a longer Greek form, and the Latin Vulgate follows a longer one. Some lines found only in the longer forms are not printed in the NRSV, which is why it has no verse 5. The Malayalam text numbers these verses differently.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: made from earth, given a fixed number of days and authority over the earth.
  • vv. 6–10: the gifts of senses, mind and knowledge, so that they may praise God.
  • vv. 11–14: the law of life and an eternal covenant.
  • v. 15: their ways are always known to him.

Key words.

  • eikōn, image: “He made them in his own image” (v. 3), from Genesis 1:27. For Ben Sira the image shows itself in authority over creation and in the power to know God and praise him.
  • nomos zōēs, law of life: The law is given not as a burden but as the way of life (compare Deuteronomy 30:15–20).
  • diathēkē aiōnos, eternal covenant: The covenant of Sinai, where “their eyes saw his glorious majesty” (v. 13).

Verse by verse.

“The Lord created human beings out of earth, and makes them return to it again.” (17:1)

Genesis 2:7 and 3:19 in one line. Humility and dignity together: we are dust, but dust shaped by God’s hands.

“He filled them with knowledge and understanding, and showed them good and evil.” (17:7)

Ben Sira speaks of knowing good and evil as God’s gift: the moral sense, conscience, by which we can choose the good.

“He said to them, “Beware of all evil.” And he gave commandment to each of them concerning the neighbor.” (17:14)

The two tables of the law in brief: turn from evil before God, and do right by your neighbour.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the human person, created in the image of God, is able to know and love the Creator and was appointed by him master of all earthly creatures, to rule them and use them while glorifying God (Gaudium et Spes 12). The Catechism adds that by reason and will the person is ordered to his true good (CCC 1704–1705). Ben Sira’s praise of human dignity is one root of the Church’s defence of every human life.

Mark 10:13–16 · Let the little children come to me

Setting.

Right after the teaching on marriage comes a scene with children. In the ancient world children were loved, but they had little standing in public life. The disciples, perhaps wanting to protect their busy Master, scold those who bring them.

Mark alone among the Gospels says that Jesus was indignant, and that he took the children in his arms.

Structure.

  • v. 13: children are brought; the disciples rebuke the people.
  • v. 14: Jesus is indignant: let them come.
  • v. 15: receive the kingdom as a little child.
  • v. 16: he embraces and blesses them.

Key words.

  • aganakteō, to be indignant: A strong word of anger. In the Gospels it is used of Jesus only here. What angers him is that the little ones are kept from him.
  • paidion, little child: A small child. The point is not innocence but dependence: a child receives what it cannot earn.
  • enankalizomai, to take in one’s arms: Only Mark uses this word, here and in 9:36. Jesus does not only teach about children; he holds them.

Verse by verse.

“Let the little children come to me; do not stop them; for it is to such as these that the kingdom of God belongs.” (10:14)

“Do not stop them”: the same Greek words as “Do not stop him” in 9:39. Twice in two chapters the disciples try to keep people away, and twice Jesus opens the door.

“whoever does not receive the kingdom of God as a little child will never enter it.” (10:15)

The kingdom is first received, then entered. We come with open hands.

“And he took them up in his arms, laid his hands on them, and blessed them.” (10:16)

Three gestures: embrace, laying on of hands, blessing. Parents who bring their children to the priest for a blessing continue this scene.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls this Gospel when it speaks of the hope of salvation for children who die without Baptism, trusting in Jesus’ tenderness toward children (CCC 1261). The Church baptises infants because the grace of salvation is a pure gift (CCC 1250). And she teaches that to become a child in relation to God is the condition for entering the kingdom (CCC 526).

Questions people ask

Why does the Church baptise babies who cannot yet believe?

Because Baptism is God’s gift before it is our achievement. Children are born into a fallen world and need new life in Christ, and the Church has baptised them from very early times, with parents and godparents promising to bring them up in the faith (CCC 1250–1255).

For the Latin reader

Today is the optional memorial of St Rita of Cascia (about 1381–1457). Married young in Umbria, she bore her husband’s harsh temper with patience and prayer; when he was murdered she forgave those who killed him and prayed that her sons would not take revenge. Widowed and alone, she entered the Augustinian convent at Cascia. Wives, mothers and families in difficulty turn to her as a patron of impossible causes.

For study and teaching

  • What in me needs to become more like a child before God: more trusting, more ready to receive?
  • Ben Sira says God gave each of us a commandment concerning the neighbour. Who is the neighbour I find hardest to do right by?
