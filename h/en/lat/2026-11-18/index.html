Word for the day

“He said to him, ‘Well done, good slave! Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities.’” Luke 19:17

Reflection

John is taken up through an open door in heaven and sees the throne, with the four living creatures singing day and night, “Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God the Almighty, who was and is and is to come”, and the twenty-four elders casting their crowns before him. This is the worship that never stops, and every Mass joins it in the Sanctus. All our work on earth finds its meaning before that throne.

Luke's parable of the pounds is told as Jesus approaches Jerusalem, because people thought the Kingdom would appear immediately. A nobleman goes away to receive a kingdom and gives each servant one pound (mina). Some trade and gain; one wraps his pound in a cloth. The point: the time between Christ's going and his return is a time to work faithfully with what he has given. “To all those who have, more will be given.” Small faithfulness now is preparation for great joy then.

Full homily

Dear friends, today John is taken up through an open door in heaven. He sees the throne of God, the four living creatures who sing day and night without ceasing: Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God the Almighty, who was and is and is to come, and the twenty-four elders casting their crowns before the throne (Revelation 4:8–10).

This is the worship that never stops, and every Mass joins it when we sing the Holy, holy, holy. At the altar we do not only remember heaven; we are united with it. All our work on earth finds its meaning before that throne.

In the Gospel a nobleman goes to a distant country to receive a kingdom and gives each servant a pound to trade with. To the one who made ten more he says: well done, good servant; because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities (Luke 19:17). The fearful servant who hid his pound lost even that.

God has entrusted gifts to each of us: time, talents, faith. Let us not bury them out of fear. Offer your work to God each morning this week, and let your ordinary tasks become part of heaven's song. Amen.

For children

Children, in heaven the angels sing Holy, holy, holy. We sing it too at Mass! Use the gifts God gave you today: your voice, your hands, your smile.

Prayer

Holy, holy, holy Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come, let my life join the worship of heaven, and make me faithful in small things while I await your return. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After the letters, Revelation lifts its eyes: a door stands open in heaven, and John sees the throne of God surrounded by worship that never ends. In the Gospel, near Jerusalem, Jesus tells of a nobleman who goes away to receive royal power and entrusts money to his servants. Heaven worships the King now; on earth his servants work faithfully until he returns. Psalm 150, the last great Hallelujah of the Psalter, answers both.

Revelation 4:1–11 · Holy, holy, holy

Setting.

Chapters 4 and 5 are the centre of the book's vision. Before any seal is opened or trumpet sounded, John is shown that God is on the throne. Christians in Asia lived under an emperor who liked to be acclaimed as lord and god; the historian Suetonius reports that Domitian wished to be called “our lord and god”. Here that acclamation is given to the Creator alone (4:11). The scene draws on Isaiah 6 and Ezekiel 1. Many interpreters see in the twenty-four elders the twelve tribes and the twelve apostles, the whole people of God of both covenants (compare 21:12–14).

Structure.

  • v. 1: an open door in heaven and the call, “Come up here.”
  • vv. 2–3: the throne and the One seated on it, shining like jewels, with a rainbow.
  • vv. 4–6a: twenty-four elders, lightning and thunder, seven torches, a sea of glass.
  • vv. 6b–8: four living creatures and their song.
  • vv. 9–11: the elders cast their crowns before the throne and sing to the Creator.

Key words.

  • thronos, throne: The key word of the chapter. History may look like chaos, but the throne is not empty.
  • hagios, hagios, hagios, holy, holy, holy: The song of the seraphim in Isaiah 6:3. To say “holy” three times is the strongest way of speaking in Hebrew: God is utterly other and utterly pure.
  • axios, worthy: A word used in public acclamations. The Creator alone is worthy of glory and honour; in chapter 5 the same word will be sung to the Lamb.

Verse by verse.

“After this I looked, and there in heaven a door stood open!” (4:1)

In 3:20 Christ knocked at our door. Now heaven's door is opened to us. Worship is where the two doors meet.

“Day and night without ceasing they sing, “Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God the Almighty, who was and is and is to come.”” (4:8)

Isaiah's song with a new line: God is Lord of past, present and future. At every Mass the Church joins this song in the Sanctus.

“they cast their crowns before the throne” (4:10)

The elders have crowns, but they do not keep them. Whatever glory we receive is returned to God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws on this chapter to describe the heavenly liturgy: “a throne stood in heaven, with one seated on the throne”, and all creation gathered in praise around him (CCC 1137–1139). The Second Vatican Council teaches that in the earthly liturgy we take part, by a foretaste, in the heavenly liturgy (Sacrosanctum Concilium 8). The Fathers took the four living creatures, a lion, an ox, a man and an eagle, as symbols of the four evangelists; the arrangement that became usual in the West, man for Matthew, lion for Mark, ox for Luke and eagle for John, comes from St Jerome.

Luke 19:11–28 · Do business with these until I come back

Setting.

Luke gives the reason for the parable: Jesus was near Jerusalem, and the people “supposed that the kingdom of God was to appear immediately” (v. 11). The story corrects that hope: the king must first go away and then return.

The parable recalls real events. When Herod the Great died (4 BC), his son Archelaus went to Rome to have his kingship confirmed by Augustus, and a delegation of Jews followed him to oppose it, as the historian Josephus reports. Archelaus later rebuilt the royal palace at Jericho, where Jesus has just been. Luke's hearers would have caught the allusion.

Structure.

  • v. 11: the occasion.
  • vv. 12–14: a nobleman leaves to receive royal power; ten servants receive a pound each; his citizens reject him.
  • vv. 15–19: he returns; two servants report ten and five pounds and receive cities.
  • vv. 20–26: the third servant and the cloth; the rule of having and not having.
  • vv. 27–28: the enemies judged; Jesus goes up to Jerusalem.

Key words.

  • mna, pound: A sum of a hundred denarii, about a hundred days' wages: far less than Matthew's talents. Here each servant receives the same, which points to a gift common to all, such as the word of the Gospel or the life of faith.
  • pragmateuesthai, to do business: The gift is not to be wrapped up and stored; it must be put to work.
  • basileia, royal power, kingdom: The same Greek word means kingdom and kingship. The nobleman goes away to receive the kingship that Jesus receives through his Passion, Resurrection and Ascension.

Verse by verse.

“Do business with these until I come back.” (19:13)

This is the time of the Church: between the King's going and his return.

“Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities.” (19:17)

A pound for ten cities: the reward is out of all proportion to the work. Compare 16:10: “Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much.”

“After he had said this, he went on ahead, going up to Jerusalem.” (19:28)

The parable ends and the real story begins. The King goes ahead of his disciples to the city where he will be crowned with thorns.

How the Church reads it.

The parable warns against two errors: thinking that the kingdom will come at once, without the Cross and the long time of the Church, and thinking that the time of waiting is a time to do nothing. The Catechism teaches that Christ's reign is already present in the Church, yet is still to be fulfilled “with power and great glory” at the King's return (CCC 671). The harsh last verse belongs to the story of an earthly king; Jesus himself will soon weep over the city that does not receive him (19:41). The judgment is real, but the heart of the true King is shown on the Cross.

Questions people ask

How is this parable different from the talents in Matthew?

They are related but not the same. In Matthew the servants receive different sums according to ability; in Luke each receives one pound, and a second thread is added: citizens who reject the king. Matthew stresses the use of varied gifts; Luke stresses faithfulness with the one gift common to all, and the rejection of the King on his way to Jerusalem.

For study and teaching

  • When I take part in Mass, do I remember that I join the song of heaven, “Holy, holy, holy”?
  • What is the “pound” the Lord has given to every Christian, and how am I putting it to work?
