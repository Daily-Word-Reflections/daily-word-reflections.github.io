Word for the day

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth; for he will not speak on his own, but will speak whatever he hears, and he will declare to you the things that are to come.” John 16:13

Reflection

Paul has been hurried out of Thessalonica and Beroea and brought, almost alone, to Athens, the most famous city of learning in the ancient world. Walking its streets, he sees temples, statues and altars everywhere. He is invited to speak at the Areopagus, the hill and council where the city's philosophers and judges met. His speech is a model of how to speak of Christ to people of another culture. He does not begin with insults; he begins with respect: “Athenians, I see how extremely religious you are in every way.” He has noticed an altar “To an unknown god,” and he builds on it: “What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you.” Then he speaks of the Creator who “made the world and everything in it,” who does not live in shrines made by hands, who gives to all “life and breath and all things,” and who made all nations from one ancestor, “so that they would search for God and perhaps grope for him and find him, though indeed he is not far from each one of us.” He even quotes their own poets: “In him we live and move and have our being,” and “For we too are his offspring.” Only at the end does he name what is new: God has fixed a day of judgment “by a man whom he has appointed, and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead.”

At the word “resurrection” the meeting breaks up. “When they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some scoffed; but others said, ‘We will hear you again about this.’” It looks like a failure, and Paul soon leaves for Corinth. Yet Luke adds: “some of them joined him and became believers, including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris, and others with them.” Seeds are sown even where the harvest is small. The Psalm calls the whole creation to praise, “kings of the earth and all peoples,” “young men and women alike, old and young together,” for the Lord's “glory is above earth and heaven.” In the Gospel Jesus explains why the Church's understanding of him can grow without changing: “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” The Spirit does not bring a new Gospel; “he will take what is mine and declare it to you.” He leads the Church deeper into the one truth that is Christ. That is what Paul did at Athens: he found in the Greeks' own searching a door for the truth of Christ. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions, while she proclaims without ceasing Christ, “the way, and the truth, and the life” (Nostra Aetate 2). In a land of many temples and many seekers like India, Paul at the Areopagus is a teacher for us: respectful, clear, and unashamed of the Resurrection.

Full homily

Dear friends, imagine Paul in Athens: a Jewish tentmaker standing before the philosophers of the most learned city in the world. What does he say? He does not mock them. He says: Athenians, I see how extremely religious you are in every way (Acts 17:22). He has seen an altar to an unknown god, and he says: what you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you.

Paul shows us how to speak about Jesus to people of other faiths. First, respect. Look for the good that is already there, the search for God, the prayer, the longing. Second, start from what we share: the one Creator who gives life and breath to all, who made every nation, in whom we live and move and have our being (Acts 17:28). God is not far from each one of us.

Third, do not hide what is new. Paul ends with the Resurrection: God has raised Jesus from the dead. And some laughed. Some said: we will hear you again. Most went away. But Dionysius and a woman named Damaris and others believed. We do not measure success by the size of the crowd. Seeds sown in Athens grew into a Church.

In the Gospel Jesus says: when the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth (John 16:13). The Spirit does not give a different Gospel; he takes what belongs to Jesus and makes it clearer. In our country of many temples and many seekers, let us ask the Spirit to give us Paul's respect and Paul's courage, and to guide us, and our neighbours, into the whole truth of the risen Christ. Amen.

For children

Children, Paul went to a big city called Athens, full of statues of gods. He saw an altar that said: to an unknown god. Paul said: I know this God! He made everything, he gives you life, and he is very close to you. His Son Jesus rose from the dead. When we talk to friends who do not know Jesus, let us be kind and polite like Paul, and tell them how good God is.

Prayer

God our Creator, in you we live and move and have our being, and you are not far from each one of us. You let the peoples of the earth search for you and grope for you; reveal to them your Son, whom you raised from the dead. Give us the respect, clarity and courage of St Paul at Athens. Send us the Spirit of truth, who takes what belongs to Jesus and declares it to us, so that we may be led into all the truth and praise you with all creation. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul stands in Athens, the city of philosophers, and begins not with condemnation but with an altar “to an unknown god”. He proclaims the God they half knew, and then the Resurrection, and many walk away. In the Gospel Jesus says that he has more to tell than the disciples can bear now; the Spirit of truth will guide them into all the truth. Psalm 148 calls kings and peoples, young and old, to praise the name of the Lord.

Acts 17:15, 22–18:1 · To an unknown god

Setting.

Driven out of Thessalonica and then Beroea, Paul is brought to Athens and waits there for Silas and Timothy. Athens was no longer a great power, but it was still a famous city of learning. Paul is deeply distressed at the many idols (17:16). He argues in the synagogue and in the marketplace, where Epicurean and Stoic philosophers take him for a preacher of foreign gods (17:17–18). Some scholars think they even took “Jesus and the resurrection”, Anastasis, for a pair of new gods. They bring him to the Areopagus.

Background. The Areopagus, the hill of Ares, gave its name to an ancient council of the city, still respected in Roman times. Paul’s speech there is not a defence at a trial, but a public account of his teaching before the city’s leading men.

Structure.

  • v. 15: Paul is brought to Athens.
  • vv. 22–23: the altar to an unknown god.
  • vv. 24–28: the God who made all things, gives life to all, and is near to each one.
  • vv. 29–31: not like gold or stone; the call to repent; the day of judgment and the man raised from the dead.
  • 17:32–18:1: mockery, delay, and a few believers; Paul goes on to Corinth.

Key words.

  • deisidaimōn, religious: Paul calls the Athenians “extremely religious” (v. 22). The word can mean devout or superstitious; Paul begins with the respectful sense. He builds a bridge before he crosses it.
  • agnōstos theos, unknown god: Ancient writers mention altars at Athens to unknown gods. Paul takes the inscription as an honest confession of ignorance and offers to fill it (v. 23).
  • anastasis, resurrection: The word that divided his hearers (v. 32). Many Greeks could accept that the soul survives death, but the rising of the dead seemed foolish to them. Paul later writes that Christ crucified is foolishness to Gentiles (1 Corinthians 1:23).

Verse by verse.

“What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you.” (17:23)

Paul does not mock their search. He honours it, and then gives it a name.

“For ‘In him we live and move and have our being’; as even some of your own poets have said, ‘For we too are his offspring.’” (17:28)

Paul quotes Greek poets, not the Hebrew Scriptures, which his hearers do not know. The second line comes from the poet Aratus; the first is often traced to Epimenides of Crete, though that is less certain. Truth found in pagan poetry can become a step toward Christ.

“now he commands all people everywhere to repent,” (17:30)

The bridge has a far side. Respect for their search does not mean leaving them where they are. The God they sought now calls them to turn to him.

“When they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some scoffed; but others said, “We will hear you again about this.”” (17:32)

Three responses: mockery, delay, and faith (v. 34). They are still the responses to preaching today.

“But some of them joined him and became believers, including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris,” (17:34)

Few, but real. Tradition makes Dionysius the first bishop of Athens. Writings that later circulated under his name are now generally dated to around the year 500; they had great influence on Christian mystical theology.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 28 when it teaches that God, the Creator, is present to the inmost being of his creatures (CCC 300). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions, which often reflect a ray of that Truth which enlightens all people, while she proclaims Christ, the way, the truth and the life (Nostra Aetate 2). St Justin Martyr, a philosopher who became a Christian, spoke of the seeds of the Word sown among all peoples (in substance), and the Council uses the same image (Ad Gentes 11). Paul at the Areopagus shows the way: respect, common ground, and then the clear proclamation of the Risen Christ.

John 16:12–15 · He will guide you into all the truth

Setting.

Still at the Last Supper. Jesus knows that the disciples cannot yet carry the full weight of what is coming: the Cross, the Resurrection, the mission to the nations. He does not overload them. He promises the Spirit, who will lead them step by step.

Structure.

  • v. 12: many things you cannot bear now.
  • v. 13: the Spirit of truth will guide you into all the truth.
  • vv. 14–15: he will glorify me, taking what is mine; all that the Father has is mine.

Key words.

  • hodēgeō, to guide: To lead along a road (v. 13). The Spirit does not hand over the truth all at once like a parcel; he walks with the Church into it.
  • bastazō, to bear, carry: They cannot “bear” it now (v. 12). The word is used of carrying a load, as Jesus carried his cross (19:17).
  • anangellō, to declare: Three times in vv. 13–15. The Spirit announces and makes known. What he declares is not a new revelation beside Christ, but what belongs to Christ.

Verse by verse.

“I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now.” (16:12)

The patience of a good teacher. God gives light as we are able to receive it.

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth;” (16:13)

Not into new truths that contradict Christ, but ever more deeply into the one truth that is Christ.

“All that the Father has is mine.” (16:15)

One of the clearest glimpses of the Trinity in the Gospels: the Father gives all to the Son, and the Spirit takes from the Son and declares it to us.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Tradition which comes from the apostles develops in the Church with the help of the Holy Spirit: there is a growth in the understanding of the realities and the words handed on, and the Spirit leads believers into all truth (Dei Verbum 8). St John Henry Newman, in his work on the development of doctrine, showed how the Church grows in understanding without changing the faith. Development is not a change of the faith but its deeper understanding.

Questions people ask

Did Paul fail at Athens?

Some have thought so, because few believed, and because Paul later says that he came to Corinth in weakness, resolved to know nothing but Jesus Christ crucified (1 Corinthians 2:1–3). But Luke does not present the speech as a failure: there were believers, and a man and a woman are named among them. Faithfulness, not numbers, is the measure.

For study and teaching

  • What “unknown god” do the people around me seek, and how could I begin from their search, as Paul did?
  • Is there something in the faith I could not bear before, but understand better now?
