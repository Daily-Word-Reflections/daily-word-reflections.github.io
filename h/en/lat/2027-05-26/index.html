Word for the day

“For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” Mark 10:45

Reflection

The first reading is a prayer, one of the most fervent in the Wisdom books. Ben Sira's people are small and pressed on every side by powerful nations, and he cries out: “Have mercy upon us, O God of all, and put all the nations in fear of you.” But his deepest wish is not revenge. It is that the nations should come to know the true God: “As you have used us to show your holiness to them, so use them to show your glory to us. Then they will know, as we have known, that there is no God but you, O Lord.” Israel's suffering and Israel's rescue are meant to become a lesson for the whole world. Then the prayer grows tender: “Gather all the tribes of Jacob,” “Have mercy, O Lord, on the people called by your name, on Israel, whom you have named your firstborn,” “Have pity on the city of your sanctuary, Jerusalem.” And he ends with a line that the Church can pray at every Mass: “Hear, O Lord, the prayer of your servants, according to your goodwill toward your people, and all who are on the earth will know that you are the Lord, the God of the ages.” The Psalm, from a time of national disaster, prays in the same spirit: “Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; deliver us, and forgive our sins, for your name's sake.”

In the Gospel Jesus is walking ahead of his disciples on the road up to Jerusalem, and “they were amazed, and those who followed were afraid.” For the third time he tells them plainly what will happen: he will be handed over, condemned, mocked, flogged and killed, “and after three days he will rise again.” And at that very moment James and John come with a request: “Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory.” They are thinking of thrones; he is thinking of a cross. “You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink?” They answer bravely, “We are able,” and in time they will: James will be the first of the Twelve to die for Christ. The other ten are angry, perhaps because they wanted the same seats. So Jesus gathers them all and turns the world upside down: among the nations, rulers “lord it over them,” “but it is not so among you; but whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wishes to be first among you must be slave of all. For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” St Philip Neri, whom we remember today, could have had honours in the Rome of the popes. He refused the cardinal's hat more than once. He spent his life hearing confessions, visiting the sick in the hospitals, walking with young men from church to church, and making people laugh so that pride would have no room. He showed that the greatness Jesus speaks of can be joyful.

Full homily

Dear friends, Ben Sira prays for his small, suffering people, and his prayer is wider than his own nation. As you have used us to show your holiness to them, he prays, so use them to show your glory to us. Then they will know, as we have known, that there is no God but you, O Lord (Sirach 36:4–5). He wants the whole world to know the true God. That is still the prayer of the Church: that all who are on the earth will know that you are the Lord.

In the Gospel Jesus is walking ahead on the road to Jerusalem. He tells the Twelve for the third time that he will suffer, die and rise. And right then James and John come and ask for the best seats: one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory (Mark 10:37). We smile at them. But how often we do the same: we follow Jesus, and quietly we hope for a little honour, a good name, a front seat.

Jesus answers gently: are you able to drink the cup that I drink? The cup is his suffering, his self-giving. Then he calls all twelve together and says: whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant. For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many (Mark 10:43, 45). In his kingdom, the chair of honour is a servant's stool.

St Philip Neri understood this. In Rome, a city full of honours and titles, he refused to become a cardinal. He spent his days in the confessional, in the hospitals, with poor young men. And he did it with joy, with jokes, with music. He showed that serving is not a sad duty; it is the joy of the Holy Spirit. Let us ask for that joy today, and look for one small chance to serve. Amen.

For children

Children, two of Jesus' friends wanted the best chairs, right next to Jesus! Jesus said: in my kingdom the most important person is the one who helps others. St Philip Neri was like that. He helped poor people and sick people, and he was always laughing and joking. Today, can you help someone at home, and do it with a big smile, like St Philip?

Prayer

O God, who never cease to bestow the glory of holiness on the faithful servants you raise up for yourself, graciously grant that the Holy Spirit may kindle in us that fire with which he wonderfully filled the heart of Saint Philip Neri. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you came not to be served but to serve. Deliver us from the wish for the first seats, and give us joy in the lowest place, so that all the earth may know that you are Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ben Sira prays for his people under foreign powers: have mercy, gather the tribes of Jacob, and let all the nations know that there is no God but you. In the Gospel Jesus walks ahead of his frightened disciples on the road to Jerusalem and tells them for the third time what will happen there. James and John ask for the best seats in his glory. Jesus answers that greatness means service, because the Son of Man came to give his life a ransom for many. On the memorial of St Philip Neri, Psalm 79 asks the Lord to show us the light of his kindness.

Sirach 36:1, 4–5, 13–22 (Lectionary 36:1, 4–5a, 10–17) · Let them know that there is no God but you

Setting.

This is Ben Sira’s great prayer of petition for the whole people, rather than for the teacher himself. It asks God to act against the foreign nations that oppress Israel, to gather the scattered tribes, and to fill Zion with his glory. Many scholars think it reflects the hard years under the Seleucid kings of Syria in the second century before Christ. Some think it was added to the book in the time of the Maccabees; others hold that Ben Sira wrote it himself.

The verse numbers differ from Bible to Bible here, because the ancient manuscripts arrange this part of the book differently. The Lectionary leaves out the verses that ask most strongly for the destruction of enemies (vv. 6–12 in our numbering).

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Have mercy upon us, O God of all.”
  • vv. 4–5: as Israel has shown God’s holiness to the nations, may the nations come to know him too.
  • vv. 13–17: gather the tribes of Jacob; have mercy on Israel, your firstborn, and on Jerusalem.
  • vv. 18–22: fill Zion with your glory, fulfil the prophecies, hear the prayer of your servants.

Key words.

  • eleos, mercy: The prayer opens with a cry for mercy, the same word as in “Kyrie eleison”. The whole petition rests on God’s faithful love, not on Israel’s merit.
  • prōtogonos, firstborn: God had called Israel “my firstborn son” when he sent Moses to Pharaoh (Exodus 4:22). The title means a special love and a special task, not that the other nations are unloved.
  • hagiasma, sanctuary: Jerusalem is named as the city of God’s sanctuary, the place of his dwelling. The prayer longs for the Temple to be filled with his glory.

Verse by verse.

“Have mercy upon us, O God of all,” (36:1)

He is “God of all”, not only of Israel. That is why the prayer can end by hoping that all on earth will know him.

“As you have used us to show your holiness to them, so use them to show your glory to us.” (36:4)

A striking request. When Israel was punished, the nations saw God’s holiness. Now Ben Sira asks that when Israel is restored, the nations themselves will see God’s glory.

“Then they will know, as we have known, that there is no God but you, O Lord.” (36:5)

The deepest hope of the prayer is not revenge but knowledge of God. The nations are to know him as Israel knows him.

“all who are on the earth will know that you are the Lord, the God of the ages.” (36:22)

The prayer ends where it began: with God over all. Christians hear in this the mission of the Church to all nations, the work of the God who keeps his promises to Israel.

How the Church reads it.

The Church prays part of this text as a canticle in the Liturgy of the Hours, a prayer of the whole people of God. She also reads it in the light of Christ: the gathering of the scattered children of God (John 11:52) and the knowledge of God among the nations. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church, the new people of God, draws sustenance from the root of that good olive tree onto which the Gentiles have been grafted (Nostra Aetate 4). God’s gifts and call to Israel are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). So the Christian reader prays with Ben Sira for Israel, and for all nations, that the God of the ages be known.

Mark 10:32–45 · Not to be served but to serve

Setting.

Mark’s central section (8:27–10:52) is a journey “on the way” to Jerusalem. Three times Jesus foretells his passion (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34), and three times the disciples fail to understand: Peter rebukes him, the disciples argue about who is the greatest, and now James and John ask for places of honour. Each time Jesus teaches about following him.

This third prediction is the most detailed. It names the handing over to the Gentiles, the mocking, the spitting and the flogging, all of which happen in the Passion narrative.

Structure.

  • vv. 32–34: Jesus walks ahead; the third prediction of the passion.
  • vv. 35–40: the request of James and John; the cup and the baptism.
  • vv. 41–45: the anger of the ten; greatness as service; the Son of Man gives his life a ransom for many.

Key words.

  • potērion, cup: In the Old Testament the cup often stands for a destiny given by God, sometimes the cup of his wrath (Isaiah 51:17). In Gethsemane Jesus will pray, “remove this cup from me” (14:36).
  • diakonos, doulos, servant, slave: Two words in rising strength (vv. 43–44). The great one must be a servant; the first must be the slave of all. From diakonos comes the word deacon.
  • lytron, ransom: The price paid to release a captive or a slave. Jesus gives his own life to release the many from sin. “Many” recalls the servant of Isaiah 53, who “bore the sin of many” (53:12).

Verse by verse.

“Jesus was walking ahead of them; they were amazed, and those who followed were afraid.” (10:32)

He goes first, deliberately, towards the city where he will die. The disciples feel the weight of it before they understand it.

“Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory.” (10:37)

They believe he will be glorified, which is to their credit. Mark’s readers know who will in fact be at his right and his left: two bandits crucified with him (15:27).

“The cup that I drink you will drink;” (10:39)

This came true. James was the first of the Twelve to die for Christ, killed with the sword by Herod (Acts 12:2). John too bore witness through long suffering.

“For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” (10:45)

Perhaps the key verse of Mark’s Gospel. The whole life of Jesus is service, and his death is its fullest act.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the redemption won by Christ consists in this, that he came to give his life as a ransom for many (CCC 622). It adds that the word “many” is not restrictive: it sets the whole of humanity over against the one Redeemer who gives himself for all (CCC 605). The Church has always read verses 42–45 as the rule for every authority in her. Pope St Gregory the Great called himself “servant of the servants of God”, a title the popes still use. Whoever holds any office, in the parish or in the family, is measured by this Gospel.

Questions people ask

To whom was the ransom paid?

Some early writers spoke as if a price were paid to the devil, but the Church has not made that a teaching. The image is not a business transaction. It means that Christ’s self-giving cost him his life, and that by it we are released from sin and death. He gave himself out of love, to the Father, for us.

For the Latin reader

St Philip Neri (1515–1595), born in Florence, spent his life in Rome and is called the Apostle of Rome. He gathered priests and lay people for prayer, the reading of Scripture and the lives of the saints, music, and works of charity, and from these gatherings came the Congregation of the Oratory. He was a priest of the confessional and of great joy, known for his humour, which kept both himself and others humble. Today’s Gospel fits him well: he sought no honours, and he served everyone who came to him.

For study and teaching

  • Where do I, like James and John, want the place of honour? What would serving look like there instead?
  • Who in my family or parish serves without being noticed? How can I thank them, and learn from them?
