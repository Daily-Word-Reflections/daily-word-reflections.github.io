Word for the day

“In the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed.” Mark 1:35

Reflection

Mark shows us a full day in the life of Jesus. After the synagogue he enters Simon's house, where Simon's mother-in-law lies sick with fever. “He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” Healing becomes service. At sunset the whole town crowds at the door, and Jesus cures many. Then, “In the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed.” The secret of his day is his night prayer.

Hebrews explains why the Son came so close: he shared our flesh and blood “so that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by the fear of death.” He became like us in every respect to be a merciful high priest. “Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested.” St Hilary of Poitiers, a married convert who became bishop, defended the divinity of Christ in exile; he knew both trials and the Lord who helps in them.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's Gospel shows us a full day in the life of Jesus. After teaching in the synagogue he goes to Simon's house, where Simon's mother-in-law is in bed with a fever.

He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them (Mark 1:31). The first thing she does with her new strength is serve. Healing leads to service.

At sunset the whole town gathers at the door, and Jesus cures many. Then: in the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed (Mark 1:35). Jesus' busy day rests on his night prayer.

Hebrews tells us why he came so close: he became like us in every respect, so that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest. Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested (Hebrews 2:18).

Today we remember St Hilary, who defended faith in Christ even in exile. Let us ask for Jesus' habit of early prayer, and let him lift us up to serve. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus prayed very early in the morning, while it was still dark. Tomorrow, say a little prayer to Jesus as soon as you wake up!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you took Simon's mother-in-law by the hand and lifted her up, and before dawn you prayed to your Father. Lift me up from what weighs me down, teach me to pray, and help me in every test, for you were tested as I am. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hebrews says today why the Son had to become like us in every respect: so that he might be merciful, and able to help. Mark shows that mercy at work on a single day in Capernaum: a sick woman lifted by the hand, a town at the door at sunset, and a man at prayer before dawn. The psalm calls us to “seek the Lord and his strength”. Both readings speak of one who comes very close, and who does not stay in any one place.

Hebrews 2:14–18 · Like his brothers and sisters in every respect

Setting.

The argument of yesterday's reading continues, the Lectionary leaping only v. 13. If the Son calls us brothers and sisters, he has to share what is ours. Here for the first time in the letter Jesus is called high priest (v. 17), the great theme that will fill chapters 4–10. Verse 16 names the people he came to help: not angels but “the descendants of Abraham”.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: he shares flesh and blood in order to destroy death's power.
  • v. 16: he came to help the descendants of Abraham.
  • vv. 17–18: he had to be like us, to be a merciful and faithful high priest and to help those being tested.

Key words.

  • archiereus, high priest: The one who represented the people before God, above all on the Day of Atonement. Hebrews alone in the New Testament gives this title to Jesus by name.
  • eleēmōn kai pistos, merciful and faithful: The two sides of a true priest: merciful towards the people, faithful towards God. These words sum up the rest of the letter.
  • peirazō, to test, to tempt: The same word serves for both. He knows from within what it is to be tried. Hebrews adds later that he was “without sin” (4:15).

Verse by verse.

“Since, therefore, the children share flesh and blood, he himself likewise shared the same things,” (2:14)

This is the Incarnation in its simplest form. St Gregory of Nazianzus gave the reason in a famous line: what has not been assumed has not been healed.

“Therefore he had to become like his brothers and sisters in every respect,” (2:17)

“Had to”, not because anything could force God, but because this is what mercy required. There is no part of our life that he has not touched from within, except sin.

“Because he himself was tested by what he suffered, he is able to help those who are being tested.” (2:18)

A word for anyone who feels that God cannot understand them. The Lord does not help us from a distance. He has been there.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes vv. 14–15 when it teaches that Christ, by dying, destroyed him who has the power of death and delivered those who were subject to lifelong bondage through fear of it (CCC 635). The Church's prayer at funerals rests on this hope. The fear of death has not vanished, but it no longer has the last word.

Mark 1:29–39 · A day in Capernaum

Setting.

The Gospel continues directly from yesterday's scene in the synagogue, and it is still the same sabbath. Archaeologists at Capernaum have uncovered a house that early Christians honoured as a place of worship, and many think that it was Peter's. The people wait until sunset, probably because the sabbath ended then, and they could carry the sick. The story of this one full day ends in a very different place: in the dark, alone, in prayer.

Structure.

  • vv. 29–31: Simon's mother-in-law is healed.
  • vv. 32–34: at sunset the whole town comes.
  • vv. 35–39: prayer before dawn, and the decision to move on.

Key words.

  • egeirō, to lift up, to raise: Mark says he “lifted her up” with the verb he will use for the Resurrection (16:6). A hand reaching out to a sick woman already shows what the Lord will do for all.
  • diakoneō, to serve: “She began to serve them.” It is the word for what Jesus says he came to do: “not to be served but to serve” (Mark 10:45). She is the first human being in Mark to do it (angels do it in 1:13), and the healed life at once becomes a life of service.
  • erēmos, deserted: The same word as the wilderness in which John preached and Jesus was tempted (1:4, 12). Jesus goes back to the place of testing to be with his Father.

Verse by verse.

“He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” (1:31)

There is no word of power here, only a hand. The disciples told him about her (v. 30), and this is the simplest form of intercession: they told him.

“In the morning, while it was still very dark, he got up and went out to a deserted place, and there he prayed.” (1:35)

After the busiest day so far, he gets up in the dark. Prayer was not what Jesus did when the work was done; it was where the work came from.

““Let us go on to the neighboring towns, so that I may proclaim the message there also; for that is what I came out to do.”” (1:38)

“Everyone is searching for you” (v. 37) is what success sounds like. He hears his mission in prayer, not in the crowd's applause, and it takes him further on.

How the Church reads it.

Christ's healings, the Catechism says, are a resplendent sign that “God has visited his people” (CCC 1503). Jesus often draws apart to pray in solitude, on a mountain, preferably at night (CCC 2602). The Church keeps that order in her own life: the morning prayer of the Liturgy of the Hours comes before the day's work.

Questions people ask

Was St Peter married?

Yes. A mother-in-law means a wife, and St Paul also mentions that Cephas took a believing wife with him on his travels (1 Corinthians 9:5). In the Latin Church today priests are normally chosen from men who live celibacy for the Kingdom, as a discipline and a gift; in the Eastern Churches married men may be ordained priests (CCC 1579–1580). Celibacy is not a judgment on marriage; both are ways of following the Lord.

For the Latin reader

St Hilary of Poitiers, bishop and doctor, may be remembered today. In the fourth century he was sent into exile by the emperor for defending the faith of Nicaea, and he wrote his great work On the Trinity. Today's reading fits him well: he defended both the true divinity of the Son and the truth of his becoming our brother.

For study and teaching

  • Whom could you “tell Jesus about” today, as the disciples told him about Simon's mother-in-law?
  • What is the first thing you do when you get up? How might you make room for even a short time of prayer before the day begins?
