Word for the day

“But he said to them, “It is I; do not be afraid.”” John 6:20

Reflection

The young Church has its first internal crisis, and it is about food and fairness. The disciples are increasing in number, and “the Hellenists complained against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution of food.” The Hellenists were Jewish believers who spoke Greek and had come from the lands of the dispersion; the Hebrews spoke Aramaic and belonged to the land. Two cultures in one community, and the poorest, the widows, were falling between them. The apostles do not ignore the complaint or blame anyone. They call the whole community together and propose a solution: “select from among yourselves seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom,” to serve the tables, “while we, for our part, will devote ourselves to prayer and to serving the word.” All seven chosen have Greek names: the community entrusts the care of the neglected to people from the neglected group. The apostles pray and lay their hands on them. The Church has long seen in this scene the beginnings of the diaconate, a ministry of service ordained by the laying on of hands (Catechism 1569–1571). And the result: “The word of God continued to spread… and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith.” A problem honestly faced became a moment of growth.

In the Gospel, after the feeding of the five thousand, the disciples go down to the sea in the evening and set out by boat for Capernaum. John adds a quiet, heavy sentence: “It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them.” The sea becomes rough with a strong wind. They have rowed three or four miles when they see Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, “and they were terrified.” Then he speaks: “It is I; do not be afraid.” In Greek it is simply ego eimi, I AM, the name God revealed to Moses at the burning bush. The One who multiplied the loaves is the Lord of creation who walks on the chaos of the waters, as the Psalms say only God can do. When they want to take him into the boat, “immediately the boat reached the land toward which they were going.” The Church has often been pictured as a boat on a dark sea. Today's two readings belong together: in the first, the boat of the Church meets a storm from within, quarrels and neglect; in the second, a storm from outside. In both, the risen Lord comes across the water and says the same words: It is I; do not be afraid.

Full homily

Dear friends, the first Church had a problem. Some widows were being forgotten in the daily distribution of food. The Greek-speaking believers complained. The apostles did not say: everything is fine. They listened, called everyone together and found a solution: seven men full of the Spirit and of wisdom, chosen to serve (Acts 6:3).

Look at the result. The forgotten widows were cared for, the apostles could give themselves to prayer and the word, and the word of God spread. A complaint, when it is heard with humility, can become a grace. Every parish has its forgotten widows. Who are they in ours?

In the Gospel the disciples are rowing at night against a strong wind. John says: it was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them (John 6:17). Many of us know that feeling. Then they see Jesus walking on the water, and he says: it is I; do not be afraid (John 6:20). In Greek it is simply: I AM, the name of God.

The storm may come from inside, like the quarrel over the widows, or from outside, like the wind on the sea. In both, the risen Lord comes to us. Today at this altar he comes again, and says: it is I. Let us take him into our boat, and trust that he will bring us to the shore. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, the friends of Jesus were in a boat at night, and a big wind was blowing. They were afraid. Then Jesus came walking on the water and said: It is I; do not be afraid! When you are scared at night, you can say: Jesus, you are with me. I am not afraid!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you walked on the stormy sea and said to your frightened disciples: It is I; do not be afraid. Come to us in every storm, from within and from without. Give your Church ministers full of the Spirit and of wisdom, who will care for the forgotten, and keep our pastors faithful to prayer and the service of the word. Let your mercy be on us, as we place our trust in you. Who live and reign for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The first Church meets its first quarrel, over food for widows, and solves it by sharing out ministry: the apostles keep to prayer and the word, and seven men full of the Spirit take up the daily service. In the Gospel the disciples cross the lake in the dark and the wind, and Jesus comes to them over the water: “It is I; do not be afraid.” Both scenes show a Church that is never left alone in its difficulties. Psalm 33 asks: “Lord, let your mercy be on us, as we place our trust in you”.

Acts 6:1–7 · Seven men full of the Spirit

Setting.

The Jerusalem Church was growing, and with growth came strain. “Hellenists” were Jewish believers who spoke Greek, many of them from Jewish communities outside the land who had settled in Jerusalem. “Hebrews” were Jewish believers whose everyday language was Aramaic. Both groups were Jewish; the difference was language and culture.

Widows without family had no income, and the Jerusalem community cared for them daily. The seven chosen all have Greek names, and one, Nicolaus, was a proselyte, a gentile who had become a Jew. The solution comes from within the group that felt neglected.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the complaint.
  • vv. 2–4: the Twelve propose a solution.
  • vv. 5–6: the community chooses seven; the apostles pray and lay hands on them.
  • v. 7: the word spreads, even among the priests.

Key words.

  • diakonia, service, ministry: The key word, used three ways: the daily “distribution” is diakonia (v. 1), “to wait on tables” is diakonein (v. 2), and the apostles’ “serving the word” is diakonia of the word (v. 4). Both are service; neither is lower in God’s eyes.
  • gongysmos, complaint, murmuring: The word recalls Israel murmuring in the wilderness (Exodus 16). Luke does not hide the Church’s tensions, but shows how the Spirit heals them.
  • epitithenai tas cheiras, to lay hands on: An ancient gesture of blessing and of handing on a task, used for Joshua by Moses (Numbers 27:18–23). In Acts it accompanies prayer and the gift of the Spirit.

Verse by verse.

“It is not right that we should neglect the word of God in order to wait on tables.” (6:2)

Not contempt for practical service, which the seven are chosen to do with the Spirit’s help. It is a question of each one’s calling: the apostles must not be pulled away from the word and prayer.

“select from among yourselves seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom,” (6:3)

Even managing food requires the Spirit. The qualities asked for are spiritual before they are practical.

“who prayed and laid their hands on them.” (6:6)

The community chooses, but the apostles commission them with prayer and the laying on of hands. The Church has seen in this the pattern of ordination.

“a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith.” (6:7)

A striking note: many of the temple priests came to faith. The first Church was a Jewish community, and the Gospel took root first among the people of the covenant.

How the Church reads it.

Acts does not call the seven “deacons”, and two of them, Stephen and Philip, are soon seen preaching and baptizing. Yet since St Irenaeus in the second century the Church has seen in this scene the beginning of the diaconate. The Second Vatican Council teaches that deacons receive the laying on of hands “not unto the priesthood, but unto a ministry of service” (Lumen Gentium 29; CCC 1569).

John 6:16–21 · It is I; do not be afraid

Setting.

After the feeding of the five thousand, Jesus has withdrawn to the mountain to avoid being made king (6:15). The disciples set out alone across the lake toward Capernaum. Mark 6:45–52 and Matthew 14:22–33 tell the same event; Matthew adds Peter’s walk on the water. John’s account is short and leads straight to the next day’s discourse.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–18: evening, darkness, a rough sea, and Jesus absent.
  • vv. 19–20: Jesus comes over the water and speaks.
  • v. 21: they want to take him in, and at once they reach land.

Key words.

  • skotia, darkness: In John darkness is never only the time of day. “It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them” describes the disciples’ state as much as the hour (cf. 1:5).
  • stadia, stades (NRSV: miles): The Greek says “twenty-five or thirty stades”, about five or six kilometres. The NRSV gives “three or four miles”. They are in the middle of the lake, far from either shore.
  • egō eimi, It is I: Literally “I am”. On the lips of Jesus in John these words often recall the divine name revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14), as in 8:58. Here they both identify him and reveal him.

Verse by verse.

“It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them.” (6:17)

Many Christians know such nights: rowing hard with the Lord seemingly absent. John’s “not yet” promises that he is coming.

“they saw Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, and they were terrified.” (6:19)

In the Old Testament it is God who “trampled the waves of the Sea” (Job 9:8). Jesus does what only God does.

“It is I; do not be afraid.” (6:20)

The Lord’s first word is his presence, the second is peace. These are the words most often repeated in Scripture to frightened people.

“immediately the boat reached the land toward which they were going.” (6:21)

Psalm 107 sings of sailors in a storm whom God brought “to their desired haven” (107:30). When Jesus is received, the journey reaches its end.

How the Church reads it.

From early times Christians have seen the boat as an image of the Church. Tertullian, writing on Baptism, says that the disciples’ little boat, tossed by the waves while the Lord slept, was a figure of the Church (in substance). The Church does not promise calm seas, but the presence of the Lord who comes over the waters and says, “It is I”.

For study and teaching

  • Where in my parish or family is someone being overlooked, as the widows were? What could I do?
  • When has the Lord seemed absent while I was rowing in the dark? What helped me hear, “It is I”?
