Word for the day

“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” Matthew 10:39

Reflection

With today's reading the Book of Exodus begins, and the story changes. A family has become a people, and a people has become a problem for a new regime. The book opens with names: the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob, seventy persons in all. In a few lines a whole era passes: “Then Joseph died, and all his brothers, and that whole generation. But the Israelites were fruitful and prolific; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong, so that the land was filled with them.” God's blessing from the first pages of Genesis, “Be fruitful and multiply,” is being fulfilled in the foreign land. Then comes the sentence that turns the page: “Now a new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.” Joseph, who saved Egypt from famine, has been forgotten; gratitude is short-lived when power is at stake. The king speaks like a frightened man: “Look, the Israelite people are more numerous and more powerful than we. Come, let us deal shrewdly with them, or they will increase and, in the event of war, join our enemies and fight against us and escape from the land.” A minority has become a threat to national security, and the answer is to be “shrewd,” step by step. First forced labour: “they set taskmasters over them to oppress them with forced labor. They built supply cities, Pithom and Rameses, for Pharaoh.” Then harsher: “The Egyptians became ruthless in imposing tasks on the Israelites, and made their lives bitter with hard service in mortar and brick and in every kind of field labor.” The bitterness will be remembered every year at the Passover table. But the plan does not work. “The more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied and spread, so that the Egyptians came to dread the Israelites.” The blessing of God cannot be stopped by a decree. So the king turns to murder. The Hebrew midwives Shiphrah and Puah, whom the Lectionary passes over, “feared God” and let the boys live: the first act of conscientious refusal in the Bible, and Scripture records their names while the king's name is not given. Then Pharaoh orders all his people: “Every boy that is born to the Hebrews you shall throw into the Nile, but you shall let every girl live.” The river that gives Egypt its life becomes a place of death, and in tomorrow's reading a baby in a basket will be drawn out of it. Notice that it is one king and one policy, not a whole people: the next chapter shows an Egyptian princess saving the child. The Psalm is the song of those who came through such a time: “If it had not been the Lord who was on our side, when our enemies attacked us, then they would have swallowed us up alive.” “We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped. Our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth.”

The Gospel brings the mission discourse to its end with some of the hardest words of Jesus. “Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth; I have not come to bring peace, but a sword.” In the same discourse he has told his disciples to bring peace into every house (Matthew 10:13), and he is the Prince of Peace. The sword is not a weapon of war. It is the knife that cuts, the line that falls between those who accept him and those who refuse him; the words that follow are taken from the prophet Micah, who described how families break up in a time of trial (Micah 7:6). Jesus is not telling anyone to quarrel with their parents; elsewhere he defends the duty to honour father and mother (Matthew 15:4–6). He is saying that no loyalty, however sacred, may stand above him: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me.” In many families, in every age, the decision to follow Christ has cost a person the peace of the home, and Jesus does not hide it. “And whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” It is the first mention of the cross in the Gospel of Matthew, before Jesus speaks of his own passion. His hearers knew the Roman cross as an instrument of shame; he turns it into the sign of the disciple. Then comes the paradox at the heart of the faith: “Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” The midwives of Egypt lost their safety and found the lives of children. And then the tone changes, and the discourse ends with welcome. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” It is a chain of presence: the Father, the Son, the disciple, the host. A prophet or a righteous person welcomed in his name brings a reward, “and whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple ... none of these will lose their reward.” The smallest hospitality counts: in Kerala, the people who set up water-sheds for travellers in the hot months know what a cup of cold water is worth. “Now when Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples, he went on from there to teach and proclaim his message in their cities.” The instruction is done; the teacher goes on preaching. When, many years later, Herod will try to kill another child, the Holy Family will go down into Egypt for refuge (Matthew 2:13–15), and the land of Pharaoh will again shelter a Hebrew child.

Full homily

Dear friends, a new chapter begins today. A new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph (Exodus 1:8). Joseph had saved the country in the famine. But a new generation has forgotten him. How quickly we forget those who did us good. And the king is afraid. Look, he says, the Israelites are too many. They may join our enemies. So he deals shrewdly with them: forced labour, hard work in mortar and brick, and at last the order to throw the baby boys into the Nile. When we forget, we begin to fear; when we fear, we begin to oppress.

But the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied (Exodus 1:12). You cannot stop the blessing of God with a decree. And God has allies in unexpected places: two midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, who feared God and let the boys live. The Bible gives their names, and forgets the king's. Whenever you see the powerful oppressing the weak, remember the midwives. Small people who say no can change the course of history.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks plainly: I have not come to bring peace, but a sword (Matthew 10:34). He means: following me will cut through everything, even the dearest loyalties. Nobody and nothing may stand above God. And whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me. Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it (Matthew 10:39). The midwives lost their safety and found the lives of many children.

But the passage ends with welcome. Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me. And a cup of cold water. Such a small thing. Yet Jesus says: none of these will lose their reward. This week, let us do a small thing for someone nobody welcomes, a migrant, a worker, a stranger. And let us give thanks to those who have done us good, and whom we may have forgotten. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time after Joseph, there was a new king in Egypt, and he forgot everything good that Joseph had done. He was afraid of the Israelites, and he made them work as slaves. But two brave women, Shiphrah and Puah, would not do what the king said, because they loved God. God was pleased with them. Jesus tells his friends: whoever gives even a cup of cold water to someone in my name will get a reward from God. A smile, a glass of water, a hello to someone who is new: Jesus sees all of these!

Prayer

Lord God of the oppressed, you saw the misery of your people in Egypt and you did not let the whip or the river destroy them. Give us a good memory, so that we may never forget those who have served us. Give us fearless consciences, like the midwives who feared you more than the king. Lord Jesus, you did not come to bring a false peace; teach us to love you above every other love, and to take up the cross and follow you. Bless those who give a cup of cold water in your name, and those who receive it. Our help is in your name, Lord, who made heaven and earth. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

From today the first reading is the Book of Exodus. The family that Joseph saved is now a people, and “a new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.” Gratitude is forgotten, and a fear of the foreigner turns into slavery and the killing of newborn boys. In the Gospel Jesus closes the mission discourse of Matthew 10 with words that are hard to hear: he has not come to bring peace but a sword, and whoever loves father or mother more than him is not worthy of him. Yet the discourse ends with a promise: whoever welcomes the disciples welcomes Christ, and even a cup of cold water given to a disciple will not lose its reward. Psalm 124 holds the two readings together: “Our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth.”

Exodus 1:8–14, 22 · A new king who did not know Joseph

Setting.

The Book of Exodus takes its Greek name from the departure (exodos) of Israel from Egypt; in Hebrew it is called by its opening words, “These are the names”. It opens with a list of the sons of Jacob who came into Egypt (1:1–5), and a first note of fulfilment: “the Israelites were fruitful and prolific; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong” (1:7). The language recalls the blessing of the creation of humankind (Genesis 1:28) and the promise to Abraham.

Background. The king is not named. Many scholars place the oppression in the thirteenth century before Christ, under the Pharaohs of the Nineteenth Dynasty, since the store cities Pithom and Rameses are in the eastern Delta; the Bible gives no name, and the date is debated. The text keeps its focus on the theme: a power that forgets the good done to it and fears the people who live in its land. The reading skips the story of the midwives Shiphrah and Puah, who refuse to kill the boys (1:15–21).

Structure.

  • vv. 8–10: a new king who does not know Joseph fears the growth of the Israelites and proposes to “deal shrewdly” with them.
  • vv. 11–14: taskmasters, forced labour, store cities, bitter lives; yet the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied.
  • v. 22: Pharaoh commands that every Hebrew boy be thrown into the Nile.

Key words.

  • lōʾ-yādaʿ, did not know: In the Bible “to know” means more than to have heard of: to recognize, to acknowledge. The king did not acknowledge what Joseph had done, and so did not recognize the people’s place in his kingdom.
  • ʿăbōdâ, service, labour: The word for the forced labour in vv. 13–14 is the same word used later for the worship of God. The question of Exodus is: whom shall Israel serve? Pharaoh, or the Lord (Exodus 4:23)?
  • mārar, to make bitter: “They made their lives bitter” (v. 14). The memory is kept in the Passover meal, where bitter herbs are eaten as a sign of the bitterness of slavery.

Verse by verse.

“Now a new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.” (1:8)

Chapter 1 stands in contrast with the end of Genesis. There, Pharaoh honoured Joseph; here, the memory of the benefactor is lost. Gratitude can be forgotten within a generation.

“Come, let us deal shrewdly with them,” (1:10)

The new policy is presented as prudence. Fear is turned into a plan that treats a whole people as a danger. The Bible uses the account to show how oppression begins in fear and calculation.

“But the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied and spread,” (1:12)

The blessing of God cannot be stopped by oppression. It is the first sign in Exodus that the Lord is at work behind the scene.

“and made their lives bitter with hard service in mortar and brick and in every kind of field labor.” (1:14)

The picture of bricks and mortar is well known from Egyptian paintings and texts. For Israel it is the memory of the house of bondage.

“Every boy that is born to the Hebrews you shall throw into the Nile, but you shall let every girl live.” (1:22)

From forced labour the king moves to the killing of children. The Church’s memory of the slaughter of the innocents at Bethlehem (Matthew 2:16) hears an echo of this verse.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the Exodus as the great foundation of Israel’s faith, first of all the story of the Jewish people’s liberation, which Jews remember each year at Passover; Christians read it as the first stage of the history that leads to Christ. The Fathers saw in the slavery of Egypt a figure of the slavery of sin, and in the crossing of the sea a figure of Baptism. The Catechism recalls this in the liturgy of Baptism (CCC 1221). The slaughter of the boys, and the Child who is saved, prepare the reader for Moses, and for Jesus, who like him was saved from a king’s decree (Matthew 2:13–15). The Catechism also teaches that the seventh commandment forbids every enterprise that leads to the enslavement of human beings (CCC 2414). Today the Church prays for those who are held in forced labour or trafficked in many lands, and for those who do such wrong.

Matthew 10:34–11:1 · Not peace, but a sword

Setting.

These are the last verses of the mission discourse. After the warning of persecution (10:16–23) and the call not to fear (10:24–33), Jesus now speaks of the cost within the household. The verses alternate between warning and promise. The discourse closes in 11:1 with the formula Matthew uses at the end of each of the five great discourses: “when Jesus had finished…”.

Background. The sword is a figure of division, as the parallel in Luke makes clear (12:51: “No, I tell you, but rather division”). Jesus is not calling for violence; he forbids Peter to use the sword in the garden (26:52). The words of vv. 35–36 come from the prophet Micah (7:6), who spoke of the breakdown of trust in a time of trouble. Jesus says that the call to follow him will split even families, as it did in the early Church, where a convert could be rejected by his house.

Structure.

  • vv. 34–36: Jesus has come not to bring peace but a sword, to divide even members of one household.
  • vv. 37–39: whoever loves father, mother, son or daughter more than Jesus is not worthy of him; take up the cross; lose your life to find it.
  • vv. 40–42: whoever welcomes the disciples welcomes Jesus and the Father who sent him; the reward of the prophet, the righteous and the cup of cold water.
  • 11:1: when Jesus had finished instructing the Twelve, he went on to teach in their cities.

Key words.

  • machaira, sword: The short sword of the soldier or the knife of the sacrificer. As used here, it is a figure for division and decision, not for war.
  • stauros, cross: This is the first mention of the cross in Matthew, long before the Passion. For his hearers it was the Roman punishment of the condemned, who carried the beam to the place of execution. The disciple is asked to follow the Master on the same road.
  • psychē, life, self: The word means life, soul and self. To “find” one’s life by clinging to it is to lose it; to give it up for Christ is to receive it back from God.

Verse by verse.

“Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth; I have not come to bring peace, but a sword.” (10:34)

The Christ whom the angels announced as bringing peace on earth (Luke 2:14) says that peace which avoids the truth is not what he brings. Following him divides, because it demands a choice.

“and one’s foes will be members of one’s own household.” (10:36)

Taken from Micah 7:6. For first-century disciples, faith in Jesus could cost them the favour of their families. The verse describes a pain that the Church has seen in every age.

“Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me;” (10:37)

Not a denial of family love, which God commands (Matthew 15:4–6), but the order of love: Christ first, and every other love in him.

“and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” (10:38)

The cross is not a general name for any trouble; it is the path of following Jesus, even to the point of giving up one’s life.

“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” (10:39)

A saying found in every Gospel. Life is a gift that is kept by being given, and lost by being clutched.

“Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” (10:40)

The chain of sending: Father, Son, apostles, and those who receive them. To welcome a messenger of Christ is to welcome God.

“whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple” (10:42)

The smallest act of kindness, given for Christ’s sake, has its reward. In Palestine, a cup of cold water was a precious courtesy in the heat.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of the fourth commandment, teaches that the bonds of family, though important, are not absolute: the call of Christ takes precedence, as Jesus says in v. 37 (CCC 2232). It teaches too that the way of perfection passes by way of the Cross, and that there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle (CCC 2015). The saints are those who took these words literally, and found that the love of Christ makes love of family deeper, not smaller. The closing verses on welcome underlie the Church’s works of mercy, and the promise that a cup of water will not lose its reward is the “small way” of every Christian. The Church also prays for families divided over faith, that the peace of Christ may reach them.

Questions people ask

How can Jesus say he came to bring a sword when he is the Prince of Peace?

He speaks of the division that his call causes, not of violence. The peace he gives is real, but it is a peace that does not come by ignoring the truth or the demands of discipleship. Matthew puts these words beside Jesus’ refusal of the sword in Gethsemane (26:52). The Church does not read this verse as a justification for violence in the name of Christ, and she reminds us that the first to bear the cost of discipleship are the disciples themselves.

For study and teaching

  • Where does following Jesus ask me to put him before something or someone I love, and how can this make my love of them deeper?
  • Who is “one of these little ones” to whom I can give a cup of cold water today, in the name of Christ?
