Word for the day

“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” Matthew 10:39

Reflection

With today's reading the Book of Exodus begins, and the story changes. A family has become a people, and a people has become a problem for a new regime. The book opens with names: the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob, seventy persons in all. In a few lines a whole era passes: “Then Joseph died, and all his brothers, and that whole generation. But the Israelites were fruitful and prolific; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong, so that the land was filled with them.” God's blessing from the first pages of Genesis, “Be fruitful and multiply,” is being fulfilled in the foreign land. Then comes the sentence that turns the page: “Now a new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.” Joseph, who saved Egypt from famine, has been forgotten; gratitude is short-lived when power is at stake. The king speaks like a frightened man: “Look, the Israelite people are more numerous and more powerful than we. Come, let us deal shrewdly with them, or they will increase and, in the event of war, join our enemies and fight against us and escape from the land.” A minority has become a threat to national security, and the answer is to be “shrewd,” step by step. First forced labour: “they set taskmasters over them to oppress them with forced labor. They built supply cities, Pithom and Rameses, for Pharaoh.” Then harsher: “The Egyptians became ruthless in imposing tasks on the Israelites, and made their lives bitter with hard service in mortar and brick and in every kind of field labor.” The bitterness will be remembered every year at the Passover table. But the plan does not work. “The more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied and spread, so that the Egyptians came to dread the Israelites.” The blessing of God cannot be stopped by a decree. So the king turns to murder. The Hebrew midwives Shiphrah and Puah, whom the Lectionary passes over, “feared God” and let the boys live: the first act of conscientious refusal in the Bible, and Scripture records their names while the king's name is not given. Then Pharaoh orders all his people: “Every boy that is born to the Hebrews you shall throw into the Nile, but you shall let every girl live.” The river that gives Egypt its life becomes a place of death, and in tomorrow's reading a baby in a basket will be drawn out of it. Notice that it is one king and one policy, not a whole people: the next chapter shows an Egyptian princess saving the child. The Psalm is the song of those who came through such a time: “If it had not been the Lord who was on our side, when our enemies attacked us, then they would have swallowed us up alive.” “We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped. Our help is in the name of the Lord, who made heaven and earth.”

The Gospel brings the mission discourse to its end with some of the hardest words of Jesus. “Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth; I have not come to bring peace, but a sword.” In the same discourse he has told his disciples to bring peace into every house (Matthew 10:13), and he is the Prince of Peace. The sword is not a weapon of war. It is the knife that cuts, the line that falls between those who accept him and those who refuse him; the words that follow are taken from the prophet Micah, who described how families break up in a time of trial (Micah 7:6). Jesus is not telling anyone to quarrel with their parents; elsewhere he defends the duty to honour father and mother (Matthew 15:4–6). He is saying that no loyalty, however sacred, may stand above him: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me.” In many families, in every age, the decision to follow Christ has cost a person the peace of the home, and Jesus does not hide it. “And whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” It is the first mention of the cross in the Gospel of Matthew, before Jesus speaks of his own passion. His hearers knew the Roman cross as an instrument of shame; he turns it into the sign of the disciple. Then comes the paradox at the heart of the faith: “Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” The midwives of Egypt lost their safety and found the lives of children. And then the tone changes, and the discourse ends with welcome. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” It is a chain of presence: the Father, the Son, the disciple, the host. A prophet or a righteous person welcomed in his name brings a reward, “and whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple ... none of these will lose their reward.” The smallest hospitality counts: in Kerala, the people who set up water-sheds for travellers in the hot months know what a cup of cold water is worth. “Now when Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples, he went on from there to teach and proclaim his message in their cities.” The instruction is done; the teacher goes on preaching. When, many years later, Herod will try to kill another child, the Holy Family will go down into Egypt for refuge (Matthew 2:13–15), and the land of Pharaoh will again shelter a Hebrew child.

Full homily

Dear friends, a new chapter begins today. A new king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph (Exodus 1:8). Joseph had saved the country in the famine. But a new generation has forgotten him. How quickly we forget those who did us good. And the king is afraid. Look, he says, the Israelites are too many. They may join our enemies. So he deals shrewdly with them: forced labour, hard work in mortar and brick, and at last the order to throw the baby boys into the Nile. When we forget, we begin to fear; when we fear, we begin to oppress.

But the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied (Exodus 1:12). You cannot stop the blessing of God with a decree. And God has allies in unexpected places: two midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, who feared God and let the boys live. The Bible gives their names, and forgets the king's. Whenever you see the powerful oppressing the weak, remember the midwives. Small people who say no can change the course of history.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks plainly: I have not come to bring peace, but a sword (Matthew 10:34). He means: following me will cut through everything, even the dearest loyalties. Nobody and nothing may stand above God. And whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me. Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it (Matthew 10:39). The midwives lost their safety and found the lives of many children.

But the passage ends with welcome. Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me. And a cup of cold water. Such a small thing. Yet Jesus says: none of these will lose their reward. This week, let us do a small thing for someone nobody welcomes, a migrant, a worker, a stranger. And let us give thanks to those who have done us good, and whom we may have forgotten. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time after Joseph, there was a new king in Egypt, and he forgot everything good that Joseph had done. He was afraid of the Israelites, and he made them work as slaves. But two brave women, Shiphrah and Puah, would not do what the king said, because they loved God. God was pleased with them. Jesus tells his friends: whoever gives even a cup of cold water to someone in my name will get a reward from God. A smile, a glass of water, a hello to someone who is new: Jesus sees all of these!

Prayer

Lord God of the oppressed, you saw the misery of your people in Egypt and you did not let the whip or the river destroy them. Give us a good memory, so that we may never forget those who have served us. Give us fearless consciences, like the midwives who feared you more than the king. Lord Jesus, you did not come to bring a false peace; teach us to love you above every other love, and to take up the cross and follow you. Bless those who give a cup of cold water in your name, and those who receive it. Our help is in your name, Lord, who made heaven and earth. Amen.
