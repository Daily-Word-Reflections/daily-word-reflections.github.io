Word for the day

“Yet even now, says the LORD, return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; rend your hearts and not your clothing.” Joel 2:12–13

Reflection

Lent opens with a trumpet. A locust plague had stripped the land of Judah bare, and the prophet Joel saw in it a call to conversion: “Yet even now, says the LORD, return to me with all your heart… rend your hearts and not your clothing.” The tearing of garments was the old sign of grief; God asks for something deeper, a heart torn open to him. And the reason is not fear but his own character: “for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love.” Joel calls everyone, the elders and the infants, the bride and the bridegroom, the priests weeping “between the vestibule and the altar”. Lent is not private self-improvement; it is the whole Church returning together.

St Paul turns the call into a plea: “we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” The ground of our reconciliation is astonishing: “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” The sinless Son took our place, so that we might take his. And there is urgency: “See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” Not someday. Now. The ashes we receive are not a mark of gloom but of honesty: we are dust, and to dust we shall return, yet the dust that God loves can be raised to glory.

In the Gospel Jesus names the three great works of Lent: almsgiving, prayer and fasting. He does not abolish them; he purifies them. “Beware of practicing your piety before others in order to be seen by them.” Give alms so quietly that your left hand does not know what your right hand is doing. Pray in your room with the door shut. Fast with your face washed and your head anointed. Three times we hear the promise: “your Father who sees in secret will reward you.” The paradox of today is plain: we go out with a cross of ash on our foreheads, yet Jesus warns us against showing off. The ash is not to be admired; it is a confession that we are sinners in need of mercy, and an invitation to a hidden life of love that only the Father sees.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Lent begins with a trumpet. The prophet Joel cries out: yet even now, says the LORD, return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; rend your hearts and not your clothing (Joel 2:12–13).

Why return? Not because God is harsh, but because he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love (Joel 2:13). And Joel calls everyone: elders and children, the newly married and the priests. Lent is the whole Church coming home together.

Paul pleads with us: we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God (2 Corinthians 5:20). He who knew no sin was made sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God. And he adds: see, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation! Not next year. Today.

Jesus gives us three works: almsgiving, prayer and fasting. But he warns: do not do them to be seen. Give so quietly that your left hand does not know what your right hand is doing; pray in your room with the door shut; fast with a washed face. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you (Matthew 6:4, 6, 18).

In a moment we will receive ashes. The priest will say: repent, and believe in the Gospel, or: remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return. The ashes are not a decoration. They are an honest confession: we are sinners, we are mortal, and we need God's mercy.

Yet dust in God's hands is never just dust. From dust he formed Adam; from the dust of the tomb he will raise us. Let us begin these forty days with torn-open hearts, hidden love and great hope. Amen.

For children

Children, today we get a cross of ashes on our foreheads. It reminds us that we need Jesus and that we want to love him more.

For Lent, choose one way to pray more, one thing to give up, and one way to help someone, and do it quietly for Jesus.

Prayer

Grant, O Lord, that we may begin with holy fasting this campaign of Christian service, so that, as we take up battle against spiritual evils, we may be armed with weapons of self-restraint. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Father of mercy, we are dust, yet you love us. As we receive these ashes, tear open our hearts, teach us to pray in secret, to fast with joy and to give without counting, and bring us to Easter made new. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Lent opens with a summons and a warning. Joel calls the whole people to fast and weep, and says that God wants the heart torn open, not the clothes. Psalm 51 gives us the words for it. Paul begs the Corinthians, “be reconciled to God”, and says that the time for this is now. Then Jesus, in the Gospel, sends us into our rooms: give, pray and fast where only the Father sees.

The readings seem to pull in two directions. Joel says, “Blow the trumpet”; Jesus says, “do not sound a trumpet”. Joel gathers everyone in public; Jesus says, “shut the door”. Both are true. The Church repents together and aloud, and each person must repent alone and in earnest. On a day when we go up in public to receive ashes, the Gospel asks what is going on underneath. The key words are return, now and in secret.

Joel 2:12–18 · Rend your hearts

Setting.

We know little about the prophet Joel. His book gives no king and no date. Since it speaks of the temple, priests and elders but of no king, many scholars place it after the return from exile, though this is not certain. A terrible plague of locusts has stripped the land (chapter 1), and there is no grain or wine left to offer in the temple. Joel sees in this disaster a sign of “the day of the Lord” (2:1) and a call to conversion.

The reading begins with the word “even now”. Even at this late hour, when everything seems lost, the door is open.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: God's own call: return with all your heart; rend your hearts; he is merciful.
  • v. 14: “Who knows?”: hope, without presuming.
  • vv. 15–16: the whole community is called together, from infants to the newly married.
  • v. 17: the priests' prayer between the porch and the altar.
  • v. 18: the turning point: the Lord has pity.

Key words.

  • šûb, to return, to turn back: The Old Testament's great word for conversion. It is a word of movement. You have been walking away, and now you turn around and walk home. The same root is used when v. 14 says that God may “turn”.
  • qir‘û, rend, tear: Tearing one's clothes was a public sign of grief (Genesis 37:34). Joel keeps the verb and changes the object. He does not forbid the outward sign, since he himself calls for a public fast; he says that the sign must be true.
  • ḥannûn wĕraḥûm, gracious and merciful: This echoes God's words about himself to Moses after the golden calf (Exodus 34:6). Raḥûm is related to the Hebrew word for the womb. This is the reason for returning: not what we have done but who God is.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love: Love that stays faithful to a covenant, even when the other side has failed. The word comes back in today's psalm.

Verse by verse.

“Yet even now, says the Lord, return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning;” (2:12)

“With all your heart” does not make the fasting unnecessary. Joel keeps both. The body takes part in the turning of the heart.

“rend your hearts and not your clothing.” (2:13)

This is the most quoted line in Joel, and it is the Church's opening word for Lent.

“Who knows whether he will not turn and relent,” (2:14)

A beautifully modest sentence. Joel does not bargain and does not claim to control God's answer. The king of Nineveh says the same “Who knows?” in Jonah 3:9. Repentance is a hope, and not a transaction.

“Let the bridegroom leave his room, and the bride her canopy.” (2:16)

Even the newly married, who would normally be excused from duty, must come. No one is too young, too old or too happy for this.

“Between the vestibule and the altar let the priests, the ministers of the Lord, weep.” (2:17)

The priests stand in the place where they usually offer sacrifice and have nothing to offer, because of the locusts. They offer tears. Their appeal rests on God's own honour: why should the nations say, “Where is their God?”

“Then the Lord became jealous for his land, and had pity on his people.” (2:18)

Here the whole book turns. God's “jealousy” is his burning love for what is his: he cannot bear to see his land in this state.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus' call to conversion, like that of the prophets, “does not aim first at outward works”, “sackcloth and ashes”, but at conversion of the heart; without it such penances remain sterile and false. Yet interior conversion urges expression in visible signs (CCC 1430, citing Joel 2:12–13 and today's Gospel). Interior penance is a radical turning of the whole life (CCC 1431), and it is God who gives the new heart (CCC 1432). So the Church places this reading at the door of Lent every year: the ashes on the head are worth nothing without the tear in the heart, and the tear in the heart looks for a sign.

Psalm 51:1–2, 3–4a, 10–11, 12 and 15 · Create in me a clean heart

Setting.

The Psalm's Hebrew title connects it with David after the prophet Nathan confronted him about Bathsheba and Uriah (2 Samuel 11–12). Many scholars think its present form comes from a later time, and its last verses suggest the exile; still, the title has taught generations of believers to pray it as the prayer of a great sinner who was forgiven. It is called the Miserere after its first word in Latin. The Church prays it at Morning Prayer on Fridays and has used it as her chief penitential psalm.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: a cry for mercy, with three words for sin and three for forgiveness.
  • vv. 3–4: confession without excuses.
  • vv. 10–12: the prayer for a new creation.
  • v. 15: lips opened in praise.

Key words.

  • ḥānan, ḥesed, raḥămîm, have mercy, steadfast love, abundant mercy: Three words for God's love in the first verse. The psalmist starts with God's mercy rather than with his own sin.
  • bārā’, to create: The verb of Genesis 1, used only of God. A clean heart cannot be repaired by effort. It has to be created, as the world was.
  • rûaḥ, spirit: Three times in vv. 10–12: a new and right spirit, your holy spirit, a willing spirit. Verse 11 is one of the very few places in the Old Testament to say “holy spirit”. Christians read it in the light of Pentecost.

Verse by verse.

“Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love;” (51:1)

The whole appeal rests on God's character, and the psalmist offers no merits.

“Against you, you alone, have I sinned, and done what is evil in your sight,” (51:4)

He has also wronged other people, and very seriously, if the title is to be followed. But he has seen what lies under every sin: it wounds love of God.

“Create in me a clean heart, O God, and put a new and right spirit within me.” (51:10)

Compare Joel: there the heart must be rent, here it must be created new. God asks for what only he can give.

“O Lord, open my lips, and my mouth will declare your praise.” (51:15)

The Church opens the Liturgy of the Hours with this verse each day. A forgiven sinner begins to sing.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this psalm when it defines sin as an offence against God (CCC 431, 1850). It takes up “a broken and contrite heart” as the true spirit of worship and of conversion, which is itself the work of grace (CCC 1428, 2100). It is the natural prayer of the sacrament of Reconciliation and a fitting psalm for receiving ashes.

2 Corinthians 5:20–6:2 · Now is the acceptable time

Setting.

Paul's relations with the Christians of Corinth had been painful. In this letter he defends his ministry, and in chapter 5 he explains what it is: God has reconciled the world to himself in Christ and has given “the ministry of reconciliation” (5:18). The reading picks up at the moment when the explanation becomes a direct appeal.

Structure.

  • 5:20: we are ambassadors; God appeals through us: be reconciled.
  • 5:21: the ground for the appeal: what God did in Christ.
  • 6:1: do not receive grace in vain.
  • 6:2: Isaiah 49:8: and the “now” of salvation.

Key words.

  • presbeuomen, we are ambassadors: An envoy does not speak for himself. He carries the words of the one who sent him. So the Apostle says that it is God himself who is pleading here.
  • katallagēte, be reconciled: The verb comes from the language of ordinary relationships, for making enemies into friends again. Note who takes the first step. God is the offended party, and God is the one who pleads.
  • kairos, time, the right moment: Not time on the clock but a moment that has come and must be seized. Paul follows Isaiah's word with his own “now”, twice.

Verse by verse.

“So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us; we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” (5:20)

Paul is writing to people who are already baptized. Reconciliation is not done once and forgotten. This is why it is read to the Church on Ash Wednesday.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (5:21)

A daring verse. Christ did not become a sinner and did not sin. In the Old Testament the Hebrew word for sin can also mean a sin offering, and many Fathers and commentators hear this here. The Catechism says that Jesus “did not experience reprobation as if he himself had sinned”, but in love he stood with sinners in their distance from God (CCC 603). An exchange is taking place: he takes our place, and we are given his.

“As we work together with him, we urge you also not to accept the grace of God in vain.” (6:1)

Grace can be received and left unused, like a letter never opened.

“See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” (6:2)

Isaiah's “day of salvation” was a promise for a future moment. Paul says that it has arrived: it is the time in which he and his hearers are living. The liturgy says the same today.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes “be reconciled to God” when it names this sacrament the sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 1424), and teaches that the bishops and priests carry on this “ministry of reconciliation” (CCC 1442). On 5:21 it teaches that Christ, sinless, took on the state of our distance from God in love (CCC 602–603). So this reading, at the start of Lent, is a direct invitation to confession. The Church's law also asks every Catholic to confess serious sins at least once a year (CCC 1457).

Matthew 6:1–6, 16–18 · Your Father who sees in secret

Setting.

This comes from the Sermon on the Mount, and it follows Jesus' teaching on a “righteousness” greater than that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20). He takes the three works which were at the centre of the piety of his people (see Tobit 12:8) and assumes that his disciples will do all three: “whenever you give”, “whenever you pray”, “whenever you fast”. His question is about the motive. The Lectionary leaves out vv. 7–15, which contain the Lord's Prayer, and so keeps the three-part pattern clear.

“The hypocrites” here are not a way of speaking about the Jewish people. Jesus is a Jew teaching Jewish practice, and his warning is aimed at a danger in all religion. Christians have needed it as much as anyone.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the general rule: beware of doing good in order to be seen.
  • vv. 2–4: almsgiving.
  • vv. 5–6: prayer.
  • vv. 16–18: fasting.
  • ¶ Each part has the same shape: when you do this, do not be like the hypocrites; “they have received their reward”; but you, do it in secret, “and your Father who sees in secret will reward you”.

Key words.

  • dikaiosynē, righteousness, piety: The NRSV says “piety” in v. 1, but the Greek word is the great word of the Sermon on the Mount: a life in right relation with God.
  • apechousin, they have received: A term from ordinary business, used on receipts for “paid in full”. The one who wants applause gets it, and the account is closed.
  • en tō kryptō, in secret: The Father “is in secret” and “sees in secret”. The secret room is not a hiding place from others so much as the place where he is. This is the phrase to carry through Lent.
  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: An actor. A person who performs for an audience.

Verse by verse.

““Beware of practicing your piety before others in order to be seen by them;” (6:1)

Jesus has just told his disciples, “let your light shine before others” (5:16). The two sayings do not conflict. Good deeds will be seen, and the question is who gets the glory: there “your Father”, here oneself.

““So whenever you give alms, do not sound a trumpet before you,” (6:2)

There is no clear evidence of any such trumpet. It seems to be a vivid figure of speech: he means blowing your own trumpet.

“But when you give alms, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing,” (6:3)

So secret that you do not even congratulate yourself.

“But whenever you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret;” (6:6)

The “room” (tameion) was the inner storeroom, in a house without many private places. This does not cancel public worship, for Jesus himself went to the synagogue and taught us to pray “Our Father”. It teaches that public prayer must grow from a hidden relationship.

“But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face,” (6:17)

Oil and washing were signs of ordinary, even festive days. Fasting should make no one else's life gloomier. Some later manuscripts add at the end of these sayings that the Father will reward you “openly”, but the older ones do not have the word, and modern Bibles leave it out.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names fasting, prayer and almsgiving as the three forms of interior penance, expressing conversion in relation to oneself, to God and to others (CCC 1434), and it says that the New Law directs these practices to the “Father who sees in secret”, in contrast with the desire to “be seen by men” (CCC 1969). St Peter Chrysologus preached that prayer, mercy and fasting are one and give life to each other: fasting is the soul of prayer, and mercy is the lifeblood of fasting (in substance). So these are not three separate tasks: what we hold back by fasting goes to the poor, and all of it goes up in prayer.

Questions people ask

If Jesus says to wash our faces when fasting, why do we walk around with ashes on our foreheads?

The Gospel is asking about motive, not forbidding every visible sign. Ashes do not announce “see how holy I am”. They say the opposite: “I am a sinner, and I am dust.” They are a public confession together with the whole Church, as Joel commands. If someone wears them with pride, this Gospel judges them. If someone is embarrassed to wear them, they may be a small act of witness.

Is Ash Wednesday a holy day of obligation?

No, it is not a holy day of obligation, though it is a day of fasting and abstinence (Code of Canon Law 1251), and many Catholics who cannot attend Mass come for the ashes. Ashes are a sacramental, not a sacrament (CCC 1667–1670). In general, abstinence binds from age 14, and fasting from age 18 to 59 (Canon 1252); those who are sick or otherwise unable are excused, and the local bishops' norms should be followed.

Doesn't “reward” make religion selfish?

The reward in this passage is not a payment for points earned. Jesus sets one reward against another: the applause of others or the Father himself. The child's joy is in being seen by the Father. As the Catechism says, our merit before God is itself a gift of his grace (CCC 2006–2008).

For the Latin reader

In the Roman Rite, Lent begins today and runs until the Mass of the Lord's Supper on Holy Thursday. The ashes come from palms blessed in a previous year, and they are blessed and given after the homily, with one of two formulas: “Repent, and believe in the Gospel” (Mark 1:15) or “Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return” (Genesis 3:19). From this day the Alleluia is not sung until the Easter Vigil. The Second Vatican Council describes Lent as having a double character, baptismal and penitential, and asks that penance be not only interior and individual but also external and social (Sacrosanctum Concilium 109–110). In the first centuries public sinners did penance in sackcloth and ashes; by around the eleventh century the custom had spread to all the faithful, who all stand in need of the same mercy. Choose your prayer, fast and alms today, and keep them small enough to be real.

For study and teaching

  • Joel calls for a torn heart and a public fast. Which comes more easily to you, the outward sign or the inner change? What would it take to join them?
  • Paul says “now”. What are you putting off with God until later?
  • Who would be surprised to learn what you give, or how you pray? Who is to know of your Lenten sacrifice, and why?
  • What will you do this Lent for each of the three: for God (prayer), for yourself (fasting), and for your neighbour (almsgiving)?
