Word for the day

“Then he touched their eyes and said, “According to your faith let it be done to you.”” Matthew 9:29

Reflection

Isaiah describes the transformation God will bring: Lebanon becomes a fruitful field, “the deaf shall hear the words of a scroll, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see”, the meek find joy, the tyrant disappears. Advent is the season of this hope.

Two blind men follow Jesus crying, “Have mercy on us, Son of David!” They call him by his messianic title, though they cannot see him. In the house Jesus asks them one question: “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” “Yes, Lord.” He touches their eyes: “According to your faith let it be done to you.” Their eyes are opened. Faith sees before the eyes do. St John Damascene, remembered today, defended the veneration of icons: in Christ the invisible God has become visible, and we may look on his face.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this First Friday of December Isaiah promises a reversal: in a very little while Lebanon shall be turned into a fruitful field. On that day the deaf shall hear the words of a scroll, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see (Isaiah 29:17–18). The meek shall obtain fresh joy in the Lord.

In the Gospel two blind men follow Jesus, crying out: have mercy on us, Son of David! It is a cry of faith; they call him by his messianic title. Jesus asks them: do you believe that I am able to do this? They say: yes, Lord. He touches their eyes and says: according to your faith let it be done to you (Matthew 9:27–29).

Jesus asks the same question of each of us this Advent: do you believe that I am able to do this? Able to heal your blindness, to change your heart, to restore your family, to forgive your sin?

There is a blindness worse than physical blindness: not seeing God's love, not seeing the needs of others, not seeing our own sins. The two blind men knew they were blind. Many of us do not.

On the First Friday we honour the Sacred Heart of Jesus. From that Heart flows the mercy the blind men begged for. The simplest prayer of the Church, Lord, have mercy, is their prayer.

Today, let us make their prayer ours. Let us name one blindness in our life and ask Jesus to touch it. If possible, let us go to confession and receive Holy Communion in honour of the Sacred Heart. And let us answer his question with faith: yes, Lord, I believe. Amen.

For children

Children, two blind men asked Jesus for help and He opened their eyes. When you pray, believe that Jesus can help you!

Prayer

Son of David, have mercy on me. Touch the eyes of my heart, increase my faith, and be my light and my salvation this Advent. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Isaiah promises a day when the deaf will hear and the blind will see. In Matthew two blind men follow Jesus through the street, calling him Son of David, and he asks them one question: “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” The promise of the first reading meets a person in the second, and what it asks of the blind men is faith.

Isaiah 29:17–24 · The eyes of the blind shall see

Setting.

Earlier in this chapter Isaiah has been severe. He told Jerusalem's leaders that they were blinded and that the prophetic word was to them like a sealed scroll (29:9–12), and that they honoured God with their lips while their hearts were far from him (29:13). Today's verses answer that darkness with a promise of reversal.

Structure.

  • v. 17: the land transformed: Lebanon and the fruitful field.
  • vv. 18–19: the deaf hear, the blind see, the poor rejoice.
  • vv. 20–21: the tyrant and the corrupt courts removed.
  • vv. 22–24: Jacob no longer ashamed; a people who sanctify God's name and learn.

Key words.

  • ʿănāwîm, the meek: The poor and lowly who have no one to rely on but God. The word stands behind “Blessed are the meek” (Matthew 5:5).
  • qādôš, holy: Isaiah's favourite title for God is “the Holy One of Israel”. Holiness is the reason for joy here, and it comes down to the poor.
  • pādâ, to redeem: To buy back or set at liberty. “The Lord, who redeemed Abraham” (v. 22) goes back to the very beginning of God's choosing.

Verse by verse.

“On that day the deaf shall hear the words of a scroll, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see.” (29:18)

The “scroll” picks up the sealed book of v. 11, which no one could read. Now even the deaf hear it. Isaiah is speaking of both the body and the understanding.

“The meek shall obtain fresh joy in the Lord, and the neediest people shall exult in the Holy One of Israel.” (29:19)

Joy begins at the bottom of society. In the next verses the people who cheated them in the courts disappear.

“And those who err in spirit will come to understanding, and those who grumble will accept instruction.” (29:24)

The end of the reading is a quiet miracle: minds and hearts healed. Opening the eyes is the easier part.

How the Church reads it.

When John the Baptist's messengers ask who he is, Jesus answers with Isaiah's signs: “the blind receive their sight” (Matthew 11:5). The Church reads these promises as fulfilled in him. His healings are messianic signs (CCC 549), and he opens the eyes of faith as well as the eyes of the body. Baptism came to be called “enlightenment” in the early Church (CCC 1216).

Matthew 9:27–31 · According to your faith

Setting.

Matthew 8–9 gathers ten deeds of power after the Sermon on the Mount. This story comes near the end of the series, just after the raising of the ruler's daughter. Soon Jesus will reply to John: “the blind receive their sight” (11:5). Matthew has a similar story later, again of two blind men, near Jericho (20:29–34).

Structure.

  • v. 27: the cry in the street.
  • v. 28: the question inside the house.
  • vv. 29–30a: the touch and the healing.
  • vv. 30b–31: the stern order, and the disobedience.

Key words.

  • eleēson, have mercy: The same cry as Kyrie eleison at Mass. It asks for God's covenant kindness, and it comes from people with nothing to offer.
  • huios Dauid, Son of David: Matthew's Gospel opens with this title (1:1), but here for the first time in the Gospel someone calls Jesus by it to his face. Blind men see what the learned do not.
  • pistis, faith: Here it is trust in a person: “Do you believe that I am able?” The reply “Yes, Lord” is a whole creed in two words.

Verse by verse.

“As Jesus went on from there, two blind men followed him, crying loudly, “Have mercy on us, Son of David!”” (9:27)

How do blind men follow? By the sound of his voice and the crowd. That in itself is a picture of faith.

“When he entered the house, the blind men came to him; and Jesus said to them, “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” They said to him, “Yes, Lord.”” (9:28)

Jesus does not stop in the street. He lets them come further, into the house, and he draws their trust into words.

“Then he touched their eyes and said, “According to your faith let it be done to you.”” (9:29)

This does not mean that faith itself is the power. The power is his touch; faith is the open door.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that this cry of the blind men, “Have mercy on us, Son of David”, is renewed in the traditional prayer known as the Jesus Prayer, and that Jesus hears the prayer of faith (CCC 2616). The signs of Jesus attest that the Father has sent him, and they invite belief in him (CCC 548). In an Advent liturgy, the cry “Lord, have mercy” at the start of Mass stands in the same line.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus tell them to keep silent, when he has healed them?

Matthew's phrase is strong: he “sternly ordered them” (9:30). “Son of David” could be heard as a political title, and Jesus would not be proclaimed in that sense. The Catechism says he accepted the title of Messiah with some reserve for this reason (CCC 439). His kingship would be shown fully only on the Cross. The two men's joy is easy to understand, but their disobedience is still disobedience.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus asks, “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” What is the “this” in my life just now, and how would I answer him?
  • Isaiah says those who grumble will accept instruction. Where am I grumbling, and what might God be trying to teach me?
