Word for the day

“They were astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.” Mark 1:22

Reflection

Moses, near the end of his life, gives Israel a promise: “The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your own people; you shall heed such a prophet.” At Sinai the people had been terrified of hearing God's voice directly, so God says: “I will put my words in the mouth of the prophet, who shall speak to them everything that I command.” Israel waited for centuries for this prophet like Moses. The Gospel shows him walking into the synagogue at Capernaum.

On the Sabbath Jesus teaches, and the people are “astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.” The scribes quoted others; Jesus speaks with his own voice: “But I say to you.” Then a man with an unclean spirit cries out: “What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are, the Holy One of God.” Jesus rebukes him: “Be silent, and come out of him!” and the spirit leaves. The people ask: “What is this? A new teaching—with authority! He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him.” His word does what it says.

The Psalm gives the response: “O that today you would listen to his voice! Do not harden your hearts.” Paul encourages the Corinthians to “unhindered devotion to the Lord”, praising those who give themselves wholly to God in celibacy, while honouring marriage as a true calling. Whatever our state of life, the question is the same: do we let the authoritative word of Jesus free us from what binds us? Today we would have honoured St John Bosco, who freed countless young people from the street through the word, education and love of Christ.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Moses promised Israel: the LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your own people; you shall heed such a prophet (Deuteronomy 18:15). For centuries Israel waited.

In today's Gospel that prophet walks into the synagogue at Capernaum. The people were astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes (Mark 1:22). The scribes quoted others; Jesus speaks as God's own Word.

Then a man with an unclean spirit cries out: I know who you are, the Holy One of God. Jesus says: be silent, and come out of him! And the spirit leaves (Mark 1:24–26). His word does what it says.

The people ask: what is this? A new teaching, with authority! (Mark 1:27). That same word is spoken to us at every Mass. It can free us from fear, from sin, from addiction, from all that binds.

The Psalm says: O that today you would listen to his voice! Do not harden your hearts (Psalm 95:7–8). Paul speaks of unhindered devotion to the Lord. Married, single or consecrated, we are all called to give our hearts to him.

Today we would have honoured St John Bosco, who freed many young people by the word and love of Christ. Let us listen today, and let Jesus set us free. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's question is whose voice to listen to. Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet like him, who will speak God's own words. The psalm pleads, “O that today you would listen to his voice!” In the synagogue at Capernaum people hear that voice and are astounded, because Jesus teaches “as one having authority”, and even an unclean spirit has to obey him. The line runs from a promise, through a warning, to its fulfilment. The second reading goes its own way, as it often does on the Sundays of Ordinary Time, when the Lectionary reads St Paul in order rather than choosing a text to match. Yet it also asks about attention, about an undivided heart. The key words are prophet, today, undivided and authority.

Deuteronomy 18:15–20 · A prophet like Moses

Setting.

Deuteronomy presents itself as the farewell addresses of Moses on the plains of Moab, on the edge of the promised land. Israel will soon live among peoples who seek the future through diviners, soothsayers and mediums, and verses 9–14 forbid all of this. How then will Israel know God's will when Moses is gone? This passage is the answer: God himself will provide a prophet.

“Horeb” is Deuteronomy's name for Sinai. Moses recalls the terror of that day, when the people begged not to hear God's voice directly (Exodus 20:19; Deuteronomy 5:23–27). God approved that request, and so began the office of a mediator of his word.

Structure.

  • v. 15: the promise: a prophet like Moses, “you shall heed such a prophet”.
  • v. 16: the origin of the promise: the people's fear at Horeb.
  • vv. 17–18: God's reply: “I will put my words in the mouth of the prophet”.
  • v. 19: the duty to listen.
  • v. 20: the danger of a false prophet.
  • ¶ The verses that follow (vv. 21–22) give a test for telling true prophets from false.

Key words.

  • nābî’, prophet: A Hebrew word usually explained as “one called” or “one who calls out”. A prophet is not first a foreteller, but one who speaks for God.
  • kāmōnî, like me: Moses is the measure. The end of the book says plainly that no one like him had yet arisen: “Never since has there arisen a prophet in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face” (34:10).
  • yāqîm, will raise up: God takes the initiative; a prophet does not appoint himself. In Greek the verb is anistēmi, the same verb the New Testament uses for raising from the dead, and Peter seems to play on it in Acts 3:22, 26.

Verse by verse.

“The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your own people; you shall heed such a prophet.” (18:15)

“From among your own people”: not a foreign seer, but one of their kin. In its first setting the promise may cover the whole line of true prophets whom God would send to Israel. By the time of Jesus some looked for one particular prophet: the Qumran text 4QTestimonia cites these verses, and John's Gospel shows the question was alive (John 1:21; 6:14).

“I will put my words in the mouth of the prophet, who shall speak to them everything that I command.” (18:18)

God says something similar when he calls Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:9). Jesus says of himself that he speaks only what the Father has given him (John 12:49–50).

“Anyone who does not heed the words that the prophet shall speak in my name, I myself will hold accountable.” (18:19)

A word from God is never neutral. It has to be answered. Peter quotes the promise in Acts 3:22.

How the Church reads it.

Peter (Acts 3:22) and Stephen (Acts 7:37) apply this promise to Jesus. The Catechism teaches that Christ, the Son of God made man, is the Father's one, perfect and unsurpassable Word, and that there will be no other word than this one (CCC 65). The Second Vatican Council says that Christ completes and perfects Revelation (Dei Verbum 4). The Letter to the Hebrews says it in a sentence: God spoke long ago through the prophets, and in these last days by a Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). The Church reads Moses' promise as fulfilled and surpassed, since the prophet like Moses is also the Word himself.

Psalm 95:1–2, 6–7, 7–9 · O that today you would listen

Setting.

Psalm 95 has two halves. It begins as a call to worship and ends with a warning from God's own mouth. For this reason it has long been used to open the daily prayer of the Latin Church, as the Invitatory of the Liturgy of the Hours. Each day the Church begins by hearing this “today”.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: come, let us sing.
  • vv. 6–7a: come, let us bow down: we are his flock.
  • vv. 7b–9: the voice changes: “Do not harden your hearts”.

Key words.

  • hayyôm, today: The hinge of the psalm. Hebrews 3:7–4:11 builds a whole sermon on this word: “today” is still open.
  • Meribah, Massah, “quarrel”, “testing”: The place in the wilderness where the people quarrelled with Moses and put God to the test over water (Exodus 17:1–7). The names themselves tell the story.

Verse by verse.

“O come, let us worship and bow down, let us kneel before the Lord, our Maker!” (95:6)

Three verbs of the body. Worship is not only a thought. In the Latin rite we stand, bow, kneel and strike the breast.

“O that today you would listen to his voice!” (95:7)

This links the psalm directly to the first reading: “you shall heed such a prophet”.

“Do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness,” (95:8)

The hard heart is not the heart that doubts or asks. At Massah the people had already seen God's works and still put him to the test (95:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that one word marks the prayer of the Church: “Today!”, echoing the prayer of the Lord and the call of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1165). In the liturgy the saving word of God is not a report about the past. It is addressed to the ones gathered here.

1 Corinthians 7:32–35 · Unhindered devotion to the Lord

Setting.

Paul writes to Corinth from Ephesus (16:8), usually dated to the mid-50s. In chapter 7 he answers questions the Corinthians have put to him (“Now concerning the matters about which you wrote”, 7:1). He has already defended marriage and the rights of spouses (7:1–16). Now, about the unmarried, he says openly that he has “no command of the Lord” and gives his own opinion (7:25).

Two things must be kept in mind. Paul speaks in the light of “the impending crisis” (7:26) and of the short time ahead (7:29). And he has said at the start that “each has a particular gift from God” (7:7). This Sunday's reading is a pastor's counsel, not a contempt of marriage.

Structure.

  • v. 32a: the aim: that you may be without anxiety.
  • vv. 32b–34a: the unmarried man and the married man.
  • v. 34b: the unmarried woman and the married woman.
  • v. 35: why he says it: not as a noose, but for good order and devotion.

Key words.

  • amerimnos, without care: The word behind “anxieties” in v. 32. It comes from merimnaō, to be anxious or concerned, which Paul then uses for all the people he describes, both married and unmarried. Concern is not a sin; the question is how it is divided.
  • brochos, restraint: Literally a noose or halter. Paul has no wish to put a rope around anyone's neck.
  • aperispastōs, unhindered: “Without being pulled about.” It is related to the word for Martha, who was “distracted” by her many tasks (Luke 10:40). The aim is a heart gathered and attentive.

Verse by verse.

“I want you to be free from anxieties.” (7:32)

The aim is the same for everyone, and it is what Jesus asks in Matthew 6:25–34.

“and his interests are divided.” (7:34)

This is a statement of what marriage rightly asks. A husband ought to care how to please his wife. Paul does not call this a sin; he describes it as a real claim on time and heart.

“I say this for your own benefit, not to put any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and unhindered devotion to the Lord.” (7:35)

This verse is the key. Every Christian state of life is to serve one end: belonging entirely to the Lord.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that both the sacrament of Matrimony and virginity for the Kingdom of God come from the Lord himself (CCC 1620), and it quotes St John Chrysostom: whoever denigrates marriage also diminishes the glory of virginity. Vatican II calls virginity or celibacy a precious gift of grace by which some give themselves to God alone with an undivided heart, citing this very passage (Lumen Gentium 42). St John Paul II teaches that virginity does not contradict the dignity of marriage but presupposes and confirms it (Familiaris Consortio 16).

Mark 1:21–28 · A new teaching, with authority

Setting.

This is the first public act of Jesus in Mark. He has announced the Kingdom (1:14–15) and called four fishermen (1:16–20). Now, with them, he comes to Capernaum, a town on the north-west shore of the Sea of Galilee which becomes the centre of his work in Galilee. Mark's Jesus is a Jew who keeps the Sabbath and goes to the synagogue to teach.

The synagogue was the place of prayer and of reading and explaining the Scriptures. The scribes were respected teachers who gave their lives to the Law and handed on what they had received. The contrast here is not that they were bad men, but that Jesus speaks in a way no one has spoken. Visitors to Capernaum today see the remains of a later limestone synagogue, built over older basalt foundations that many archaeologists think may go back to the time of Jesus.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–22: Jesus teaches, and the people are astounded.
  • vv. 23–24: the man with the unclean spirit cries out.
  • vv. 25–26: Jesus rebukes the spirit, and it comes out.
  • vv. 27–28: amazement again and the spread of his fame.
  • ¶ The story of the exorcism is framed by two statements about his teaching (v. 22 and v. 27). Mark shows that the word and the deed are one: his word does what it says.

Key words.

  • exousia, authority: The right and the power to act, as one who holds a commission. Later Jesus claims “authority on earth to forgive sins” (2:10). In Mark the crowd sees what the disciples will take much longer to understand.
  • kainē didachē, a new teaching: Kainos means new in kind, not merely recent. The same word describes the new covenant (Hebrews 8:8).
  • ho hagios tou theou, the Holy One of God: A rare title in the Gospels; Peter uses it in John 6:69. The first to name Jesus in Mark's public story is an enemy.
  • phimōthēti, be silent: Literally “be muzzled”. The spirit's speech is not a compliment to be accepted but a threat to be silenced.

Verse by verse.

“They were astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.” (1:22)

Mark does not tell us what Jesus said. He tells us how it landed. The Catechism notes that Jesus was not content to propose his interpretation alongside those of the teachers of the Law, for he taught as one who had authority (CCC 581).

““What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are, the Holy One of God.”” (1:24)

“What have you to do with us” is an old biblical idiom of protest (“leave us alone”). The “us” is plural: a whole realm feels itself threatened. Some commentators think that naming an opponent was a way of trying to gain power over him.

“But Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be silent, and come out of him!”” (1:25)

Jesus uses no ritual and no name of a higher power, only his own word. This command to silence is the first of several (1:34; 3:12).

“They were all amazed, and they kept on asking one another, “What is this? A new teaching … with authority! He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him.”” (1:27)

Today's Gospel ends where it began: with astonishment, and with a question that the reader must answer.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees Jesus' exorcisms as signs that release people from the domination of demons and anticipate his great victory over “the ruler of this world”, for the coming of God's Kingdom means the defeat of Satan's (CCC 550). St Augustine explains that the demons knew Christ not as eternal life but by certain effects of his power (City of God 9.21, in substance). So knowledge alone is not faith: “Even the demons believe” (James 2:19). The Church therefore asks more of us than correct words about Jesus.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus silence the demon when what it says is true?

Three reasons are often given. Truth from such a source is no praise, and Jesus does not accept the testimony of his enemy. The spirit's words are not faith but an attempt at defence. And in Mark, who Jesus is can only be rightly understood at the Cross, where a human being, the centurion, says “Truly this man was God's Son!” (15:39). Many scholars call this pattern in Mark the “messianic secret”.

Is Paul saying that marriage is a lesser way of life?

No, not in the sense of marriage being a poor second. He calls each state a gift (7:7), and he does not say that the married are less holy. He does judge the unmarried state better for undivided devotion (7:38), and the Church has always defended the excellence of virginity for the Kingdom (Familiaris Consortio 16). Yet every state is called to holiness. He is giving counsel about availability, in a time he sees as pressing, and he says that he does not mean to restrain anyone. The Church honours consecrated virginity as a special sign of the Kingdom, and honours marriage as a sacrament. Both come from the Lord (CCC 1618–1620).

Did people in the Bible confuse illness with possession?

Ancient people did not always draw the lines we draw, and the Church today asks for careful medical help before any question of the demonic is raised (CCC 1673). But the Gospels do distinguish illness from possession (Mark 1:34), and the Church holds that the power of the evil one is real and that Christ has overcome it (CCC 395, 550).

For the Latin reader

In Year B the Lectionary reads the Gospel of Mark through Ordinary Time, and from here on these Sundays will often show Jesus' word at work. When the Scriptures are read in the Church, it is Christ himself who speaks (Sacrosanctum Concilium 7). The same Lord whose word astonished Capernaum then gives himself in the Eucharist, so the Mass has both the “today” of the psalm and the authority of the Gospel. In 2027 this Sunday falls on 31 January, so the memorial of St John Bosco is not kept. He was a priest of Turin (1815–1888), and his care for the young is known across Kerala through the Salesians. His life answers the second reading well: an undivided heart, spent wholly for others.

For study and teaching

  • Whose voices shape my decisions most? What would it mean to give Jesus' voice the first place today?
  • Psalm 95 says “today”. What have I been putting off hearing?
  • In my own state of life, married, single, religious or ordained, what scatters my attention from the Lord, and what helps to gather it?
  • Mark does not record what Jesus taught in Capernaum, only that it had authority. Where have I felt the authority of God's word, and what did it change in me?
