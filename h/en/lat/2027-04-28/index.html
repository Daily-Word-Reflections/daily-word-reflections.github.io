Word for the day

“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” John 15:5

Reflection

The door of faith has been opened to the Gentiles, and with that joy comes the Church's first great argument. “Certain individuals came down from Judea” to Antioch and taught: “Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved.” These were believers in Jesus; the question was real. Must a Gentile first become a Jew in order to become a Christian? Luke does not hide that there was “no small dissension and debate.” What matters is what the Church did next. No one walked out to found a rival church. Paul and Barnabas “and some of the others were appointed to go up to Jerusalem to discuss this question with the apostles and the elders.” On the way, through Phoenicia and Samaria, they told of the conversion of the Gentiles “and brought great joy to all the believers.” In Jerusalem they are welcomed by “the church and the apostles and the elders,” some believers from the party of the Pharisees state their view, and “The apostles and the elders met together to consider this matter.” This is the Council of Jerusalem, about the year 49, the pattern for every council since: when the faith is disputed, the Church gathers around the apostles and their successors and waits on the Holy Spirit. The Psalm sings of the city where the tribes go up together: “Jerusalem, built as a city that is bound firmly together.”

The Gospel of the true vine, heard on Sunday, now sounds beside this first dispute. “I am the vine, you are the branches.” Branches do not choose one another; they are joined because each one is joined to the vine. The unity the apostles seek in Jerusalem is not a committee's agreement but life flowing from Christ into every member. Jesus also speaks of a painful grace: “Every branch that bears fruit he prunes to make it bear more fruit.” Anyone who has seen a vine cut back in winter knows that pruning looks like loss. Today the Church remembers two priests who were pruned to the root. St Peter Chanel (1803 to 1841), a French priest of the Society of Mary, laboured for over three years on the Pacific island of Futuna with few visible results, and was clubbed to death on 28 April 1841; within a few years almost the whole island had asked for baptism, and he is honoured as the first martyr of Oceania. St Louis Grignion de Montfort (1673 to 1716) preached missions across western France, often rejected and even banned from dioceses, and wrote of total consecration to Jesus through the hands of the Blessed Virgin Mary; his little book, found long after his death, gave St John Paul II his motto, Totus tuus. Both stayed on the vine, and the fruit came after them.

Full homily

Dear friends, the young Church had hardly begun when it faced a serious question. Some believers came to Antioch and taught: unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved (Acts 15:1). Paul and Barnabas disagreed strongly. Luke says honestly: there was no small dissension and debate.

What did they do? Nobody started a new church. They went up to Jerusalem to the apostles and the elders, and on the way they told of the Gentiles' conversion and brought great joy to all the believers. In Jerusalem the apostles and the elders met together to consider this matter (Acts 15:6). When Christians disagree, the way of the Church is not to split, but to gather with the successors of the apostles and listen to the Holy Spirit.

Jesus tells us why this matters: I am the vine, you are the branches. Apart from me you can do nothing (John 15:5). Branches are united because each one draws life from the same vine. And the vinegrower prunes every fruitful branch to make it bear more. Pruning hurts. It looks like loss. But it is the Father's care.

St Peter Chanel worked for years on a Pacific island with little fruit and was killed there; soon the whole island believed. St Louis de Montfort was rejected again and again, and his writing on giving oneself to Jesus through Mary bore fruit long after his death. Let us stay on the vine, accept the pruning, and trust the Father with the fruit. Amen.

For children

Children, have you seen a grape vine or a pepper vine climbing a tree? The leaves and fruit grow only on branches that stay joined to the plant. Jesus says: I am the vine, you are the branches. When we pray, go to Mass and love one another, we stay joined to Jesus. St Peter Chanel went to a faraway island to tell people about Jesus. He was killed there, but later everyone on the island came to love Jesus. His love bore fruit!

Prayer

Father, you are the vinegrower and your Son is the true vine. Keep us joined to him and to one another, so that, when we disagree, we may seek the truth together in the communion of your Church, as the apostles did in Jerusalem. Prune us where we need it and make us bear fruit that lasts. Through the prayers of St Peter Chanel, who gave his life on Futuna, and of St Louis Grignion de Montfort, who gave himself wholly to Jesus through Mary, make us faithful branches. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A hard question reaches the young Church: must Gentile believers be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses in order to be saved? Antioch does not settle it alone. It sends Paul and Barnabas up to Jerusalem, to the apostles and the elders. The Gospel repeats Sunday’s passage of the vine and the branches, and today it is heard beside a Church learning how to stay one. Psalm 122 is a pilgrim song of going up to Jerusalem, the city built to be bound firmly together. Today the Church also remembers St Peter Chanel, martyr, and St Louis Grignion de Montfort, priest.

Acts 15:1–6 · They went up to Jerusalem

Setting.

Since Peter baptised the Roman centurion Cornelius and his household (chapters 10–11), Gentiles had been entering the Church. At Antioch Jews and Gentiles believed and ate together. Now some believers from Judea teach that Gentiles must first be circumcised. The apostles’ letter will later say that these men had no instructions from them (15:24).

The question was not foolish. Circumcision was the sign of God’s covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17:10–14), and the Law was Israel’s treasured gift. The believers from the party of the Pharisees (v. 5) were taking God’s covenant seriously; Paul himself had been a Pharisee (Philippians 3:5). Many scholars connect this meeting with the visit to Jerusalem Paul describes in Galatians 2:1–10, while others think that was an earlier visit. It is usually dated around AD 49.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: teachers from Judea at Antioch; sharp debate; Paul and Barnabas are sent to Jerusalem.
  • v. 3: on the way through Phoenicia and Samaria they bring great joy.
  • vv. 4–5: welcome in Jerusalem; some believers from the Pharisees insist on circumcision.
  • v. 6: the apostles and the elders meet.

Key words.

  • peritomē, circumcision: The covenant sign given to Abraham. The question was whether Gentiles who believed in Israel’s Messiah had to become Jews first.
  • presbyteroi, elders: Leaders of the Jerusalem church who now appear beside the apostles (11:30). The word gives us the English “priest” through the Latin presbyter.
  • zētēma, question: Literally a matter to be searched out. The Church does not pretend the question is easy; she gathers to seek the answer together.

Verse by verse.

“Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved.” (15:1)

The issue is not a custom but salvation itself. That is why it could not be left to each local church.

“Paul and Barnabas had no small dissension and debate with them,” (15:2)

Luke does not hide that the first Christians quarrelled. What matters is what they did with the quarrel: they took it to the apostles.

“they reported the conversion of the Gentiles, and brought great joy to all the believers.” (15:3)

On the road to a difficult meeting they tell good news. The joy of the believers in Phoenicia and Samaria is already part of the answer.

“The apostles and the elders met together to consider this matter.” (15:6)

A Church that listens, debates and decides together under the apostles. The pattern of every later council is here.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has long seen in this assembly at Jerusalem the pattern of her councils: the bishops, with Peter, gather, listen to what God has done, consider the Scriptures, and decide. The disagreement was real and was not resolved by one side shouting louder, but by coming together in the Spirit. Disputes in parishes and families can learn from this road: neither silence nor division, but going up together to seek the truth.

John 15:1–8 · The Father, the vinegrower

Setting.

The passage was read on Sunday; today the weekday Lectionary reaches it in its course through John. Heard after the first reading, it has a fresh note. The branches at Antioch and Jerusalem were very different, Jew and Gentile, and yet there is one vine. Unity does not come from making all branches alike but from all abiding in Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: Christ the vine, the Father the vinegrower, the word that cleanses.
  • vv. 4–6: abiding, or withering.
  • vv. 7–8: his words abiding in us, and the Father glorified.

Key words.

  • geōrgos, vinegrower, farmer: One who works the earth. The Father does not stand at a distance; he labours patiently over the vine.
  • logos / rhēmata, word / words: The disciples are cleansed “by the word” (v. 3), and his “words” are to abide in them (v. 7). Abiding in Christ includes keeping his words in the heart.
  • doxazō, to glorify: In John the Father is glorified in the Son’s Passion and resurrection (13:31–32). Here he is glorified also in the fruit of the disciples.

Verse by verse.

“You have already been cleansed by the word that I have spoken to you.” (15:3)

Said after the washing of the feet (13:10). Every time we listen to his word with faith, the vinegrower is at work in us.

“If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask for whatever you wish,” (15:7)

This is not a blank cheque. One whose heart is filled with his words learns to ask for what Christ wants, and such prayer is heard.

“My Father is glorified by this, that you bear much fruit and become my disciples.” (15:8)

The Father’s glory is not taken from us. It shines when we become fully alive as disciples.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of the Holy Spirit in the liturgy, says that the Spirit is like the sap of the Father’s vine which bears fruit on its branches (CCC 1108). At the Jerusalem assembly the same Spirit kept the branches on the one vine, and at every Mass he gathers into one body people of every origin.

For the Latin reader

St Peter Chanel was a French priest of the Society of Mary (the Marists) who went as a missionary to the island of Futuna in the Pacific. He was killed there on 28 April 1841, the first martyr of Oceania, and soon after his death the island became Christian. St Louis-Marie Grignion de Montfort (died 28 April 1716) was a French priest who preached parish missions among the poor and wrote True Devotion to the Blessed Virgin, teaching that we go to Jesus through Mary. Both are optional memorials in the Roman calendar. Each in his own way abided in the vine and bore fruit beyond his own lifetime.
