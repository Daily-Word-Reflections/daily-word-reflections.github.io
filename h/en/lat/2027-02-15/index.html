Word for the day

“And the king will answer them, ‘Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.’” Matthew 25:40

Reflection

The first full week of Lent opens with the great charter of holiness in Leviticus: “You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy.” We might expect holiness to mean temple rituals, but the commandments that follow are startlingly ordinary. Do not steal; do not lie to one another; do not keep back a labourer's wages until morning; do not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind; do not slander; do not judge unjustly, favouring either the poor or the great. Then the commandments go down into the heart itself: “You shall not hate in your heart anyone of your kin… You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge… but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD.” Each command is sealed with the Name of God. Holiness is God's own life, and it shows itself in honest scales, prompt wages, careful words and a heart without grudges.

Jesus draws back the curtain on the last day. The Son of Man sits on his throne of glory, and the nations are gathered before him like a flock at evening. The judgment turns on six simple works: food for the hungry, drink for the thirsty, welcome for the stranger, clothing for the naked, care for the sick, visits to prisoners. Both groups are surprised: “Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry?” And the King answers: “Just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” The neighbour of Leviticus has a face, and it is Christ's own. Lent is not first about what we give up but about whom we see. The Church calls these the corporal works of mercy, and on the last day they will be the language in which our love is read.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this first Monday of Lent God speaks to Moses: you shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy (Leviticus 19:2). And then, surprisingly, he talks about wages, lies, slander and grudges.

Holiness is not far away. It is in paying the labourer on time, in not putting a stumbling block before the blind, in refusing to hate in our heart. The commandment ends: you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD (Leviticus 19:18).

In the Gospel Jesus shows us the last day. The King will say: I was hungry and you gave me food, I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was sick and you took care of me, I was in prison and you visited me (Matthew 25:35–36). Both the blessed and the cursed are surprised. Neither saw him.

Lent is a time to open our eyes. Just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me (Matthew 25:40). Let us look for Christ this week in someone poor, sick or forgotten. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: when you share your food or visit a sick friend, you are doing it for me! This week, do one kind thing for someone who needs help, and remember that Jesus is hiding there.

Prayer

Holy Father, you call us to be holy as you are holy. Free our hearts from every grudge, make our dealings honest and our words kind, and open our eyes to see your Son in the hungry, the stranger, the sick and the prisoner, so that on the last day we may hear his voice say: Come, you that are blessed. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The first full weekday of Lent after the First Sunday puts one plain question before us: what does holiness look like on an ordinary day? Leviticus answers with wages, courts, the deaf and the blind, and a grudge kept in the heart. Matthew answers with the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner, and with the astonishing word of the King: “you did it to me.” God’s holiness is not kept away from daily life. It is met there. The key words are holy, neighbor and the least.

Leviticus 19:1–2, 11–18 · Be holy, for I am holy

Setting.

Leviticus 17–26 is often called the Holiness Code, because of its refrain that Israel must be holy as God is holy. Chapter 19 is its heart. It mixes worship and ethics without any sense of changing the subject: the sabbath stands next to fair weights, and the offerings next to care for the poor.

The Lectionary takes the opening call, then the block of commands about the neighbor. Much of the chapter echoes the Ten Commandments, here applied to the life of a village: its workers, its courts, its gossip.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the call to the whole congregation: “You shall be holy.”
  • vv. 11–12: honesty, in dealings and in oaths.
  • vv. 13–14: the worker, the deaf and the blind.
  • vv. 15–16: the court and the tongue.
  • vv. 17–18: the heart, ending in the command to love.
  • ¶ Each of these four small groups closes with the same words, “I am the Lord”. Every duty to a neighbor is signed with God’s own name.

Key words.

  • qādôš, holy: The basic idea is set apart, belonging to God. Israel is holy not by being better than others but by being his. Then that belonging has to show in how people live.
  • rēa‘, neighbor: The fellow member of the community, the person next to you. Later in this chapter the same love is commanded for the resident alien (19:34). Jesus opens the word wider still in the parable of the Samaritan (Luke 10:29–37).
  • kāmôkā, as yourself: Not a command to love oneself first, but a measure: the care I naturally take for my own life is the measure of what I owe my neighbor.

Verse by verse.

“You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy.” (19:2)

This is the root of the chapter. Moral life begins not from a list but from who God is. 1 Peter 1:16 passes the same call on to Christians.

“and you shall not keep for yourself the wages of a laborer until morning.” (19:13)

A day laborer lived on that day’s pay. Holding it back overnight meant a hungry family. The Catechism names the withholding of a just wage as a possible grave injustice and cites this verse (CCC 2434).

“You shall not revile the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind; you shall fear your God” (19:14)

The deaf cannot hear the curse and the blind cannot see who laid the block. The sin can go undetected by its victim, but not by God.

“You shall not hate in your heart anyone of your kin; you shall reprove your neighbor, or you will incur guilt yourself.” (19:17)

The law reaches inside. Silent hatred is the sin, and honest correction is one of its cures (compare Matthew 18:15).

“but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the Lord.” (19:18)

Rabbi Akiba called this a great principle of the Torah (in substance). Jesus joined it to the love of God as the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39), and Paul says the commandments are summed up in it (Romans 13:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the whole Law hangs on the two commandments of love (CCC 2055). The Second Vatican Council took up “Be holy” for every baptized person, not for a few: all the faithful are called to the fullness of the Christian life (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013). This is a useful text for an examination of conscience in Lent, since it goes through work, speech and the secret heart.

Matthew 25:31–46 · You did it to me

Setting.

This is the last teaching of Jesus in Matthew before the Passion. It closes the discourse on the end of the age (chapters 24–25), and only Matthew has it. The scene joins two images from the Old Testament: the Son of Man who receives everlasting dominion over all peoples (Daniel 7:13–14), and God as the shepherd who judges within his flock, “between sheep and sheep” (Ezekiel 34:17).

Structure.

  • vv. 31–33: the King on his throne; all the nations; the separation.
  • vv. 34–40: “Come”: six works of love, the surprise of the righteous, and the King’s answer.
  • vv. 41–45: “depart”: the same list, the same surprise, the opposite answer.
  • v. 46: two destinies.

Key words.

  • ta ethnē, all the nations: Often this word means the Gentiles, but here the judgment reaches everyone. Scholars discuss the exact scope. The sense of the scene is that no one stands outside it.
  • elachistoi, the least: The superlative of “small”. These are people with no standing, who have nothing to give back.
  • adelphoi, brothers; NRSV “members of my family”: Some commentators, ancient and modern, take these to be Christ’s disciples, above all those sent out in poverty (compare 10:40–42). Others take them to be all who are in need. The Church’s teaching applies the text to every person in need, without exception (Gaudium et Spes 27).

Verse by verse.

“Come, you that are blessed by my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world;” (25:34)

The kingdom is an inheritance, prepared long before anyone earned anything. The works that follow do not buy it. Done in grace, they are how the gift is received and kept, and their merit is itself God's gift (CCC 2009).

“Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food,” (25:37)

The righteous are surprised. They did not serve the poor as a clever way of serving Christ. They simply saw a hungry person and gave food.

“Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” (25:40)

Jesus does not say “it is as if”. He says “to me”. This is the centre of the passage.

“You that are accursed, depart from me into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels;” (25:41)

Compare v. 34. The kingdom was prepared for human beings. The fire was prepared for the devil and his angels. God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). Notice too what these people are condemned for: not for cruelty, but for doing nothing.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws from this passage the works of mercy (CCC 2447) and the warning that we are separated from God if we fail to meet the serious needs of the poor and the little ones (CCC 1033). It also quotes St John of the Cross: at the evening of life we shall be judged on love (CCC 1022). St John Chrysostom told his people that the Christ they honor on the altar is the same Christ they neglect when he is cold and naked at the door (in substance), and the Catechism links the Eucharist with the poor in the same spirit (CCC 1397). Sulpicius Severus tells how St Martin of Tours, still a catechumen, shared his cloak with a beggar and that night saw Christ wearing it.

Questions people ask

Does this mean we are saved by good works?

No. The kingdom is “prepared” and “inherited”, and salvation is the gift of God’s grace (Ephesians 2:8). But a faith that never feeds anyone is not the faith the New Testament means. Paul’s own phrase is “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6). Grace comes first, and it bears fruit; and by that same grace our good works truly merit eternal life, as his gift and not as our own achievement (CCC 2008–2010).

For study and teaching

  • Leviticus 19 goes through wages, courts, gossip and grudges. Which of these touches my own life this Lent?
  • Who are “the least” within my reach today, the ones who cannot repay me? What one work of mercy can I do for them this week?
