Word for the day

“Jesus answered them, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.”” John 2:19

Reflection

On the Third Sunday of Lent Year B places two great signs side by side: the Law given on Sinai and the Temple cleansed in Jerusalem. The first reading begins not with a command but with a reminder of grace: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” Only then come the Ten Words: no other gods, no idols, no misuse of God's name, keep holy the sabbath, honour father and mother, do not kill, commit adultery, steal, bear false witness or covet. The commandments are not chains placed on slaves; they are the way of life for a people already set free. That is why the Psalm can sing that the law of the LORD is “perfect, reviving the soul,” more to be desired than gold, “sweeter also than honey.” The Church's response puts Peter's words on our lips: “Lord, you have the words of everlasting life.”

In the Gospel Jesus goes up to Jerusalem for the Passover and finds the Temple courts turned into a market: oxen, sheep and doves for sale, money changers at their tables. “Making a whip of cords, he drove all of them out of the temple,” poured out the coins and overturned the tables: “Stop making my Father's house a marketplace!” The disciples remember the Psalm: “Zeal for your house will consume me.” This is not a loss of temper; it is a prophetic sign. Malachi had promised that the Lord would suddenly come to his temple to purify it, and now he has come. When the leaders demand a sign, Jesus gives a riddle: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” They think of the stone building, forty-six years in the making. “But he was speaking of the temple of his body.” The true meeting place of God and humanity is no longer a building but the Body of Christ, destroyed on the cross and raised on the third day. Through baptism we are built into that Body; we become, in St Paul's words, God's temple.

St Paul tells us what sign we preach: “Jews demand signs and Greeks desire wisdom, but we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles, but to those who are the called… Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.” The cross looks like weakness and folly, yet “God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God's weakness is stronger than human strength.” Lent is the time to let Jesus cleanse the temple of our hearts: to overturn the tables where we bargain with God, drive out the idols of money, pleasure and pride, and keep his commandments with love. Where there are catechumens, the Church today reads the Year A Gospel of the Samaritan woman at the well and celebrates the First Scrutiny, praying that the elect may be freed from what is weak and sinful and filled with the living water of Christ. Both Gospels lead to the same place: the new temple, from which living water flows, is Jesus himself.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today's first reading begins: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery (Exodus 20:2). Before God gives a single commandment, he reminds Israel: I have set you free. The Ten Commandments are not chains. They are the road map of freedom.

That is why the Psalm can say the law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul, sweeter also than honey. And we answer with Peter: Lord, you have the words of everlasting life.

In the Gospel Jesus goes into the Temple and finds a market. He makes a whip of cords, overturns the tables and says: stop making my Father's house a marketplace! (John 2:16). His disciples remember: zeal for your house will consume me.

Then he says: destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up (John 2:19). He was speaking of the temple of his body. The true meeting place of God and humanity is Jesus himself, crucified and risen. And by baptism we are built into him.

St Paul says: we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles, but to those who are the called, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:23–24). The cross looks weak, but it is stronger than all human strength.

This Lent, let Jesus walk into the temple of your heart. Let him overturn the tables where you bargain with God, drive out the idols, and make your heart a house of prayer. And let us pray for the elect at their scrutinies, that they may come to the living water at Easter. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Third Sunday of Lent, in the Year B readings, puts three great signs of God’s presence before us: the Law, the Cross and the Temple. The Ten Commandments are given by a God who has already set his people at liberty. The Cross, a scandal to all human reckoning, is the power and wisdom of God. The Temple, the pride of Jerusalem, points to a body that will be destroyed and rise in three days.

Psalm 19 is the response, with its answer, “Lord, you have the words of everlasting life”. The readings are also about how the worship and the life of a believer fit together, and that question touches every Christian who has come to Lent wanting something to change. The key words are word, foolishness and temple.

Exodus 20:1–17 (or 20:1–3, 7–8, 12–17) · I am the Lord your God, who brought you out

Setting.

Israel has come out of Egypt and reached Sinai. God has offered a covenant: “you shall be my treasured possession” (19:5). Thunder and lightning have shaken the mountain. Now God speaks directly. Everything else in the Law of Moses comes through Moses; these words are given to all (20:19; Deuteronomy 5:22).

The same commandments are repeated, with some differences, in Deuteronomy 5:6–21. Scholars discuss how the text grew. The Church’s concern is with what it says, and with the place that Jesus himself gives to it (Matthew 19:17–19). The shorter form of today’s reading leaves out vv. 4–6 on images and vv. 9–11 on the sabbath rest.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: God names himself: the Liberator.
  • vv. 3–6: no other gods, and no idols.
  • v. 7: the Name.
  • vv. 8–11: the sabbath.
  • v. 12: parents, with a promise.
  • vv. 13–16: life, marriage, property, truth in court.
  • v. 17: coveting: the commands reach into the heart.
  • ¶ The numbering of the ten varies. Catholic and Lutheran practice, following St Augustine, joins vv. 3–6 and divides v. 17; the Orthodox and Reformed count vv. 3 and 4–6 as two; Jewish tradition counts v. 2 as the first “word” (CCC 2066).

Key words.

  • ‘ăśeret haddəbārîm, the ten words: The Hebrew name for them (Exodus 34:28, where the NRSV has “ten commandments”; Deuteronomy 4:13; 10:4), which became Decalogue in Greek. The Catechism points out the same thing: they are words spoken by God (CCC 2056).
  • ’ēl qannā’, a jealous God: The word is used only of God in this form. It is the jealousy of a spouse, a love that will not share. The POC’s word (അസഹിഷ്ണു) can sound harsher than the sense intended. The next verse shows the proportion: third and fourth generation, against the thousandth.
  • zākôr, remember: Sabbath is to be kept in memory. Deuteronomy 5:12 has “observe”, and it gives a different reason: “Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt” (Deuteronomy 5:15). Exodus points to creation, Deuteronomy to liberation. Together they make a rich theology of rest.
  • lō’ tirṣaḥ, you shall not murder: The verb is about taking human life unjustly. The NRSV’s “murder” is more exact than the older “kill”, which the POC keeps (കൊല്ലരുത്). Jesus goes further, to anger in the heart (Matthew 5:21–22), and the Church’s teaching on the dignity of life flows from this (CCC 2258–2262).

Verse by verse.

“Then God spoke all these words:” (20:1)

God speaks first. They are given as a word, and the people can answer. The Catechism notes that everything is said in the first person, “I”, and is addressed to a “you” (CCC 2063). The Hebrew “you” is singular: each one is addressed in person.

“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery;” (20:2)

The most important verse, and it is not a command. Before any law comes the gift. These are not conditions for being rescued; they are the way of life of those already rescued. The Catechism says that the Decalogue must first be understood in the context of the Exodus (CCC 2057, 2061).

“but showing steadfast love to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments.” (20:6)

The punishment spoken of in v. 5, to the third and fourth generation, is, as many commentators explain, about the span of one household living together. The steadfast love reaches past every horizon. Ezekiel 18:20 later makes clear that each person answers for his own sins.

“Remember the sabbath day, and keep it holy.” (20:8)

This is the longest commandment, and its rest extends even to slaves, animals and foreigners (v. 10). It is an early and striking protection of those with no power. Christians keep this commandment’s meaning on Sunday, the day of the Resurrection (CCC 2175–2176; St John Paul II, Dies Domini).

“Honor your father and your mother,” (20:12)

This opens the part about the neighbor. The words were spoken first to adults about aging parents, not only to children. It is also the first with a promise (Ephesians 6:2).

“You shall not covet your neighbor’s house;” (20:17)

The last commandment no judge could ever enforce, for it deals with what no court can see. The law ends in the heart, where Jeremiah said God would write it (Jeremiah 31:33).

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, quoted by the Catechism, says that as charity comprises the two commandments, so the Ten Commandments are given on two tablets: three concern love of God and seven the love of neighbor (CCC 2067). When Jesus is asked about life, he points to them first: “If you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments” (Matthew 19:17), and sums up all in the two great commandments (CCC 2055). They bind Christians still (CCC 2068, 2072), because they express what it is to be human before God.

1 Corinthians 1:22–25 · Christ crucified, the power of God

Setting.

Paul founded the church in Corinth, a busy Greek port city, and wrote to it about AD 54 or 55 from Ephesus (16:8). The community was splitting into parties around favorite leaders (1:12), and some were proud of their eloquence and knowledge. Paul’s answer is to put the Cross back in the center.

Crucifixion was the shameful death reserved for slaves and rebels. Cicero, a Roman orator, called it the most cruel and disgusting penalty. An old graffito scratched on a wall on the Palatine Hill in Rome, often dated around AD 200, shows a crucified figure with a donkey’s head, and mocks a Christian named Alexamenos for worshipping his god. So to proclaim a crucified man as Lord sounded absurd to everyone.

Structure.

  • v. 22: what people look for: signs, wisdom.
  • v. 23: what Paul preaches: Christ crucified.
  • v. 24: how those who are called receive it: power and wisdom.
  • v. 25: the general rule.

Key words.

  • skandalon, stumbling block: A trap, or something that trips someone up; our “scandal” comes from it. To many of Paul’s fellow Jews a crucified Messiah was a contradiction, since Deuteronomy says that one hung on a tree is under God’s curse (Deuteronomy 21:23; compare Galatians 3:13). Paul himself had once persecuted the Church (Galatians 1:13).
  • mōria, foolishness: This word gives us “moron”. To educated Greeks, a god who suffered and died made no sense.
  • klētoi, the called: This is the whole difference. Faith does not rest on being cleverer or more religious; it rests on God’s call, to “both Jews and Greeks”.

Verse by verse.

“For Jews demand signs and Greeks desire wisdom,” (1:22)

Paul, a Jew himself, is not mocking anyone. He names the two good things that people reach for: proof of God’s action and understanding of the world. Both are good, and either can become a way of setting terms for God. Jesus was asked for a sign, as in today’s Gospel (John 2:18).

“but we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles,” (1:23)

The verb form “crucified” in Greek is a perfect passive: the Risen One remains the Crucified One. Paul’s preaching does not soften the shameful part.

“but to those who are the called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.” (1:24)

Note the reply. To those who demand a sign of power, he gives power; to those seeking wisdom, wisdom. God gives them what they wanted, in a form they would never have looked for.

“For God’s foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God’s weakness is stronger than human strength.” (1:25)

A startling way of speaking. God is neither foolish nor weak. Paul means: what looks like foolishness and weakness in God’s action is greater than our best.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism turns to this text at a very difficult point. Faith in God the almighty can be put to the test by the experience of evil and suffering. It answers that God has revealed his almighty power in the most mysterious way, in the voluntary humiliation and Resurrection of his Son (CCC 272). St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom, wrote that the Cross is a stumbling block to those who do not believe, but to us salvation and eternal life (To the Ephesians 18, in substance). This is why a crucifix stands at the altar at Mass: we do not hide the very thing the world finds foolish.

John 2:13–25 · He was speaking of the temple of his body

Setting.

John places this event at the beginning of Jesus’ ministry, soon after the wedding at Cana. Matthew, Mark and Luke place it in the last week of his life (Mark 11:15–17). Many scholars think there was one such event and that John placed it first for theological reasons, so that the whole Gospel is read in its light; some hold there were two. In either case the meaning is clear in John’s telling.

For the Passover, Jews came to Jerusalem by the thousand. Those travelling from afar needed animals for sacrifice that met the Law’s standards, and the half-shekel Temple offering (Exodus 30:13) was paid, according to later Jewish sources, in a particular coin. So the traders offered a real service. The problem was their presence in the Temple itself, probably in the large outer court where non-Jews were allowed to pray.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: the place and what Jesus found.
  • vv. 15–16: his action and his word.
  • v. 17: what the disciples remembered.
  • vv. 18–20: the demand for a sign and the answer.
  • vv. 21–22: John’s explanation.
  • vv. 23–25: many believe; Jesus does not entrust himself.

Key words.

  • hieron / naos, temple: John uses two words. Hieron (vv. 14–15) is the whole Temple area. Naos (vv. 19–21) is the sanctuary itself, the dwelling of God. Jesus’ saying is about the naos.
  • phragellion, whip: Found only here in the New Testament. Only John mentions it. Jesus makes it himself, perhaps from cords used for the animals.
  • zēlos, zeal: The burning devotion of Psalm 69. In the Old Testament it is used of Phinehas and Elijah, who were zealous for God (Numbers 25:11; 1 Kings 19:10).
  • egeirō, raise: The verb is the one used for raising up a building, and for the resurrection of the dead. It makes the double meaning possible.

Verse by verse.

“Making a whip of cords, he drove all of them out of the temple, both the sheep and the cattle.” (2:15)

The Greek ties “all of them” closely to the sheep and cattle, and the text says nothing of his striking anyone. The Catechism speaks of it as zeal for his Father’s house (CCC 584). It was a prophetic sign.

“Stop making my Father’s house a marketplace!” (2:16)

Luke 2:49 gives us the twelve-year-old in the same place, “in my Father’s house”. Here this is the first public claim of Jesus in Jerusalem. Zechariah had looked to the day when “there shall no longer be traders in the house of the Lord” (Zechariah 14:21). And Jesus’ respect for the Temple is real (CCC 583).

“Zeal for your house will consume me.” (2:17)

From Psalm 69:9, with one change. The psalm says “has consumed”; John says “will consume”. It points forward: this zeal will cost him his life.

“The Jews then said to him, “What sign can you show us for doing this?”” (2:18)

In John’s Gospel “the Jews” often means the Temple authorities, those who opposed Jesus, and not the Jewish people. Jesus and all his disciples were Jews. The Church firmly rejects any blame laid on the Jewish people as such (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). The question itself was a fair one: on what authority?

“Jesus answered them, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.”” (2:19)

The Greek imperative has the force of “if you destroy”. The saying was remembered and used in distorted form at his trial (Mark 14:58).

“This temple has been under construction for forty-six years,” (2:20)

Herod the Great began the rebuilding around 20–19 BC, according to Josephus, so this fits a date of about AD 27–28. The work was still going on, and it would stand finished only a few years before the Romans destroyed it in AD 70.

“But he was speaking of the temple of his body.” (2:21)

The key to the whole passage. God’s dwelling among us is now a person (compare 1:14, “lived among us”; the Greek is “pitched his tent”). The Catechism says that Jesus presents himself as God’s definitive dwelling-place among men (CCC 586).

“But Jesus on his part would not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people” (2:24)

“Believed” in v. 23 and “entrust” here are the same Greek verb, pisteuō. They believed in him, but he did not believe in them. Enthusiasm based on signs alone is not yet faith. “He himself knew what was in everyone” echoes Jeremiah’s God who searches the heart.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus went up to the Temple as his Father’s house, with deep respect, and that he identified himself with the Temple (CCC 583–586). St Paul takes it one step further, telling Christians, “you are God’s temple” (1 Corinthians 3:16; CCC 797). So there are three temples: the body of Christ risen, the Church his body, and each baptized person. St Augustine, preaching on this passage, says that those who sell in the Church are those who seek their own interests there and not those of Jesus Christ (Tractates on John 10, in substance). Lent asks what needs to be overturned in the temple of my own life.

Questions people ask

Do Catholics break the commandment against images when they have statues and icons?

No. The commandment forbids making an image to worship as God. God himself told Moses to make cherubim for the ark (Exodus 25:18). The Seventh Ecumenical Council, at Nicaea in 787, defended the veneration of images on the basis of the Incarnation: God has now shown himself in a human face. The honor given to an image passes to the person it portrays, and we adore only God (CCC 2129–2132).

Did Jesus cleanse the Temple once or twice? Why does John put it at the start, and the others at the end?

The Gospels are faithful witnesses, and they do not always follow the order of the calendar. Many scholars think there was one event and that John moved it forward to set the theme for his whole Gospel: Jesus’ body is the new meeting place with God. Others think that it may have happened twice. The Church has not settled this question, and nothing in the faith depends on it.

Does this Gospel mean that Christians have replaced Israel’s worship and that the Temple was worthless?

No. Jesus loved the Temple, and his disciples kept going there to pray after Easter (Acts 3:1). The Church recognizes all she has received through Israel (Nostra Aetate 4), and she still prays Israel’s psalms. What Christians confess is that in Jesus God’s presence among us has reached its fullness (CCC 586). This must be said with gratitude and never with contempt.

For the Latin reader

On the Third, Fourth and Fifth Sundays of Lent, parishes with adults preparing for baptism at Easter celebrate the Scrutinies, and for those Masses the Year A readings (the Samaritan woman on this Sunday) may be used even in Year B. Elsewhere the Year B readings are heard. Today’s First Reading gives a natural shape to a Lenten Confession: the commandments have long been used in the Latin Church as a guide for the examination of conscience. The same Gospel, John 2:13–22, returns on 9 November for the Dedication of the Lateran Basilica, the Pope’s cathedral, where the Church thinks about stone buildings and the living temple together.

For study and teaching

  • God says first, “I brought you out,” and only then, “you shall not.” Do I see the commandments as a list of rules or as the way of life of someone already rescued? What difference does that make for me?
  • Go through the commandments slowly, as an examination of conscience. Which one has God been putting his finger on this Lent?
  • Where have I wanted God to meet my terms, with a sign or a reason, instead of accepting the Cross? How does “God’s foolishness” speak to the suffering in my life?
  • If Jesus came into the temple of my life with a whip of cords, what tables would he overturn? What marketplace has crept in?
