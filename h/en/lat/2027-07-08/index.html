Word for the day

“Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons. You received without payment; give without payment.” Matthew 10:8

Reflection

The story of Joseph reaches its turning point. The silver cup has been found in Benjamin's sack, and Joseph, still unrecognised, has ruled that only Benjamin shall stay behind as his slave; the rest may go home in peace. Then Judah steps forward, and he makes the longest speech in the Book of Genesis. He does not defend himself and he does not bargain. He simply tells the story of an old father: “We have a father, an old man, and a young brother, the child of his old age. His brother is dead; he alone is left of his mother's children, and his father loves him.” He reports what Jacob said when asked to let Benjamin go: “You know that my wife bore me two sons; one left me, and I said, Surely he has been torn to pieces; and I have never seen him since. If you take this one also from me, and harm comes to him, you will bring down my gray hairs in sorrow to Sheol.” In the verses the Lectionary passes over, Judah makes the offer that settles everything: “please let your servant remain as a slave to my lord in place of the boy; and let the boy go back with his brothers.” This is the same Judah who, twenty years earlier, had proposed that Joseph be sold to traders. Now he offers himself as a slave so that another favoured son will not be lost to his father. The test is passed: these brothers will not abandon a beloved brother again. Joseph can no longer control himself. “Send everyone away from me,” he cries, and weeping so loudly that the Egyptians hear it, he says, “I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?” His brothers cannot answer him, “so dismayed were they at his presence.” “Come closer to me,” he says, and they come closer. There is no reproach. Joseph does not pretend that nothing happened: “I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt.” But he looks at the wrong with the eyes of faith: “do not be distressed, or angry with yourselves, because you sold me here; for God sent me before you to preserve life.” Later in the chapter he will say it more boldly still: “it was not you who sent me here, but God.” Joseph does not call the evil good; he sees that God has brought good out of it. The Christian tradition has long seen in Joseph a figure of Christ, handed over by his own brothers for silver, brought low, and then raised up to give life to the world, forgiving those who wronged him; and in Judah, one who offers himself in the place of another. The Psalm sings the whole story: “he had sent a man ahead of them, Joseph, who was sold as a slave. His feet were hurt with fetters, his neck was put in a collar of iron; until what he had said came to pass, the word of the Lord kept testing him.”

In the Gospel Jesus goes on instructing the Twelve. “As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’ Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons.” These are the very works Jesus himself has done in chapters 8 and 9 of St Matthew: the leper cleansed, the girl raised, the demons driven out. Now he hands them on. And then comes the sentence that sums up the whole of grace: “You received without payment; give without payment.” Nothing the apostles have, they earned. Healing, forgiveness and the word of God are gifts, and a gift cannot be sold. So the messengers travel light: “Take no gold, or silver, or copper in your belts, no bag for your journey, or two tunics, or sandals, or a staff.” Their poverty is a sign that the message does not belong to them and that they trust the One who sends them. They are not beggars, however, for Jesus adds, “laborers deserve their food”: those who serve the Gospel have a right to be supported by the people they serve, and the Church still lives by that rule. They depend on the hospitality of ordinary homes. “Whatever town or village you enter, find out who in it is worthy, and stay there until you leave.” Anyone who knows the welcome of a Kerala household, where the priest or the visitor is given the best chair and a hot meal, will understand how much the early Church grew in family homes. At the door the messenger offers the greeting of the East: “As you enter the house, greet it,” that is, say Shalom, peace. The peace is real, not a polite formula: “If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you.” A gift refused is not wasted; it comes back to the giver. When a place will not listen, the disciples are to “shake off the dust from your feet.” It is a solemn sign that they have done their part and that responsibility now rests with those who refused; it is not an act of hatred, and Jesus himself weeps over the towns that do not receive him. The warning about Sodom and Gomorrah teaches how serious it is to reject the word of God, not that any people may be despised. Joseph asked no payment for his forgiveness, though his brothers owed him everything. That is the way of God, and the way of the disciple: you received without payment; give without payment.

Homily outline

Theme: “You received without payment; give without payment.”

Judah offers himself, Joseph forgives, and the Twelve are sent out with nothing but a gift to give.

1. In place of the boy

  • Judah the changed man.
  • The speech that saves a family.
  • A love that offers itself.
“Now therefore, please let your servant remain as a slave to my lord in place of the boy; and let the boy go back with his brothers.” Genesis 44:33
“No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” John 15:13

Apply: Quietly take one burden off someone near you this week, and do not tell anyone.

2. I am Joseph

  • Send everyone away.
  • The brothers cannot answer.
  • Come closer to me.
“Joseph said to his brothers, “I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?” But his brothers could not answer him, so dismayed were they at his presence.” Genesis 45:3
“Jesus himself stood among them and said to them, “Peace be with you.”” Luke 24:36

Apply: Think of the person you find hardest to forgive and pray for them by name every day this week.

3. God sent me before you

  • Evil is not called good.
  • God draws good out of it.
  • Joseph, a figure of Christ.
“he had sent a man ahead of them, Joseph, who was sold as a slave.” Psalm 105:17
“We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.” Romans 8:28

Apply: Write down one hurt in your past and, next to it, one good that God has since brought out of it.

4. Without payment

  • Heal, cleanse, proclaim.
  • Travel light.
  • Peace that returns to the giver.
“How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, who brings good news, who announces salvation, who says to Zion, “Your God reigns.”” Isaiah 52:7
“Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons. You received without payment; give without payment.” Matthew 10:8

Apply: Bring a greeting of peace into one house this week: visit a neighbour, a lonely person, a family in trouble.

Judah's repentance and Joseph's forgiveness show how God brings a broken family home and draws good even out of betrayal.

Jesus sends the Twelve with a gift they did not earn and cannot sell, and asks us to give as we have received.

Take-home resolution: Give one gift this week without payment, and forgive one debt that you have been holding.

Full homily

Dear friends, Judah is one of the most moving men in the Bible. In the first part of the story he is the brother who proposes to sell Joseph, because it will be more profitable than killing him. Twenty years later, he stands before the governor of Egypt and offers himself as a slave in place of the youngest brother (Genesis 44:33). Something has happened to Judah. He has looked at his father's grief, he has learned what his sin cost, and he has become another man. That is what repentance is: not only feeling sorry, but changing.

Then Joseph cannot hold back any longer. Send everyone away from me (Genesis 45:1). In the privacy of the room he says: I am Joseph. Is my father still alive? His brothers cannot speak. They are afraid. They remember the pit, the silver, the lie. But Joseph says: Come closer to me. And then: do not be distressed, or angry with yourselves, because you sold me here; for God sent me before you to preserve life (Genesis 45:5). Not a word of reproach. This is how God forgives: he draws the sinner closer, and he uses even the evil done to bring about good.

In the Gospel Jesus sends out the Twelve with a few words of instruction: proclaim, heal, cleanse. And then he says the sentence we should write on our hearts: You received without payment; give without payment (Matthew 10:8). We have received so much. Life, faith, baptism, forgiveness, the Eucharist. None of it was earned. How, then, can we hold it back or sell it? The apostles are to travel light, trust in God and the welcome of ordinary people, and bring peace into every house.

So this week, let us give something without payment. A visit, a favour, a forgiveness. And if someone has done you wrong, imagine Joseph standing in front of his brothers and saying: come closer to me. Pharaoh's palace was not too small for that mercy. Neither is your home. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph's brothers were mean to him when he was young. Years later they came to him for food, and they were afraid. But Joseph did not shout at them. He cried, and he said: I am your brother Joseph! Come close. God has used even this to save our family. In the Gospel Jesus says to his friends: God gave you his love as a gift, so give it as a gift to others. Let us share what we have, and say sorry and forgive, just like Joseph!

Prayer

Lord God, you brought good out of a brother's betrayal and you taught Joseph to forgive. Give us Judah's courage to offer ourselves for the people we love, and Joseph's tears to forgive those who have wronged us. Lord Jesus, you have given us healing, pardon and your own Body and Blood without any payment from us. Make us light travellers on your road, trusting in you, glad to be welcomed, and quick to give what we have received. Where we are refused, let your peace return to us and keep us from bitterness. St Joseph, pray for us. Amen.
