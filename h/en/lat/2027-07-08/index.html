Word for the day

“Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons. You received without payment; give without payment.” Matthew 10:8

Reflection

The story of Joseph reaches its turning point. The silver cup has been found in Benjamin's sack, and Joseph, still unrecognised, has ruled that only Benjamin shall stay behind as his slave; the rest may go home in peace. Then Judah steps forward, and he makes the longest speech in the Book of Genesis. He does not defend himself and he does not bargain. He simply tells the story of an old father: “We have a father, an old man, and a young brother, the child of his old age. His brother is dead; he alone is left of his mother's children, and his father loves him.” He reports what Jacob said when asked to let Benjamin go: “You know that my wife bore me two sons; one left me, and I said, Surely he has been torn to pieces; and I have never seen him since. If you take this one also from me, and harm comes to him, you will bring down my gray hairs in sorrow to Sheol.” In the verses the Lectionary passes over, Judah makes the offer that settles everything: “please let your servant remain as a slave to my lord in place of the boy; and let the boy go back with his brothers.” This is the same Judah who, twenty years earlier, had proposed that Joseph be sold to traders. Now he offers himself as a slave so that another favoured son will not be lost to his father. The test is passed: these brothers will not abandon a beloved brother again. Joseph can no longer control himself. “Send everyone away from me,” he cries, and weeping so loudly that the Egyptians hear it, he says, “I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?” His brothers cannot answer him, “so dismayed were they at his presence.” “Come closer to me,” he says, and they come closer. There is no reproach. Joseph does not pretend that nothing happened: “I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt.” But he looks at the wrong with the eyes of faith: “do not be distressed, or angry with yourselves, because you sold me here; for God sent me before you to preserve life.” Later in the chapter he will say it more boldly still: “it was not you who sent me here, but God.” Joseph does not call the evil good; he sees that God has brought good out of it. The Christian tradition has long seen in Joseph a figure of Christ, handed over by his own brothers for silver, brought low, and then raised up to give life to the world, forgiving those who wronged him; and in Judah, one who offers himself in the place of another. The Psalm sings the whole story: “he had sent a man ahead of them, Joseph, who was sold as a slave. His feet were hurt with fetters, his neck was put in a collar of iron; until what he had said came to pass, the word of the Lord kept testing him.”

In the Gospel Jesus goes on instructing the Twelve. “As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’ Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons.” These are the very works Jesus himself has done in chapters 8 and 9 of St Matthew: the leper cleansed, the girl raised, the demons driven out. Now he hands them on. And then comes the sentence that sums up the whole of grace: “You received without payment; give without payment.” Nothing the apostles have, they earned. Healing, forgiveness and the word of God are gifts, and a gift cannot be sold. So the messengers travel light: “Take no gold, or silver, or copper in your belts, no bag for your journey, or two tunics, or sandals, or a staff.” Their poverty is a sign that the message does not belong to them and that they trust the One who sends them. They are not beggars, however, for Jesus adds, “laborers deserve their food”: those who serve the Gospel have a right to be supported by the people they serve, and the Church still lives by that rule. They depend on the hospitality of ordinary homes. “Whatever town or village you enter, find out who in it is worthy, and stay there until you leave.” Anyone who knows the welcome of a Kerala household, where the priest or the visitor is given the best chair and a hot meal, will understand how much the early Church grew in family homes. At the door the messenger offers the greeting of the East: “As you enter the house, greet it,” that is, say Shalom, peace. The peace is real, not a polite formula: “If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you.” A gift refused is not wasted; it comes back to the giver. When a place will not listen, the disciples are to “shake off the dust from your feet.” It is a solemn sign that they have done their part and that responsibility now rests with those who refused; it is not an act of hatred, and Jesus himself weeps over the towns that do not receive him. The warning about Sodom and Gomorrah teaches how serious it is to reject the word of God, not that any people may be despised. Joseph asked no payment for his forgiveness, though his brothers owed him everything. That is the way of God, and the way of the disciple: you received without payment; give without payment.

Full homily

Dear friends, Judah is one of the most moving men in the Bible. In the first part of the story he is the brother who proposes to sell Joseph, because it will be more profitable than killing him. Twenty years later, he stands before the governor of Egypt and offers himself as a slave in place of the youngest brother (Genesis 44:33). Something has happened to Judah. He has looked at his father's grief, he has learned what his sin cost, and he has become another man. That is what repentance is: not only feeling sorry, but changing.

Then Joseph cannot hold back any longer. Send everyone away from me (Genesis 45:1). In the privacy of the room he says: I am Joseph. Is my father still alive? His brothers cannot speak. They are afraid. They remember the pit, the silver, the lie. But Joseph says: Come closer to me. And then: do not be distressed, or angry with yourselves, because you sold me here; for God sent me before you to preserve life (Genesis 45:5). Not a word of reproach. This is how God forgives: he draws the sinner closer, and he uses even the evil done to bring about good.

In the Gospel Jesus sends out the Twelve with a few words of instruction: proclaim, heal, cleanse. And then he says the sentence we should write on our hearts: You received without payment; give without payment (Matthew 10:8). We have received so much. Life, faith, baptism, forgiveness, the Eucharist. None of it was earned. How, then, can we hold it back or sell it? The apostles are to travel light, trust in God and the welcome of ordinary people, and bring peace into every house.

So this week, let us give something without payment. A visit, a favour, a forgiveness. And if someone has done you wrong, imagine Joseph standing in front of his brothers and saying: come closer to me. Pharaoh's palace was not too small for that mercy. Neither is your home. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph's brothers were mean to him when he was young. Years later they came to him for food, and they were afraid. But Joseph did not shout at them. He cried, and he said: I am your brother Joseph! Come close. God has used even this to save our family. In the Gospel Jesus says to his friends: God gave you his love as a gift, so give it as a gift to others. Let us share what we have, and say sorry and forgive, just like Joseph!

Prayer

Lord God, you brought good out of a brother's betrayal and you taught Joseph to forgive. Give us Judah's courage to offer ourselves for the people we love, and Joseph's tears to forgive those who have wronged us. Lord Jesus, you have given us healing, pardon and your own Body and Blood without any payment from us. Make us light travellers on your road, trusting in you, glad to be welcomed, and quick to give what we have received. Where we are refused, let your peace return to us and keep us from bitterness. St Joseph, pray for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the story of Joseph reaches its climax. Judah, who once proposed selling Joseph, steps forward and offers himself in place of Benjamin; and Joseph can no longer hold back. “I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt.” In the Gospel Jesus goes on instructing the Twelve: heal, raise, cleanse, “You received without payment; give without payment.” Both readings are about gift. Joseph gives his brothers a life they did not deserve, and the disciples are to give as a gift what they have received as a gift. Psalm 105 sings the same story from God’s side: he sent a man ahead of them, Joseph, who was sold as a slave.

Genesis 44:18–21, 23b–29; 45:1–5 · I am Joseph, your brother

Setting.

On their second journey to Egypt the brothers brought Benjamin, the youngest, as the governor demanded. Joseph then had his silver cup hidden in Benjamin’s sack and had the brothers arrested on the road, saying that the one in whose sack it was found would become his slave (44:1–17). This was the last test: would they again abandon a favoured brother of Rachel, as they once abandoned Joseph?

Background. Judah’s plea is one of the longest speeches in Genesis. The Lectionary gives it in part, leaving out vv. 22, 23a and 30–34. In the part left out Judah says that he will remain as a slave in Benjamin’s place so that the boy may go home to his father. It is the same Judah who once said, “Come, let us sell him” (37:27). Years ago he spoke of selling a brother; now he offers to be sold himself. The brothers have changed, and this is what Joseph needed to know.

Structure.

  • 44:18–21, 23b–26: Judah begins humbly and recalls how the governor asked about a father and a brother, and how the brothers refused to leave Benjamin behind.
  • 44:27–29: he quotes the words of old Jacob: one son lost, and the loss of the other would send him to the grave in sorrow.
  • 45:1–3: Joseph cannot control himself; he sends the Egyptians out, weeps aloud and reveals himself, asking first: “Is my father still alive?”
  • 45:4–5: he calls the brothers close and tells them not to be distressed, for God sent him ahead to preserve life.

Key words.

  • ʿebed, servant: Judah calls himself “your servant” over and over in this speech, and calls Jacob “your servant my father”. The humility of the word shows how far Judah has come from the man who sold his brother.
  • lĕhiṯʾappēq, to control himself: Joseph could no longer hold himself back in front of all those who stood by (45:1). The verb means to restrain. Long hidden love breaks through.
  • lĕmiḥyâ, to preserve life: Joseph interprets the whole story with this word (45:5). What the brothers meant as death for him, God turned into life for many.

Verse by verse.

“Then Judah stepped up to him and said,” (44:18)

Judah steps forward as spokesman and advocate. In Jewish tradition he becomes the one who takes responsibility, and from his tribe David and, as Matthew’s genealogy says, Jesus will come (Matthew 1:2–3).

“his father loves him.” (44:20)

The brothers once hated Joseph because their father loved him more (37:4). Now Judah speaks for the favoured child with love, not envy.

“Then Joseph could no longer control himself before all those who stood by him,” (45:1)

The man who has been hard and strange for two chapters breaks. His heart was never closed.

“I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt.” (45:4)

He names the wrong plainly, without hiding it, and in the next breath he forgives. Forgiveness does not pretend that nothing happened.

“for God sent me before you to preserve life.” (45:5)

This is a reading of history in faith: not that the crime was good, but that God was able to use it for life. Verse 8 adds that it was God who sent him.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has read the scene of the discovery as a picture of reconciliation, in which the one who was wronged takes the first step. The Catechism teaches that it is not in our power not to feel an offence, but the heart that offers itself to the Holy Spirit turns injury into compassion and purifies memory by turning hurt into intercession (CCC 2843). The Fathers also saw in Judah, who offers himself in place of Benjamin, a figure of Christ, who gave himself in our place; this is a reading in the tradition, not the sense of the text itself.

Matthew 10:7–15 · Give without payment

Setting.

The mission discourse continues. In yesterday’s verses Jesus named the twelve apostles and began the instructions; today he says what they are to do, what they are to take, and how they are to be received. The setting is the Galilee of the early ministry, where the disciples go out to the villages around them.

Background. The disciples are to travel light: no money in the belt, no bag, no second tunic. The point is not hardship but trust. In Jewish custom a hosting household was a duty of hospitality, and the messenger of a teacher was received as the teacher himself. The greeting of peace (shalom) was the usual greeting on entering a house; here it becomes a gift that can rest or return.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: proclaim the kingdom; heal, raise, cleanse, cast out; what was given as a gift is to be given as a gift.
  • vv. 9–10: no gold, no bag, no second tunic: the labourer deserves his food.
  • vv. 11–13: look for a worthy house; greet it; let your peace rest on it, or return to you.
  • vv. 14–15: if a town will not receive you, shake off the dust; its fate is grave.

Key words.

  • dōrean, without payment, as a gift: The same Greek word is used in Romans 3:24 for the grace by which we are justified, a gift that cannot be earned. Everything the apostles have is gift, so they must not trade on it.
  • eirēnē, peace: The Greek translates shalom: wholeness, well-being and a right relation with God and neighbour. The disciples carry it with them, and it is not lost if it is refused.
  • axios, worthy: Used for the house, in vv. 11–13. It means a house ready to welcome, not one that has earned the right by merit.

Verse by verse.

“Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons.” (10:8)

The disciples continue the works of Jesus in chapters 8 and 9. The kingdom is announced in word and in deed.

“You received without payment; give without payment.” (10:8)

The root of the apostolic ministry: the gift of God cannot be sold. The Church has always taken this word as a warning against making money from sacred things.

“for laborers deserve their food.” (10:10)

Those who serve the Gospel may rightly be supported by the community. A similar saying is echoed in 1 Timothy 5:18, and Paul gives the same principle in 1 Corinthians 9:14.

“let your peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you.” (10:13)

The disciples’ peace is not wasted if it is refused; it returns to them. They carry it as a gift they never lose.

“shake off the dust from your feet as you leave that house or town.” (10:14)

A sign of leaving without taking anything away, and without any anger of revenge. The disciples are not to curse a town; they leave its fate with God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, on the sin of simony, recalls that spiritual things cannot be bought or sold, and quotes Peter’s answer to Simon the magician (CCC 2121). The Church applies the word “You received without payment” above all to the sacraments, which are given as gifts of Christ and are not sold. The faithful, for their part, are to provide for the needs of the Church and her ministers, following the word “laborers deserve their food”. The Church has also read the apostles’ lack of means as a call to evangelical poverty and simplicity in every ministry. The warning about Sodom and Gomorrah is a warning to take the word of God seriously; it is not a reason for contempt toward any people.

Questions people ask

Does “shake off the dust” mean we should leave anyone who rejects the Gospel?

No. The gesture belongs to the first mission of the Twelve, who were to go on quickly to the next village, and it says that they are not responsible for the choice of those who refuse. Jesus himself never stopped loving those who rejected him, and he told the disciples to forgive (Matthew 18:21–22). Dialogue and patient witness remain the way of the Church.

For study and teaching

  • Joseph named the wrong and forgave in the same breath. Is there a wrong I have been unable to speak of, or to forgive?
  • What gift have I received from God that I am meant to hand on as a gift to others this week?
