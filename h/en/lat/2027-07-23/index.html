Word for the day

“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery; you shall have no other gods before me.” Exodus 20:2–3

Reflection

Today's first reading is the centre of the Old Testament: “Then God spoke all these words.” They are given at Sinai, three months after the Exodus, to a people just brought out of slavery. Before any commandment comes a name and a deed: “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” The law is not given to slaves to bind them again, but to keep a liberated people in their liberty. In Hebrew the ten are called the Ten Words, and the Catechism teaches that they are a path of life, the “ten words” by which God says to his people what love of God and of neighbour require (Catechism 2056–2057). The Catholic tradition counts them in this way: the first three are about God (no other gods and no idols; the name of the Lord not misused; the sabbath kept holy), and the last seven about our neighbour (father and mother; no murder; no adultery; no theft; no false witness; no coveting of the neighbour's wife; no coveting of the neighbour's goods). The first word is stern because it protects what love is: “for I the Lord your God am a jealous God.” A jealous God is a God whose love wants all of us, as a faithful spouse wants a faithful spouse. The sabbath is not a rule that makes life harder but a gift: a day of rest, in imitation of the Creator, for family, servants, the foreigner and even the cattle. “Honor your father and your mother” is the first commandment with a promise attached, and the one that holds family and society together. The last words go to the heart: “You shall not covet.” The law reaches inside the heart where every sin begins. For Christians, the sabbath rest is fulfilled on the Lord's Day, the day of the Resurrection (Catechism 2174–2176), and Jesus has told us that he came “not to abolish but to fulfill” the law (Matthew 5:17) and that all of it hangs on the love of God and of neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40). The psalm is the sung answer of a people who delight in this gift: “The law of the Lord is perfect, reviving the soul; the decrees of the Lord are sure, making wise the simple … More to be desired are they than gold, even much fine gold; sweeter also than honey, and drippings of the honeycomb.” We honour the Jewish people, who first received these words and who still cherish the Torah as their delight.

In the Gospel the disciples have heard Jesus tell the parable of the sower, and now he explains it. It is one of only two parables in Matthew's thirteenth chapter that Jesus explains in detail (the other is the parable of the weeds), and he explains it to those who ask. “When anyone hears the word of the kingdom and does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away what is sown in the heart; this is what was sown on the path.” The word is heard but never allowed inside. “As for what was sown on rocky ground, this is the one who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy; yet such a person has no root, but endures only for a while, and when trouble or persecution arises on account of the word, that person immediately falls away.” This is the enthusiast whose joy is shallow. “As for what was sown among thorns, this is the one who hears the word, but the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke the word, and it yields nothing.” This is the busy person, caught up in worries and wanting more. “But as for what was sown on good soil, this is the one who hears the word and understands it, who indeed bears fruit and yields, in one case a hundredfold, in another sixty, and in another thirty.” In Matthew to “understand” is not only to follow an argument; it is to take the word to heart and put it into practice. Put the two readings side by side. At Sinai God gives the law on tablets of stone; the prophets promised that one day he would do something more: “I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts” (Jeremiah 31:33). The parable of the sower shows how this happens: the word is a seed that must be received into the soil of the heart, where it takes root and bears fruit. The law of the Lord, as Psalm 19 says, revives the soul only when it is received. Today the Church remembers St Bridget of Sweden, a medieval noblewoman, wife and mother of eight children, a widow, a mystic and the founder of an order. Her life shows what good soil looks like: a heart in which the word of God took root, and in which the cares of the court did not choke it. The Church calls her one of the patron saints of Europe.

Full homily

Dear friends, the first reading gives us the Ten Commandments. We know them, but listen to how they begin: I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery (Exodus 20:2). Before any command, God says what he has done. The law is not the chain of a master; it is the road for people who have just been set at liberty. God says: I have brought you out; now live as a people at liberty. No other gods; do not misuse my name; keep the sabbath; honor your father and mother; do not kill; do not commit adultery; do not steal; do not lie; do not covet.

The psalm sings of these laws as something sweet: more to be desired than gold, sweeter than honey. Do we think of God's commandments in this way? Sometimes we treat them as a list of things we may not do. But a father who tells his child, do not touch the fire, does so out of love. The commandments are the Father's love in words. And Jesus told us that all of them hang on two: love of God with all your heart and love of your neighbour as yourself. If we love, we keep them.

In the Gospel Jesus explains the parable of the sower. The word of God is the seed, and our hearts are the soil. There is the soil of the path: the word is heard but not understood, and the evil one snatches it away. There is the rocky soil: we receive it with joy, but there are no roots, and when trouble comes we fall away. There is the soil full of thorns: the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke it. And there is the good soil: the one who hears the word and understands it, and bears fruit. It is not enough to hear the commandments; they must be received into the heart, as the prophet says: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33).

Today we remember St Bridget of Sweden. She was a wife and a mother of eight, she lived in a royal court, and she was not spared sorrow. And yet the word of God took root in her heart, and it bore fruit in prayer, in service of the poor, in a new religious order, and in a long pilgrimage to Rome and the Holy Land. She shows us that the good soil does not belong only to monks and nuns. Let us ask her to help us, in these rainy days when the land is soft, to make our hearts soft too, and let us give the word of God a fixed hour every day. Amen.

For children

Children, God gave his people ten rules, the Ten Commandments, because he loves us and wants us to be happy and unafraid. They tell us to love God, to respect our parents, not to lie, not to steal, and to be kind. Jesus told a story about a farmer and seeds. Some seeds fell on the road, some on rocks, some among thorns, and some on good soil. The seeds are God's words and the soil is our heart. Let us listen carefully, so that our hearts are good soil, and let us ask St Bridget to pray for us.

Prayer

Lord our God, you gave your people the ten words on Mount Sinai and wrote your law in the hearts of those who love you. Make us hear your word and understand it; take the stones out of our hearts and cut away the thorns, so that your word may take root in us and bear fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold. Through the prayers of St Bridget of Sweden, mother, widow and servant of your word, make us faithful to your commandments, glad in your law and generous to the poor. Lord Jesus, who fulfilled the law by love, write your law on our hearts, and let us love you with all our heart and our neighbour as ourselves. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Three months after leaving Egypt, the people stand at Mount Sinai, and God speaks the Ten Commandments. They begin not with a demand but with a reminder: “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” The law is given to a people already rescued. In the Gospel Jesus explains the parable of the sower to his disciples, naming the path, the rock, the thorns and the good soil, and showing what hinders the Word and what lets it bear fruit. Both readings are about hearing God’s word and doing it. Psalm 19 praises the law: “The decrees of the Lord are sure, making wise the simple.” The Church may also remember St Bridget of Sweden.

Exodus 20:1–17 · The ten words

Setting.

Israel has come to Sinai in the third month (19:1). Moses has gone up and down the mountain, and the people have been told to prepare. Now, in the hearing of the whole people, God speaks. The Decalogue is the heart of the covenant at Sinai. The book of Exodus places it before the longer collection of laws (chapters 21–23), and the covenant is sealed in chapter 24, which the Mass reads tomorrow.

The Ten Commandments appear twice in the Bible, here and in Deuteronomy 5:6–21, with small differences, such as the reason given for the Sabbath. The Hebrew Bible calls them the “ten words” (34:28). Different traditions divide them in different ways. The Catholic Church follows the numbering of St Augustine, as the Catechism notes (CCC 2066): the first commandment forbids other gods and idols together (vv. 2–6), and the ninth and tenth are the two commandments against coveting (v. 17).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: God speaks, and introduces himself by what he has done: he brought them out of slavery.
  • vv. 3–6: no other gods; no idols; the Lord is a jealous God, whose mercy reaches to the thousandth generation.
  • v. 7: the name of the Lord is not to be misused.
  • vv. 8–11: remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy, because God rested on the seventh day.
  • vv. 12–17: honour father and mother; do not murder, commit adultery, steal, bear false witness, or covet.

Key words.

  • ʾănōkî YHWH ʾĕlōhêkā, I am the Lord your God: The Ten Words begin with a statement of who God is and what he has done, before they say what he asks. Obedience is the answer of a rescued people.
  • qannāʾ, jealous: In Hebrew this word means a passionate love that will not be shared, like that of a husband for his wife (compare Hosea 2). It is not envy. The punishment that “reaches the third and fourth generation” is set beside mercy that reaches “the thousandth”, so mercy is far greater. Ezekiel 18 later makes clear that each person answers for his or her own sin.
  • tirṣāḥ, you shall not murder: The verb names the unlawful killing of a person, not every taking of life (see, for example, the laws of war and of the court in Exodus 21–22). The Church understands the commandment as a strong protection of every human life (CCC 2258, in substance).

Verse by verse.

“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery;” (20:2)

The law is rooted in grace. God does not first give commands and then rescue those who keep them; he rescues, and then gives the commands as the way of life for those he has rescued.

“you shall have no other gods before me.” (20:3)

The first commandment asks for the whole heart. “Before me” can also be heard as “in my presence”: there is no place where another god may stand.

“but showing steadfast love to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments.” (20:6)

The last word of the first table is mercy. The Hebrew ḥesed is the covenant love that does not fail.

“Remember the sabbath day, and keep it holy.” (20:8)

The Sabbath is a gift of time to be with God. The Church, in memory of the Lord’s Resurrection on the first day of the week, keeps Sunday as the Lord’s Day (CCC 2174).

“Honor your father and your mother, so that your days may be long in the land that the Lord your God is giving you.” (20:12)

This commandment stands at the hinge between the two tables, between God and neighbour. The family is where we first learn what it is to be loved and to owe love.

“You shall not covet your neighbor’s house;” (20:17)

The last commandments look into the heart: wrongdoing begins as desire. Jesus will say the same in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:21–30).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Decalogue means literally “ten words” and that God revealed these words to his people on the holy mountain (CCC 2056). When a rich young man asked what he must do, Jesus answered, “If you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments” (Matthew 19:17; compare CCC 2053), and he sums them up in the double commandment of love of God and neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40). The ban on images does not forbid the Christian veneration of holy images, which honours the person they represent, and the Incarnation of the Son of God has changed what may be shown (CCC 2131–2132, in substance). In the New Covenant the law is written on the heart by the Holy Spirit; it is not set aside. Christians share this reading with the Jewish people, for whom the Ten Words are also a gift, and we honour that bond.

Matthew 13:18–23 · The sower explained

Setting.

After the parable of the sower (13:1–9) Jesus explained to his disciples why he spoke in parables (13:10–17). Now he explains this parable itself. It is the first of three parables of this chapter that he explains, the others being the weeds (13:36–43) and the net (13:49–50). The reading is the heart of the chapter: how does the Word of God meet different hearers?

Many scholars see in the explanation the early Church’s way of applying the parable, shaped by its own experience of preaching; the Church receives it as part of the inspired Gospel, in which Jesus himself teaches how the word is received (compare Mark 4:13–20 and Luke 8:11–15).

Structure.

  • v. 18: “Hear then the parable of the sower.”
  • v. 19: the seed on the path: the word is heard but not understood, and the evil one snatches it away.
  • vv. 20–21: the seed on rocky ground: received with joy, but with no root, it falls away in trouble or persecution.
  • v. 22: the seed among thorns: the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke it.
  • v. 23: the seed on good soil: the one who hears and understands, and bears fruit, a hundredfold, sixty or thirty.

Key words.

  • syniēmi, to understand: Matthew uses it twice in this passage (vv. 19, 23). In the Bible, to understand is more than knowing facts: it is to take the word to heart, so that it shapes the life. The fruit grows from that understanding.
  • skandalizetai, falls away, stumbles: The one on rocky ground “falls away” (literally “is made to stumble”) in trouble or persecution. Matthew’s community knew such pressure. The word names a failure of endurance, not a hatred of the word.
  • merimna, apatē, cares, lure: “Cares of the world” and “the lure of wealth” are the thorns. The Greek word for “lure” can also mean deceit: wealth promises security, and does not keep the promise.

Verse by verse.

“When anyone hears the word of the kingdom and does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away what is sown in the heart;” (13:19)

The seed that is not understood has no defence. The Word needs the effort of the mind and the opening of the heart.

“yet such a person has no root, but endures only for a while,” (13:21)

Joy is a good beginning, but without root, in prayer, the sacraments and the community, it cannot meet hardship.

“but the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke the word, and it yields nothing.” (13:22)

The Word is not driven out by great sins but crowded out by many small cares. Ordinary anxieties and the love of money can leave a good heart without fruit.

“this is the one who hears the word and understands it, who indeed bears fruit” (13:23)

The good soil is not a perfect heart but one that hears, understands and bears fruit. Fruit comes in different measures: a hundred, sixty or thirty, and each is a harvest.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this explanation a call to hear the Word of God with faith and to put it into practice. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she has venerated the Lord’s Body, and urges all the faithful to read them with prayer (Dei Verbum 21–25, in substance). The Catechism teaches that in Baptism the Word is sown in the heart, and that perseverance in faith needs prayer, the sacraments and the support of the community (compare CCC 162, in substance). The four soils are not four kinds of people fixed for ever; most of us are all four at different times, and the Church offers her help, in Word and sacrament, to deepen the soil.

For the Latin reader

St Bridget of Sweden (about 1303–1373) was a noblewoman, the wife of Ulf Gudmarsson, and the mother of eight children, among them St Catherine of Sweden. After her husband’s death she gave herself to prayer and penance and founded the Order of the Most Holy Saviour, the Brigittines, at Vadstena. She spent her last decades in Rome, from 1349, and made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land in 1372. She died in Rome on 23 July 1373. Her visions were gathered in the Revelations; the Church leaves such private revelations to the judgement of faith and does not require belief in them. In 1999 Pope St John Paul II named her, with St Catherine of Siena and St Teresa Benedicta of the Cross, a co-patroness of Europe.

For study and teaching

  • God began by reminding the people what he had done for them, and then he gave the commandments. How does remembering what God has done for me change the way I keep his law?
  • Which “thorn” most often crowds out the Word in my life: anxiety, work, money, or something else? What could I do this week to give the Word more room?
