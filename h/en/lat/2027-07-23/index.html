Word for the day

“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery; you shall have no other gods before me.” Exodus 20:2–3

Reflection

Today's first reading is the centre of the Old Testament: “Then God spoke all these words.” They are given at Sinai, three months after the Exodus, to a people just brought out of slavery. Before any commandment comes a name and a deed: “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” The law is not given to slaves to bind them again, but to keep a liberated people in their liberty. In Hebrew the ten are called the Ten Words, and the Catechism teaches that they are a path of life, the “ten words” by which God says to his people what love of God and of neighbour require (Catechism 2056–2057). The Catholic tradition counts them in this way: the first three are about God (no other gods and no idols; the name of the Lord not misused; the sabbath kept holy), and the last seven about our neighbour (father and mother; no murder; no adultery; no theft; no false witness; no coveting of the neighbour's wife; no coveting of the neighbour's goods). The first word is stern because it protects what love is: “for I the Lord your God am a jealous God.” A jealous God is a God whose love wants all of us, as a faithful spouse wants a faithful spouse. The sabbath is not a rule that makes life harder but a gift: a day of rest, in imitation of the Creator, for family, servants, the foreigner and even the cattle. “Honor your father and your mother” is the first commandment with a promise attached, and the one that holds family and society together. The last words go to the heart: “You shall not covet.” The law reaches inside the heart where every sin begins. For Christians, the sabbath rest is fulfilled on the Lord's Day, the day of the Resurrection (Catechism 2174–2176), and Jesus has told us that he came “not to abolish but to fulfill” the law (Matthew 5:17) and that all of it hangs on the love of God and of neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40). The psalm is the sung answer of a people who delight in this gift: “The law of the Lord is perfect, reviving the soul; the decrees of the Lord are sure, making wise the simple … More to be desired are they than gold, even much fine gold; sweeter also than honey, and drippings of the honeycomb.” We honour the Jewish people, who first received these words and who still cherish the Torah as their delight.

In the Gospel the disciples have heard Jesus tell the parable of the sower, and now he explains it. It is one of only two parables in Matthew's thirteenth chapter that Jesus explains in detail (the other is the parable of the weeds), and he explains it to those who ask. “When anyone hears the word of the kingdom and does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away what is sown in the heart; this is what was sown on the path.” The word is heard but never allowed inside. “As for what was sown on rocky ground, this is the one who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy; yet such a person has no root, but endures only for a while, and when trouble or persecution arises on account of the word, that person immediately falls away.” This is the enthusiast whose joy is shallow. “As for what was sown among thorns, this is the one who hears the word, but the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke the word, and it yields nothing.” This is the busy person, caught up in worries and wanting more. “But as for what was sown on good soil, this is the one who hears the word and understands it, who indeed bears fruit and yields, in one case a hundredfold, in another sixty, and in another thirty.” In Matthew to “understand” is not only to follow an argument; it is to take the word to heart and put it into practice. Put the two readings side by side. At Sinai God gives the law on tablets of stone; the prophets promised that one day he would do something more: “I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts” (Jeremiah 31:33). The parable of the sower shows how this happens: the word is a seed that must be received into the soil of the heart, where it takes root and bears fruit. The law of the Lord, as Psalm 19 says, revives the soul only when it is received. Today the Church remembers St Bridget of Sweden, a medieval noblewoman, wife and mother of eight children, a widow, a mystic and the founder of an order. Her life shows what good soil looks like: a heart in which the word of God took root, and in which the cares of the court did not choke it. The Church calls her one of the patron saints of Europe.

Full homily

Dear friends, the first reading gives us the Ten Commandments. We know them, but listen to how they begin: I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery (Exodus 20:2). Before any command, God says what he has done. The law is not the chain of a master; it is the road for people who have just been set at liberty. God says: I have brought you out; now live as a people at liberty. No other gods; do not misuse my name; keep the sabbath; honor your father and mother; do not kill; do not commit adultery; do not steal; do not lie; do not covet.

The psalm sings of these laws as something sweet: more to be desired than gold, sweeter than honey. Do we think of God's commandments in this way? Sometimes we treat them as a list of things we may not do. But a father who tells his child, do not touch the fire, does so out of love. The commandments are the Father's love in words. And Jesus told us that all of them hang on two: love of God with all your heart and love of your neighbour as yourself. If we love, we keep them.

In the Gospel Jesus explains the parable of the sower. The word of God is the seed, and our hearts are the soil. There is the soil of the path: the word is heard but not understood, and the evil one snatches it away. There is the rocky soil: we receive it with joy, but there are no roots, and when trouble comes we fall away. There is the soil full of thorns: the cares of the world and the lure of wealth choke it. And there is the good soil: the one who hears the word and understands it, and bears fruit. It is not enough to hear the commandments; they must be received into the heart, as the prophet says: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33).

Today we remember St Bridget of Sweden. She was a wife and a mother of eight, she lived in a royal court, and she was not spared sorrow. And yet the word of God took root in her heart, and it bore fruit in prayer, in service of the poor, in a new religious order, and in a long pilgrimage to Rome and the Holy Land. She shows us that the good soil does not belong only to monks and nuns. Let us ask her to help us, in these rainy days when the land is soft, to make our hearts soft too, and let us give the word of God a fixed hour every day. Amen.

For children

Children, God gave his people ten rules, the Ten Commandments, because he loves us and wants us to be happy and unafraid. They tell us to love God, to respect our parents, not to lie, not to steal, and to be kind. Jesus told a story about a farmer and seeds. Some seeds fell on the road, some on rocks, some among thorns, and some on good soil. The seeds are God's words and the soil is our heart. Let us listen carefully, so that our hearts are good soil, and let us ask St Bridget to pray for us.

Prayer

Lord our God, you gave your people the ten words on Mount Sinai and wrote your law in the hearts of those who love you. Make us hear your word and understand it; take the stones out of our hearts and cut away the thorns, so that your word may take root in us and bear fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold. Through the prayers of St Bridget of Sweden, mother, widow and servant of your word, make us faithful to your commandments, glad in your law and generous to the poor. Lord Jesus, who fulfilled the law by love, write your law on our hearts, and let us love you with all our heart and our neighbour as ourselves. Amen.
