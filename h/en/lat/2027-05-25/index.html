Word for the day

“With every gift show a cheerful face, and dedicate your tithe with gladness. Give to the Most High as he has given to you, and as generously as you can afford.” Sirach 35:11–12

Reflection

Ben Sira, the wise teacher of Jerusalem, wrote about two centuries before Christ, when the Temple sacrifices were offered every day. He loved the Temple, and he does not mock the sacrifices. But he wants his students to understand what makes an offering pleasing to God. He begins with a surprising list: “The one who keeps the law makes many offerings; one who heeds the commandments makes an offering of well-being. The one who returns a kindness offers choice flour, and one who gives alms sacrifices a thank offering.” Kindness repaid, alms given, a commandment kept: each of these is already an offering laid on God's altar. “To keep from wickedness is pleasing to the Lord, and to forsake unrighteousness is an atonement.” Then he turns to the gifts themselves, and his advice is warm and practical: “Do not appear before the Lord empty-handed.” “Be generous when you worship the Lord, and do not stint the first fruits of your hands. With every gift show a cheerful face, and dedicate your tithe with gladness.” The measure is simple: “Give to the Most High as he has given to you, and as generously as you can afford. For the Lord is the one who repays, and he will repay you sevenfold.” And one warning: “Do not offer him a bribe, for he will not accept it.” God cannot be bought. The Psalm says the same thing in God's own voice: “Not for your sacrifices do I rebuke you,” but “offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving,” for “those who bring thanksgiving as their sacrifice honor me.”

In the Gospel Peter speaks for all the Twelve. They have just watched a rich young man walk away sad, and heard Jesus say how hard it is for the wealthy to enter the kingdom. Peter blurts out: “Look, we have left everything and followed you.” There is a question hidden in the sentence: and what will we get? Jesus does not scold him. He answers with a promise that is both generous and honest: “there is no one who has left house or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or fields, for my sake and for the sake of the good news, who will not receive a hundredfold now in this age.” The hundredfold is real: the one who leaves a family for Christ finds brothers and sisters and mothers in every Christian community, houses opened in every town. But Jesus adds two words no salesman would add: “with persecutions.” And then the true reward: “in the age to come eternal life.” Ben Sira said God repays sevenfold; Jesus says a hundredfold. You cannot outgive God. St Bede the Venerable, whom the Church remembers today, gave his whole life from the age of seven to the monastery of Jarrow in northern England. He never travelled far, yet through his books he taught all of Europe. Tradition says that on his last day he finished dictating a passage of St John's Gospel in English and died singing “Glory be to the Father.” That is the cheerful face Ben Sira asks for, kept to the end.

Full homily

Dear friends, the wise man Ben Sira lived at a time when the Temple in Jerusalem was full of sacrifices: lambs, bulls, flour, oil. He loved the Temple. But he tells us that there are other sacrifices too. The one who returns a kindness offers choice flour, and one who gives alms sacrifices a thank offering (Sirach 35:3–4). A kind word to a neighbour, a coin to a beggar, a commandment kept when it is hard: all of these are laid on God's altar.

Then he says something very human: with every gift show a cheerful face (Sirach 35:11). We all know people who give, but with a long face, so that everyone knows how much it cost them. Ben Sira says: do not give like that. Give to the Most High as he has given to you. God did not give us life and family and daily bread with a long face. He gave gladly. Our giving should look like his.

And he warns us: do not offer him a bribe, for he will not accept it (Sirach 35:14). Sometimes we treat God like an officer who must be pleased before he signs our paper. I will give this candle, this offering, if you give me that job. God is not like that. We give because he has already given, not to buy his favour.

In the Gospel Peter asks the question we all ask quietly: Look, we have left everything and followed you. What will we get? Jesus answers: a hundredfold now in this age, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life (Mark 10:30). He does not hide the cost. But he promises that nobody who gives to him will be the loser. Today, let us give something gladly, and trust the God who repays sevenfold and a hundredfold. Amen.

For children

Children, imagine your friend gives you a present but makes a grumpy face while giving it. Would you be happy? A wise man in the Bible says: when you give something to God, give it with a smiling face! Jesus told his friends: if you give up something for me, I will give you back a hundred times more. God is the most generous Giver of all. Let us give to him with a big smile!

Prayer

Most High God, everything we have has come from your hand. Teach us to give back to you as you have given to us, generously and with a cheerful face, not to buy your favour but to thank you for it. Lord Jesus, you promised a hundredfold to all who leave everything for your sake; give us courage when following you costs us something, and keep our eyes on the life of the age to come. Through the prayers of St Bede the Venerable, may we serve you gladly to our last breath. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ben Sira, a teacher who loved the Temple, tells his students what makes an offering pleasing to God: keeping the commandments, kindness, almsgiving, a cheerful face, and honest hands. In the Gospel Peter, still thinking of the rich man who went away sad, reminds Jesus that the disciples have left everything. Jesus answers with a promise: a hundredfold now, with persecutions, and eternal life in the age to come. Psalm 50 joins the two: the sacrifice God wants is thanksgiving from a heart that walks in his way.

Sirach 35:1–15 (Lectionary 35:1–12) · Give as he has given to you

Setting.

Jesus Ben Sira taught in Jerusalem about 180 years before Christ, while the Temple sacrifices were still offered every day. He was no enemy of worship. Later in the book he describes the high priest Simon at the altar with deep admiration (chapter 50). But he wants his students to understand what gives the offering its worth.

The passage moves from the inward to the outward. First, a life of obedience and mercy is itself a sacrifice (vv. 1–5). Then, because that is so, the outward offering must not be neglected either: no one should come before the Lord empty-handed (v. 6). The Lectionary stops at v. 12; the further verses in our Bible add the reason: God repays, and he cannot be bribed.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: obedience, kindness, almsgiving and turning from wrong are offerings in their own right.
  • vv. 6–9: do not come empty-handed; the gift of the righteous is accepted and remembered.
  • vv. 10–13: give generously and gladly, as God has given to you, for he repays sevenfold.
  • vv. 14–15: no bribe, no dishonest sacrifice; the Lord is a judge without partiality.

Key words.

  • prosphora, offering: The general word for what is brought to God. Ben Sira lines up the Temple offerings one by one (the offering of well-being, choice flour, the thank offering, atonement) and sets a moral act beside each.
  • eleēmosynē, almsgiving: From eleos, mercy. Alms are mercy made visible. Ben Sira, like Tobit, gives almsgiving a high place among the works pleasing to God (compare Tobit 12:8–9).
  • Hypsistos, the Most High: Ben Sira’s favourite name for God (vv. 8, 12). The one who receives our gifts is above all things and needs nothing from us; that is why he cannot be bribed (v. 14), and why a poor man’s small gift is not despised.

Verse by verse.

“The one who keeps the law makes many offerings;” (35:1)

Ben Sira does not set the law against worship. A faithful life is already worship, and it gives the Temple gift its truth.

“and one who gives alms sacrifices a thank offering.” (35:4)

The thank offering was the sacrifice of a grateful heart. To share one’s goods with the poor is to thank God for what he has given.

“Do not appear before the Lord empty-handed,” (35:6)

The inward attitude does not cancel the outward gift. Israel had been told the same at its pilgrim feasts (Deuteronomy 16:16). The body, the hands and the purse also belong to God.

“Give to the Most High as he has given to you, and as generously as you can afford.” (35:12)

The measure of our giving is God’s giving to us. The verse is realistic too: “as you can afford”. God does not ask for what we do not have; he asks for a generous heart within what we do have.

How the Church reads it.

The Church keeps Ben Sira’s balance. Inward conversion comes first, but it is shown in visible acts. The Catechism notes that Scripture and the Fathers insist above all on three forms of penance, fasting, prayer and almsgiving (CCC 1434). St Paul writes in the same spirit that “God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7). At every Mass the gifts are brought to the altar together with the offering of the faithful for the Church and the poor. Ben Sira would recognise the scene: an honest gift, given with gladness, from a life that keeps the commandments.

Mark 10:28–31 · A hundredfold, with persecutions

Setting.

The scene continues directly from the meeting with the rich man, who went away grieving because he had many possessions (10:17–22), and from Jesus’ words about how hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom (10:23–27). Peter speaks for the Twelve. They have done what the rich man could not.

Structure.

  • v. 28: Peter: “we have left everything”.
  • vv. 29–30: the promise: a hundredfold now, with persecutions, and eternal life in the age to come.
  • v. 31: the first will be last and the last first.

Key words.

  • hekatontaplasiona, a hundredfold: An image of overflowing abundance, like the seed that yields a hundredfold in the parable (4:8, 20).
  • diōgmos, persecution: Only Mark adds “with persecutions” to the list of rewards. His readers, many think in Rome after the persecution under Nero, knew what this meant.
  • aiōn, age: Jewish teaching spoke of “this age” and “the age to come”. Jesus speaks of both: a reward now and eternal life then.

Verse by verse.

“Look, we have left everything and followed you.” (10:28)

It is partly true and partly a question. Peter wants to know whether it was worth it. Jesus does not scold him.

“who will not receive a hundredfold now in this age … houses, brothers and sisters, mothers and children, and fields, with persecutions” (10:30)

The new family is the community of disciples, where those who left home find many homes, many brothers and sisters, many mothers. Notice that “fathers” is not repeated in the list. Some commentators see here a hint that in the new family there is one Father, God (compare Matthew 23:9).

“But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first.” (10:31)

A warning to Peter as well. Those who have left everything must not now count themselves first. The kingdom is always a gift.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone, and to renounce all they have for his sake and that of the Gospel (CCC 2544). The Church has seen in this promise the life of priests and religious who leave home and family, and also the life of every Christian who puts Christ first. The hundredfold is not a business deal. It is the experience of those who find, in the Church, a family wider than blood, and in the midst of trials a joy that does not depend on what they own.

Questions people ask

Does this promise mean that those who give up things will become rich?

No. Jesus speaks of houses and fields in the new family of disciples, shared and given, and he adds “with persecutions”. The promise is about a life made rich in relationships and in God, not about money. The prosperity idea, that faith brings wealth, does not fit a Gospel that has just sent the rich man away sad.

For the Latin reader

Today three optional memorials may be kept. St Bede the Venerable (died 735), a monk of Jarrow in northern England, wrote commentaries on Scripture, including Mark and Luke, and the Ecclesiastical History of the English People; Pope Leo XIII declared him a Doctor of the Church. St Gregory VII, pope from 1073 to 1085, worked to end the control of rulers over the Church’s appointments and died in exile at Salerno. St Mary Magdalene de’ Pazzi (died 1607) was a Carmelite nun of Florence, known for her love of the Eucharist and her prayer for the renewal of the Church. Each of them left something for Christ, and each found the hundredfold.
