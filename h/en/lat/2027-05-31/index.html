Word for the day

“And blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of what was spoken to her by the Lord.” Luke 1:45

Reflection

On the last day of the month of Mary the Church follows a young woman on a journey. The prophet Zephaniah, writing in a dark time for Jerusalem, suddenly breaks into a song of joy addressed to “daughter Zion”: “Sing aloud, O daughter Zion; shout, O Israel! Rejoice and exult with all your heart, O daughter Jerusalem!” The reason for the joy is a presence: “The king of Israel, the LORD, is in your midst; you shall fear disaster no more.” And again: “Do not fear, O Zion; do not let your hands grow weak. The LORD, your God, is in your midst, a warrior who gives victory; he will rejoice over you with gladness, he will renew you in his love; he will exult over you with loud singing as on a day of festival.” The Hebrew for “in your midst” can also mean “within you,” “in your womb.” The Church has always heard in these words the voice of the angel to Mary: “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you” (Luke 1:28), a greeting that in Greek begins with the word “Rejoice,” and then: do not be afraid. Mary is the true daughter Zion, and the Lord is now, literally, within her. The responsorial psalm, the song of Isaiah 12, carries the same joy: “Shout aloud and sing for joy, O royal Zion, for great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel.” Where the second option is read, St Paul's words in Romans 12 sound like a portrait of what Mary does in today's Gospel: “Let love be genuine,” “extend hospitality to strangers,” “Rejoice with those who rejoice,” “do not be haughty, but associate with the lowly.”

Then the Gospel. Mary has just heard the angel's message, and has heard too that her elderly relative Elizabeth, “who was said to be barren,” is in her sixth month. Her first response to the gift of God is not to stay at home and wonder, but to go and serve: “In those days Mary set out and went with haste to a Judean town in the hill country.” It was a journey of perhaps four days on foot, from Nazareth in the north to the hills near Jerusalem, where tradition places the village of Ein Karem. Mary carries the Son of God within her, hidden and very small, and she brings him to another home. St Luke tells the story with words that echo the Old Testament account of the Ark of the Covenant being brought into the hill country of Judah (2 Samuel 6). There, David asked in awe, “How can the ark of the LORD come into my care?”; here Elizabeth asks, “And why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?” There, David leaped and danced before the ark; here, “the child leaped in her womb.” There, the ark stayed three months in the house of Obed-edom and brought blessing; here, “Mary remained with her about three months.” Mary is the new Ark, carrying not the tablets of the Law but the Word made flesh.

Elizabeth, “filled with the Holy Spirit,” speaks the words that the Church repeats millions of times a day in the Hail Mary: “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb.” She calls Mary “the mother of my Lord,” the first time in St Luke's Gospel that a human voice calls Jesus Lord, and the basis of the Church's faith that Mary is truly the Mother of God (Catechism 495). And she praises Mary's faith: “blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of what was spoken to her by the Lord.” Then Mary sings. The Magnificat is woven out of the Psalms and the song of Hannah (1 Samuel 2), the prayer of a young woman who knew the Scriptures by heart. “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior, for he has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant.” She does not boast of herself; she boasts of God, who “has done great things for me.” And then the song widens to the whole of history: God “has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty.” The God who chose a poor girl from Nazareth is the God who takes the side of the poor. Every evening, at Vespers, the Church sings this song with Mary. On this last day of May we may ask her for two gifts: her haste to serve, and her joy in God.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today we close the month of May with a beautiful journey. A young woman from Nazareth, who has just said yes to God, sets out on foot over the hills. She is carrying the Son of God within her. Nobody can see him yet. But wherever she goes, he goes.

The prophet Zephaniah had sung long before: sing aloud, O daughter Zion! The Lord, your God, is in your midst (Zephaniah 3:14, 17). The Hebrew words can mean in your womb. In Mary the prophecy is fulfilled literally. God is not only near his people; he is living inside a young girl from a small village. That is why Mary's whole life is joy.

And what does she do with this joy? St Luke says: Mary set out and went with haste (Luke 1:39). She does not stay at home thinking about her privilege. She hears that her old relative Elizabeth is expecting a baby, and she goes to help. The first thing Mary does with Jesus is to carry him to someone in need. That is the pattern for every Christian: the one who receives Jesus goes out to serve.

When Mary greets her, Elizabeth's child leaps for joy, and Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit. She cries out: blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb. Why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me? (Luke 1:42–43). Every time we pray the Hail Mary, we repeat Elizabeth's words. And she adds: blessed is she who believed. Mary's greatness is first her faith.

Then Mary sings: my soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior (Luke 1:46–47). She sings of a God who looks at the lowly, who brings down the powerful and lifts up the humble, who fills the hungry with good things. Mary is not proud. She is happy because God is great and God is good to the poor. If we want to honour Mary, we must also care for the lowly and the hungry.

Today let us thank Mary for this month of May, for every Rosary prayed in our homes. And let us ask her for her haste: when we see someone in need, let us not delay. Let us visit the sick, the old, the lonely, the expectant mother. Let us carry Jesus to them, as Mary did. And then, with her, our souls too will magnify the Lord. Amen.

For children

Children, after the angel told Mary she would be the mother of Jesus, she heard that her cousin Elizabeth was going to have a baby too. Did Mary say, I am too important now? No! She ran over the hills to help Elizabeth. And when Mary said hello, the baby inside Elizabeth jumped for joy, because baby Jesus was inside Mary!

Today is the last day of May, Mary's month. Let us be like Mary. Is there someone at home you can help today, quickly and with a smile? Then you are bringing Jesus to them, just like Mary did.

Prayer

Almighty ever-living God, who, while the Blessed Virgin Mary was carrying your Son in her womb, inspired her to visit Elizabeth, grant us, we pray, that, faithful to the promptings of the Spirit, we may magnify your greatness with the Virgin Mary at all times. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Holy Mary, Mother of God, you went with haste to serve, carrying Christ within you. Teach us your joy and your readiness. As this month of May ends, take our families under your care, and help us to bring Jesus to every home we enter. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The month of May closes with the feast of the Visitation. Zephaniah calls Daughter Zion to sing, because the King of Israel, the Lord, is in her midst. In the Gospel Mary, who now carries that Lord within her, goes in haste to Elizabeth. The child in Elizabeth’s womb leaps for joy, and Mary sings the Magnificat.

The responsorial canticle from Isaiah 12 repeats the theme: “Great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel.” Instead of Zephaniah, the Mass may use Romans 12:9–16, Paul’s call to sincere love and to rejoicing with those who rejoice: the charity Mary shows in going to serve her kinswoman. The feast replaces the readings of Monday of the Ninth Week in Ordinary Time.

Zephaniah 3:14–18 (or Romans 12:9–16) · The Lord is in your midst

Setting.

Zephaniah prophesied in Judah “in the days of King Josiah” (1:1), in the seventh century before Christ, probably before Josiah’s reform. Most of his book is a stern warning about the “day of the Lord” against the pride and idolatry of Jerusalem and the nations. But the book ends with a promise: a humble and lowly remnant will seek refuge in the Lord (3:12), and the Lord will rejoice over his people.

Our passage is that final song of joy. The mood changes completely: from judgment to celebration, from fear to the presence of God.

Structure.

  • v. 14: the call to joy: sing, shout, rejoice, O daughter Zion.
  • v. 15: the reason: the Lord has taken away the judgments; the King of Israel is in your midst.
  • v. 16: do not fear, O Zion.
  • vv. 17–18: the Lord, a warrior who gives victory, rejoices over you with singing.

Key words.

  • bat-ṣiyyôn, daughter Zion: A tender name for Jerusalem and her people, pictured as a young woman. The Church has seen in Mary the Daughter of Zion in person (Lumen Gentium 55).
  • bəqirbēk, in your midst: Used twice (vv. 15, 17). The same Hebrew word can mean “within you”. Christian readers have heard in it a hint of Mary, in whose womb the Lord came to dwell.
  • rānnî, sing aloud: The first of four cries of joy in v. 14. The Greek translation begins with chaire, “rejoice”, the same word with which the angel greets Mary in Luke 1:28; many commentators hear an echo of Zephaniah there.

Verse by verse.

“Sing aloud, O daughter Zion; shout, O Israel!” (3:14)

The prophet who warned of the day of wrath ends with a song. God’s last word is not judgment but joy.

“The king of Israel, the Lord, is in your midst; you shall fear disaster no more.” (3:15)

The reason for joy is not a change of circumstances but a presence. God himself is with his people.

“he will rejoice over you with gladness, he will renew you in his love; he will exult over you with loud singing” (3:17)

Not only the people rejoice in God; God rejoices over his people. He sings over them like a bridegroom or a mother.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this song on the feast of the Visitation because it is fulfilled in Mary. The Second Vatican Council calls her the exalted Daughter of Zion, in whom, after the long waiting, the times are fulfilled (Lumen Gentium 55). The Catechism calls her the Ark of the Covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells: she is the dwelling of God among men (CCC 2676). The Lord who was in the midst of Zion is now in the midst of Mary, and she carries him into a house in the hill country.

Luke 1:39–56 · The mother of my Lord comes to me

Setting.

The angel Gabriel has told Mary that she will conceive the Son of the Most High, and has given her a sign: her kinswoman Elizabeth, old and called barren, is in her sixth month (1:36). Mary sets out at once. The hill country of Judea lay some days’ walk south of Nazareth. An ancient tradition places Zechariah’s home at Ein Karem, near Jerusalem.

Background. Luke tells the story with echoes of David bringing the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6). David asked, “How can the ark of the Lord come into my care?” (6:9); he danced before the Lord; and the ark stayed in the house of Obed-edom three months (6:11). Elizabeth asks a similar question, John leaps for joy, and Mary stays about three months. Many commentators see in this a picture of Mary as the new Ark, carrying the Lord himself.

Structure.

  • vv. 39–40: Mary goes in haste to the house of Zechariah and greets Elizabeth.
  • vv. 41–45: the child leaps; Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, blesses Mary and her child.
  • vv. 46–50: the Magnificat, first part: what God has done for Mary.
  • vv. 51–55: second part: what God does for the lowly and for Israel.
  • v. 56: Mary stays about three months and returns home.

Key words.

  • meta spoudēs, with haste: Not nervous hurry but eager readiness. Mary does not wait to be served as the mother of the Lord; she goes to serve.
  • eskirtēsen, leaped: The same verb is used in the Greek Old Testament for the twins struggling in Rebekah’s womb (Genesis 25:22). Here the leap is one of joy (v. 44): the forerunner greets the Lord before either is born.
  • megalynei, magnifies: “My soul magnifies the Lord” (v. 46). To magnify is to make great, to proclaim greatness. Mary does not make God greater; she lets his greatness be seen. The Latin Magnificat gives the song its name.

Verse by verse.

“In those days Mary set out and went with haste to a Judean town in the hill country,” (1:39)

St Ambrose comments that the grace of the Holy Spirit knows no slow efforts (in substance). Faith makes Mary set out.

“Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb.” (1:42)

These words, with the angel’s greeting, form the first part of the Hail Mary. When we pray it, we speak with Gabriel and with Elizabeth.

“And why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?” (1:43)

“My Lord” is the name of God. Elizabeth, filled with the Spirit, confesses that the child in Mary’s womb is the Lord. The Church therefore calls Mary the Mother of God (CCC 495).

“And blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of what was spoken to her by the Lord.” (1:45)

The first beatitude in Luke’s Gospel is for faith. Mary is blessed not only because she is the mother but because she believed (CCC 148).

“He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly;” (1:52)

The song echoes the song of Hannah (1 Samuel 2:1–10) and the psalms. God reverses the world’s order, and the poor of the Lord, the anawim, are lifted up.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that John was filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother’s womb, by Christ himself whom the Virgin Mary had just conceived, and that Mary’s visit to Elizabeth became God’s visit to his people (CCC 717). The Magnificat is the song both of the Mother of God and of the Church (CCC 2619); the Church sings it every evening at Vespers. St Ambrose wrote: let the soul of Mary be in each of us to magnify the Lord, let the spirit of Mary be in each to rejoice in God (in substance).

Pope St John Paul II, in Redemptoris Mater 12, saw in the Visitation Mary’s faith confirmed by Elizabeth’s words and her charity shown in service. The feast is also a feast of human life before birth: two unborn children meet, and one of them is God.

Questions people ask

Why do Catholics call Mary “blessed” and honour her so much?

Because Scripture itself says that all generations will call her blessed (1:48), and Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, was the first to do it. The honour given to Mary is not worship, which belongs to God alone. It is praise of what God has done in her, and it always leads to him: “the Mighty One has done great things for me”.

Did Mary herself compose the Magnificat?

Luke presents it as Mary’s song, and the Church prays it as hers. Many scholars note that it is woven from Old Testament songs and psalms, and some think Luke drew on a hymn sung by the early Jewish Christians of Jerusalem. Neither view takes anything away from it: a young woman of Israel who knew the Scriptures by heart could pray in their words, as Jesus himself prayed in the words of the psalms.

What does the leaping of John say about the unborn child?

Luke shows an unborn child responding with joy to the presence of the Lord, himself unborn. The Church sees here the dignity of human life from its beginning. The Catechism teaches that human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception (CCC 2270).

For the Latin reader

The feast of the Visitation was first kept by the Franciscans in the thirteenth century and was extended to the whole Latin Church by Pope Urban VI in 1389. It used to fall on 2 July. Since the reform of the calendar in 1969 it is kept on 31 May, between the Annunciation (25 March) and the birth of St John the Baptist (24 June), so that the order of the Gospel is followed. In many parishes and homes the May devotions to Our Lady end today, often with a procession, the Rosary and the crowning of her statue. The Second Joyful Mystery of the Rosary is this Gospel.
