Word for the day

“For it is the God who said, “Let light shine out of darkness,” who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” 2 Corinthians 4:6

Reflection

Yesterday Paul spoke of Moses coming down from Sinai with a face shining so brightly that the people could not look at him. Exodus tells us that Moses then put a veil over his face, taking it off only when he went in to speak with the Lord. Paul turns this into a picture of how anyone, then or now, can read the Scriptures without seeing their goal: “Indeed, to this very day whenever Moses is read, a veil lies over their minds; but when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.” We must hear this as Paul meant it. He is speaking with grief about his own people, whom he loved so much that he wished he could be cut off for their sake (Romans 9:3); the Church today, following the Second Vatican Council, rejects every blame laid on the Jewish people as a whole and honours them as the people of the covenant God has never revoked. And the veil is not only theirs. Every Christian who reads the Bible coldly, as a book of rules or old stories, has a veil over the heart. The veil falls when one turns to the Lord. Then comes the most beautiful sentence of the chapter: “Now the Lord is the Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom. And all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another.” Moses' face shone for a time; the Christian is being changed day by day into the likeness of Christ. The reading ends at the first page of the Bible: “For it is the God who said, ‘Let light shine out of darkness,’ who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” The Psalm promises the same: “that his glory may dwell in our land,” where “righteousness and peace will kiss each other.”

In the Gospel Jesus begins to show what he meant by fulfilling the Law. “Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.” The scribes and Pharisees were serious, devout men; Jesus asks his disciples to go deeper still, to the root. “You have heard that it was said to those of ancient times, ‘You shall not murder’ … But I say to you that if you are angry with a brother or sister, you will be liable to judgment.” Murder begins in the heart, with anger nursed, with insults like “You fool.” Then Jesus gives an astonishing instruction: “So when you are offering your gift at the altar, if you remember that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go; first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.” God would rather wait for our offering than receive it from a heart at war with a brother. The face of Christ that Paul speaks of is turned towards each person we meet; we cannot see his glory and refuse to look at our neighbour. In these monsoon days the sky can be dark at noon and suddenly bright when the clouds open. So it is with a reconciled heart: the light of Christ breaks through as soon as the quarrel is ended.

Full homily

Dear friends, Moses came down from Mount Sinai with his face shining, and he covered it with a veil. Paul uses this story to speak about our hearts. Sometimes we read the Bible, we hear it at Mass, but it does not touch us. There is a veil over our hearts. Paul says: when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed (2 Corinthians 3:16).

And then something wonderful happens. All of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another (2 Corinthians 3:18). Every time we pray, every time we receive Holy Communion, every time we forgive, we become a little more like Jesus. Holiness is not a sudden jump. It is a slow change, from glory to glory.

But the Gospel tells us where this change must begin. Jesus says: you have heard that it was said, You shall not murder. But I say to you that if you are angry with a brother or sister, you will be liable to judgment (Matthew 5:21–22). Most of us have never killed anyone. But how many of us carry anger for years? How many insults have we spoken? Jesus goes to the root.

Then he says something that should make us stop: if you are offering your gift at the altar and remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there; first be reconciled, and then come and offer your gift (Matthew 5:24). God would rather wait for our gift than receive it from a heart at war. Before the next Mass, let us take one step towards peace. Then our faces too will shine with the light of Christ. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever had a fight with your brother, sister or friend, and then you did not want to look at them? Jesus says that God wants us to make peace quickly. If you come to church to pray and you remember that you hurt someone, go and say sorry first! St Paul says that when we turn to Jesus, our faces shine with his light, like a mirror in the sun. A face that has said sorry shines the brightest!

Prayer

God of light, you said at the beginning, Let there be light, and now you have shone in our hearts in the face of Jesus Christ. Take away every veil from our minds when we read your word, and change us from glory to glory into the image of your Son. Lord Jesus, you see the anger we hide and the quarrels we do not end; give us courage to be reconciled before we bring our gifts to your altar. Let righteousness and peace kiss in our homes and in our land. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul continues his reflection on Moses’ veil: when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed, and all of us, with unveiled faces, are being transformed into Christ’s image from glory to glory. In the Gospel Jesus asks for a righteousness that goes deeper than outward observance: not only no murder, but no anger and no insult, and reconciliation before worship. The glory Paul describes becomes visible in a reconciled community. Psalm 85 sings: the glory of the Lord will dwell in our land.

2 Corinthians 3:15–4:1, 3–6 · With unveiled faces

Setting.

Paul takes up the detail of Exodus 34: after speaking with God, Moses put a veil over his shining face, and removed it when he went in before the Lord. Paul uses the veil as an image. He speaks of his own people, many of whom had not accepted the gospel, and says that a veil lies over their reading of Moses. These are words of a man who loved his people deeply (Romans 9:1–5) and who believed that God would not abandon them (Romans 11:1–2, 29). They must never be used to look down on the Jewish people.

The Lectionary leaves out 4:2, where Paul defends his honesty, and continues with the gospel veiled to those who are perishing and the light shining in our hearts.

Structure.

  • 3:15–16: a veil lies over minds when Moses is read; when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.
  • 3:17–18: the Lord is the Spirit; with unveiled faces we are transformed from glory to glory.
  • 4:1: by God’s mercy we have this ministry and do not lose heart.
  • 4:3–6: the gospel veiled to some; we proclaim Christ, the image of God; God has shone in our hearts.

Key words.

  • katoptrizomenoi, seeing as in a mirror: The word can mean both beholding as in a mirror and reflecting like a mirror. Both meanings fit: we look at the glory of the Lord, and our faces begin to shine with it.
  • metamorphoumetha, we are being transformed: The same verb is used for the Transfiguration of Jesus (Matthew 17:2; Mark 9:2). What happened to Christ on the mountain is to happen slowly in us.
  • eikōn, image: Christ is the image of God (4:4). Humanity was created in God’s image (Genesis 1:27); in Christ that image is restored and made perfect.

Verse by verse.

“but when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed.” (3:16)

Paul echoes Exodus 34:34, where Moses removed the veil when he turned to the Lord. Conversion, not cleverness, opens Scripture.

“Now the Lord is the Spirit, and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom.” (3:17)

The freedom of the Spirit is freedom for love and for God, not freedom from every rule.

“And all of us, with unveiled faces, seeing the glory of the Lord as though reflected in a mirror, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another;” (3:18)

Moses alone saw the glory; now “all of us”. The change is gradual: holiness is growth, day by day, prayer by prayer, Communion by Communion.

“who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” (4:6)

An echo of the first day of creation (Genesis 1:3). Many commentators also hear Paul’s own conversion on the road to Damascus, when a light flashed around him (Acts 9:3). Faith is a new creation.

How the Church reads it.

The Eastern and Western Fathers loved this text. St Athanasius wrote that the Son of God became man so that we might become God (CCC 460), that is, share in the divine nature by grace (2 Peter 1:4). The Church teaches that God’s gifts and call to Israel are irrevocable, and that the Jewish people are not to be presented as rejected (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). The Pontifical Biblical Commission, in its document The Jewish People and their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible (2001), noted that the Jewish reading of the Bible is a possible reading, in continuity with the Jewish Sacred Scriptures of the Second Temple period. Christians read Moses in the light of Christ; they pray that the veil be lifted from their own hearts first.

Matthew 5:20–26 · First be reconciled

Setting.

Verse 20 states the theme of everything that follows: a righteousness that exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees. The scribes and Pharisees were respected teachers who took the Law seriously; Jesus is not mocking their zeal but asking for something deeper, a righteousness of the heart. Then comes the first of six contrasts: “You have heard … but I say to you.”

Structure.

  • v. 20: the greater righteousness.
  • vv. 21–22: not only murder, but anger and insult bring judgment.
  • vv. 23–24: leave your gift at the altar; first be reconciled.
  • vv. 25–26: come to terms quickly with your accuser on the way.

Key words.

  • dikaiosynē, righteousness: In Matthew, right living before God, doing his will. It comes from a heart changed by grace, not from outward correctness alone.
  • raka, empty head: An Aramaic insult kept in the Greek text of v. 22; the NRSV renders it “insult”. Jesus treats contemptuous words as seriously as the courts treated murder.
  • geenna, hell: The valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, once a place of idolatrous child sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:31), became an image of final punishment.

Verse by verse.

“unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.” (5:20)

“Exceeds” means not more rules but more depth: the Law kept from within.

“But I say to you that if you are angry with a brother or sister, you will be liable to judgment;” (5:22)

Jesus speaks with an authority greater than that of a teacher interpreting the Law. He goes to the root: murder begins in anger and contempt.

“leave your gift there before the altar and go; first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.” (5:24)

Jesus imagines a pilgrim in the Temple who remembers a quarrel. Worship and reconciliation belong together; God does not want gifts from a heart that refuses peace.

“Truly I tell you, you will never get out until you have paid the last penny.” (5:26)

The “penny” is the quadrans, the smallest Roman coin. The plain sense is urgency: settle quarrels while there is time. Some Fathers and later writers also heard in it an echo of the purification after death.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in the Sermon on the Mount the Lord recalls the commandment “You shall not kill” and adds to it the forbidding of anger, hatred and vengeance (CCC 2262). It also lists reconciliation with one’s brother before presenting an offering at the altar among the marks of the conversion of heart Jesus asks for in prayer (CCC 2608). The early Christian teaching called the Didache told those who had a quarrel not to join the Sunday Eucharist until they were reconciled (Didache 14). In the Roman Mass the sign of peace before Communion keeps this Gospel before our eyes.

Questions people ask

Is all anger sinful?

No. The Catechism teaches that anger becomes sin when it desires harm for someone (CCC 2302); Scripture also knows a just anger at evil (Ephesians 4:26). Jesus himself showed anger at the hardness of hearts (Mark 3:5). What Jesus forbids here is anger that holds on to resentment and turns into contempt and insult.

For study and teaching

  • Is there someone with whom I need to be reconciled before I next come to the altar? What first step can I take?
  • How does the sign of peace at Mass change, or fail to change, the way I treat people at home?
