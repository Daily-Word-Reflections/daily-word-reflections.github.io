Word for the day

“He woke up and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” Then the wind ceased, and there was a dead calm.” Mark 4:39

Reflection

Few readers in Kerala need to be told what a storm at sea means. In these weeks of the monsoon the waves come high on the beaches of Vizhinjam, Chellanam and Ponnani, the small boats are pulled up on the sand, and fishing families watch the sky. Today's readings are full of that same sea. The first comes from the end of the Book of Job. For thirty-seven chapters Job has suffered, argued with his friends and demanded an answer from God. Now at last “the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind.” But God does not explain Job's suffering. He asks Job to look at creation, and in the verses we hear today he speaks of the sea: “Or who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb?” The sea in the Bible is the image of all that is wild, dark and threatening, the chaos that God tamed at creation. And God speaks of it as a mother speaks of a newborn child: “when I made the clouds its garment, and thick darkness its swaddling band.” The terrifying ocean is, to God, an infant in swaddling clothes. Then he sets its limits: “and prescribed bounds for it, and set bars and doors, and said, ‘Thus far shall you come, and no farther, and here shall your proud waves be stopped.’” Job is not answered with an argument; he is answered with a Presence greater than his storm. The Psalm tells the story of sailors caught in such a storm: “They mounted up to heaven, they went down to the depths; their courage melted away in their calamity.” Then “they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he brought them out from their distress; he made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed.”

Mark's Gospel takes us onto the Sea of Galilee at evening. Jesus has spent the day teaching the crowds in parables from a boat. Now he says, “Let us go across to the other side,” to the Gentile shore. The disciples take him “just as he was,” tired from the day. A sudden windstorm comes down on the lake, as often happens there, and “the waves beat into the boat, so that the boat was already being swamped.” Several of these men were fishermen who knew this lake; they are terrified. And Jesus? “He was in the stern, asleep on the cushion.” Only Mark gives us that small detail, the cushion, the kind of thing an eyewitness remembers; the ancient tradition says Mark wrote down Peter's preaching. They wake him with a cry that is half prayer, half reproach: “Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?” Many of us have prayed that prayer. Jesus rises. “He woke up and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, ‘Peace! Be still!’ Then the wind ceased, and there was a dead calm.” He speaks to the storm as he spoke to the unclean spirit in the synagogue of Capernaum, with a word of command. Then he turns to his disciples: “Why are you afraid? Have you still no faith?” And they are filled with a new and greater fear, the awe that falls on people in the presence of God: “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” Every Jew who knew Job 38 and Psalm 107 knew the answer. Only the Lord shuts in the sea with doors; only the Lord makes the storm be still. The man asleep on the cushion is the Creator of the sea. Mark does not write the answer; he leaves it for us to say.

St Paul gives us the answer in the second reading, and shows us what follows from it. “For the love of Christ urges us on, because we are convinced that one has died for all; therefore all have died. And he died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and was raised for them.” The one who calmed the sea is the one who went down into the deepest storm of all, death itself, and rose. He did it for all. So Paul can say: “From now on, therefore, we regard no one from a human point of view.” We can no longer look at anyone only by caste, wealth, language or religion; each one is a person for whom Christ died. “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” The God who in the beginning set bounds to the sea is making all things new in Christ. The early Christians loved to see the Church in the boat on the lake: tossed by the waves of history, often afraid, sometimes feeling that the Lord is asleep, but never alone, because he is in the boat. Pope Francis preached on this Gospel on the evening of 27 March 2020, alone in a rain-soaked St Peter's Square during the pandemic, and said that we had discovered that we are all in the same boat. Jesus does not promise us a sea without storms. He promises that he is in the boat, and that the storm has a limit: thus far, and no farther.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, in these monsoon weeks we know what a storm is. The rain beats on the roof, the rivers rise, and on our coasts the fishing boats lie on the sand while the sea roars. Today the Word of God takes us out onto the water, into a storm, and asks us a simple question: who is in the boat with you?

In the first reading God answers Job out of the whirlwind. Job had suffered terribly and asked God why. God does not give him an explanation. He speaks of the sea. Who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb? (Job 38:8). To us the sea is huge and frightening. To God it is like a newborn baby wrapped in cloth. And God says to it: thus far shall you come, and no farther, and here shall your proud waves be stopped (Job 38:11). Every storm has a limit, and God sets it.

In the Gospel the disciples are on the lake at night. A great windstorm comes, the waves fill the boat, and these strong fishermen are terrified. And Jesus is asleep on a cushion in the stern. They wake him: Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing? (Mark 4:38). Have we not prayed like that? Lord, my child is sick, my husband has no work, my family is breaking: do you not care?

Jesus gets up. He rebukes the wind and says to the sea: Peace! Be still! And there is a dead calm (Mark 4:39). Then he asks them: why are you afraid? Have you still no faith? And they ask each other: who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him? The answer is in the first reading. Only God can command the sea. The man sleeping on the cushion is the Lord of creation. He is truly man, tired enough to sleep in a storm, and truly God, able to calm it with a word.

St Paul tells us what this Lord has done for us: the love of Christ urges us on, because we are convinced that one has died for all (2 Corinthians 5:14). He went down into the greatest storm, death itself, and came out victorious. So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation. Jesus does not promise that our boat will never meet a storm. He promises that he is in the boat, and that the storm will not have the last word.

Today many of us remember our fathers. A good father is not a man who never faces storms. He is a man who stays in the boat with his family and keeps calling on the Lord. Let us pray for our fathers, for our fishermen at sea, and for everyone in a storm today. And when we are afraid, let us not be ashamed to wake the Lord with our prayer. He hears, and he says to us: Peace! Be still! Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever been frightened by thunder and lightning at night? Jesus' friends were in a boat when a huge storm came, and the waves splashed in. They were very scared. But Jesus was asleep! They woke him up, and Jesus stood up and said to the wind: Peace! Be still! And the storm stopped.

Jesus is stronger than every storm. So when you hear thunder, or when something at school or at home makes you afraid, you can say: Jesus, you are with me. I am not afraid. And today, give your Papa a big hug and say thank you to him.

Prayer

Grant, O Lord, that we may always revere and love your holy name, for you never deprive of your guidance those you set firm on the foundation of your love. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you slept in the boat and rose to rebuke the wind. When the waves beat into our homes and our hearts, and we cry out that you do not care, wake in us the faith that you are with us in the boat. Say to our storms: Peace! Be still! Protect fishermen and all who travel on the sea, bless our fathers, and let your love urge us on, so that we live no longer for ourselves but for you, who died and were raised for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The readings of this Sunday are held together by the sea. In the first reading God answers Job out of the whirlwind and reminds him who shut in the sea and said to it, “Thus far shall you come, and no farther.” Psalm 107 sings of sailors caught in a storm who cried to the Lord, and “he made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed.” In the Gospel Jesus, asleep in the boat, rises, rebukes the wind and says to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” The disciples ask the question the whole of Mark’s Gospel wants us to answer: “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” The first reading has already given the clue: only the Creator commands the sea.

The second reading, from Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, gives the reason a Christian can face the storms of life without fear: the love of Christ, who died for all, so that those who live might live for him. In him there is a new creation. The Sundays of Ordinary Time in Year B read Mark’s Gospel, and this passage closes the chapter of parables with an act that reveals who Jesus is.

Job 38:1, 8–11 · Thus far shall you come

Setting.

The Book of Job tells of a righteous man who loses his children, his wealth and his health. Through a long debate (chapters 3–37) his three friends insist that suffering must be punishment for sin. Job protests his innocence and calls on God to answer him. At last, in chapter 38, God speaks “out of the whirlwind”. He does not explain why Job has suffered. Instead he asks Job a long series of questions about creation (chapters 38–41), opening before him a world far larger than human sight. Job’s answer at the end is humble trust (42:1–6).

Background. Many peoples of the ancient Near East pictured the sea as a power of chaos, and some of their stories told of gods who fought against it. In the Bible the sea is no rival god. It is a creature. In today’s verses God handles the mighty sea as a mother handles a newborn child: it bursts from the womb, and he wraps it in clouds and swaddling bands, and then sets it within limits. The Lectionary leaves out vv. 2–7, where God asks Job where he was when the foundations of the earth were laid.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the Lord answers Job out of the whirlwind.
  • v. 8: who shut in the sea with doors when it burst from the womb?
  • v. 9: the clouds its garment, thick darkness its swaddling band.
  • vv. 10–11: bounds, bars and doors; “Thus far shall you come, and no farther.”
  • ¶ The questions are not meant to crush Job but to enlarge him. The God who knows the limits of the sea also knows the limits of Job’s suffering.

Key words.

  • sĕʿārâ, whirlwind, storm: God speaks from the storm, the very power that frightens human beings. The storm is not outside his rule; it is the place from which he speaks.
  • yām, sea: For Israel, a people of the land, the sea was a place of danger and the picture of everything wild and threatening. That God sets its limits means that no chaos is beyond his reach.
  • ḥōq, bound, decree: A word for a fixed limit or a statute. The God who gives laws to Israel gives a law to the sea (compare Proverbs 8:29; Jeremiah 5:22).

Verse by verse.

“Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind:” (38:1)

After thirty-five chapters of human words, God speaks. The answer is first of all a presence: Job is no longer talking about God, but hearing him.

“Or who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb?” (38:8)

The sea is pictured as a newborn bursting into the world. Its strength is real, but it is the strength of a creature, not of a god.

“when I made the clouds its garment, and thick darkness its swaddling band,” (38:9)

A tender image. The ocean that terrifies sailors is, to its Maker, like an infant wrapped in cloth.

“Thus far shall you come, and no farther, and here shall your proud waves be stopped” (38:11)

The waves are “proud”: they rise as if they could overrun everything. God’s word stops them. The Gospel will show the same word on the lips of Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as a confession of God the Creator, who does not abandon his creatures but upholds them in being and guides them (CCC 301). It does not solve the mystery of suffering with a formula. The Catechism says that there is no quick answer to the question why evil exists; only the Christian faith as a whole constitutes the answer (CCC 309). At its heart stand the Cross and Resurrection of Christ. St Gregory the Great wrote a great commentary on Job, the Moralia, in which he read Job’s patience as a figure of Christ and of the Church in its trials.

2 Corinthians 5:14–17 · A new creation

Setting.

In chapters 1–7 of this letter Paul explains to the Corinthians the ministry he has received. Some accused him of acting for his own advantage. In 5:11–21 he tells them what really drives him: not self-interest, but the love of Christ, and the conviction that Christ’s death has changed everything. Just after our verses he will speak of God “reconciling the world to himself” in Christ, and of the ministry of reconciliation (5:18–20).

Structure.

  • v. 14: the love of Christ urges us on: one has died for all, therefore all have died.
  • v. 15: he died for all, so that the living might live no longer for themselves but for him.
  • v. 16: from now on we regard no one from a human point of view.
  • v. 17: if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation.

Key words.

  • synechei, urges us on: The verb means to hold tightly, to press in on every side, to control. The love of Christ grips Paul and leaves him no other way. “The love of Christ” here is first Christ’s love for us, which then awakens our love for him.
  • kata sarka, from a human point of view: Literally “according to the flesh”: judging by outward and worldly standards, such as status, eloquence, or success. Paul once judged Jesus that way, as a failed and crucified man. Now he sees him as the risen Lord.
  • kainē ktisis, new creation: Not only a new start but a new act of the Creator. Isaiah had heard God say, “I am about to do a new thing” (Isaiah 43:19). In Christ that new thing has begun.

Verse by verse.

“For the love of Christ urges us on,” (5:14)

The engine of Paul’s whole ministry. He does not serve out of fear or for reward, but because he has been loved.

“And he died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves,” (5:15)

Christ’s death has a purpose in us: a life turned outward, toward him and toward others.

“From now on, therefore, we regard no one from a human point of view;” (5:16)

A new way of seeing people follows. No one is judged by wealth, caste, language or rank; each is someone for whom Christ died.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (5:17)

“See” is the voice of wonder. The world is the same, yet for the one in Christ it is seen and lived anew.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches, with the apostles, that Christ died for all people without exception (CCC 605, citing this passage). Baptism makes the newly baptised “a new creature” (CCC 1265), and the baptised person belongs no longer to himself but to him who died and rose for us (CCC 1269). This is why a Christian can face the storms of life without despair: life is no longer lived for oneself alone, but in the hands of the One who has already passed through death.

Mark 4:35–41 · Who then is this?

Setting.

It is the evening of the day on which Jesus taught the crowds in parables from a boat by the shore (4:1–34). Now he says, “Let us go across to the other side.” The other side is the eastern shore of the lake, a largely Gentile region, where he will heal the man possessed by many demons (5:1–20).

Background. The Sea of Galilee is a lake about twenty-one kilometres long, lying some two hundred metres below sea level and ringed by hills. Cold winds can drop suddenly into the basin and whip up dangerous waves within minutes. Several of the disciples were fishermen who knew the lake well; their terror tells us how violent this storm was.

Structure.

  • vv. 35–36: evening; “Let us go across”; they take him in the boat “just as he was”.
  • v. 37: a great windstorm; the boat is being swamped.
  • v. 38: Jesus asleep on the cushion; “Teacher, do you not care?”
  • v. 39: he rebukes the wind and speaks to the sea; a dead calm.
  • vv. 40–41: “Why are you afraid?” and “Who then is this?”
  • ¶ The story ends with two questions, one from Jesus and one from the disciples. Jesus asks about their faith; they ask about his identity. The two belong together: to have faith is to know who is in the boat.

Key words.

  • epitimaō, to rebuke: The same verb Mark uses when Jesus rebukes unclean spirits (1:25; 3:12; 9:25). Jesus speaks to the storm as to a hostile power that must yield to him.
  • siōpa, pephimōso, “Peace! Be still!”: Literally “Be silent! Be muzzled!” The second verb is the one Jesus used to the unclean spirit in the synagogue at Capernaum: “Be silent” (1:25). One word from him, and the chaos is silenced.
  • megas, great: Mark uses this word three times: a great windstorm (v. 37), a great calm (v. 39, “dead calm”), and, literally, “they feared a great fear” (v. 41). The great fear of the storm becomes the greater awe before Jesus himself.

Verse by verse.

“But he was in the stern, asleep on the cushion;” (4:38)

Only Mark mentions the cushion, a vivid detail that early tradition linked with the preaching of Peter. Jesus is truly human and tired after a long day. His sleep is also the sleep of perfect trust (compare Psalm 4:8).

“Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?” (4:38)

The cry of every believer in a storm. Mark does not hide the disciples’ rough words; Matthew and Luke soften them (Matthew 8:25; Luke 8:24).

“He woke up and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!”” (4:39)

Psalm 107, today’s psalm, says of the Lord: “he made the storm be still”. Jesus does with a word what the psalms say only God does.

“Why are you afraid? Have you still no faith?” (4:40)

Jesus does not blame them for waking him, but for thinking that, with him in the boat, they could be lost.

“Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” (4:41)

The question is left open for the reader. The Old Testament has already answered it: God, of whom the psalm says, “You silence the roaring of the seas” (Psalm 65:7).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs worked by Jesus attest that the Father has sent him, and invite belief in him (CCC 548). The fathers loved this story. St Augustine told his people that when the storm of temptation or anger rises in us, it is because Christ is asleep in our heart, that is, we have forgotten our faith; we must wake him by remembering him, and the sea will grow calm (in substance). Tertullian already saw in the little boat a figure of the Church, tossed by the waves of the world but carrying the Lord (On Baptism 12, in substance). Many commentators also hear an echo of Jonah, asleep in the ship during the storm while the sailors feared greatly (Jonah 1). But here is one greater than Jonah: he does not need to be thrown into the sea; he commands it.

Questions people ask

Did the stilling of the storm really happen, or is it only a picture?

The Gospel tells it as an event that the disciples remembered, with vivid details such as the cushion in the stern. The Church believes that Jesus truly worked miracles, as signs that the kingdom of God is present in him (CCC 547–548). Because it really happened, it can also become a picture for us: the boat of our life and of the Church, the storms, and the Lord who is present even when he seems to sleep.

Why does God seem to sleep when we are suffering?

The psalms themselves cry, “Rouse yourself! Why do you sleep, O Lord?” (Psalm 44:23). Scripture does not forbid this cry; Jesus does not reject the disciples who wake him. Yet Psalm 121:4 says that he who keeps Israel “will neither slumber nor sleep”. Jesus was asleep in the boat, but he was in the boat. His seeming silence is not absence. Faith learns to hold on in the dark, as Job did, knowing that the one who sets limits to the sea also sets limits to our trials (compare 1 Corinthians 10:13).

Does God ever answer Job’s question about suffering?

Not with an explanation. God answers Job with himself: he speaks, he shows his care for all creation, and Job finds peace in his presence. Only the Christian faith as a whole answers the question of evil (CCC 309), and its heart is Christ, who enters our suffering, dies, and rises again. The disciples in the boat do not get an explanation of the storm either; they get Jesus.

For the Latin reader

This Twelfth Sunday in Ordinary Time falls in the season of the monsoon, when the sea along the coasts of India is at its roughest. Many Latin Catholic parishes along the western and eastern coasts are communities of fishermen, and they know from long experience what the disciples felt in the boat. On the western coast, in June and July boats are often held back from the deep sea by the weather and the seasonal ban on fishing, and families feel the strain. This Gospel invites us to pray for all who work at sea, and for migrants and refugees who cross dangerous waters in many lands. The Church has a special ministry for seafarers, the Apostleship of the Sea, now called Stella Maris, and in many coastal parishes boats and nets are blessed. At Mass we confess the same Lord whom the disciples met in the boat.

For study and teaching

  • God speaks to Job out of the storm. Has a hard time in my life become a place where I heard God more clearly?
  • Jesus asks, “Why are you afraid?” What storm is frightening me now, and what would it mean to remember that he is in the boat?
  • Paul says that those who live should live no longer for themselves. In my family, where could I live a little less for myself this week?
  • How can our parish, or our family, be like a boat that carries the Lord to “the other side”, to people who do not yet know him?
