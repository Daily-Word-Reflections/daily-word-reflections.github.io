Word for the day

“He sat down, called the twelve, and said to them, “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.”” Mark 9:35

Reflection

After the great feasts of Easter and Pentecost, the Church takes us back to the school of daily life, and the teacher she chooses is Jesus ben Sira, a wise man of Jerusalem who wrote about two hundred years before Christ. His first lesson for anyone who wants to belong to God is honest and a little startling: “My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing.” Faith is not an insurance policy against trouble. Ben Sira does not promise an easy road; he promises a faithful God. “Set your heart right and be steadfast, and do not be impetuous in time of calamity. Cling to him and do not depart.” Then comes the image that has comforted believers for centuries: “For gold is tested in the fire, and those found acceptable, in the furnace of humiliation.” The goldsmith does not put gold in the fire to destroy it but to make it pure. Three times the reading repeats “You who fear the Lord”: wait for his mercy, trust in him, hope for good things. And it ends with a challenge to look at history: “Consider the generations of old and see: has anyone trusted in the Lord and been disappointed?” The answer the whole Bible gives is no, “for the Lord is compassionate and merciful; he forgives sins and saves in time of distress.” The Psalm adds its counsel: “Trust in the Lord, and do good.”

In the Gospel Jesus is walking through Galilee, quietly, “for he was teaching his disciples.” For the second time he tells them plainly what lies ahead: “The Son of Man is to be betrayed into human hands, and they will kill him, and three days after being killed, he will rise again.” This is the testing Ben Sira spoke of, and Jesus walks into it first. “But they did not understand what he was saying and were afraid to ask him.” While their Master speaks of the cross, the disciples are busy with another question. At Capernaum he asks them, “What were you arguing about on the way?” Nobody answers, “for on the way they had argued with one another who was the greatest.” Jesus does not scold them. He sits down, as a teacher does, and gives them a new measure of greatness: “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.” Then he takes a little child, in that world a person with no rank or rights, puts the child in the middle of them, and holds the child in his arms: “Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me.” Today the Church remembers St John I, an elderly pope who in 526 died in a prison at Ravenna after being sent on a difficult mission by a suspicious king. He did not seek the first place; he accepted the furnace, and was found to be gold.

Full homily

Dear friends, Pentecost is over and the ordinary weeks of the year have begun again. The first word the Church gives us is not a soft one. My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing (Sirach 2:1). Some people imagine that once we give our lives to God, everything will go smoothly. Ben Sira, a wise teacher of Jerusalem, says the opposite: get ready.

But he gives us a beautiful picture. Gold is tested in the fire (Sirach 2:5). A goldsmith in Thrissur or Kozhikode heats the gold not to spoil it but to burn away what is not gold. When the fire of trouble comes, God is not destroying us. He is purifying us. And Ben Sira asks: has anyone trusted in the Lord and been disappointed? Look back at your own life, and the lives of your parents. God has never failed those who clung to him.

In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples for the second time that he will be betrayed, killed and raised. And what are they talking about on the road? Who is the greatest (Mark 9:34). It is almost funny, and it is very human. We do the same: in families, in offices, even in parish committees. Jesus does not shout. He sits down, takes a little child in his arms, and says: whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.

Today we remember St John I, an old pope who was sent on a hard journey, then thrown into prison by a king who did not trust him, where he died. He did not choose the first place. He accepted the fire and was found to be pure gold. Let us ask for his courage: to cling to the Lord in trial, and to spend our lives serving the little ones. Amen.

For children

Children, the disciples once had a big argument: who is the most important? Jesus took a small child, maybe as small as you, held the child in his arms and said: if you want to be first, help everybody. So in God's eyes the most important person is the one who serves. Can you help someone today without being asked?

Prayer

Lord our God, compassionate and merciful, you test your servants as gold is tested in the fire, and you never forsake those who trust in you. Keep our hearts steadfast in every trial. Lord Jesus, you walked to your cross as the servant of all; cure us of the desire to be the greatest, and teach us to welcome the little ones in your name. Through the prayers of St John I, who suffered in prison for the Church, make us faithful to the end. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ordinary Time has resumed. Sirach warns the one who comes to serve the Lord to be ready for testing, and encourages him: look at the generations of old; has anyone trusted in the Lord and been disappointed? In the Gospel Jesus again tells the disciples that he will be handed over, killed and rise, and they argue about who is the greatest. He places a child among them. Psalm 37 answers: “Commit your life to the Lord, and he will help you.”

Sirach 2:1–11 · Prepare yourself for testing

Setting.

Jesus ben Sira was a teacher of wisdom in Jerusalem around 180 BC. His grandson translated the book into Greek in Egypt some time after 132 BC, as he explains in his prologue. Large parts of the Hebrew text have been found in modern times, in the Cairo Genizah and at Masada. The book is one of the deuterocanonical books, received by the Catholic Church as Scripture. Its Latin name, Ecclesiasticus, “the church book”, comes from its wide use in teaching the faithful.

Chapter 1 praised wisdom and the fear of the Lord. Chapter 2 turns to the one who sets out to live it: the road will include trials.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–6: whoever serves the Lord will be tested: be steadfast, cling to him, accept, trust; gold is tested in fire.
  • vv. 7–9: three calls: “You who fear the Lord”: wait for his mercy, trust him, hope for good things.
  • vv. 10–11: look at the generations of old; the Lord is compassionate and merciful.

Key words.

  • peirasmos, testing: The trial that proves and purifies faith. It is not the same as temptation to sin. The same Greek word can mean both, and the context decides.
  • phobos kyriou, fear of the Lord: Not terror but reverent love that keeps one faithful; “the beginning of wisdom” (Sirach 1:14). The phrase returns again and again in today’s passage.
  • eleos, mercy: God’s steadfast, faithful kindness. Verse 11 echoes the great self-revelation of God to Moses: “merciful and gracious” (Exodus 34:6).

Verse by verse.

“My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing.” (2:1)

A teacher speaks to his pupil as a father to a son. He does not promise an easy road; he prepares the young believer for it.

“For gold is tested in the fire, and those found acceptable, in the furnace of humiliation.” (2:5)

The same image appears in Wisdom 3:6 and 1 Peter 1:7. Fire does not destroy gold; it purifies it.

“has anyone trusted in the Lord and been disappointed?” (2:10)

An appeal to memory. Israel’s history, with Abraham, Joseph, Moses and David, shows that God does not abandon those who trust him.

“For the Lord is compassionate and merciful; he forgives sins and saves in time of distress.” (2:11)

The last word is not the trial but the mercy of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, explaining the petition “lead us not into temptation”, teaches that the Holy Spirit helps us to discern between trials, which are necessary for the growth of the inner person, and temptation, which leads to sin and death (CCC 2847). The New Testament takes up Sirach’s teaching: “whenever you face trials of any kind, consider it nothing but joy” (James 1:2), because tested faith produces endurance.

Mark 9:30–37 · Whoever wants to be first must be last

Setting.

This is the second of Jesus’ three predictions of his Passion in Mark (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34). It comes after the Transfiguration and the healing of the boy with a spirit (9:2–29). Jesus is on the way that leads to Jerusalem. Each prediction is followed by the disciples’ misunderstanding and by teaching about the way of discipleship.

Structure.

  • vv. 30–32: passing through Galilee in secret; the prediction; they do not understand and are afraid to ask.
  • vv. 33–34: in the house at Capernaum: “What were you arguing about?” Silence.
  • v. 35: the first must be last of all and servant of all.
  • vv. 36–37: the child in his arms; whoever welcomes a child welcomes me.

Key words.

  • paradidotai, is to be betrayed, handed over: A present tense: it is already happening. The same verb is used of Judas handing Jesus over, and of God giving up his Son for us (Romans 8:32).
  • diakonos, servant: One who waits at table. Jesus will say that he came “not to be served but to serve” (Mark 10:45).
  • paidion, little child: In that world a child had no status and no rights. To welcome a child was to welcome one who could give nothing in return.

Verse by verse.

“But they did not understand what he was saying and were afraid to ask him.” (9:32)

They are afraid of the answer. Sometimes we too prefer not to ask what following Jesus will cost.

“But they were silent, for on the way they had argued with one another who was the greatest.” (9:34)

While Jesus speaks of the Cross, they speak of rank. Their silence shows that they know they were wrong.

“He sat down, called the twelve, and said to them, “Whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all.”” (9:35)

He sits, as a teacher sits to teach. He does not forbid the desire to be first; he shows the way to it: service.

““Whoever welcomes one such child in my name welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes not me but the one who sent me.”” (9:37)

Jesus identifies himself with the least. To welcome the little one is to welcome Christ, and in him the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Council teaches that the office the Lord gave to the pastors of his people is a true service, which Scripture significantly calls diakonia, ministry (Lumen Gentium 24). Since St Gregory the Great the Pope has called himself “servant of the servants of God”. The Church hears in this Gospel a call to every Christian, and above all to those who lead, to measure greatness by service, and to welcome the smallest: children, the poor, the sick and the unborn.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus forbid ambition?

No. He does not condemn the wish to be first; he turns it upside down. The way to true greatness is to be “servant of all”. Using our gifts well and aiming high are good when they serve others and not our own pride.

For the Latin reader

Today is the optional memorial of St John I, Pope and Martyr. He was Bishop of Rome from 523 to 526. The Arian king Theodoric, ruler of Italy, sent him to Constantinople to plead with the emperor Justin on behalf of the Arians in the East. When he returned, the king, suspecting him, held him in prison at Ravenna, where he died in 526. He is honoured as a martyr. Sirach’s word fits him: “when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing.”

For study and teaching

  • Sirach asks, “has anyone trusted in the Lord and been disappointed?” When has God been faithful to me in a time of trial?
  • Who is the “child” in my life, the person with no status, whom Jesus asks me to welcome in his name?
