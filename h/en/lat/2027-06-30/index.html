Word for the day

“And God heard the voice of the boy; and the angel of God called to Hagar from heaven, and said to her, “What troubles you, Hagar? Do not be afraid; for God has heard the voice of the boy where he is.” Genesis 21:17

Reflection

The promise is fulfilled at last: “Abraham was a hundred years old when his son Isaac was born to him.” Sarah's laugh of doubt has become a laugh of joy. But today's reading does not let us rest in the happy ending. At the feast for Isaac's weaning Sarah sees Ishmael, the son of the Egyptian slave woman Hagar, “playing with her son Isaac,” and she demands: “Cast out this slave woman with her son.” Scripture does not hide the hurt in this family. “The matter was very distressing to Abraham on account of his son,” for Ishmael too is his child. God tells Abraham to let them go, because the covenant line will pass through Isaac, but adds a promise for the other son: “As for the son of the slave woman, I will make a nation of him also, because he is your offspring.” Early in the morning Abraham gives Hagar bread and a skin of water and sends her away with the child. The scene in the wilderness of Beer-sheba is one of the most heartbreaking in the Bible. The water runs out. Hagar puts the boy under a bush and sits down “about the distance of a bowshot,” because she cannot bear to watch him die, and she weeps aloud. And then: “God heard the voice of the boy.” The name Ishmael means “God hears.” The angel calls from heaven: “What troubles you, Hagar? Do not be afraid.” Then “God opened her eyes and she saw a well of water.” The well was there all along; she could not see it through her tears. “God was with the boy, and he grew up.” This is the second time Hagar has met God in the desert (Genesis 16), and she is the only person in the Old Testament who gives God a name: “You are El-roi,” the God who sees (Genesis 16:13). The God of Abraham is not the God of one family only; he hears the cry of the outcast, the foreign slave woman, the thirsty child. Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions all remember Hagar and Ishmael. The Psalm sings the same truth: “This poor soul cried, and was heard by the LORD, and was saved from every trouble.”

In the Gospel Jesus crosses the lake to “the country of the Gadarenes,” Gentile territory east of the Sea of Galilee, where pigs were kept. Two men possessed by demons come out of the tombs to meet him, so violent that “no one could pass that way.” They live among the dead, cut off from every human community. The demons recognise Jesus at once and cry out: “What have you to do with us, Son of God? Have you come here to torment us before the time?” They know that their time is short, that the Son of God has come to destroy their power. They beg to be sent into a herd of swine, and Jesus says one word: “Go!” The whole herd rushes down the steep bank into the sea and perishes. The two men are set at liberty. But the ending is sad and surprising: “the whole town came out to meet Jesus; and when they saw him, they begged him to leave their neighborhood.” They had lost their pigs; they did not want to lose anything more. Two human beings had been restored, but the town counted its losses. It is possible to prefer our comfort, our business and our habits to the Lord who sets people at liberty. Hagar's eyes were opened and she saw the well; the Gadarenes saw the Lord and asked him to go. Today the Church also remembers the first martyrs of the Church of Rome, the many Christians whom Nero blamed for the great fire of the city in the year 64. The Roman historian Tacitus records that they were mocked, torn by dogs, crucified, and burned as torches in the night. Some were probably among those to whom St Paul had written his Letter to the Romans. Their names are known to God alone. Unlike the Gadarenes, they did not ask Jesus to leave; they let everything go rather than lose him.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's first reading is a hard story. At last Isaac is born, the son of the promise. But in the same house there is another boy, Ishmael, the son of the slave woman Hagar. Sarah wants them sent away. Abraham is deeply distressed, because Ishmael is his son too. And early in the morning Hagar and her boy are sent into the desert with a little bread and a skin of water.

The water runs out. Hagar lays the child under a bush, goes a bowshot away and weeps, because she cannot watch him die. And then the Bible says: God heard the voice of the boy (Genesis 21:17). Do not be afraid. God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. The well was there all the time. She could not see it through her tears. How often that happens to us. We are so full of fear that we do not see the help God has already placed beside us.

And notice whom God hears: not the important people at the feast, but a foreign slave woman and a crying child in the desert. Ishmael's name means God hears. Our God is the God who hears the poor. If we want to be his people, we must hear them too: the migrant worker, the abandoned mother, the child who cries at night.

In the Gospel Jesus delivers two men who lived among the tombs. A whole herd of pigs is lost. And the people of the town come out and beg Jesus to leave. They preferred their pigs to their neighbours' deliverance, and their business to the Son of God. Today we also remember the first martyrs of Rome, who were burned and crucified under Nero. They did the opposite of the Gadarenes: they lost everything rather than lose Jesus. Let us ask for their courage, and let us never ask the Lord to leave. Amen.

For children

Children, a mother named Hagar and her little boy Ishmael were walking in the hot desert. Their water was finished, and the boy was crying. His mother was crying too. But God heard the boy! An angel said: do not be afraid. God opened Hagar's eyes and she saw a well full of water! God always hears children when they cry. So when you are sad or afraid, talk to God. He is listening.

Prayer

God of Abraham, you heard the voice of the boy Ishmael and opened Hagar's eyes to see the well. Hear the cry of the poor, the outcast and the thirsty in our land, and open our eyes to the help you have already placed before us. Lord Jesus, you crossed the sea to release captives, and some asked you to leave; never let us prefer our possessions to you. Through the prayers of the first martyrs of the Church of Rome, who gave up everything rather than lose you, make us faithful to the end. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Isaac is born and weaned, and Sarah demands that Hagar and her son be sent away. Abraham, distressed, obeys God and sends them into the wilderness of Beer-sheba. When the water runs out and Hagar cannot bear to watch the child die, God hears the voice of the boy and opens her eyes to a well. In the Gospel Jesus crosses to Gentile land, delivers two men possessed by demons, and the townspeople, instead of rejoicing, ask him to leave. Psalm 34 answers both: “The Lord hears the cry of the poor.” Today the Church may remember the first martyrs of the Church of Rome.

Genesis 21:5, 8–20a · God has heard the voice of the boy

Setting.

The promise has been fulfilled: Isaac is born when Abraham is a hundred. At the feast of his weaning, Sarah sees Ishmael, the son of Hagar the Egyptian slave woman, “playing” with Isaac, and fears for her son’s inheritance. The story is painful and honest. Scripture does not hide the jealousy and harshness in the family of the patriarchs.

Hagar had once run away into the wilderness before Ishmael’s birth, and the angel had met her there and named the child “God hears” (16:7–14). Now she is in the wilderness again, sent away, and God hears once more.

Structure.

  • vv. 5, 8: Isaac born; the feast of his weaning.
  • vv. 9–10: Sarah demands that Hagar and her son be cast out.
  • vv. 11–13: Abraham is distressed; God tells him to listen, and promises that Ishmael too will be a nation.
  • vv. 14–16: bread and a skin of water; the wilderness; Hagar weeps at a bowshot’s distance.
  • vv. 17–20a: God hears; the angel calls; the well; God is with the boy.

Key words.

  • naʿar, boy: The text never names Ishmael in this passage; he is “the boy”, “the child”, “your offspring”. But the story keeps saying that God “heard”, the meaning of his name.
  • šāmaʿ, to hear: “God heard the voice of the boy” (v. 17). In Scripture God hears the cry of the weak, as he heard Israel in Egypt (Exodus 3:7).
  • bəʾēr, well: Water in the desert means life. God opens Hagar’s eyes to see what was already there.

Verse by verse.

“The matter was very distressing to Abraham on account of his son.” (21:11)

Abraham loves Ishmael. The father of faith is not shown as cold; he suffers.

“As for the son of the slave woman, I will make a nation of him also, because he is your offspring.” (21:13)

God’s promise reaches beyond the line of the covenant. Ishmael is not abandoned.

“And God heard the voice of the boy;” (21:17)

Hagar wept, but it is the boy’s voice that God hears. The angel says “Do not be afraid”, as angels so often do in Scripture.

“Then God opened her eyes and she saw a well of water.” (21:19)

Despair had hidden the well from her. God’s help often comes as the opening of our eyes.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul uses Hagar and Sarah in an allegory about slavery and sonship in Galatians 4:21–31. That allegory concerns living by grace, not a verdict on any people, and the Church is careful never to use it against the Jewish people, to whom “belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants” (Romans 9:4; CCC 839). The Second Vatican Council notes that Muslims link their faith with Abraham, and asks Christians and Muslims to forget past quarrels and work together for justice and peace (Nostra Aetate 3). Psalm 34 gives the Church’s reading of today’s story: the Lord hears the cry of the poor, of the outcast mother and the thirsty child.

Matthew 8:28–34 · They begged him to leave their neighbourhood

Setting.

Jesus has just calmed the storm on the lake (8:23–27). He lands on the eastern shore, in the region of the Decapolis, mostly Gentile country, where pigs were kept. Mark and Luke tell the same story with one man; Matthew has two, as he does elsewhere (compare 20:30). The place name differs in the manuscripts: Gadarenes, Gerasenes or Gergesenes.

Structure.

  • v. 28: two fierce demoniacs come out of the tombs.
  • v. 29: “What have you to do with us, Son of God? Have you come here to torment us before the time?”
  • vv. 30–32: the demons beg to enter the swine; “Go!”; the herd rushes into the sea.
  • vv. 33–34: the swineherds tell the town; the townspeople ask Jesus to leave.

Key words.

  • daimonizomenoi, possessed by demons: People under the power of evil spirits. They live among the tombs, the place of death, and no one can pass that way.
  • pro kairou, before the time: The demons know that a time of judgment is coming when their power will end. In Jesus that end has already begun.
  • parakaleō, to beg: The same verb is used of the demons begging (v. 31) and of the townspeople begging Jesus to leave (v. 34). Both want him at a distance.

Verse by verse.

“What have you to do with us, Son of God?” (8:29)

The demons recognise who Jesus is before the disciples fully do. Knowing is not the same as believing (compare James 2:19).

“And he said to them, “Go!”” (8:32)

One word is enough. Jesus does not struggle with the demons; he commands them.

“when they saw him, they begged him to leave their neighborhood.” (8:34)

They count the loss of the pigs and are afraid. The healing of two men matters less to them than their property and their quiet.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ exorcisms release people from the domination of demons and anticipate his great victory over “the ruler of this world” (CCC 550). The Church continues this ministry: in baptism she prays for deliverance from evil, and in grave cases a priest authorised by the bishop may perform a solemn exorcism (CCC 1673). The Gospel also asks a hard question: do I prefer my comfort, my possessions or my routine to the presence of Christ who changes things?

For the Latin reader

Today the Church may keep the memorial of the First Martyrs of the Holy Roman Church. After the great fire of Rome in the year 64, the emperor Nero blamed the Christians, and the Roman historian Tacitus records that a vast multitude were put to death with cruel tortures. St Clement of Rome later spoke of “a great multitude of the elect” who suffered there (in substance). They are remembered on the day after Saints Peter and Paul, who died in the same persecution. Their names are unknown, but like Hagar’s child, their cry was heard by God. Christians in many lands still suffer for their faith; we pray for them, and for those who wrong them.

For study and teaching

  • When have I, like Hagar, been unable to see the “well” God had placed near me? Who helped open my eyes?
  • Is there any part of my life where I have quietly asked Jesus to “leave”, because his presence would cost me something?
