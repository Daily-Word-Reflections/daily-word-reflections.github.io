Word for the day

“Then he took the book of the covenant, and read it in the hearing of the people; and they said, “All that the Lord has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient.” Moses took the blood and dashed it on the people, and said, “See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you in accordance with all these words.”” Exodus 24:7–8

Reflection

Today's first reading ends the long scene of the giving of the law at Sinai with the sealing of the covenant, and it is one of the great liturgies of the Old Testament. Moses has received the words and the ordinances of the Lord, and he comes down to tell the people everything. They answer “with one voice”: “All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.” Moses does not let the words go with the wind: he “wrote down all the words of the Lord.” Early the next morning he builds an altar at the foot of the mountain, and around it he sets up twelve pillars, “corresponding to the twelve tribes of Israel.” The altar is God's side of the covenant, the pillars are the people's; between them is the sacrifice. Young men offer burnt offerings and “offerings of well-being,” meals shared in joy with God. Then comes the central action. Moses takes the blood of the sacrifice, which in the Old Testament stands for life, and divides it: half is put in basins; half is dashed against the altar. He reads the “book of the covenant” in the hearing of the people, who answer again, “All that the Lord has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient.” In Hebrew, na'aseh ve-nishma: we will do, and we will hear. And then Moses takes the blood in the basins and “dashed it on the people, and said, ‘See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you in accordance with all these words.’” The same blood touches the altar and the people: God and Israel are bound together in one life. The Letter to the Hebrews will remember this moment (Hebrews 9:18–20), and Jesus will use almost the same words at the Last Supper: “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Mark 14:24). The Christian Church sees in Sinai a shadow of what Christ does at the Eucharist: the new covenant sealed in his blood, shared at the table. The psalm takes up the same theme from the other side. God summons the earth: “Gather to me my faithful ones, who made a covenant with me by sacrifice!” and he teaches what pleases him: “Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving, and pay your vows to the Most High. Call on me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me.” The Greek word for thanksgiving is eucharistia. The sacrifice that God most loves is a grateful heart.

In the Gospel Jesus gives another parable of the kingdom, found only in Matthew: the wheat and the weeds. A householder sows good seed in his field; “while everybody was asleep, an enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat, and then went away.” The weed meant here, probably the bearded darnel, looks very like wheat until the ears appear, when the difference is clear. “So when the plants came up and bore grain, then the weeds appeared as well.” The slaves ask, with amazement, “Master, did you not sow good seed in your field? Where, then, did these weeds come from?” The householder answers, “An enemy has done this.” Evil in the world and in the Church is not God's doing; there is an enemy. And the slaves, quite naturally, ask, “Then do you want us to go and gather them?” The householder says, “No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them. Let both of them grow together until the harvest.” Only at harvest will the reapers separate them, and the wheat be gathered “into my barn.” Jesus will explain the parable in a few days (Matthew 13:36–43). Meanwhile the point is plain: patience. The Church on earth is a field where good and bad grow together, and it is not our work to decide, before the time, who is wheat and who is weed. Often we cannot tell: the harsh person may be the one who most needs mercy, and a seeming saint may be a weed. The Catechism teaches that the Church is “at once holy and always in need of being purified” (Catechism 827, quoting Lumen Gentium 8). This is not an excuse for indifference to evil: the householder knows there is an enemy, and the harvest will come. But it is a strong teaching on God's patience, which gives everyone time to repent: “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, says the Lord God, and not rather that they should turn from their ways and live?” (Ezekiel 18:23). Today the Church remembers one who quietly allowed God to cultivate him: St Sharbel Makhluf, a Lebanese Maronite monk and hermit of the nineteenth century, who spent his last twenty-three years in a mountain hermitage, hidden and silent, and who now draws pilgrims of all faiths to his tomb at Annaya.

Full homily

Dear friends, the first reading is the sealing of the covenant at Sinai. Moses tells the people all the words of the Lord, and they answer with one voice: all the words that the Lord has spoken we will do (Exodus 24:3). Moses writes everything down. In the morning he builds an altar, with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes, and the sacrifice is offered. Then Moses reads the book of the covenant, and the people say again: we will do, and we will be obedient. How many times have we said at Mass, yes, Lord, we will do it? And how many times have we done it?

Then Moses does something dramatic. He takes the blood of the sacrifice, half on the altar, and half he sprinkles on the people: see the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you (Exodus 24:8). Blood is life. The same life touches the altar, which is God's, and the people. This is what Jesus does at the Last Supper: this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many (Mark 14:24). At every Mass, the same blood is given to us, not sprinkled on our skin but taken into our lives. The Eucharist makes the covenant new every day, and it asks us to do what we promise.

In the Gospel Jesus tells of an enemy who sowed weeds among the wheat. The workers want to pull them out at once. But the owner says: no, you will uproot the wheat with them. Let both of them grow together until the harvest. This is God's patience. In our families, our parishes and even in our own hearts, the wheat and the weeds grow together. We want to uproot the people who annoy us. God waits, because he wants everyone to be saved. And he gives us time too. Whom do I judge too quickly? And who has been patient with me?

Today we remember St Sharbel Makhluf, a monk of Lebanon, a hermit who lived in silence for twenty-three years in a little cell on a mountain. He did not judge the world; he prayed for it, hidden with his Father who is in secret. When he died, God showed that this quiet prayer was not lost: many people were healed at his tomb, Christians and non-Christians alike. Let us pray with Sharbel for Lebanon and for peace, and let us ask for the grace to be doers of the word, patient with each other and faithful in secret prayer. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a farmer who planted good wheat. At night an enemy planted weeds. The workers wanted to pull them out right away, but the farmer said: wait, or you will pull up the wheat too! God is like that farmer. He is patient with us and gives us time to grow. Today we also remember St Sharbel, a monk who loved to pray alone in a little room on a mountain. He prayed for everybody, and God heard his prayer. Let us also set aside a few quiet minutes today to talk to God.

Prayer

God of the covenant, you bound yourself to your people at Sinai by the sprinkled blood, and in your Son you sealed a new and everlasting covenant. Make us a people who say, We will do, and then do what we say. Lord Jesus, you teach us patience with the wheat and the weeds; give us the patience of the householder and the mercy of the Father who wills that all should come to repentance. Through the prayers of St Sharbel, who sought you in silence and hidden prayer, teach us to seek you in secret and to give you a sacrifice of thanksgiving. Keep Lebanon and all the Middle East in your peace. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s first reading is the sealing of the covenant at Sinai. Moses tells the people all the words of the Lord, and they answer with one voice, “All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.” Then he builds an altar, offers sacrifice and sprinkles the blood: half on the altar, half on the people. “See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you.” In the Gospel Jesus tells the parable of the wheat and the weeds, in which the owner will not pull up the weeds early, “for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them.” Both readings speak of God’s patient dealing with his people and of the day of harvest. The Church may also remember St Sharbel Makhluf, a hermit of Lebanon.

Exodus 24:3–8 · The blood of the covenant

Setting.

Chapters 20–23 gave the Ten Commandments and the “Book of the Covenant”, a collection of laws for life in the land. Now the covenant is formally made. In the ancient world a covenant between a ruler and a people was sealed with an oath and a ritual. At Sinai the sealing has three parts: the people hear the words and promise obedience; an altar is built with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes; and the blood of the sacrifice is shared between God and the people.

The reading leaves out nothing important in the sealing. The verses that follow (vv. 9–11) tell how Moses, Aaron and the elders go up the mountain and eat and drink in God’s presence: the covenant ends in a meal. The sacrifices are offered by “young men”, because the priesthood of Aaron has not yet been established (compare 28:1).

Structure.

  • v. 3: Moses tells the people all the words and ordinances; they answer, “All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.”
  • v. 4: he writes down the words, builds an altar at the foot of the mountain and sets up twelve pillars.
  • v. 5: young men offer burnt offerings and offerings of well-being.
  • vv. 6–8: Moses puts half the blood in basins and dashes half against the altar; he reads the book of the covenant; the people promise to obey; he sprinkles the people with the blood: “See the blood of the covenant.”

Key words.

  • bĕrît, covenant: A covenant is a solemn bond, more than a contract. In it God binds himself to the people, and the people bind themselves to him. It is the basic word of the Old Testament. The New Testament speaks of the “new covenant” in Christ’s blood (Luke 22:20).
  • dām, blood: In Israel’s understanding the life of a living creature is in its blood (Leviticus 17:11). The blood on the altar and on the people shows that one life now joins God and the people. The act is a sign of binding union, not of violence.
  • naʿăśeh wĕnišmāʿ, we will do and we will hear: In v. 7 the people say they “will do” and “will be obedient” (literally, “will hear”, and in Hebrew hearing means obeying). Their promise is generous and sincere. Chapter 32 will show how quickly it is tested, which is why the covenant rests on God’s mercy too.

Verse by verse.

“All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.” (24:3)

The people speak with one voice. God has given the commandments to a rescued people, and they accept them willingly. The covenant is not forced on them.

“Moses wrote down all the words of the Lord.” (24:4)

God’s word is written down. This is the beginning of Scripture as the Church receives it: a book that keeps the covenant, and is read to the people.

“Moses took half of the blood and put it in basins, and half of the blood he dashed against the altar.” (24:6)

The altar represents God. The blood shared between altar and people shows that the two parties are joined by one life.

“Then he took the book of the covenant, and read it in the hearing of the people;” (24:7)

The covenant is sealed by reading and hearing, as at every Liturgy of the Word. The people respond only after they have heard.

““See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you in accordance with all these words.”” (24:8)

These words are echoed at the Last Supper. The covenant of Sinai is the pattern, and Jesus takes it to its fulfilment.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in Moses’ words the foreshadowing of the Last Supper. Jesus says over the cup, “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Mark 14:24; compare Matthew 26:28, Luke 22:20, 1 Corinthians 11:25), and the Letter to the Hebrews draws the comparison in full (Hebrews 9:18–22). The Catechism teaches that Christ’s death is the sacrifice of the New Covenant, which restores us to communion with God through the “blood of the covenant” (CCC 613). At every Mass, the Liturgy of the Word, with its reading and its “Amen”, and then the sacrifice and the meal, follows the pattern of Sinai: God speaks, the people respond, and the blood of the covenant is shared. The first covenant, never revoked (Romans 11:29), remains the root, and Christians honour it.

Matthew 13:24–30 · The wheat and the weeds

Setting.

This is the second parable of Matthew 13, after the sower. It is found only in Matthew. Jesus tells it to the crowds beside the sea; later, in the house, he explains it privately to his disciples (13:36–43). The picture is one a Galilean farmer knew well. The “weeds” are probably darnel, a grass that looks very like wheat until the ear forms, and whose roots can tangle with those of the wheat, so that one cannot easily be pulled up without the other.

The parable answers a question that every generation asks: if the kingdom of heaven is God’s field, and the seed is good, why is there evil in it, even within the community of believers?

Structure.

  • vv. 24–26: a man sows good seed; while everyone sleeps, an enemy sows weeds; both come up.
  • vv. 27–28: the servants ask where the weeds came from; the master says an enemy did this; they ask if they should gather them.
  • vv. 29–30: the master says no, lest the wheat be uprooted; let both grow together until the harvest, when the reapers will separate them.

Key words.

  • zizania, weeds, darnel: The Greek word names the darnel that grows in grain fields. To the eye, until the harvest, it is hard to tell from wheat. This is why no one can be sure who is wheat and who is weed.
  • echthros, enemy: The weeds are not the farmer’s mistake but the work of an enemy. In the explanation Jesus names him as the devil (13:39). Evil is real, and it is not from God.
  • therismos, harvest: In the Old Testament and the Gospels the harvest is the time of God’s final judgment (compare Joel 3:13; Revelation 14:15). The parable moves the sorting out of human hands and into God’s.

Verse by verse.

“while everybody was asleep, an enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat, and then went away.” (13:25)

The enemy works in secret and leaves. The weeds are noticed only when the plants have grown.

“‘An enemy has done this.’” (13:28)

The master does not blame his servants or his seed. Evil has an enemy’s hand behind it, and it is not part of the good God has sown.

“‘No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them.” (13:29)

The servants’ zeal is natural, but they cannot see well enough. Judging and uprooting by human hands risks harming the good.

“Let both of them grow together until the harvest;” (13:30)

This is God’s patience. It gives time for repentance, since the weeds of today may still turn, by grace, to wheat. It is not indifference to evil.

“but gather the wheat into my barn.” (13:30)

The parable ends with the gathering of the good. God’s purpose is the harvest, and the barn is the sign of what the whole field is for.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read this parable as a teaching about patience and the final judgment. St Augustine used it against the Donatists, who wanted a Church of the pure only: on earth, he said, the Church holds good and bad together until the harvest (in substance). The Catechism teaches that the Church is “at once holy and always in need of purification”, embracing sinners in her bosom, and that all her members, ministers included, must acknowledge that they are sinners (CCC 827, in substance). The parable does not forbid correction or discipline: Jesus himself teaches how to correct a brother (Matthew 18:15–17). It forbids hasty judgment of persons, which belongs to God, who alone sees the heart. Meanwhile, it calls each of us to examine our own field, and to pray for the conversion of those who do wrong.

Questions people ask

If God is patient with evil, does that mean we should do nothing about it?

No. The parable speaks of what the servants must not do: uproot persons by their own judgment before the harvest. It does not tell the Church to ignore sin. Jesus tells his disciples to correct a brother who sins (Matthew 18:15–17), to love enemies, to feed the hungry, and to work for justice. The parable asks us to resist evil with patience and mercy, and to leave to God the final separation, because he alone knows who will bear fruit and who may yet repent.

For the Latin reader

St Sharbel Makhluf (1828–1898) was a Maronite monk and priest of Lebanon. Born Youssef Makhlouf in a mountain village, he entered the monastery of St Maron at Annaya in 1851, took the name Sharbel, and was ordained priest in 1859. In 1875 he withdrew to the hermitage of Saints Peter and Paul above the monastery, where he lived for twenty-three years in prayer, poverty and obedience. He died on 24 December 1898, eight days after a stroke suffered while celebrating the Divine Liturgy, and his tomb at Annaya has drawn pilgrims of many faiths. Pope St Paul VI canonised him in 1977. The Maronite Church, in full communion with Rome, belongs to the Syriac tradition, a heritage shared by the Eastern Churches of Kerala, and devotion to him has spread far beyond Lebanon. His memorial on 24 July is optional in the Roman calendar.

For study and teaching

  • The people said, “We will do,” and later they failed. How can I keep my own promises to God, knowing my weakness, by relying on his mercy?
  • At Mass I hear the Word, answer “Amen” and receive the Lord’s Body and Blood. How can I take the “blood of the covenant” more seriously the next time I am there?
  • In the parable, patience is not indifference. Whom am I tempted to “uproot” in my judgment, and how might God be patient with them, and with me?
