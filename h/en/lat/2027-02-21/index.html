Word for the day

“Then a cloud overshadowed them, and from the cloud there came a voice, “This is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him!”” Mark 9:7

Reflection

Every Second Sunday of Lent leads us up the mountain of the Transfiguration, and in Year B the Church sets beside it the hardest story in Genesis. “After these things God tested Abraham.” He calls him by name, and Abraham answers as the saints always answer: “Here I am.” Then the terrible command: “Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering.” Each phrase tightens the knot: your son, your only son, whom you love. Isaac is the child of the promise, the one through whom all nations are to be blessed. Abraham is asked to give back to God the very gift God gave him. He goes, he builds the altar, he binds his son and lifts the knife. Then the angel calls: “Abraham, Abraham!… Do not lay your hand on the boy… for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.” A ram caught in a thicket takes Isaac's place. God never wanted the death of Isaac. He wanted Abraham's whole heart, and he received it.

St Paul reads this story in the light of Calvary. “He who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us, will he not with him also give us everything else?” Paul is echoing Genesis on purpose. What God spared Abraham, he did not spare himself. On Moriah the son was released and a substitute was found; on Golgotha there was no substitute, because the Son himself is the Lamb. The Fathers loved to point out that Isaac carried the wood of the sacrifice up the hill, as Jesus would carry his cross. And Abraham's words to his son, “God himself will provide the lamb for a burnt offering”, find their full meaning at the Jordan when John points to Jesus: here is the Lamb of God. That is why Paul can ask with such confidence: “If God is for us, who is against us?… Who is to condemn? It is Christ Jesus, who died, yes, who was raised, who is at the right hand of God, who indeed intercedes for us.”

Then Mark takes us up “a high mountain apart”. Jesus has just told the disciples that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected and be killed. Now, for a moment, Peter, James and John see who is walking towards that death. “He was transfigured before them, and his clothes became dazzling white, such as no one on earth could bleach them.” Moses and Elijah, the Law and the Prophets, talk with him. Peter, terrified and delighted, wants to build three dwellings and stay. But a cloud overshadows them, and the Father's voice speaks the words Abraham might have spoken over Isaac: “This is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him!” When they look again, “they saw no one with them any more, but only Jesus.” The vision passes; Jesus remains. Coming down, he tells them to keep silence “until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead”. The glory of the mountain is given to carry them through the darkness of the cross. Lent is the same: a climb with Jesus towards Jerusalem, sustained by moments of light, and above all by the command to listen to him.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, after these things God tested Abraham. He said, take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love… and offer him there as a burnt offering (Genesis 22:1–2). Every word of that command is heavy. Isaac was the gift of God, the son of the promise.

Abraham went. He built the altar, laid the wood, bound his son and took the knife. Then the angel called: do not lay your hand on the boy… for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me (Genesis 22:12). God did not want Isaac's death. He wanted Abraham's whole heart.

St Paul looks at Calvary and says: he who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us, will he not with him also give us everything else? (Romans 8:32). What God spared Abraham, he did not spare himself. On Moriah a ram was found; on Golgotha the Son himself is the Lamb.

In the Gospel Jesus leads Peter, James and John up a high mountain, and he was transfigured before them, and his clothes became dazzling white (Mark 9:2–3). They see for a moment the glory of the one who will soon be rejected and crucified.

Then a cloud overshadowed them, and the Father said: this is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him! (Mark 9:7). And suddenly they saw no one with them any more, but only Jesus (Mark 9:8). The vision passes; Jesus remains. That is enough.

This Lent, let us offer God our Isaac, whatever we hold too tightly, and trust that he will provide. And let us listen to his Son every day. The light of the mountain will carry us through the cross to Easter. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Every Second Sunday of Lent the Church goes up the mountain of the Transfiguration, after the desert of the First Sunday. This year, in the Year B readings, a second mountain stands beside it: Moriah. The two mountains belong together. On Moriah, Abraham is told to offer “your only son… whom you love”, and God stops his hand. On the high mountain, the Father’s voice says, “This is my Son, the Beloved”, and that Son will not be spared.

St Paul, in between, makes the link explicit: “He who did not withhold his own Son”. The readings are not a pause from the Cross but a glimpse of what lies beyond it. Abraham goes up not knowing, the three disciples go up and come down not understanding, and we are asked with them to “listen to him” on the way to Jerusalem. The key words are beloved, not withheld and listen.

Genesis 22:1–2, 9a, 10–13, 15–18 · Your son, your only son, whom you love

Setting.

This is the summit of the story of Abraham. God called him with a promise of descendants (Genesis 12:2), and after long years of waiting Isaac was born. All God’s promises now rest on this one boy. “After these things” God asks for him.

Jewish tradition calls this chapter the Akedah, the Binding of Isaac, and it holds a place of great honor in Jewish prayer and reading, including at the New Year. Jews and Christians share reverence for this father of faith (Nostra Aetate 4).

The Lectionary leaves out the journey (vv. 3–8), which many people know best: the three days’ walk, Isaac carrying the wood, and his question, “where is the lamb?” Abraham’s answer there is, “God himself will provide the lamb for a burnt offering, my son” (22:8). A preacher may well read them, or tell them, on this Sunday. 2 Chronicles 3:1 identifies Mount Moriah with the place where Solomon’s Temple would stand.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the test and the command.
  • v. 9a: the place, the altar.
  • vv. 10–13: the knife, the angel’s voice, the ram.
  • vv. 15–18: the second call from heaven: God swears by himself and renews the promise.
  • ¶ The reader is told at the start what Abraham does not know: “God tested Abraham”. So we are never in doubt about what God intends, and the story can hold our attention on Abraham’s obedience.

Key words.

  • nissâ, tested: A test in the Bible is meant to bring out and strengthen what is in a person, not to trip him. The same word is used of Israel in the desert (Exodus 16:4; Deuteronomy 8:2).
  • hinnēnî, here I am: The answer of one who is fully available. Abraham says it to God at the start and to the angel in v. 11 (and to Isaac in v. 7, a verse not read today). Moses (Exodus 3:4) and Samuel (1 Samuel 3:4) give the same answer.
  • yāḥîd, only son: The only son of the promise, as Ishmael has by then been sent away (chapter 21). The Greek Old Testament translates this word in v. 2 as agapētos, “beloved”: the very word of the Father’s voice in today’s Gospel.
  • ‘āqad, to bind: The verb of v. 9, “He bound his son Isaac”, from which the name Akedah comes. It is used nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible.

Verse by verse.

“Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love,” (22:2)

The words come slowly, each one tightening. God names the love, and so does not hide what he is asking.

“But the angel of the Lord called to him from heaven, and said, “Abraham, Abraham!” And he said, “Here I am.”” (22:11)

The same words as v. 1. The name is called twice, as if in haste. God’s last word is not the command but the stopping of it.

“for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.” (22:12)

“Now I know” is human language: God already knows, but in this act Abraham’s faith has become visible. “Not withheld” is the phrase St Paul will echo.

“And Abraham looked up and saw a ram, caught in a thicket by its horns.” (22:13)

Something is given in the place of his son, and it was given by God. The verse that follows names the place “The Lord will provide” (22:14).

“and by your offspring shall all the nations of the earth gain blessing for themselves, because you have obeyed my voice.” (22:18)

The promise reaches the whole world. A note on the text. The Hebrew verb can be read “shall bless themselves” or “shall be blessed”; the NRSV takes the first, and the POC the second (അനുഗ്രഹിക്കപ്പെടും). In Acts 3:25 Peter applies this to what God has done in Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Abraham’s faith, in this last purification, conforms “the father of believers” to the likeness of the Father who will not spare his own Son but will deliver him up for us all (CCC 2572). Hebrews 11:19 says of Abraham: “He considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead”. Origen and others among the Fathers saw in Isaac carrying the wood a figure of Christ carrying his cross (in substance). The likeness is real and so is the difference: for Isaac a ram is found, and for the Son of God there is no substitute, because he himself is the Lamb.

Romans 8:31b–34 · If God is for us

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome, a community he had not founded, around AD 57–58, most likely from Corinth. Chapter 8 is the summit of the letter’s teaching on life in the Spirit. Verses 31–39 are its conclusion: a hymn of confidence in the form of questions, which ends with the certainty that nothing can separate us from the love of God in Christ.

Structure.

  • v. 31b: the opening question.
  • v. 32: the proof: God has already given his Son.
  • v. 33: who will accuse? God justifies.
  • v. 34: who will condemn? Christ intercedes.
  • ¶ The setting is a court of law, in which the accuser finds that the judge is on the side of the accused. In Greek vv. 33–34 can be punctuated in more than one way, as questions or as statements, and Bibles differ. The sense does not change.

Key words.

  • ouk epheisato, did not withhold (spare): This is the verb of Genesis 22:12 and 22:16 in the Greek Old Testament. Paul is plainly thinking of Abraham.
  • paredōken, gave him up, handed over: The word is used for Judas’ betrayal and for the handing over of Jesus in the Passion accounts. Human sin is in these acts, yet deeper down, Paul says, the Father’s gift is at work (CCC 599–600).
  • entynchanei, intercedes: To approach someone on another’s behalf. It is used of the Spirit a few verses earlier (8:27) and of Christ the high priest (Hebrews 7:25).

Verse by verse.

“If God is for us, who is against us?” (8:31)

Paul does not say that no one is against us. He has just spoken of suffering, and he will soon list hardship, famine and the sword (8:35). He says no one can finally stand against us.

“He who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us, will he not with him also give us everything else?” (8:32)

This is the argument at its strongest: God has already given the most he could give. There is no reason for him to refuse lesser things. Note “with him”: everything else comes with the Son, not instead of him.

“It is Christ Jesus, who died, yes, who was raised, who is at the right hand of God, who indeed intercedes for us.” (8:34)

Four steps, rising: died, raised, at the right hand, interceding. This may echo an early statement of faith. The one person who could rightly condemn us is the one who is pleading for us.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Romans 8:32 to teach that Jesus took on our condition out of love for us, and that the giving of the Son shows God’s plan for us to be one of benevolent love (CCC 603–604). It also teaches that Christ’s intercession is the foundation of all Christian intercession: he “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25; CCC 2634, 2741). When we pray “through Christ our Lord” at Mass, we are joining a prayer already being offered.

Mark 9:2–10 · This is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him!

Setting.

The moment comes at the turning point of Mark’s Gospel. Peter has just confessed Jesus as the Messiah. Jesus has then said for the first time that “the Son of Man must undergo great suffering… and be killed, and after three days rise again” (8:31), Peter has protested, and Jesus has told all who would follow him to “take up their cross” (8:34).

“Six days later”: Mark rarely gives such an exact link, and this one ties the vision to those hard words. The mountain is not named. Christian tradition from early times has pointed to Tabor in Galilee; some scholars suggest Hermon, nearer to Caesarea Philippi. The Gospel does not tell us, because its interest is elsewhere.

Structure.

  • vv. 2–3: the change in Jesus.
  • v. 4: Elijah and Moses.
  • vv. 5–6: Peter’s proposal and the disciples’ fear.
  • v. 7: the cloud and the voice.
  • v. 8: only Jesus.
  • vv. 9–10: coming down: the command to silence and the question.

Key words.

  • metemorphōthē, he was transfigured: The word gives us “metamorphosis”. A passive: this is God’s doing. Paul uses the same verb for what happens in Christians (Romans 12:2; 2 Corinthians 3:18).
  • skēnai, dwellings, tents: The word for the tents of the Exodus and of the Feast of Tabernacles. Peter may want to keep the moment, and to honor the three equally. He has not yet understood.
  • episkiazō, overshadow: The same word is used of the cloud over the Tent of Meeting in the Greek of Exodus 40:35, and of the Spirit coming upon Mary (Luke 1:35). It speaks of God’s own presence.
  • agapētos, beloved: It was said at the baptism (1:11) and comes again here, now for the disciples to hear. It recalls Isaac, the beloved son of Genesis 22:2 in the Greek.
  • akouete autou, listen to him: This recalls Moses’ promise of “a prophet like me” (Deuteronomy 18:15; the Greek has the same words). And the last thing Jesus has said is about the Cross.

Verse by verse.

“And he was transfigured before them, and his clothes became dazzling white, such as no one on earth could bleach them.” (9:2–3)

Mark, with a homely touch, thinks of the laundry. This whiteness is not of earth. In Daniel 7:9 white clothing belongs to the Ancient One.

“And there appeared to them Elijah with Moses, who were talking with Jesus.” (9:4)

Both met God on a mountain (Exodus 24; 1 Kings 19). They are often read as the Law and the Prophets. Elijah was expected before the great day (Malachi 4:5), and Moses had promised a prophet to come.

“He did not know what to say, for they were terrified.” (9:6)

Mark is honest about the disciples. An early tradition connects this Gospel with Peter’s preaching, so this may be Peter’s own humble memory.

“Then a cloud overshadowed them, and from the cloud there came a voice, “This is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him!”” (9:7)

The whole scene comes down to this one command. The Father does not say “build”. He says “listen”.

“Suddenly when they looked around, they saw no one with them any more, but only Jesus.” (9:8)

The glory is gone and the great figures are gone. There is only the Jesus who will walk to Jerusalem. That is what the disciples have, and it is what we have.

“So they kept the matter to themselves, questioning what this rising from the dead could mean.” (9:10)

The vision cannot be understood before the Resurrection, and neither can the Cross. That is why they are to tell no one “until after” (9:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that for a moment Jesus discloses his divine glory, and so confirms Peter’s confession, and also that he will have to go by way of the Cross at Jerusalem in order to enter into his glory (CCC 555). It quotes St Thomas Aquinas: the whole Trinity appeared, the Father in the voice, the Son in the man, the Spirit in the shining cloud (CCC 555). St Leo the Great preached that the chief purpose of the Transfiguration was to take the scandal of the Cross from the hearts of the disciples (in substance; Sermon 51). 2 Peter 1:17 keeps the memory of the voice. The Transfiguration is also a promise for our own bodies, which will be conformed to his (CCC 556).

Questions people ask

Would God really ask someone to kill a child?

No, and the story itself says so: God stops the knife. The reader is told from the first line that this is a test, and its outcome is that God does not want the death of Isaac. Israel’s prophets later condemn child sacrifice in the strongest terms: “which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind” (Jeremiah 7:31), and the Church teaches that no one may ever claim the right to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258). No one today can take this chapter as permission to harm anyone, whatever voice they think they hear.

When Paul says God “did not withhold” his Son, does it mean an angry Father punished Jesus?

No. The Father and the Son are never set against each other in the Passion. Paul’s argument is that the gift of the Son proves how much God is for us. The Catechism says that God’s plan for us is one of benevolent love, which comes before any merit of ours (CCC 604). Jesus laid down his life of his own accord (John 10:18), and the love of the Father and the Son is one love.

Why did Jesus tell the disciples to say nothing?

Because “Son of God” and “glory” would be misunderstood without the Cross and the Resurrection. Until then the disciples themselves do not understand (9:10). The silence has a term: “until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead” (9:9). After Easter, it becomes the Church’s preaching.

For the Latin reader

The Roman Church reads a Transfiguration Gospel on every Second Sunday of Lent, in all three years of the Lectionary, and the Preface of the day says why: Jesus, after telling his disciples of his coming Death, showed them his glory, so that the Law and the Prophets might witness that the Passion leads to the glory of the Resurrection (in substance). The first Eucharistic Prayer, the Roman Canon, names “the sacrifice of Abraham, our father in faith” just after the consecration, and so this Sunday’s first reading is not far from what happens on the altar. The Transfiguration is also a Luminous Mystery of the Rosary (St John Paul II, Rosarium Virginis Mariae, 2002), and it may be prayed this week for those who are preparing for Baptism at Easter.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham obeyed without seeing where it would end. What is God asking of me now that I cannot see the end of?
  • “He who did not withhold his own Son”: where do I doubt God’s care for me? How does Romans 8:32 answer that doubt?
  • Peter wanted to stay on the mountain and build. What moments of consolation have I wanted to keep, and what is waiting for me back down the mountain?
  • “Listen to him.” What concrete time will I set aside this Lent to listen to Jesus in the Gospels?
