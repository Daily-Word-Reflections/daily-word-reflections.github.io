Word for the day

“Then the Lord said to Moses, “I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day.” Exodus 16:4

Reflection

A month after leaving Egypt, in the wilderness of Sin, the whole community of Israel grumbles against Moses and Aaron. The reading is honest about hunger: it is the same hunger we feel when food is short and the future is uncertain. But look at what the hunger has done to their memory. “If only we had died by the hand of the Lord in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the fleshpots and ate our fill of bread; for you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger.” The slave camp has become a restaurant. Hunger has a way of making the old chains look like comfort. The Lord does not answer with a rebuke; he answers with a promise: “I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day. In that way I will test them, whether they will follow my instruction or not.” Daily bread, and no more than daily: the test is whether Israel can live from one day's gift to the next without hoarding and without fear. On the sixth day they will gather twice as much, so that the seventh day can be a day of rest (verse 5), the first hint of the Sabbath before the Law is given at Sinai. The reading skips Moses' words to the people, that their complaint is really against the Lord, and goes straight to the moment when Aaron calls them to “draw near to the Lord, for he has heard your complaining,” and “the glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud.” That evening quails cover the camp; in the morning a layer of dew lifts and leaves “a fine flaky substance, as fine as frost on the ground.” The Israelites ask “What is it?”; in Hebrew man hu, which is why the bread was called manna. Moses answers: “It is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat.” The manna is not earned and not understood; it is received. The psalm remembers both sides of the story: “Can God spread a table in the wilderness?” was the people's doubtful question, and the answer was that he “rained down on them manna to eat, and gave them the grain of heaven. Mortals ate of the bread of angels; he sent them food in abundance.” Jesus will teach us to pray, “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). In the old Kerala memory, Karkidakam, the month that has just begun, was the lean month, when the granary was low and the new harvest not yet ripe; many a family has known what a day's bread means.

In the Gospel Jesus has just met misunderstanding in the house (Matthew 12). Now “that same day” he goes out and sits beside the sea; the crowds are so great that he gets into a boat, and the boat becomes his pulpit and the shore his church. He begins to teach “many things in parables,” the third great discourse of Matthew's Gospel, which contains seven parables. The first is the Sower. In Galilee, a farmer scattered the seed by hand and ploughed it in afterwards, so some seed would naturally fall on the footpath that crossed the field, on the thin soil over the limestone, and among the thorn roots. The picture is not of a careless sower but a generous one: seed is thrown with open hands because the harvest depends on the soil as well as the sower. Three kinds of soil fail, each for a different reason: the path where birds eat the seed, the rock where the sun scorches the shoot, the thorns that choke it. The fourth soil is good, and brings forth “grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.” A hundredfold was a blessing of the patriarchs: Isaac reaped “in the same year a hundredfold” (Genesis 26:12). The point of this parable is both warning and hope: the word of God will be heard in many different hearts, and some will bear great fruit. Jesus closes with a call: “Let anyone with ears listen!” The seed is, as Jesus will explain, “the word of God” (Luke 8:11). Let us hold the two readings together. The manna was bread from heaven for the body on the journey; the word is bread from heaven for the heart. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she venerates the Body of the Lord, receiving the bread of life from the table of the word of God and of the Body of Christ (Dei Verbum 21). Today the Church remembers St Lawrence of Brindisi, a Capuchin friar of the sixteenth century, one of the great preachers of the Church, who handed the bread of the word to the hungry in many countries and languages. He was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1959 and is called the Apostolic Doctor.

Full homily

Dear friends, the people of Israel are hungry. They are in the wilderness, far from home, and they say: if only we had died in Egypt, when we sat by the fleshpots and had our fill of bread! Hunger makes us forget that we were slaves. Hunger makes the old chains look like comfort. Do we not do the same when life is hard? How quickly we begin to say: the old days were better.

And what does God do? He does not scold them. He says: I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day (Exodus 16:4). In the morning there is something on the ground, fine as frost. They ask: what is it? Moses says: it is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat. They did not earn it. They did not even understand it. They received it. That is how God feeds us. And it is only for one day at a time. Give us this day our daily bread, Jesus teaches us to pray.

In the Gospel Jesus sits in a boat and tells of a sower who went out to sow. The seed falls on the path and the birds eat it. On rock, and it withers. Among thorns, and they choke it. And on good soil, and it bears a hundredfold. Jesus is telling us that the word of God is seed, and our hearts are the soil. The Sower is generous. He scatters the seed everywhere, even on the path where we are in a hurry, even on the rock where we are shallow, even among the thorns of our worries. What kind of soil am I today?

Today we remember St Lawrence of Brindisi, a Capuchin friar who gave his life to sowing the word. He learned many languages in order to preach to many people, he preached to soldiers and to kings, and he loved the Bible so much that he is called a Doctor of the Church. The Lord gives us bread from heaven in the word and in the Eucharist. Let us receive both with thanks and make them bear fruit in our lives. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago God's people were hungry in the desert. And every morning God sent a special bread called manna, enough for everybody for that day! In the Gospel Jesus tells about a farmer who planted seeds. Some seed fell on the path and birds ate it. Some fell on rocks. Some fell among thorns. But some fell on good soil and grew into lots of grain. The seed is God's word. Let us listen and be good soil. And do not forget to say thank you to God before you eat.

Prayer

God of mercy, you rained bread from heaven on your people in the wilderness and you sow your word in our hearts. Teach us to receive each day's bread with thanks, without fear and without greed. Lord Jesus, the Sower who goes out to sow, make our hearts good soil: take the stones from them, pull out the thorns, and let your word bear fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold. Through the prayers of St Lawrence of Brindisi, who preached your word with courage and love, make us hearers and doers of what we hear. Give us this day our daily bread, and the bread of life that is yourself. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A month or so after leaving Egypt the people are hungry, and they grumble against Moses and Aaron. God’s answer is bread from heaven: manna each morning, quail each evening, enough for each day. In the Gospel Jesus sits beside the sea and tells the parable of the sower: the seed falls on the path, on rock, among thorns and, at last, on good soil, where it yields a hundredfold. In both readings God gives generously, and the question is how we receive it. Psalm 78 recalls the manna: “The Lord gave them bread from heaven.” Today the Church may also remember St Lawrence of Brindisi, a great preacher of the Word.

Exodus 16:1–5, 9–15 · Bread from heaven

Setting.

The people have been out of Egypt for about a month. They have crossed the sea and sung the song of Moses (15:1–21), and they have come to the wilderness of Sin, a desert region between Elim and Sinai. Here the food they brought is running out. The story that follows is the first of several “murmuring” stories in the wilderness (compare 15:22–24; 17:1–7; Numbers 11).

The reading leaves out vv. 6–8 and 10–11, which tell how Moses and Aaron answered the complaint and how the glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the whole congregation complains; they remember the fleshpots of Egypt.
  • vv. 4–5: the Lord promises bread from heaven, a daily portion, and a double portion on the sixth day; it is a test.
  • vv. 9–12: Aaron summons the congregation; the glory of the Lord appears in the cloud; the Lord has heard and promises meat at twilight and bread in the morning.
  • vv. 13–15: quail cover the camp; in the morning a fine flaky substance lies on the ground; the people ask “What is it?”; Moses says it is the bread the Lord has given.

Key words.

  • mān, manna: The name comes from the people’s question in v. 15, “What is it?” (mān hû). It is thus a gift so strange that the people have to ask what it is. Scholars note that wilderness insects can produce a sweet substance on desert shrubs, but the story speaks of the sign that no natural account fully explains: the daily portion, and the double portion on the sixth day.
  • nissâ, to test: In v. 4 God says he will test the people “whether they will follow my instruction or not”. The test is simple: to take only what they need for each day, and trust that tomorrow’s bread will come.
  • lûn, to complain: The people’s complaining, repeated in this chapter, is turned by Moses in vv. 7–8: it is not against us, but against the Lord. Hunger is real; the problem is that they forget the God who rescued them.

Verse by verse.

“If only we had died by the hand of the Lord in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the fleshpots and ate our fill of bread;” (16:3)

Memory has become selective. They remember the pots and the bread, and forget the slavery. Hunger can do this to anyone.

“I am going to rain bread from heaven for you,” (16:4)

The Lord answers complaint with a gift. Bread “from heaven” will become a great biblical picture, taken up in Psalm 78 and John 6.

“and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day.” (16:4)

Daily bread means daily trust. The people may not store up, because the gift comes again each morning.

“In the evening quails came up and covered the camp;” (16:13)

Meat in the evening, bread in the morning, exactly as promised. God’s word is fulfilled even in the wilderness.

““What is it?” For they did not know what it was.” (16:15)

The people receive what they cannot name. Faith often begins with a gift we do not yet understand.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers and the Church read the manna as a sign of the Eucharist. The Lord himself draws this line in the Gospel of John, saying that the true bread from heaven is not what Moses gave but what the Father gives, and “I am the bread of life” (John 6:32–35); that discourse will be read from next Sunday. The Our Father also asks for “daily bread”, and the Catechism teaches that this petition is a petition for the bread of every day and for the Bread of life (CCC 2828–2837, in substance). The manna teaches the Church two things: that God feeds his people in the wilderness, and that we are to receive his gifts with trust and thanks, not with grumbling.

Matthew 13:1–9 · A sower went out to sow

Setting.

With chapter 13 begins the third of the five great speeches of Matthew’s Gospel, the “parables of the kingdom”. Jesus has just spoken of true family (12:46–50). Now he leaves the house, sits in a boat beside the Sea of Galilee, and teaches the crowd on the shore. The sower’s picture was familiar to every farmer in his audience. Seed was scattered by hand over the field and ploughed in afterward, so some inevitably fell on the path at the edge, on rocky ground where the limestone lay close under the soil, or among thorn roots.

This is the first parable in the chapter. On Friday the Mass gives Jesus’ own explanation of it (13:18–23).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3a: Jesus teaches the crowd from a boat, speaking in parables.
  • vv. 3b–7: the seed falls on the path, on rocky ground and among thorns, and fails.
  • v. 8: the seed on good soil yields a hundredfold, sixty and thirty.
  • v. 9: “Let anyone with ears listen!”

Key words.

  • parabolē, parable: From the Greek verb “to throw alongside”. In Hebrew māshāl is any wise saying or comparison. A parable puts something known beside something new, so that the listener must think and choose. The Catechism notes that parables invite us to enter the kingdom (CCC 546).
  • karpos, fruit, grain: The point of the story is the harvest. Fruit is a favourite word of Matthew for a life changed by the Word (compare 3:8, 7:16–20).
  • akousatō, let him listen: To “have ears” is to be ready to hear. In the Bible hearing means obeying: the word must be received with the whole person.

Verse by verse.

“Listen! A sower went out to sow.” (13:3)

The parable begins with a call to attention. The sower is generous, scattering seed generously, and the seed is good.

“Other seeds fell on rocky ground, where they did not have much soil,” (13:5)

Many things start well and die when the sun rises. Faith that has no depth cannot meet trouble or time.

“Other seeds fell among thorns, and the thorns grew up and choked them.” (13:7)

The thorns are not wicked: they are only other growing things. Good things, crowded, can choke the best thing.

“Other seeds fell on good soil and brought forth grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.” (13:8)

A hundredfold was an extraordinary harvest in Galilee. The surprise of the parable is the harvest: the sower’s labour is not wasted, and the Word bears fruit beyond measure.

“Let anyone with ears listen!” (13:9)

Jesus does not say that only some can hear. He invites everyone to hear the Word and let it take root.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read this parable as a picture of how the Word of God is received. The Catechism teaches that, as the sower scatters the seed, so Christ teaches in parables, and the kingdom grows in the hearts of those who accept the word with a good heart (CCC 546, 543, in substance). The lesson is not discouragement at the three failures, but wonder at the harvest, and an invitation to examine our own ground. At every Mass the Church makes ready the soil in two ways: the Liturgy of the Word, where the seed is sown, and the Eucharist, which strengthens us to bear fruit.

For the Latin reader

St Lawrence of Brindisi (1559–1619) was an Italian Capuchin friar, a preacher and a linguist who knew several languages, including Hebrew and Greek, and was so steeped in the Bible that he could, it is said, quote it almost by heart. He served his order as a superior and was sent on diplomatic missions by the Popes. In 1959 Pope St John XXIII declared him a Doctor of the Church. His memorial on 21 July is optional in the Roman calendar. The sower who scatters the Word without counting the cost is a fitting picture of him.

For study and teaching

  • The Israelites remembered only the food of Egypt, not its slavery. When have I remembered the past in a way that hid the truth from me?
  • Which of the soils in the parable is closest to my own heart today: the path, the rock, the thorns or good soil? What is one small step to give the Word more room?
