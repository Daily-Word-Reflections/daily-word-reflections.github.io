Word for the day

“Then the Lord said to Moses, “I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day.” Exodus 16:4

Reflection

A month after leaving Egypt, in the wilderness of Sin, the whole community of Israel grumbles against Moses and Aaron. The reading is honest about hunger: it is the same hunger we feel when food is short and the future is uncertain. But look at what the hunger has done to their memory. “If only we had died by the hand of the Lord in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the fleshpots and ate our fill of bread; for you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger.” The slave camp has become a restaurant. Hunger has a way of making the old chains look like comfort. The Lord does not answer with a rebuke; he answers with a promise: “I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day. In that way I will test them, whether they will follow my instruction or not.” Daily bread, and no more than daily: the test is whether Israel can live from one day's gift to the next without hoarding and without fear. On the sixth day they will gather twice as much, so that the seventh day can be a day of rest (verse 5), the first hint of the Sabbath before the Law is given at Sinai. The reading skips Moses' words to the people, that their complaint is really against the Lord, and goes straight to the moment when Aaron calls them to “draw near to the Lord, for he has heard your complaining,” and “the glory of the Lord appeared in the cloud.” That evening quails cover the camp; in the morning a layer of dew lifts and leaves “a fine flaky substance, as fine as frost on the ground.” The Israelites ask “What is it?”; in Hebrew man hu, which is why the bread was called manna. Moses answers: “It is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat.” The manna is not earned and not understood; it is received. The psalm remembers both sides of the story: “Can God spread a table in the wilderness?” was the people's doubtful question, and the answer was that he “rained down on them manna to eat, and gave them the grain of heaven. Mortals ate of the bread of angels; he sent them food in abundance.” Jesus will teach us to pray, “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). In the old Kerala memory, Karkidakam, the month that has just begun, was the lean month, when the granary was low and the new harvest not yet ripe; many a family has known what a day's bread means.

In the Gospel Jesus has just met misunderstanding in the house (Matthew 12). Now “that same day” he goes out and sits beside the sea; the crowds are so great that he gets into a boat, and the boat becomes his pulpit and the shore his church. He begins to teach “many things in parables,” the third great discourse of Matthew's Gospel, which contains seven parables. The first is the Sower. In Galilee, a farmer scattered the seed by hand and ploughed it in afterwards, so some seed would naturally fall on the footpath that crossed the field, on the thin soil over the limestone, and among the thorn roots. The picture is not of a careless sower but a generous one: seed is thrown with open hands because the harvest depends on the soil as well as the sower. Three kinds of soil fail, each for a different reason: the path where birds eat the seed, the rock where the sun scorches the shoot, the thorns that choke it. The fourth soil is good, and brings forth “grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.” A hundredfold was a blessing of the patriarchs: Isaac reaped “in the same year a hundredfold” (Genesis 26:12). The point of this parable is both warning and hope: the word of God will be heard in many different hearts, and some will bear great fruit. Jesus closes with a call: “Let anyone with ears listen!” The seed is, as Jesus will explain, “the word of God” (Luke 8:11). Let us hold the two readings together. The manna was bread from heaven for the body on the journey; the word is bread from heaven for the heart. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she venerates the Body of the Lord, receiving the bread of life from the table of the word of God and of the Body of Christ (Dei Verbum 21). Today the Church remembers St Lawrence of Brindisi, a Capuchin friar of the sixteenth century, one of the great preachers of the Church, who handed the bread of the word to the hungry in many countries and languages. He was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1959 and is called the Apostolic Doctor.

Homily outline

Theme: “Let anyone with ears listen!”

Bread from heaven for the body, the word from heaven for the heart, and the Sower who scatters it everywhere.

1. Enough for that day

  • Hunger and a bad memory.
  • Bread from heaven, no hoarding.
  • The first hint of the Sabbath.
“Then the Lord said to Moses, “I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day.” Exodus 16:4
“Give us this day our daily bread.” Matthew 6:11

Apply: Say the grace before meals slowly this week, and remember a hungry family by name.

2. What is it?

  • Man hu, the origin of the name manna.
  • Bread received, not earned.
  • The bread of angels.
“he rained down on them manna to eat, and gave them the grain of heaven.” Psalm 78:24
“Jesus said to them, “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” John 6:35

Apply: Next Sunday, listen to the Gospel of the bread of life with the manna in mind, and receive Communion with thanks.

3. A sower went out to sow

  • A generous Sower.
  • Path, rock, thorns and good soil.
  • The word that does not return empty.
“For as the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and do not return there until they have watered the earth, making it bring forth and sprout, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater, so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose, and succeed in the thing for which I sent it.” Isaiah 55:10–11
“Other seeds fell on good soil and brought forth grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.” Matthew 13:8

Apply: Name the thorns that choke the word in you, a worry, an addiction, a distraction, and take one step to cut them back.

4. A light for the mind, a fire for the will

  • Lawrence, the Capuchin preacher.
  • A lamp to my feet.
  • Holding it fast and bearing fruit.
“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.” Psalm 119:105
“But as for that in the good soil, these are the ones who, when they hear the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance.” Luke 8:15

Apply: Read one Gospel passage slowly every day this week, and write down one thing it asks of you.

God feeds his hungry people with bread from heaven, enough for each day, and asks them to trust.

Jesus the Sower scatters the word generously; may our hearts be good soil, as St Lawrence of Brindisi taught by his preaching.

Take-home resolution: Every day this week read the Gospel slowly, receive your daily bread with thanks, and share some of it.

Full homily

Dear friends, the people of Israel are hungry. They are in the wilderness, far from home, and they say: if only we had died in Egypt, when we sat by the fleshpots and had our fill of bread! Hunger makes us forget that we were slaves. Hunger makes the old chains look like comfort. Do we not do the same when life is hard? How quickly we begin to say: the old days were better.

And what does God do? He does not scold them. He says: I am going to rain bread from heaven for you, and each day the people shall go out and gather enough for that day (Exodus 16:4). In the morning there is something on the ground, fine as frost. They ask: what is it? Moses says: it is the bread that the Lord has given you to eat. They did not earn it. They did not even understand it. They received it. That is how God feeds us. And it is only for one day at a time. Give us this day our daily bread, Jesus teaches us to pray.

In the Gospel Jesus sits in a boat and tells of a sower who went out to sow. The seed falls on the path and the birds eat it. On rock, and it withers. Among thorns, and they choke it. And on good soil, and it bears a hundredfold. Jesus is telling us that the word of God is seed, and our hearts are the soil. The Sower is generous. He scatters the seed everywhere, even on the path where we are in a hurry, even on the rock where we are shallow, even among the thorns of our worries. What kind of soil am I today?

Today we remember St Lawrence of Brindisi, a Capuchin friar who gave his life to sowing the word. He learned many languages in order to preach to many people, he preached to soldiers and to kings, and he loved the Bible so much that he is called a Doctor of the Church. The Lord gives us bread from heaven in the word and in the Eucharist. Let us receive both with thanks and make them bear fruit in our lives. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago God's people were hungry in the desert. And every morning God sent a special bread called manna, enough for everybody for that day! In the Gospel Jesus tells about a farmer who planted seeds. Some seed fell on the path and birds ate it. Some fell on rocks. Some fell among thorns. But some fell on good soil and grew into lots of grain. The seed is God's word. Let us listen and be good soil. And do not forget to say thank you to God before you eat.

Prayer

God of mercy, you rained bread from heaven on your people in the wilderness and you sow your word in our hearts. Teach us to receive each day's bread with thanks, without fear and without greed. Lord Jesus, the Sower who goes out to sow, make our hearts good soil: take the stones from them, pull out the thorns, and let your word bear fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold. Through the prayers of St Lawrence of Brindisi, who preached your word with courage and love, make us hearers and doers of what we hear. Give us this day our daily bread, and the bread of life that is yourself. Amen.
