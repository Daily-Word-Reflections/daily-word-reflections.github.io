Word for the day

“This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” John 15:12

Reflection

The council in Jerusalem has reached its decision, and now the Church puts it into words and sends it on its way. “The apostles and the elders, with the consent of the whole church,” choose two respected men, Judas called Barsabbas and Silas, to travel with Paul and Barnabas to Antioch with a letter. The letter is warm and humble. It admits that some had gone out “with no instructions from us” and had “unsettled your minds.” It calls Barnabas and Paul “our beloved,” men “who have risked their lives for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ.” And it contains one of the most remarkable sentences in the New Testament: “it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to impose on you no further burden than these essentials.” The Holy Spirit and us: the apostles do not claim to have invented the answer, nor do they hide behind private visions. The Spirit speaks through the gathered Church and its shepherds. The essentials they ask, avoiding food offered to idols, blood, what is strangled, and sexual immorality, allowed Jewish and Gentile Christians to live and eat together in peace. When the letter is read in Antioch, “they rejoiced at the exhortation.” Good teaching, given with love, brings joy, not fear.

In the Gospel Jesus gives the one commandment that sums up all the rest: “This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.” Not simply as we love ourselves, but as he loves us, to the end: “No one has greater love than this, to lay down one's life for one's friends.” Then he changes our name: “I do not call you servants any longer… but I have called you friends.” And he reminds us who took the first step: “You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last.” Today the Church remembers St Pius V (1504 to 1572), a shepherd boy from Piedmont who became a Dominican friar and then Pope from 1566. He gave the Church the fruit of the Council of Trent in lasting form: the Roman Catechism (1566), the reformed Breviary (1568) and the Roman Missal (1570), used for four centuries; he named St Thomas Aquinas a Doctor of the Church. As Pope he kept the poverty of a friar, visited the sick in hospitals and washed the feet of the poor. In the troubled year 1571 he called on the faithful to pray the Rosary, and in thanksgiving he instituted the feast now kept on 7 October as Our Lady of the Rosary. Like the apostles' letter, his work was meant to hand on the essentials of the faith clearly, so that the friends of Jesus might love one another in the truth.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Church in Jerusalem has made its decision, and now it writes a letter. Listen to its humble, joyful tone. It calls Paul and Barnabas our beloved. And it says: it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to impose on you no further burden than these essentials (Acts 15:28).

The Holy Spirit and us. The apostles do not say: we decided by ourselves. Nor: each of you decide for yourself. The Spirit speaks through the Church gathered with its shepherds. And when the letter is read in Antioch, they rejoiced at the exhortation (Acts 15:31). True teaching, given with love, does not crush; it lifts the heart and brings joy.

In the Gospel Jesus says what lies at the heart of every teaching: this is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you (John 15:12). He calls us friends, not servants. And he reminds us: you did not choose me but I chose you, and I appointed you to go and bear fruit, fruit that will last (John 15:16). Before we ever looked for him, he had chosen us.

St Pius V, a shepherd boy who became Pope, spent his life handing on the essentials of the faith: the Catechism, the Breviary, the Missal. Yet he kept the humility of a friar and served the sick with his own hands. Teaching and love go together. Tomorrow begins the month of Mary; let us ask her, and St Pius V who loved her Rosary, to help us live as chosen friends of Jesus. Amen.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you have called us not servants but friends, and you chose us to go and bear fruit that will last. Give us the Holy Spirit who guided the apostles in Jerusalem, so that we may receive the teaching of your Church with joy and love one another as you have loved us. Through the prayers of St Pius V, who handed on the faith clearly and served the poor humbly, keep your Church faithful and united. Who live and reign for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The decision of Jerusalem goes out as a letter, carried by trusted men, and when it is read at Antioch the believers rejoice. The letter speaks a sentence the Church has made her own: “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us”. In the Gospel Jesus gives his commandment of love, calls his disciples friends, and tells them, “You did not choose me but I chose you”. Psalm 57 gives thanks among the peoples. Today the Church also remembers St Pius V, pope.

Acts 15:22–31 · It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us

Setting.

The assembly chooses two leaders to go with Paul and Barnabas: Judas called Barsabbas, and Silas. Both are prophets (15:32). Silas will soon become Paul’s companion on his second journey (15:40). He is usually identified with the Silvanus named in Paul’s letters (1 Thessalonians 1:1; 2 Corinthians 1:19). The letter is addressed to Gentile believers in Antioch, Syria and Cilicia, the region where the trouble began.

Structure.

  • v. 22: the choice of messengers.
  • vv. 23–29: the letter: greeting, disowning of the troublemakers, praise of Barnabas and Paul, the decision.
  • vv. 30–31: the letter is read at Antioch, and the believers rejoice.
  • ¶ A note on the text: some ancient manuscripts, chiefly the so-called Western text, give the decree in a different form, without “what is strangled” and with the saying not to do to others what one would not want done to oneself.

Key words.

  • edoxen, it seemed good: The usual word for the decision of an assembly. The Church joins the Holy Spirit to her own judgment: “to the Holy Spirit and to us”.
  • epanankes, necessary, essential: Only a few necessary things are asked. The apostles lay on the Gentiles no heavier burden than charity and the life of the community require.
  • paraklēsis, exhortation, encouragement: The letter is received as paraklēsis (v. 31). The decision of the Church comes not as a weight but as comfort.

Verse by verse.

“though with no instructions from us,” (15:24)

The apostles make clear that the men who caused the trouble did not speak for them. Authority in the Church includes honestly correcting what was said in its name.

“who have risked their lives for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ.” (15:26)

High praise for Barnabas and Paul. The Jerusalem church stands publicly behind the missionaries.

“For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to impose on you no further burden than these essentials:” (15:28)

The Holy Spirit is named first. The apostles do not claim to have invented the decision; they have discerned it.

“When its members read it, they rejoiced at the exhortation.” (15:31)

A dispute that began with dissension ends with joy. Truth spoken in charity unites.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has often echoed this formula, and it expresses the faith that the Holy Spirit guides the Church’s shepherds when they decide together with Peter. The episode shows how authority serves peace: the decision was reached together, sent with living witnesses, written with respect, and received with joy.

John 15:12–17 · I have called you friends

Setting.

The farewell discourse continues. The commandment of love was first given after the washing of the feet (13:34). Here it is repeated at the beginning and end of the passage (vv. 12, 17), which frames everything in between.

Structure.

  • v. 12: love one another as I have loved you.
  • v. 13: the greatest love: to lay down one’s life.
  • vv. 14–15: no longer servants but friends.
  • vv. 16–17: chosen and appointed to bear lasting fruit; love one another.

Key words.

  • philos, friend: A friend shares what he knows. The disciples are friends because the Son has made known to them all that he heard from the Father (v. 15).
  • tithēmi tēn psychēn, to lay down one’s life: The same expression as the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (10:11).
  • eklegomai, to choose: The initiative is his. Disciples did not apply for the post; they were chosen and sent.

Verse by verse.

“No one has greater love than this, to lay down one’s life for one’s friends.” (15:13)

He speaks of his own death the next day. And Paul will add that Christ died for us while we were still sinners (Romans 5:8): he makes friends of those for whom he dies.

“but I have called you friends, because I have made known to you everything that I have heard from my Father.” (15:15)

Friendship with God was said of Abraham (Isaiah 41:8) and Moses, who spoke with God face to face, as one speaks to a friend (Exodus 33:11). Now it is given to all disciples.

“You did not choose me but I chose you.” (15:16)

Grace comes first. Our choice of him is always an answer to his choice of us.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the New Law is a law of love which lets us pass from the condition of a servant who does not know what his master is doing to that of a friend of Christ (CCC 1972). It also sees in v. 13 the love with which Jesus willingly offered his life (CCC 609).

For the Latin reader

St Pius V (1504–1572) was a Dominican friar, Michele Ghislieri, who became pope in 1566. He put into effect the reforms of the Council of Trent, publishing the Roman Catechism, the reformed Breviary and the Roman Missal of 1570. He called Christians to pray the Rosary for the victory at Lepanto in 1571, and from this came the feast of Our Lady of the Rosary on 7 October. His optional memorial falls on 30 April. Like the apostles in today’s first reading, he served the unity of the Church by giving clear teaching for the life of the faithful.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus calls me a friend. How would my prayer change if I spoke to him as a friend speaks to a friend?
  • Is there a decision of the Church that I find hard? Have I tried to read it, as the Antioch believers did, as encouragement rather than as burden?
