Word for the day

“Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets; I have come not to abolish but to fulfill.” Matthew 5:17

Reflection

In the Second Letter to the Corinthians Paul has to defend his ministry against people who came to Corinth with impressive letters of recommendation. He answers: I need no letters; “You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts,” “written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts.” Today's reading continues: “Not that we are competent of ourselves to claim anything as coming from us; our competence is from God, who has made us competent to be ministers of a new covenant, not of letter but of spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” Paul is thinking of Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant, when God would write his law on the heart, and of Ezekiel's promise of a new spirit. He compares the giving of the Law on Sinai, which came “in glory so that the people of Israel could not gaze at Moses' face because of the glory of his face,” with the ministry of the Spirit, and concludes: “how much more will the ministry of the Spirit come in glory?” We must read this carefully. Paul, a Jew who loved the Law, does not say the Law was bad. Elsewhere he writes: “the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and just and good” (Romans 7:12). He means that a law written outside us, on stone, can show us what is right but cannot give us the power to do it; it can only condemn the one who fails. The Spirit writes God's will within us and gives life. The glory of Sinai was real; the glory of the Spirit is greater and lasting. The Psalm sings the holiness of the God who spoke to Moses and Aaron and Samuel “in the pillar of cloud” and was “a forgiving God to them.”

The Gospel guards us against a wrong conclusion. If the Spirit gives life and the letter kills, should Christians throw away the commandments? Jesus answers clearly: “Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets; I have come not to abolish but to fulfill.” He respects even the smallest marks of the Scripture: “not one letter, not one stroke of a letter, will pass from the law until all is accomplished.” The Ten Commandments still stand; Jesus will show in the coming days that he asks for more, not less: not only no murder, but no anger; not only no adultery, but a pure heart. Fulfilment means bringing the Law to its full meaning, as a flower fulfils the bud. Jesus fulfils the Law by living it perfectly, by revealing its heart, which is love, and by giving the Spirit who makes us able to keep it. St Ephrem the Syrian, whom we may remember today, was born around 306 in Nisibis, served the Church there as a deacon and teacher, and spent his last years in Edessa, where he died in 373. He put the whole faith into hymns and taught choirs to sing them; the Syriac Churches call him the Harp of the Spirit. He wrote (in substance) that no one who drinks from the spring of God's word should be sad that he cannot drink it all; let him rejoice that it is inexhaustible. In 1920 Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the Church. The Kerala Churches of the Syriac tradition still sing his words.

Full homily

Dear friends, some preachers in Corinth carried impressive letters of recommendation, and they asked: where are Paul's letters? Paul answers: you are my letter. You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts (2 Corinthians 3:2), written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God. The best proof of a minister's work is not a certificate but a changed life.

Then Paul says: the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life (2 Corinthians 3:6). He does not mean that God's Law is bad. Paul loved the Law. He means that a law written only on stone, outside us, can tell us what is right, but it cannot give us the strength to do it. When we fail, it can only condemn us. The Holy Spirit writes God's will inside us, in our hearts, and gives us the power to live it. That is the new covenant God promised through the prophet Jeremiah.

But the Gospel warns us not to go to the other extreme. Jesus says: do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets; I have come not to abolish but to fulfill (Matthew 5:17). The commandments are not thrown away. Jesus shows their full meaning and gives us his Spirit to keep them with love. A child obeys his mother first because he must, and later because he loves her. That is the journey from letter to Spirit.

Today the Church remembers St Ephrem, a deacon of the fourth century, whom our Syriac sister Churches in Kerala call Mar Aprem. He turned the faith into songs and taught people to sing it. He is called the Harp of the Holy Spirit. Let us ask him to help us sing God's commandments in our hearts, and to become letters of Christ that our families and neighbours can read. Amen.

For children

Children, imagine your mother writes on a paper: brush your teeth, say your prayers, help your little brother. You can obey the paper because you have to. But Jesus wants something better: he wants the Holy Spirit to write God's rules inside your heart, so that you do good because you love God. Jesus said: I did not come to throw away God's commandments, but to fulfill them. Today we remember St Ephrem, who wrote beautiful songs about Jesus. Sing a hymn to Jesus today!

Prayer

Holy God, you spoke to Moses in the cloud and gave your people the holy Law. Now through Jesus Christ you have made us ministers of a new covenant, not of letter but of Spirit. Write your will on our hearts, so that we keep your commandments not from fear but from love. Lord Jesus, you came not to abolish but to fulfill; bring our lives to their full meaning in you. Through the prayers of St Ephrem, Harp of the Spirit, may our words and songs give you glory. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul contrasts the ministry of Moses, written on stone, with the ministry of the new covenant, written by the Spirit: “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” In the Gospel Jesus says that he has come not to abolish the Law and the prophets but to fulfil them. The two readings must be read together: the Spirit does not throw away the Law; he writes it on the heart. On the memorial of St Ephrem, Psalm 99 proclaims: holy is the Lord our God.

2 Corinthians 3:4–11 · Ministers of a new covenant

Setting.

Some preachers had come to Corinth with letters of recommendation and questioned Paul’s credentials. Paul answers that the Corinthians themselves are his letter, written by the Spirit on human hearts (3:1–3). He then compares his ministry with that of Moses, using the story of Moses’ shining face when he came down from Sinai (Exodus 34:29–35).

Paul argues “from the lesser to the greater”, a method well known among the Jewish teachers of his time: if the first ministry had glory, how much more the second. He is not saying that the Law was bad. Elsewhere he calls it “holy and just and good” (Romans 7:12). He is saying that the new covenant, promised by Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:31–34), surpasses it.

Structure.

  • vv. 4–6: our competence is from God, who made us ministers of a new covenant, of spirit and not of letter.
  • vv. 7–8: the old ministry came in glory, so that Moses’ face shone; much more the ministry of the Spirit.
  • vv. 9–11: the ministry of condemnation had glory; the ministry of justification abounds in glory; what is permanent surpasses what was set aside.

Key words.

  • kainē diathēkē, new covenant: The phrase of Jeremiah 31:31, and of Jesus at the Last Supper: “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25). Paul is its servant.
  • gramma, letter: The written text by itself, carved on stone. Paul does not mean the literal sense of Scripture as opposed to the spiritual sense; he means a law written outside a person, which tells what to do but cannot give the power to do it.
  • doxa, glory: The visible radiance of God’s presence, Hebrew kabod. It shone on Moses’ face. In the new covenant it shines in Christ and, through the Spirit, in believers.

Verse by verse.

“our competence is from God,” (3:5)

Paul’s confidence is not self-confidence. Every minister of the Church, and every Christian, works from a gift received.

“not of letter but of spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” (3:6)

The Law shows sin but cannot heal it; only the Spirit pours God’s love into our hearts (Romans 5:5). This is how St Augustine read the verse in his work On the Spirit and the Letter (in substance).

“how much more will the ministry of the Spirit come in glory?” (3:8)

If a reflection of God’s glory on one man’s face made the people afraid to look, what will the gift of the Spirit to all believers be?

“for if what was set aside came through glory, much more has the permanent come in glory!” (3:11)

The old covenant came “through glory”: Paul honours it. The new is permanent because it is founded on Christ’s death and resurrection.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that, in Christian tradition, the Law is holy, spiritual and good, yet still imperfect; it is a first stage on the way to the kingdom (CCC 1963). The New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit given through faith in Christ (CCC 1966). The Church also holds that God’s covenant with Israel has never been revoked (Romans 11:29; CCC 121). So Paul’s contrast is never a cause for pride or contempt. It is a call to let the Spirit write God’s will on our hearts.

Matthew 5:17–19 · Not to abolish but to fulfil

Setting.

These verses are the key to the whole Sermon on the Mount. In the next part Jesus will say six times, “You have heard that it was said … But I say to you” (5:21–48). Some might think he was setting the Law aside. Matthew, writing for a community with deep Jewish roots, shows at the start that Jesus fulfils the Law and the prophets.

Structure.

  • v. 17: I have come not to abolish but to fulfil.
  • v. 18: not one letter, not one stroke will pass from the Law until all is accomplished.
  • v. 19: whoever breaks and teaches others to break the least commandment will be least; whoever does and teaches them will be great.

Key words.

  • plēroō, fulfil: To fill up, to bring to full measure. Matthew uses it again and again for the prophecies fulfilled in Jesus. Jesus fulfils the Law by obeying it perfectly and by revealing its deepest meaning.
  • iōta, keraia, letter, stroke: The iota is the smallest Greek letter, corresponding to the Hebrew yod; the “stroke” is a tiny hook that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another. Not even the smallest detail of God’s word is without value.
  • entolē, commandment: Jewish teachers distinguished “light” and “heavy” commandments. Jesus says that even the least must be kept and taught.

Verse by verse.

“I have come not to abolish but to fulfill.” (5:17)

“I have come” is a phrase of mission: Jesus speaks of why he was sent. He is the goal towards which the Law and the prophets were moving.

“not one letter, not one stroke of a letter, will pass from the law until all is accomplished.” (5:18)

The Law endures, but it is now read in the light of Christ. The Church does not keep the ritual laws of the Old Testament, because Christ has accomplished what they foreshadowed; the moral law remains.

“but whoever does them and teaches them will be called great in the kingdom of heaven.” (5:19)

Doing comes before teaching. A teacher of the faith is measured first by his own life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Law of the Gospel fulfils, refines, surpasses and leads the Old Law to its perfection (CCC 1967), and that Jesus fulfilled the Law so perfectly that he took upon himself its curse for our sake (CCC 580). The Second Vatican Council, following St Augustine, taught that the New Testament lies hidden in the Old and the Old is made plain in the New (Dei Verbum 16). This is why the Church reads the Old Testament at Mass: it is not an old book left behind, but God’s word fulfilled in Christ.

Questions people ask

If Jesus did not abolish the Law, why do Christians not keep the food laws or circumcision?

The Church distinguishes within the Law. The moral law, summed up in the Ten Commandments, remains and is deepened by Christ. The ritual and ceremonial laws prepared for Christ and are fulfilled in him; the apostles at the Council of Jerusalem decided that Gentile believers were not bound by them (Acts 15). Christ has fulfilled them, and they now teach us about him.

For the Latin reader

Today is the optional memorial of St Ephrem the Syrian, deacon and Doctor of the Church. Born at Nisibis about 306, he taught there and later at Edessa, where he died in 373. He wrote hymns, poetic homilies and commentaries in Syriac, and taught the faith to his people through song; tradition calls him the Harp of the Holy Spirit. Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1920. Ephrem loved to find Christ hidden in the symbols of the Old Testament, which is today’s Gospel lived as prayer: the Law and the prophets fulfilled in Christ. The Syriac Churches of Kerala sing his hymns to this day.
