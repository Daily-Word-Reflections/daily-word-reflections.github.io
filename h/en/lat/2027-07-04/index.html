Word for the day

““Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” And he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them.” Mark 6:4–5

Reflection

The first reading brings us to the river Chebar in Babylon, where a young priest named Ezekiel lives among the exiles from Jerusalem, about 593 years before Christ. The Lord speaks to him, and a spirit enters him and sets him on his feet. God addresses him with a title that he will use ninety times: “Mortal,” in Hebrew ben adam, son of man, a creature of dust. Jesus will take this title for himself. The task is not pleasant: “I am sending you to the people of Israel, to a nation of rebels who have rebelled against me; they and their ancestors have transgressed against me to this very day. The descendants are impudent and stubborn.” God does not promise Ezekiel success. He says: “Whether they hear or refuse to hear (for they are a rebellious house), they shall know that there has been a prophet among them.” A prophet is not measured by his results. His work is to say, “Thus says the Lord God,” and the Lord will look after the rest. The Psalm gives the posture of such a servant: the eyes of the servant fixed on the hand of his master, “so our eyes look to the Lord our God, until he has mercy upon us,” even when we have had “more than enough of contempt.”

In the second reading St Paul tells the Corinthians one of the most personal secrets of his life. He has been given extraordinary revelations, he says, and “to keep me from being too elated, a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me.” Nobody knows what the thorn was: an illness, a temptation, an opponent, a weakness of speech. What matters is what he did with it: “Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me.” Three times, like Jesus in Gethsemane, and the answer is not what he asked for. The Lord does not take away the thorn; he gives something better: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” Paul's reaction is astonishing: “So, I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may dwell in me.” This is not a love of suffering for its own sake. Paul has learned that God's power does not need our strength; it is more clearly seen when our own strength is out of the way. “For whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” Elsewhere he says that we have this treasure “in clay jars, so that it may be made clear that this extraordinary power belongs to God and does not come from us” (2 Corinthians 4:7). The Church has always seen here a word for all who carry a hidden cross, an illness, a failure, a loneliness: God does not always remove it, but he promises his grace in the midst of it (Catechism 309–314).

The Gospel takes Jesus home. He comes “to his hometown,” Nazareth, a small village in Galilee, with his disciples following him, and on the Sabbath he teaches in the synagogue. At first the people are astounded: “Where did this man get all this? What is this wisdom that has been given to him? What deeds of power are being done by his hands!” But the astonishment turns into offence. “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon, and are not his sisters here with us?” The Greek says tekton, a craftsman who worked with wood or stone, and this is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus is called a carpenter. It is also the only place where Mark calls him “son of Mary.” They know his family, his trade and his face, and for this very reason they cannot see who he is. The Church reads “brothers and sisters” in the sense of near relatives, which the Aramaic and Hebrew word covers, and teaches that Mary remained a virgin throughout her life (Catechism 500). They “took offense at him,” literally they stumbled over him. Jesus answers with a proverb: “Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” Then Mark says something sobering: “And he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them.” It is not that his power is limited, but that faith is needed for it to be received. “And he was amazed at their unbelief.” Jesus, who marvelled at the faith of the centurion (Matthew 8:10), is here astonished by its absence. Yet he does not give up: “Then he went about among the villages teaching.” In the same way, the one who spent thirty years in an obscure village shows us that God hides himself in what is ordinary (Catechism 531–533); and the one who can be rejected by his own continues to knock at the next door.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, have you ever noticed how hard it is to be a prophet in your own house? We find it easier to listen to a stranger from far away than to the person whom we have known for years. That is the story of today's Gospel. Jesus comes home to Nazareth, to the village where he grew up. On the Sabbath he teaches in the synagogue, and at first the people are amazed. Where did this man get all this? What is this wisdom? (Mark 6:2). But in a moment their amazement turns into offence: Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary? (Mark 6:3). We know him. We know his family. How can he be anything special?

Dear friends, this is the scandal of the Incarnation. God did not come to us as a thunderbolt, or as a king in a palace. He came as a baby, he grew up in a village, he learned a trade and he worked with his hands. For thirty years nobody noticed anything special. And this is a gift for us. If God became so ordinary, then our ordinary days, our work, our kitchens and our fields are the place where he meets us. But the people of Nazareth could not see him in the ordinary, and Mark says: he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them. He was amazed at their unbelief (Mark 6:5–6). Faith opens the door. Where there is no faith, even the Lord's power does not enter.

In the first reading God sends Ezekiel to a rebellious people. He does not promise him success. He says: whether they hear or refuse to hear, they shall know that there has been a prophet among them (Ezekiel 2:5). What does it mean for us? Each of us, by baptism, is a prophet. In our family, our office, our school, we are asked to speak and live the truth of the Gospel. We may not be listened to. But our task is not to be successful. Our task is to be faithful. God looks after the results.

And what if we are weak? St Paul gives the answer in the second reading. He had a thorn in the flesh, and three times he asked the Lord to remove it. And the Lord said: My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). Dear friends, each of us has a thorn: an illness, a failure, a sin that we keep repeating, a lonely situation, a child who has left the faith. The Lord may not take it away today. But he promises his grace. And Paul tells us the surprising secret: whenever I am weak, then I am strong. Our weakness is not an obstacle to God. It is the empty space that he fills.

Today, as we receive the Lord in a small piece of bread, let us ask him for three graces: faith that sees him in the ordinary, courage to speak his word whether people hear it or not, and trust in his grace when we are weak. And let us also thank the people at home whom we take for granted. They may be the prophets God has sent us. The Lord who was not honoured in Nazareth is honoured here today. Let us honour him.

For children

Children, Jesus grew up in a village called Nazareth. When he was a grown man he went back there and taught the people about God. They were amazed, and then they said: we know him! He is the carpenter, Mary's son! They did not believe, and Jesus was sad. But he did not give up. He went to other villages and taught them. Today we also learn about St Paul, who had something hard in his life, and God said to him: my grace is enough for you. When something is hard for us, let us remember that Jesus is with us and that his help is enough. And let us always say thank you to the people at home!

Prayer

O God, who in the abasement of your Son have raised up a fallen world, fill your faithful with holy joy, for on those you have rescued from slavery to sin you bestow eternal gladness. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you came to your own and your own did not receive you. Give us faith to recognise you in the ordinary, in the poor, in the Eucharist and in those whom we think we know. Like Paul, teach us to boast in our weakness, so that your power may rest on us. Let your grace be sufficient for us this week. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

All three readings ask how God’s word comes to us, and why we so easily miss it. Ezekiel is sent to a people who may not listen, with the promise that they will at least know that a prophet has been among them. In Nazareth, Jesus is the prophet, and his neighbours cannot see beyond the carpenter they know. Between them stands Paul, who prayed three times to be rid of a thorn and heard the Lord answer: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” In all three, God works through what looks small, ordinary or weak. Psalm 123 gives the prayer: “our eyes look to the Lord our God, until he has mercy upon us.”

Ezekiel 2:2–5 · A prophet among them

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest of Jerusalem, carried into exile in Babylon in 597 BC with King Jehoiachin and thousands of others. He received his call in the fifth year of that exile, by the river Chebar (1:1–3). Before our passage he has seen the glory of God on a moving throne (chapter 1), and has fallen on his face. Now God speaks to him, calls him “mortal” (literally “son of man”, the title God uses of him some ninety times), and sends him.

Background. The people to whom Ezekiel is sent are his own: Israel, in exile or still in Jerusalem. The prophets speak from within the covenant people, not against them, and their sharp words belong to Israel’s own Scripture. The book that begins with judgment also holds some of the Old Testament’s greatest promises of restoration (chapters 34 to 37). “A nation of rebels” describes a people who have broken faith, as Israel itself confesses.

Structure.

  • v. 2: the Spirit enters Ezekiel and sets him on his feet.
  • v. 3: the mission: to Israel, to those who have rebelled.
  • v. 4: the messenger formula: “Thus says the Lord God.”
  • v. 5: whether they listen or not, they will know a prophet has been among them.

Key words.

  • ben ’ādām, son of man, mortal: The usual address to Ezekiel, a reminder that he is a frail human being. Jesus later uses the same expression of himself in the Gospels.
  • rûaḥ, spirit, breath, wind: The one Hebrew word covers the Spirit of God and the breath that lifts Ezekiel to his feet. The prophet cannot stand to receive his mission until the Spirit stands him up.
  • bêt mĕrî, rebellious house: A repeated phrase in Ezekiel for a people that has turned from God. It is spoken from within, by a prophet who loves his people.

Verse by verse.

“a spirit entered into me and set me on my feet” (2:2)

The call comes in two stages: God speaks, and the Spirit makes the prophet able to listen and to stand. God does not send anyone without giving them strength.

“I am sending you to the people of Israel, to a nation of rebels who have rebelled against me” (2:3)

The mission begins with a hard truth. The prophet is not promised that his people will welcome him.

“Thus says the Lord God.” (2:4)

The prophet’s authority is not his own. He is a messenger, and the word he carries is the Lord’s.

“they shall know that there has been a prophet among them” (2:5)

The success of the prophet is not measured by whether people obey. The word will have been spoken, and Israel will not be able to say that God did not warn them.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that God formed his people through the prophets in the hope of salvation and of a new and everlasting covenant (CCC 64). Jesus calls himself “Son of Man”, and the Letter to the Hebrews says that God, who spoke by the prophets, has spoken in these last days by a Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). Pope St Gregory the Great preached a series of homilies on Ezekiel in Rome during a time of great danger for the city, and pastors have found in this call a picture of their own task: to speak God’s word, whether it is welcomed or not.

2 Corinthians 12:7–10 · My grace is sufficient for you

Setting.

Paul has been forced into what he calls “foolish” boasting (11:1 to 12:13), because some newly arrived teachers at Corinth were claiming greater status than he had. He tells of an unnamed man in Christ caught up to the third heaven fourteen years before, and of things “that no mortal is permitted to repeat” (12:1–4). He now says why he does not boast of this. The thorn comes in at once.

Background. Paul never says what the thorn was. Interpreters through the centuries have suggested an illness, a temptation, a handicap, or the opposition of enemies. The Church has not defined it, and the silence may be intended: every reader can supply their own thorn. Paul calls it “a messenger of Satan”, but he also writes that it was “given” to him, which means that God permitted it for a purpose.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the thorn, given to keep Paul from pride.
  • v. 8: his triple prayer for its removal.
  • v. 9a: the Lord’s answer.
  • vv. 9b–10: Paul’s response: gladness in weakness, and the paradox of strength.

Key words.

  • skolops, thorn, stake: A sharp point such as a splinter or a stake. It suggests acute and continuing pain rather than a passing trouble.
  • charis, grace: God’s undeserved gift of his own life and help (CCC 1996). Paul’s answer is not a lesson in patience but a promise: the Lord’s own grace is enough.
  • astheneia, weakness: The same word is used of illness and of human frailty in general. In this chapter it becomes the very place where the power of Christ shows itself.

Verse by verse.

“a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me” (12:7)

The verb is passive: the thorn “was given”. Paul recognizes that God allowed it, while its torment is evil. He does not say that God wished him to suffer; he says that God used it to guard him from pride.

“Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me” (12:8)

Paul prays as Jesus prayed in Gethsemane, asking three times that the cup pass (Mark 14:32–42). Even an apostle’s prayer does not always receive the answer he asks for.

“My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” (12:9)

Textual note: many manuscripts read “my power”, others omit “my”; the sense is the same. The Lord’s word is not an explanation but a presence: his grace is enough.

“whenever I am weak, then I am strong” (12:10)

Not a boast of weakness for its own sake, but trust that when Paul has nothing of his own to lean on, the strength he relies upon is Christ’s.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that only faith can embrace the mysterious ways of God’s almighty power, and that this faith “glories in its weaknesses in order to draw to itself Christ’s power”, with the Virgin Mary as its supreme model (CCC 273). St Augustine, writing on prayer, held that when God does not give what we ask for, it can be mercy: Paul was not granted the removal of the thorn, because what he received was better (in substance, Letter 130). The passage is also often read at the Anointing of the Sick and in prayer with those who suffer: it does not say that suffering is good, but that God’s grace does not leave the sufferer.

Mark 6:1–6 · A prophet without honour

Setting.

Jesus has just worked two great signs: the calming of the storm and the raising of Jairus’ daughter, with the healing of the woman who touched his cloak (4:35 to 5:43). Now he comes to “his hometown”, which Mark does not name but which is Nazareth, with his disciples. In Mark’s account it is his first return since leaving to begin his mission, and on the Sabbath he teaches in the synagogue.

Background. The people of Nazareth are Jesus’ own neighbours and kin, a small village where everyone knew everyone. They are not villains; they are astonished by his wisdom and works. Their trouble is a human one: it is hard to see someone great in a person you have known all your life. Jesus and his family and disciples are themselves faithful Jews of Galilee, and Mark’s story is of the village’s struggle to believe, not of a nation.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2a: Jesus comes to his hometown and teaches in the synagogue on the Sabbath.
  • vv. 2b–3: astonishment and offence: where does he get all this?
  • v. 4: Jesus’ saying about the prophet.
  • vv. 5–6a: few miracles; his amazement at their unbelief.
  • v. 6b: Jesus goes on teaching in the surrounding villages.

Key words.

  • exeplēssonto, they were astounded: A strong word, “struck out of themselves”. Mark uses it elsewhere for the crowd’s reaction to Jesus’ teaching (1:22; 11:18). Astonishment is not yet faith.
  • tektōn, carpenter: A craftsman in wood, and perhaps in stone as well. This is the only time Mark calls Jesus a carpenter. Matthew (13:55) says “the carpenter’s son”.
  • eskandalizonto, they took offense: From skandalon, a stumbling block. What trips them is the ordinariness of the one who teaches, and their own sense that they know where he comes from.

Verse by verse.

“On the sabbath he began to teach in the synagogue, and many who heard him were astounded.” (6:2)

Jesus teaches in the synagogue as a faithful Jew, and the people recognize authority in him. The question is not whether his words are good, but where they come from.

“Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon” (6:3)

Villagers are usually identified by their father’s name; “son of Mary” is unusual. “Brother” here and “sisters” later are used in the Old Testament sense of close relatives (CCC 500). Mark gives them as an ordinary family, to make the point that Jesus’ origins seemed to the neighbours quite unremarkable.

“Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” (6:4)

Jesus applies a proverb to himself and places himself among the prophets such as Ezekiel who were not welcomed. He also shows the loneliness of the Son who is not recognized by his own.

“he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them.” (6:5)

Mark’s language is strong: “could do no deed”. It does not mean that his power failed, but that miracles are signs offered to faith, not shows to compel it. Matthew softens it to “he did not do many” (13:58).

“he was amazed at their unbelief” (6:6)

Jesus is amazed here and, in Matthew and Luke, at the faith of the centurion (Matthew 8:10; Luke 7:9). The one who astonishes everyone is himself astonished, once by faith and once by its lack.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that during the greater part of his life Jesus shared the condition of the vast majority of human beings: a daily life spent without evident greatness, a life of manual labour (CCC 531). The scandal of Nazareth is the scandal of the Incarnation itself: God comes in what is ordinary. Preachers have long seen in the neighbours’ question a warning to every believer: familiarity can dull wonder, even before the Eucharist, which seems so plain. The same question is asked of the Church: how can God’s power and wisdom be present in people, words and bread that are so familiar?

Questions people ask

What was Paul’s thorn in the flesh?

Paul does not say, and the Church has not defined it. Illness, temptation, persecution and opposition from rivals have all been proposed. The unnamed thorn lets every reader hear the Lord’s answer for their own suffering: “My grace is sufficient for you”.

Why does Mark say that Jesus “could do no deed of power” in Nazareth?

Mark does not suggest that Jesus’ power failed, but that miracles are signs that call for faith, not tricks to compel it. Where people are closed to him, the sign cannot do its work. Even so, Jesus heals a few, and goes on teaching. The same Gospel shows him giving power to others in the next verses (6:7–13).

Who are the “brothers and sisters” of Jesus?

The Greek adelphos and the Hebrew word behind it can mean close relatives such as cousins. The Catholic Church holds that Mary is ever virgin. James and Joses, named here, are described elsewhere as sons of another Mary who was a disciple (CCC 500; compare Mark 15:40).

For the Latin reader

This is the Fourteenth Sunday in Ordinary Time, in Year B of the Sunday cycle, the year of Mark. Mark is the shortest Gospel and cannot fill a whole year, so for five Sundays from 25 July the Church reads the Bread of Life discourse of John 6. The Sunday Mass is where the prophet’s word is still proclaimed: the Church hears the readings as spoken to her now, and the homily is meant to open them (CCC 1154). The Sunday Mass is also the place where the ordinary is the carrier of Christ’s presence, as it was in Nazareth: simple bread and wine, a priest the parish knows, a congregation of familiar faces.

For study and teaching

  • Ezekiel is told that the people may not listen. When have I spoken a word of faith and not been heard, and how did I respond?
  • Paul’s thorn was not taken away. What is my own “thorn”, and what would it mean to hear “my grace is sufficient” about it?
  • The people of Nazareth could not see beyond what they knew. Where does familiarity dull my wonder at Jesus: in the Eucharist, in my parish, in my own family?
  • Psalm 123 prays with eyes fixed on the Lord “until he has mercy upon us”. How do I wait on God when I am treated with contempt or when I feel small?
