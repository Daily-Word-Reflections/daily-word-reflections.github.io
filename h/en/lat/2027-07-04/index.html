  • Responsorial Psalm Psalm 123:1–4 (Lectionary 123:1–2a, 2bcd, 3–4) · Our eyes are fixed on the Lord, pleading for his mercy

Word for the day

““Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” And he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them.” Mark 6:4–5

Reflection

The first reading brings us to the river Chebar in Babylon, where a young priest named Ezekiel lives among the exiles from Jerusalem, about 593 years before Christ. The Lord speaks to him, and a spirit enters him and sets him on his feet. God addresses him with a title that he will use ninety times: “Mortal,” in Hebrew ben adam, son of man, a creature of dust. Jesus will take this title for himself. The task is not pleasant: “I am sending you to the people of Israel, to a nation of rebels who have rebelled against me; they and their ancestors have transgressed against me to this very day. The descendants are impudent and stubborn.” God does not promise Ezekiel success. He says: “Whether they hear or refuse to hear (for they are a rebellious house), they shall know that there has been a prophet among them.” A prophet is not measured by his results. His work is to say, “Thus says the Lord God,” and the Lord will look after the rest. The Psalm gives the posture of such a servant: the eyes of the servant fixed on the hand of his master, “so our eyes look to the Lord our God, until he has mercy upon us,” even when we have had “more than enough of contempt.”

In the second reading St Paul tells the Corinthians one of the most personal secrets of his life. He has been given extraordinary revelations, he says, and “to keep me from being too elated, a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me.” Nobody knows what the thorn was: an illness, a temptation, an opponent, a weakness of speech. What matters is what he did with it: “Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me.” Three times, like Jesus in Gethsemane, and the answer is not what he asked for. The Lord does not take away the thorn; he gives something better: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” Paul's reaction is astonishing: “So, I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may dwell in me.” This is not a love of suffering for its own sake. Paul has learned that God's power does not need our strength; it is more clearly seen when our own strength is out of the way. “For whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” Elsewhere he says that we have this treasure “in clay jars, so that it may be made clear that this extraordinary power belongs to God and does not come from us” (2 Corinthians 4:7). The Church has always seen here a word for all who carry a hidden cross, an illness, a failure, a loneliness: God does not always remove it, but he promises his grace in the midst of it (Catechism 309–314).

The Gospel takes Jesus home. He comes “to his hometown,” Nazareth, a small village in Galilee, with his disciples following him, and on the Sabbath he teaches in the synagogue. At first the people are astounded: “Where did this man get all this? What is this wisdom that has been given to him? What deeds of power are being done by his hands!” But the astonishment turns into offence. “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon, and are not his sisters here with us?” The Greek says tekton, a craftsman who worked with wood or stone, and this is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus is called a carpenter. It is also the only place where Mark calls him “son of Mary.” They know his family, his trade and his face, and for this very reason they cannot see who he is. The Church reads “brothers and sisters” in the sense of near relatives, which the Aramaic and Hebrew word covers, and teaches that Mary remained a virgin throughout her life (Catechism 500). They “took offense at him,” literally they stumbled over him. Jesus answers with a proverb: “Prophets are not without honor, except in their hometown, and among their own kin, and in their own house.” Then Mark says something sobering: “And he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them.” It is not that his power is limited, but that faith is needed for it to be received. “And he was amazed at their unbelief.” Jesus, who marvelled at the faith of the centurion (Matthew 8:10), is here astonished by its absence. Yet he does not give up: “Then he went about among the villages teaching.” In the same way, the one who spent thirty years in an obscure village shows us that God hides himself in what is ordinary (Catechism 531–533); and the one who can be rejected by his own continues to knock at the next door.

Homily outline

Theme: “My grace is sufficient for you.”

A prophet sent to people who may not listen, an apostle whose thorn is not removed, and the carpenter's son whom his own town could not recognise.

1. A prophet among them

  • The son of man on his feet by the Spirit.
  • A rebellious house.
  • Faithful, whether they hear or not.
“Whether they hear or refuse to hear (for they are a rebellious house), they shall know that there has been a prophet among them.” Ezekiel 2:5
“And he said, “Truly I tell you, no prophet is accepted in the prophet’s hometown.” Luke 4:24

Apply: Say one true and kind word this week about your faith to someone at home or at work, and leave the result to God.

2. Power in weakness

  • A thorn in the flesh.
  • Three times he asked.
  • When I am weak, then I am strong.
“so our eyes look to the Lord our God, until he has mercy upon us.” Psalm 123:2
“but he said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.”” 2 Corinthians 12:9

Apply: Offer your own thorn to God every morning this week in one sentence: My grace is sufficient for me.

3. Is not this the carpenter?

  • The village of Nazareth.
  • Too familiar to see.
  • God in the ordinary.
“For he grew up before him like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground; he had no form or majesty that we should look at him, nothing in his appearance that we should desire him.” Isaiah 53:2
“Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon, and are not his sisters here with us?” And they took offense at him.” Mark 6:3

Apply: Offer your ordinary work to the carpenter of Nazareth, and do it as well as you can.

4. Amazed at their unbelief

  • He could do no deed of power.
  • Faith opens the door.
  • He goes on to other villages.
“Have mercy upon us, O Lord, have mercy upon us, for we have had more than enough of contempt.” Psalm 123:3
“And he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them. And he was amazed at their unbelief.” Mark 6:5–6

Apply: At Communion this week, say: Lord, I believe; help my unbelief, and open my heart.

God sends Ezekiel to a rebellious people and calls him to be faithful, not successful; he answers Paul's thorn with grace, because power is made perfect in weakness.

Jesus is rejected by his own town because he is too familiar, and yet he goes on teaching; faith opens the door to his power.

Take-home resolution: Thank one person at home whom you take for granted, and offer your thorn to God each day.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, have you ever noticed how hard it is to be a prophet in your own house? We find it easier to listen to a stranger from far away than to the person whom we have known for years. That is the story of today's Gospel. Jesus comes home to Nazareth, to the village where he grew up. On the Sabbath he teaches in the synagogue, and at first the people are amazed. Where did this man get all this? What is this wisdom? (Mark 6:2). But in a moment their amazement turns into offence: Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary? (Mark 6:3). We know him. We know his family. How can he be anything special?

Dear friends, this is the scandal of the Incarnation. God did not come to us as a thunderbolt, or as a king in a palace. He came as a baby, he grew up in a village, he learned a trade and he worked with his hands. For thirty years nobody noticed anything special. And this is a gift for us. If God became so ordinary, then our ordinary days, our work, our kitchens and our fields are the place where he meets us. But the people of Nazareth could not see him in the ordinary, and Mark says: he could do no deed of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and cured them. He was amazed at their unbelief (Mark 6:5–6). Faith opens the door. Where there is no faith, even the Lord's power does not enter.

In the first reading God sends Ezekiel to a rebellious people. He does not promise him success. He says: whether they hear or refuse to hear, they shall know that there has been a prophet among them (Ezekiel 2:5). What does it mean for us? Each of us, by baptism, is a prophet. In our family, our office, our school, we are asked to speak and live the truth of the Gospel. We may not be listened to. But our task is not to be successful. Our task is to be faithful. God looks after the results.

And what if we are weak? St Paul gives the answer in the second reading. He had a thorn in the flesh, and three times he asked the Lord to remove it. And the Lord said: My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). Dear friends, each of us has a thorn: an illness, a failure, a sin that we keep repeating, a lonely situation, a child who has left the faith. The Lord may not take it away today. But he promises his grace. And Paul tells us the surprising secret: whenever I am weak, then I am strong. Our weakness is not an obstacle to God. It is the empty space that he fills.

Today, as we receive the Lord in a small piece of bread, let us ask him for three graces: faith that sees him in the ordinary, courage to speak his word whether people hear it or not, and trust in his grace when we are weak. And let us also thank the people at home whom we take for granted. They may be the prophets God has sent us. The Lord who was not honoured in Nazareth is honoured here today. Let us honour him.

For children

Children, Jesus grew up in a village called Nazareth. When he was a grown man he went back there and taught the people about God. They were amazed, and then they said: we know him! He is the carpenter, Mary's son! They did not believe, and Jesus was sad. But he did not give up. He went to other villages and taught them. Today we also learn about St Paul, who had something hard in his life, and God said to him: my grace is enough for you. When something is hard for us, let us remember that Jesus is with us and that his help is enough. And let us always say thank you to the people at home!

Prayer

O God, who in the abasement of your Son have raised up a fallen world, fill your faithful with holy joy, for on those you have rescued from slavery to sin you bestow eternal gladness. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you came to your own and your own did not receive you. Give us faith to recognise you in the ordinary, in the poor, in the Eucharist and in those whom we think we know. Like Paul, teach us to boast in our weakness, so that your power may rest on us. Let your grace be sufficient for us this week. Amen.
