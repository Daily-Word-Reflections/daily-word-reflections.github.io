Word for the day

“Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.” Matthew 11:28–30

Reflection

Moses has met God at the burning bush and been sent to Egypt, and now he asks a practical question: “If I come to the Israelites and say to them, ‘The God of your ancestors has sent me to you,’ and they ask me, ‘What is his name?’ what shall I say to them?” In the ancient world a name was not a label; it opened a person's character and a way into a relationship. God answers with the most mysterious sentence in the Old Testament: “I am who I am.” The Hebrew, ehyeh asher ehyeh, can mean “I am who I am,” or “I will be who I will be,” or “I will be there as who I will be”: the God who simply is, and who is present and faithful. Then God gives a shorter form that Israel would speak with great reverence, the divine Name written with four Hebrew letters (YHWH), which our Bibles print as “the Lord” in capitals, and which pious Jews never pronounce out of respect. The Church has the same reverence and does not pronounce it in worship. “This is my name forever, and this my title for all generations.” Notice that the name comes joined to a history: “the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob.” God is not an idea; he is the God of a family and a promise. Moses is to gather the elders and say: “I have given heed to you and to what has been done to you in Egypt.” The God who is is the God who looks. He also tells the truth about the road ahead: “the king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand.” The liberation will be costly and slow, but God says plainly: “I will stretch out my hand.” The psalm answers with thanksgiving for what he has done: “Remember the wonderful works he has done, his miracles,” and especially that he “sent his servant Moses, and Aaron whom he had chosen.”

From the mountain of God we go to the lake of Galilee, and to words of rest. After the reproach of the towns and the thanksgiving of Jesus, there is this invitation: “Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest.” The people who heard him knew about burdens: the labour of poor farmers and fishermen, taxes, and a religion that some teachers had loaded with many rules. In Jewish teaching “the yoke” was a familiar picture for the study and keeping of the Law and for the service of God; Ben Sira had said: “Put your neck under her yoke, and let your souls receive instruction.” Jesus takes up the picture and changes it: “Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me.” He does not abolish the yoke; he offers his own, which is himself. A yoke was usually worn by two animals together, so to take his yoke is to be joined to him and to walk at his side. And the teacher is not a hard master: “I am gentle and humble in heart.” Learning from Jesus means learning meekness, and rest is the fruit: “you will find rest for your souls.” This is the sentence that echoes Jeremiah's “ancient paths,” where Israel was told to “find rest for your souls.” His yoke is “easy” because love makes burdens light, and because he carries it with us. The Exodus reading and the Gospel fit: the God who says “I am” has come to the weary, and the Son who knows the Father invites everyone to come.

Full homily

Dear friends, Moses asks a very honest question: what is his name? He is afraid that when he goes to Egypt the people will ask, and he will have no answer. And God says: I am who I am (Exodus 3:14). It is the most mysterious of all answers. God does not say: I am the strongest, or the greatest. He says: I am. I am here, I am faithful, I am the one who is. And he adds: the God of your ancestors, the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob. His name is joined to the names of a family and a promise.

And listen to what God says next: I have given heed to you and to what has been done to you in Egypt (Exodus 3:16). He has seen. Whoever is suffering today should hear this first: God has seen. And he tells the truth: the king will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand (Exodus 3:19). The road will be hard. But God says: I will stretch out my hand. He does not promise an easy road; he promises himself.

And now Jesus, in the Gospel, says almost the same thing in the language of a friend: Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest (Matthew 11:28). Who is weary today? Come. Who carries a burden? Come. Not first to an idea or a rule, but to him. And what does he give? Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart (Matthew 11:29). The yoke of two. We do not pull alone.

St Bonaventure, whom we remember today, was one of the most learned men of the Middle Ages, and yet he said that learning must end in love. He had a mind like a library and a heart like a flame, and the flame was fed by the Crucified. Let us do the same: let us study, let us pray, and let us come to the Lord and put our burden on his shoulder. Amen.

For children

Children, Moses asked God: what is your name? And God said: I am. That means: I am always here for you. Then Jesus says to us: Come to me, all who are tired and sad, and I will give you rest. He says: I am kind and gentle. When something is too heavy for you, you can give it to Jesus, like giving your school bag to a big brother to carry! St Bonaventure was a very clever teacher who loved Jesus very much. He taught that the most important thing is to love. Let us tell Jesus today what is heavy for us.

Prayer

God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, you told Moses your name and you promised to be with your people. Teach us to say your name with love and reverence, and to trust that you have seen what is done to us. Lord Jesus, gentle and humble of heart, we are weary and burdened: we come to you. Take your yoke upon us, and carry it with us, and give us rest. As you gave St Bonaventure a mind enlightened by learning and a heart on fire with love for the Crucified, give us a faith that studies, adores and serves. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s readings are about a name and about rest. In Exodus Moses asks God for his name before he goes to the elders of Israel, and receives the great answer “I am who I am”. God makes himself known as the one who is, who was the God of the fathers, and who sees and will deliver. In the Gospel Jesus, the Son who alone knows the Father, speaks in his own person and says: “Come to me, all you that are weary… and I will give you rest.” The God who gave Moses his name now gives us himself, gentle and humble of heart. On this day the Church keeps the memorial of St Bonaventure, bishop and Doctor of the Church.

Exodus 3:13–20 · I AM has sent me to you

Setting.

This is the second half of the scene at the burning bush. In vv. 1–12 God has called Moses and promised, “I will be with you.” Moses now raises a different difficulty. The Israelites in Egypt may ask which God has sent him, since the gods of Egypt were known by many names. Moses asks for the name that will make this God known.

Structure.

  • v. 13: Moses asks what he is to say if they ask God’s name.
  • vv. 14–15: God gives the name, “I am who I am”, and tells Moses what to tell Israel; “This is my name forever.”
  • vv. 16–17: Moses is to assemble the elders and tell them of God’s visit and promise.
  • vv. 18–20: the elders and Moses will ask Pharaoh to let the people go three days’ journey to sacrifice; Pharaoh will not agree until God’s hand and wonders compel him.

Key words.

  • ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh, I am who I am: A Hebrew phrase that can also mean “I will be what I will be” or “I will be who I will be”. It is a name of presence, not of definition: God is the one who is there, who is with his people, and who cannot be reduced to an idol.
  • YHWH, the Lord: The four letters of the divine name, which sounds like the verb “to be”. Out of reverence the people of Israel do not pronounce it, and in reading Scripture they say Adonai, “Lord”. The Greek Old Testament puts Kyrios, “Lord”, and Christians follow the same practice.
  • ʾĕlōhê ʾăbōtêkem, God of your fathers: God does not make a new beginning. He is the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, who kept the promises of old and will keep them now.

Verse by verse.

“God said to Moses, “I am who I am.”” (3:14)

The words are mysterious on purpose. God is not one being among many; he is the source of all being. The Greek Old Testament renders them “I am the One who is”, which later shaped Christian thought about God.

“‘I am has sent me to you.’” (3:14)

The name is given for the sake of the people. They are not to be left with a distant power but with a God who is present and acts.

“This is my name forever, and this my title for all generations.” (3:15)

The God who is with Moses is the same God who is with his people in every generation, including ours.

“I know, however, that the king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand.” (3:19)

God does not hide the difficulty from Moses. He tells him in advance that Pharaoh will resist and that the deliverance will cost struggle.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in the revelation of the name God shows himself as the God who is always present, faithful and near, and that this name is mysterious and holy (CCC 205–206; 213). It notes also that the people of Israel do not pronounce the name out of reverence and that in the readings of Scripture the divine name is replaced by “Lord” (CCC 209). In the Gospel of John, Jesus speaks with the same words, “I am”, to reveal who he is (John 8:58; 18:5–6). Christian readers have long seen in the name an early announcement of the God of the Gospel, who is not a distant ruler but one who comes down to his people.

Matthew 11:28–30 · Come to me and I will give you rest

Setting.

These three verses follow directly the thanksgiving of Jesus to the Father (11:25–27), where he says that the Son alone reveals the Father. Now the Son turns from the Father to the crowds. The Gospel of Matthew has just described cities that did not repent and people who found fault with both John and Jesus. In this setting Jesus makes his invitation without conditions: come.

Structure.

  • v. 28: the invitation: come to me, you who are weary and heavy laden; I will give you rest.
  • v. 29: take my yoke and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart; you will find rest for your souls.
  • v. 30: my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.

Key words.

  • anapausis, rest: Not only sleep, but refreshment and peace. Rest in the Old Testament is a gift from God (Exodus 33:14), and a promise for those who walk in the ancient paths (Jeremiah 6:16, the words “find rest for your souls”).
  • zygos, yoke: The wooden bar that joined two animals to pull together. Teachers of Israel spoke of the “yoke” of the Law and of wisdom, as in Sirach 51:26. Jesus offers his own yoke, not a heavier load, but a new way of walking with him.
  • chrēstos, easy, kindly: The word means good, kind, well fitted. A yoke that is “easy” is one made to fit the animal, so that it does not rub. Jesus’ commandments are fitted to us by grace.

Verse by verse.

“Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest.” (11:28)

The invitation is addressed to everyone. Jesus does not first ask for good works, but only that people come. He himself is the place of rest.

“Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart” (11:29)

Jesus is a teacher, and to take his yoke is to become a disciple who learns. What he teaches is gentleness and humility, and he teaches it by being so himself.

“For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.” (11:30)

This does not mean that discipleship is without cost, for Jesus speaks elsewhere of the cross (16:24). It means that the one who carries it with Jesus is not alone and does not carry it by his own strength.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 29 in teaching that Christ is the model of holiness: “Learn from me” (CCC 459). St Augustine’s famous words, “our heart is restless until it rests in you” (Confessions, book 1), are often read as a commentary on Jesus’ promise of rest (in substance). The Church also remembers that the easy yoke is made light by grace, because the Holy Spirit pours the love of God into our hearts (Romans 5:5) and makes the commandments a way of freedom and joy.

Questions people ask

Why do the Church’s Bibles write “the Lord” where the Hebrew has the divine name?

Jewish tradition does not pronounce the holy name, out of reverence, and the Greek and Latin translations, and the Church after them, follow this practice. The Church keeps this practice, out of respect for the Jewish people who first received the name from God, and replaces it with “Lord” in the readings of Scripture (CCC 209).

For the Latin reader

St Bonaventure was born about 1217 in Bagnoregio in Italy and studied and taught at the University of Paris. He became a Franciscan, and in 1257 was elected Minister General of the Order. He wrote the Itinerary of the Mind into God and a Life of St Francis. In 1273 he was made a cardinal and bishop of Albano, and he died on 15 July 1274 while taking part in the Second Council of Lyons. He was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1588 and is called the Seraphic Doctor. His life shows how study and prayer belong together: he held that the mind grows by learning and the heart grows by love, and that both lead to the God who invites the weary to rest.
