Word for the day

“For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” Matthew 6:21

Reflection

St Paul's rivals in Corinth boasted of their pedigree, their visions and their eloquence. Paul, who hates boasting, decides to answer them in their own language, but he warns that he is “speaking as a fool.” First, his roots: “Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they descendants of Abraham? So am I.” Paul never stopped being proud of his Jewish people. Then, “Are they ministers of Christ? I am talking like a madman, I am a better one.” But instead of listing successes, he lists sufferings: “far greater labors, far more imprisonments, with countless floggings, and often near death.” He received the synagogue's punishment of “forty lashes minus one” five times (Deuteronomy 25:3), a discipline he accepted because he remained a member of the synagogue and went on preaching Christ in it; it is no charge against the Jewish people, among whom Paul counted himself. He was beaten with rods by Roman magistrates three times, stoned once, shipwrecked three times, “adrift at sea” for a night and a day; he faced danger from rivers, bandits, his own people and Gentiles, in city and wilderness and sea; he knew “toil and hardship,” sleepless nights, hunger and thirst, “cold and naked.” And above all, “I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches.” This is the heart of a true pastor: “Who is weak, and I am not weak?” Then the surprising conclusion: “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” Paul's glory is not his strength but the power of Christ shining through his weakness. The Psalm is the prayer of such a man: “This poor soul cried, and was heard by the Lord, and was saved from every trouble.”

In the Gospel Jesus continues the Sermon on the Mount with two short sayings. The first is about treasure: “Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal.” In the ancient world wealth was kept in fine clothes, which moths eat, and in coins and metals, which rust and which thieves dig through mud walls to steal. Everything earthly passes. “But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven,” which nothing can destroy: love, mercy, faith, the good done for the poor. Then comes the line that unlocks the whole passage: “For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” Our heart follows what we treasure. The second saying is about the eye: “The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light.” The Greek word for healthy, haplous, also means single, undivided, generous; and in Jewish speech an “evil eye” meant a greedy, stingy heart. An eye fixed on God and generous to others fills the whole life with light; an eye fixed on possessions darkens everything. In this month of June, dedicated by popular devotion to the Sacred Heart of Jesus, today's Gospel invites a simple question: where is my heart? The heart of Jesus has only one treasure: the Father's will and our salvation. To love his Heart is to let our hearts be drawn to the same treasure.

Homily outline

Theme: “Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.”

The apostle who boasts of weakness, the treasure that lasts, and the eye that fills life with light.

1. Boasting of weakness

  • Hebrew, Israelite, son of Abraham.
  • Labours, dangers and daily anxiety for the churches.
  • Power made perfect in weakness.
“Thus says the Lord: Do not let the wise boast in their wisdom, do not let the mighty boast in their might, do not let the wealthy boast in their wealth; but let those who boast boast in this, that they understand and know me,” Jeremiah 9:23–24
“but he said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” So, I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may dwell in me.” 2 Corinthians 12:9

Apply: Offer to Christ one weakness you are ashamed of, and ask him to show his power there.

2. Treasure in heaven

  • Moth, rust and thieves.
  • Riches that no one can steal.
  • Where the heart follows.
“if riches increase, do not set your heart on them.” Psalm 62:10
“but store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust consumes and where thieves do not break in and steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” Matthew 6:20–21

Apply: Look at how you spent your money last month, and move one part of it this month to the poor or the Church.

3. The lamp of the body

  • A healthy, undivided eye.
  • The evil eye of greed.
  • A heart drawn to the Sacred Heart.
“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.” Psalm 119:105
“The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light;” Matthew 6:22

Apply: Each day this week, spend five minutes before the Blessed Sacrament or an image of the Sacred Heart, giving him your heart.

Paul's glory is his weakness, through which the power of Christ shines.

Our heart follows our treasure: let it be in heaven, in the Heart of Jesus, and our whole life will be full of light.

Take-home resolution: This week, give away one thing you are attached to, and give your heart to Jesus each day.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul's rivals boasted of their gifts and successes. Paul answers them with a list of his sufferings: beatings, imprisonments, shipwrecks, dangers, hunger, cold. And then he adds: besides other things, I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches (2 Corinthians 11:28). This is the heart of a true shepherd. Let us remember today our priests and bishops, who carry so many burdens we do not see.

And Paul ends with words that turn the world upside down: if I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness (2 Corinthians 11:30). The world admires the strong, the rich, the successful. Paul says: my glory is my weakness, because there the power of Christ is seen. When we are weak, sick, poor or tired, God is not far from us. He is closest.

In the Gospel Jesus says: do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal (Matthew 6:19). We all know how quickly things pass: the new phone is old in a year, the gold can be stolen, the house needs repair. But there is a treasure no thief can take: the love we give, the prayers we pray, the good we do.

Then Jesus says the key word: for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also (Matthew 6:21). In this month of the Sacred Heart, let us ask ourselves: where is my heart? With my money, my property, my worries? Or with Jesus? His Heart was pierced for us; his only treasure is our salvation. Let us give him our hearts today, and our hearts will be full of light. Amen.

For children

Children, what is your favourite treasure? A toy, a bicycle, a game? Jesus says toys get old and break, and things can get lost. But there are treasures that last forever: when you are kind, when you pray, when you share. These go into your treasure box in heaven! And Jesus says: where your treasure is, there your heart will be. Let us keep our hearts with Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, your apostle Paul boasted only of his weakness, so that your power might dwell in him. Strengthen all who labour and suffer for your Church, and give our pastors a heart full of care for all the churches. Lord, you have taught us that where our treasure is, there our heart will be also: deliver us from greed for what moth and rust consume, make our eye healthy and generous, and draw our hearts to your own Sacred Heart, where our true treasure is. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul’s rivals in Corinth boasted of their pedigree and their gifts. Paul answers them, “speaking as a fool”, with a list of sufferings for Christ, and ends: “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples not to store up treasures on earth but in heaven, for where your treasure is, there your heart will be. Paul shows a heart whose treasure is Christ and the churches.

2 Corinthians 11:18, 21–30 · I will boast of my weakness

Setting.

This is the centre of what is often called Paul’s “fool’s speech” (11:1–12:13). The visiting preachers in Corinth claimed to be better apostles. Paul unwillingly matches them claim for claim, but then turns the boasting upside down: instead of successes he lists hardships.

Background. Many of these sufferings are not told in Acts, which shows how much of Paul’s life we do not know. Acts mentions a beating with rods at Philippi (Acts 16:22–23), a stoning at Lystra (Acts 14:19), and later the shipwreck on the way to Rome (Acts 27), which happened after this letter was written.

Structure.

  • v. 18: since many boast, Paul will boast too.
  • vv. 21–23a: Hebrew, Israelite, descendant of Abraham, minister of Christ.
  • vv. 23b–27: a list of labours, beatings, journeys, dangers, hunger and cold.
  • vv. 28–29: daily anxiety for all the churches.
  • v. 30: if I must boast, I will boast of my weakness.

Key words.

  • kauchaomai, to boast: A key word of this part of the letter. Paul uses it many times, always with irony. True boasting is “in the Lord” (10:17, quoting Jeremiah 9:24).
  • tesserakonta para mian, forty lashes minus one: The law limited a beating to forty strokes (Deuteronomy 25:3), and the custom of giving thirty-nine kept a margin of mercy. This was a discipline of the synagogue. Many scholars note that Paul’s receiving it five times shows that he remained within the synagogue community and kept preaching there.
  • astheneia, weakness: The word Paul chooses to boast of. In the next chapter he will hear the Lord say, “power is made perfect in weakness” (12:9).

Verse by verse.

“Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they descendants of Abraham? So am I.” (11:22)

Paul is proud of his people. He never stopped being a son of Israel (Romans 9:3–5; 11:1).

“Three times I was beaten with rods. Once I received a stoning. Three times I was shipwrecked;” (11:25)

Beating with rods was a Roman punishment. Paul suffered at the hands of many: his own people, Gentiles, robbers, the sea, even false brothers (v. 26).

“I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches.” (11:28)

After all the outward sufferings comes the inward one: the care of a pastor for his people. This is often the heaviest burden.

“If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” (11:30)

Paul turns the world’s values upside down. His weakness lets Christ’s strength be seen.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Paul’s experience, “power is made perfect in weakness” (12:9), when it teaches that only faith can embrace the mysterious ways of God’s almighty power, shown most fully in Christ crucified (CCC 273). Paul’s anxiety for all the churches has become a model for bishops and priests; the Second Vatican Council asks bishops to have care for all the churches, as members of the college of bishops (Christus Dominus 6). Paul’s words about suffering should never be read against the Jewish people: he speaks of trials from many sides, and he loved his own people to the end.

Matthew 6:19–23 · Where your treasure is

Setting.

The Sermon on the Mount now turns from devotion to possessions. In a land where wealth was kept in fine clothes, stored grain and buried coins, every listener understood the dangers Jesus names: moths eat clothes, “rust” (literally “eating”) destroys stores, and thieves dig through mud-brick walls.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: do not store up treasures on earth, but in heaven.
  • v. 21: where your treasure is, there your heart will be.
  • vv. 22–23: the eye is the lamp of the body; healthy eye, body full of light; unhealthy eye, darkness.

Key words.

  • thēsauros, treasure: A store or treasury. Jesus does not condemn having goods, but making them the treasure the heart rests on.
  • haplous, healthy: Literally single, simple, the word family of Paul’s “generosity” (2 Corinthians 8:2). The single eye looks at God alone, and is also generous.
  • ponēros, unhealthy, evil: In Jewish speech an “evil eye” meant envy or meanness (Deuteronomy 15:9; Proverbs 28:22). The one who looks at others’ goods with greed fills himself with darkness.

Verse by verse.

“but store up for yourselves treasures in heaven,” (6:20)

Treasure in heaven is stored by love, mercy and almsgiving (compare Matthew 19:21). It cannot be stolen.

“For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” (6:21)

A simple test of the heart: what do I think about most, worry about most, protect most?

“The eye is the lamp of the body.” (6:22)

The eye lets in the light. If our way of looking is generous and single, our whole life is lit up; if it is greedy, our whole life darkens.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone, and to renounce all that they have for his sake and that of the Gospel (CCC 2544). The Lord grieves over the rich, because they find their consolation in the abundance of goods (CCC 2547). The treasure that no thief can take is Christ himself, given to us in the Eucharist and found again in every act of love.

For study and teaching

  • Paul’s deepest anxiety was “for all the churches”. Whom will I pray for today: my parish priest, my bishop, a persecuted church?
  • If a thief broke into my house tonight, what would I most fear to lose? What treasure can no thief take?
