Word for the day

“For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost.” Luke 19:10

Reflection

To the church of Laodicea, rich and comfortable, Christ speaks his sharpest words: “you are neither cold nor hot… because you are lukewarm… I am about to spit you out of my mouth.” Then, immediately, the tenderest: “Listen! I am standing at the door, knocking; if you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in to you and eat with you, and you with me.” Christ does not break doors; he knocks.

Zacchaeus is a chief tax collector, rich and despised. He climbs a sycamore just to see Jesus. Jesus stops, looks up, and calls him by name: “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today.” Grace comes first; conversion follows. Zacchaeus gives half his goods to the poor and repays fourfold. St Elizabeth of Hungary, a queen who spent her wealth on the sick and poor and died at twenty-four, shows the same transformation: when Christ comes in, the purse opens.

Full homily

Dear friends, to the church of Laodicea, rich and comfortable, Christ speaks his sharpest words: because you are lukewarm, neither cold nor hot, I am about to spit you out of my mouth (Revelation 3:16). Comfort can make a Christian lukewarm without his noticing.

But immediately follow the tenderest words in the book: I am standing at the door, knocking; if you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in and eat with you, and you with me (Revelation 3:20). Christ does not break doors. He knocks, and waits.

Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector and rich, wanted to see Jesus, and because he was short he climbed a sycamore tree. Jesus stopped, looked up and said: Zacchaeus, hurry down, for I must stay at your house today. And salvation came to that house as Zacchaeus gave half his goods to the poor (Luke 19:5–9).

Today we remember St Elizabeth of Hungary, a young princess who opened the door of her heart and her treasury to the poor. Where is Christ knocking in your life today? Open the door: one generous gift, one visit, one reconciliation. Amen.

For children

Children, Zacchaeus climbed a tree to see Jesus, and Jesus came to his house! Open your heart to Jesus today by sharing with someone in need.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you stand at the door and knock. Let me hear your voice and open, rescue me from lukewarmness, and like Zacchaeus and St Elizabeth, let my heart and hands open to the poor. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The letters to Sardis and Laodicea are among the sharpest in Revelation: a church that seems alive but is dead, and a church that is lukewarm and thinks it needs nothing. Yet the letter ends with Christ knocking at the door and asking to share a meal. In the Gospel he does exactly that at Jericho: he invites himself into the house of Zacchaeus, and salvation comes to it. Today is also the memorial of St Elizabeth of Hungary, who, like Zacchaeus, gave her goods to the poor.

Revelation 3:1–6, 14–22 · I am standing at the door, knocking

Setting.

Sardis had once been the rich capital of the kingdom of Lydia. Its citadel stood on a steep hill and was thought impossible to take, yet it was captured by surprise more than once because its guards did not keep watch. The warning “I will come like a thief” would have been felt there.

Laodicea was a wealthy city of banking, of fine black wool and of a well-known school of medicine. When an earthquake struck in AD 60, it rebuilt itself without help from Rome, as the historian Tacitus records. Its water came by aqueduct and arrived tepid, unlike the hot springs of nearby Hierapolis or the cold water of Colossae. Each detail of the letter touches the city's pride: rich, well clothed, with eye medicine, and lukewarm.

Structure.

  • 3:1–3: Sardis: a name of being alive, but dead; wake up, remember, repent.
  • 3:4–6: the faithful few in white; the book of life.
  • 3:14–17: Laodicea: neither cold nor hot; “I am rich”.
  • 3:18–20: Christ's counsel: gold, white robes, salve; then the knock at the door.
  • 3:21–22: the promise of a place with him on his throne.

Key words.

  • chliaros, lukewarm: Neither the healing heat of a hot spring nor the refreshment of cold water: useless, and so spat out. The fault is not a lack of feeling but complacency.
  • deipnein, to eat, to share supper: The word for the main evening meal. In the East, to share a table is to share life.
  • nikōn, the one who conquers: Each letter ends with a promise to the one who conquers. The victory is first Christ's (3:21) and is shared through faithfulness.

Verse by verse.

“you have a name of being alive, but you are dead.” (3:1)

A reputation can outlive the reality. Activity and fame are not the same as life in Christ.

“For you say, ‘I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing.’ You do not realize that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked.” (3:17)

The most dangerous poverty is the one we do not see. Christ offers what money cannot buy: faith refined by fire, the white garment of baptism, and eyes that see.

“Listen! I am standing at the door, knocking; if you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in to you and eat with you, and you with me.” (3:20)

The letter that began with the threat to spit out ends with a knock. Christ does not break the door down. Many readers have heard here a call to the Eucharistic table. Holman Hunt's famous painting The Light of the World shows the door with no handle on the outside: it can be opened only from within.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in prayer God's initiative of love always comes first, and our prayer is a response (CCC 2567). Revelation 3:20 is that initiative in a single image. The Catechism also names lukewarmness, with indifference and acedia, among the sins against the love of God (CCC 2094). The remedy is not effort alone but opening the door: “Be earnest, therefore, and repent” (3:19).

Luke 19:1–10 · Today salvation has come to this house

Setting.

Jericho, near the Jordan, was a rich city on the trade road from the east, with Herod's winter palace and groves of balsam and palm. It was a natural place for collecting tolls. Zacchaeus was a “chief tax collector”, a man who had bought the right to collect taxes and had others working under him. Such men were despised as collaborators with Roman power and as cheats. His name comes from the Hebrew for “pure” or “innocent”, which in the town's eyes he was not.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: Zacchaeus wants to see who Jesus is and climbs a tree.
  • vv. 5–7: Jesus looks up and invites himself; the crowd grumbles.
  • vv. 8–10: Zacchaeus' promise and Jesus' declaration.

Key words.

  • sēmeron, today: A word Luke loves: “to you is born this day” (2:11), “Today this scripture has been fulfilled” (4:21), “today you will be with me in Paradise” (23:43). Salvation is not only future; it comes now, when Christ is welcomed.
  • dei, must: “I must stay at your house today.” Luke uses the same small word for the divine necessity of the Passion (9:22). This visit is part of God's plan.
  • sykomorea, sycamore: The sycomore fig, a tree with a short trunk and low, wide branches, easy to climb. A rich official climbing a tree like a boy shows how much he wanted to see.

Verse by verse.

“He was trying to see who Jesus was” (19:3)

It begins with curiosity. Jesus can use even a small desire to see him.

“Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today.” (19:5)

Zacchaeus wanted to see Jesus; it turns out that Jesus was looking for him. Jesus calls him by name before he has said a word of repentance.

“Look, half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much.” (19:8)

The Law required repaying what was taken plus a fifth (Numbers 5:6–7); fourfold was the penalty for a stolen sheep (Exodus 22:1). Zacchaeus goes far beyond what the Law demands. Joy at being welcomed overflows into justice and generosity.

“For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost.” (19:10)

A summary of Luke's whole Gospel. It recalls God's promise as shepherd: “I will seek the lost” (Ezekiel 34:16).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to Zacchaeus as the model of restitution: justice requires that what was unjustly taken be given back (CCC 2412). St Augustine, reading the tree's name as “foolish fig”, saw in it a figure of the Cross, which the world thinks foolish (1 Corinthians 1:23); whoever wants to see Jesus must climb it (in substance). The story shows the order of grace: Jesus' welcome comes first, conversion follows, and its fruit is love for the poor.

Questions people ask

Did Zacchaeus earn salvation by his generosity?

No. Jesus calls him and enters his house before he promises anything. His generosity is the response to grace, the sign that salvation has come. Some scholars read his words as a description of what he already did rather than a new promise; either way, the initiative is Jesus'.

For the Latin reader

Today the Latin Church keeps the memorial of St Elizabeth of Hungary (1207–1231). A king's daughter, married young to Louis of Thuringia, she was known for her care of the poor, the sick and the hungry. After her husband died on his way to the crusade, she left court life, joined the Franciscan penitents, and spent her last years serving the sick in a hospital she founded at Marburg. She died at twenty-four and was canonised in 1235. Like Zacchaeus, she understood that wealth received from God is meant to pass into the hands of the poor.

For study and teaching

  • Where am I tempted to say, like Laodicea, “I need nothing”?
  • Zacchaeus' joy turned at once into generosity. What would “half of my possessions” look like in my life today?
