Word for the day

“Their bodies are buried in peace, but their name lives on generation after generation. The assembly declares their wisdom, and the congregation proclaims their praise.” Sirach 44:14–15

Reflection

Moses comes down from Sinai carrying “the two tablets of the covenant,” written on both sides, “the work of God.” Below him is the noise of a party. Joshua thinks it is the sound of war; Moses knows better: “it is the sound of revelers that I hear.” When he sees the calf and the dancing his anger burns, and he shatters the tablets at the foot of the mountain, a picture of a covenant broken. He burns the calf, grinds it to powder and makes the people drink it, to show that the idol is nothing. Then he faces Aaron, who gives one of the most comic and most tragic excuses in the Bible: “I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf!” Scripture knows our habit of blaming everything but ourselves. The next day Moses does what the great intercessors do. He tells the people the truth, “You have sinned a great sin,” and then he goes back up the mountain: “perhaps I can make atonement for your sin.” His prayer breaks off in the middle of a sentence: “if you will only forgive their sin” and then, “if not, blot me out of the book that you have written.” He puts his own name on the line for a people who have just betrayed God. The Psalm remembers it: “had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath from destroying them.” The Church sees in Moses a shadow of Christ, who “always lives to make intercession for them” (Hebrews 7:25).

In the Gospel Jesus tells two short parables, the mustard seed and the yeast. The kingdom of heaven does not arrive with trumpets; it begins small, hidden, and works from within. The seed is “the smallest of all the seeds,” yet it becomes a tree where birds nest; the yeast is hidden in “three measures of flour,” enough for a whole village's bread, “until all of it was leavened.” St Matthew adds that Jesus speaks in parables to fulfil the word of the Psalm: “I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world” (Psalm 78:2). Beside these parables the Church today places the memory of two hidden people, Joachim and Anne. The Gospels never name them. Their names come from the Protoevangelium of James, a second-century writing that the Church does not count as Scripture, but whose names the faithful have honoured in East and West for many centuries. What the Church knows with certainty is simple: Mary had parents. They received her as God's gift, taught her the psalms and the promises of Israel, and handed on a faith in which she could answer the angel: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord.” The Church teaches that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception, yet she was born into a human family, nursed, and taught like any child. A mustard seed of faith passed from grandparents to a girl in Nazareth, and from her the Lord of all took flesh. In Kerala the name of Anne is in every parish, in Annamma and Anna and Ann, and the old stories of Joachim and Anne praying for a child echo the stories of Sarah and Hannah, whose prayer we hear in the Book of Samuel: “if only you will look on the misery of your servant, and remember me” (1 Samuel 1:11).

Full homily

Dear friends, the people of Israel have made a golden calf, and Moses has to come down the mountain with the tablets in his hands. He sees the dancing and breaks the tablets. And Aaron, the priest, says: I threw the gold in the fire, and out came this calf! (Exodus 32:24). How like us. We make a mess and then say it just happened.

But look at what Moses does next. He does not wash his hands of them. He goes back up the mountain and prays: forgive them, but if not, blot me out of the book that you have written (Exodus 32:32). That is a leader who stands in the breach. The Psalm says it: had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath (Psalm 106:23). Every one of us has someone who stood in the breach for us: a mother who prayed, a grandfather who fasted, a grandmother who held the rosary.

In the Gospel Jesus says that the kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed, the smallest of all seeds, and like yeast hidden in flour. God works small and hidden. And today the Church remembers two hidden people. We do not find Joachim and Anne in the Gospels. Their names come from an old book. But we know something certain: Mary had parents, and they taught her to pray and to trust the promises of God. How else could a girl in Nazareth say: Here am I, the servant of the Lord? (Luke 1:38). Faith is always handed on, as St Paul tells Timothy: a faith that lived first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, lives in you (2 Timothy 1:5).

Yesterday we kept the World Day for Grandparents and the Elderly, and today we honour the grandparents of Jesus. So let us ask: who planted the seed of faith in me? And whose seed am I planting? Let us thank God for them, pray for them, and, like Moses, stand in the breach for those who have strayed. Amen.

For children

Children, a mustard seed is tiny, but when it is planted it grows into a big tree where birds can make their nests. Jesus says God's kingdom is like that. Today we remember Mary's mother and father, Joachim and Anne. They loved little Mary and taught her to pray, and she grew up to be the Mother of Jesus. So Joachim and Anne were Jesus' grandparents! Thank God for your own grandparents today, and ask them to tell you a story about when they were small.

Prayer

O God of our fathers, who gave Saints Joachim and Anne the grace of bringing forth the Mother of your incarnate Son, grant, through the prayers of both, that we may attain the salvation you have promised to your people. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, you hide your kingdom in small seeds and quiet lives. Bless our grandparents and parents, who plant the faith in the heart of a child. Make us faithful to what they handed on, and, like Moses, ready to stand in the breach for those who have strayed. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Moses comes down from Sinai carrying the tablets and finds his people dancing round a golden calf. He breaks the tablets, destroys the calf, and then climbs the mountain again to plead for the people, even offering his own name for theirs. In the Gospel Jesus gives two short parables of the kingdom: the mustard seed, smallest of seeds and greatest of shrubs, and the yeast hidden in three measures of flour. One reading shows how great a sin can be, the other how quietly grace works. Today the Church also keeps the memorial of Saints Joachim and Anne, the parents of the Blessed Virgin Mary. The proper readings of the memorial (Sirach 44:1, 10–15 and Matthew 13:16–17) may be used instead of these.

Exodus 32:15–24, 30–34 · Alas, this people has sinned a great sin

Setting.

Moses has been on Mount Sinai for forty days receiving the law and the pattern of the tabernacle. At the foot of the mountain the people grew impatient, and Aaron made a calf of gold from their earrings (32:1–6). God told Moses what was happening and threatened to destroy the people, and Moses interceded (32:7–14). Our reading begins as he comes down the mountain.

A calf, or young bull, was a symbol of strength and fertility in the religions around Israel. The people probably meant the calf as a throne or pedestal for the Lord (Aaron proclaims “a festival to the Lord,” 32:5), not as another god, yet the First Commandment forbids making any image of him. The story is the first great test of the covenant just sealed in Exodus 24.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: Moses carries the tablets, the work of God, written on both sides.
  • vv. 17–18: Joshua hears a noise of war; Moses hears the sound of singing.
  • vv. 19–20: Moses sees the calf and the dancing, breaks the tablets, destroys the calf and makes the people drink the powder.
  • vv. 21–24: Moses questions Aaron; Aaron blames the people and says the calf “came out” of the fire.
  • vv. 30–32: the next day Moses goes back up to the Lord and asks for forgiveness, offering to be blotted out himself.
  • vv. 33–34: the Lord says that those who sin will be blotted out, yet tells Moses to lead the people on; punishment will come “when the day comes”.

Key words.

  • ʿēgel, calf: A young bull. The word is used for the idol again in Psalm 106:19 (the psalm used today) and Nehemiah 9:18. In 1 Kings 12:28 Jeroboam makes two golden calves for the northern kingdom, and later writers see in them the same sin.
  • ʾăkappĕrâ, I may make atonement: From the verb kipper, to cover over or make amends. Moses does not claim to take away the sin by himself; he says “perhaps”. Atonement is God’s gift, and the same root names the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur).
  • sēper, book: The book that God has written, the list of the living. Moses is ready to be struck from it for the sake of the people. Similar images are found in Psalm 69:28, Daniel 12:1 and Revelation 3:5.

Verse by verse.

“The tablets were the work of God, and the writing was the writing of God, engraved upon the tablets.” (32:16)

The reader is told twice how holy the tablets are before Moses breaks them. The people have shattered what God gave long before Moses does.

“it is the sound of revelers that I hear.” (32:18)

The sound of singing: no war, no victory, only noise of feasting round the calf. The text moves slowly toward the scene, and the reader waits with Moses.

“So I said to them, ‘Whoever has gold, take it off’; so they gave it to me, and I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf!” (32:24)

Aaron’s excuse is almost comic. He describes the calf as if it appeared on its own. He cannot hide his part (32:2–4), and Scripture does not hide it for him.

“blot me out of the book that you have written.” (32:32)

The sentence breaks off before its second half, as though Moses cannot finish it. His love for the people is greater than his concern for himself.

“Nevertheless, when the day comes for punishment, I will punish them for their sin.” (32:34)

Forgiveness is not cheap. God keeps his promise to lead them, but sin still has consequences.

How the Church reads it.

Moses is the pattern of the intercessor in Scripture. The Catechism says that his prayer is the typical example of intercessory prayer, which will be fulfilled in Jesus Christ, the one mediator between God and men (CCC 2577). It also teaches that idolatry is not confined to ancient paganism; it remains a constant temptation to faith, whenever a person honours and reveres a creature in place of God (CCC 2113). St Paul’s own wish to be accursed for the sake of his people (Romans 9:3) shows the same love, and the Church sees in Moses’ offer a foreshadowing of Christ, who actually became a curse for us (Galatians 3:13) so that sins might be forgiven.

Matthew 13:31–35 · The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed … like yeast

Setting.

Matthew 13 is the third great discourse of the Gospel and gathers parables of the kingdom. Jesus has just told the sower and the weeds; now he gives two short parables about growth, one from the field and one from the kitchen. One image comes from a man’s sowing and the other from a woman’s baking, so that all his listeners may recognise their own daily work.

Both parables answer a question the disciples and crowds might ask: if the kingdom has come, why does it look so small? Jesus answers that it begins small and hidden, and grows by its own hidden power.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–32: the mustard seed: smallest of seeds, greatest of shrubs, a home for the birds.
  • v. 33: the yeast a woman hid in three measures of flour until all was leavened.
  • vv. 34–35: Jesus speaks to the crowds only in parables, fulfilling the word of the prophet.

Key words.

  • sinapi, mustard: The black mustard plant, whose seed is tiny, was proverbial for smallness, though it can grow to a height of a few metres. Jesus uses ordinary experience to make a point and does not teach botany.
  • zymē, yeast, leaven: Often a sign of corruption in Scripture (1 Corinthians 5:6–8), but here a sign of the kingdom’s hidden and spreading power. Jesus uses the same image in different senses elsewhere, which shows that a parable has one main point.
  • tria sata, three measures: Three seah, a large quantity of flour, the amount Sarah used for her three visitors (Genesis 18:6). The yeast works on a great mass, so the kingdom is meant for a great many.

Verse by verse.

“it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree,” (13:32)

The contrast is the point: the very small becomes the very large. The tree where the birds come to nest echoes Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4:12, where a great tree shelters the nations.

“like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” (13:33)

The yeast is hidden, but it works from within. The kingdom does not begin with power or display but in a heart or a community, and changes the whole.

“I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.” (13:35)

Matthew quotes Psalm 78:2, where the psalmist says he will tell the story of Israel in riddles. The hidden things are the mysteries of the kingdom, now made known in Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The Council taught that the Church is, on earth, the seed and the beginning of the kingdom, and that this kingdom grows slowly through history (Lumen Gentium 5). Some of the Fathers heard in the mustard seed Christ himself, buried like a seed and rising as the great tree (in substance). These readings give the Church a calm patience: small beginnings and unseen work are the way of God.

For the Latin reader

Joachim and Anne are the traditional names of the parents of the Blessed Virgin Mary. They are not named in the Gospels. The names come from an early Christian writing, the Protoevangelium of James, probably of the second century, which the Church does not count as Scripture but which has shaped devotion and art. Their joint memorial on this date has been kept in the Roman calendar since its reform in 1969. The Church honours them as grandparents of Jesus according to the flesh and as the first teachers of Mary in the faith of Israel. The Mass today may therefore be a prayer for grandparents and for the transmission of faith in families.

For study and teaching

  • Moses went back up the mountain for a people who had wronged God. For whom, even someone who has hurt me, can I plead in prayer this week?
  • The yeast is hidden but changes all the dough. What small, hidden thing, a kind word or a daily prayer, might change my home from within?
