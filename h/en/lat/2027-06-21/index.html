Word for the day

“Now the Lord said to Abram, “Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.”” Genesis 12:1

Reflection

This week the first readings take us back to the beginning of the story of salvation: the call of Abraham. The chapters before it are sad ones. Adam and Eve are sent out of the garden, Cain kills Abel, the flood covers the earth, and at Babel people try to build a tower to heaven and end up scattered and unable to understand one another. Humanity has broken into pieces. And then, quietly, God begins again with one man: “Now the LORD said to Abram, ‘Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.’” Three things to leave, in rising order of cost: the country, the clan, the father's house. And no address for the new land, only “the land that I will show you.” Then come the promises, five blessings in a row: “I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you, and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and the one who curses you I will curse; and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” Babel scattered the families of the earth; Abraham's call is God's plan to bless them all. St Paul will say that this was the Gospel preached in advance (Galatians 3:8), fulfilled in Christ, the son of Abraham. The answer is told in a single line: “So Abram went, as the LORD had told him.” He is seventy-five years old. He goes with Sarai his wife and Lot his nephew, and wherever he stops, at Shechem by the oak of Moreh and on the hill east of Bethel, he builds an altar and calls on the name of the Lord. Abraham lives in a tent but marks the land with altars: a pilgrim who worships. The Letter to the Hebrews sums it up: “he set out, not knowing where he was going” (Hebrews 11:8). Jews, Christians and Muslims all look to Abraham; the Church calls him “our father in faith,” and the Second Vatican Council reminds us that the Church is spiritually bound to the stock of Abraham, the Jewish people, to whom the promises first belong (Nostra Aetate 4). The Psalm sings of that people: “Happy is the nation whose God is the LORD, the people whom he has chosen as his heritage.”

The Gospel continues the Sermon on the Mount with a word that touches every family, parish and office: “Do not judge, so that you may not be judged. For with the judgment you make you will be judged, and the measure you give will be the measure you get.” Jesus is not telling us to stop telling right from wrong; a few verses later he will tell us to beware of false prophets and to know a tree by its fruits. What he forbids is the harsh verdict on another person's heart, the condemning spirit that sees every fault in others and none in ourselves. And he paints it with a carpenter's humour: a man with a beam of wood sticking out of his eye, leaning over his brother to remove a speck of sawdust. “You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor's eye.” Notice that Jesus does not say the speck does not matter. He says that only a person who has first dealt honestly with his own sin can help his brother with mercy. St Aloysius Gonzaga, whose memory we keep today, is a good teacher of both readings. Born in 1568, the eldest son of a marquis in northern Italy, he was raised to be a soldier and a ruler, and served as a page at the courts of Florence and Madrid. Like Abraham he left his father's house: at seventeen, after a long struggle with his father, he gave up his right to inherit in favour of his younger brother and entered the Society of Jesus in Rome. He was very strict with himself, and his companions remembered him as gentle and humble with others. In 1591, when plague and famine struck Rome, he begged in the streets for the sick and carried them to the hospital himself; he caught the disease and died on 21 June 1591, at twenty-three. The Church gives him to young people as their patron. In June, when schools and colleges reopen across Kerala, he is a good friend for every student.

Homily outline

Theme: “So Abram went, as the Lord had told him.”

A call to leave and a promise to bless, and a Lord who asks us to look at our own eye first, as St Aloysius did.

1. Go, to the land I will show you

  • After Babel, a new beginning.
  • Country, kindred, father's house.
  • No map, only a promise.
“Now the Lord said to Abram, “Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.”” Genesis 12:1
“By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to set out for a place that he was to receive as an inheritance; and he set out, not knowing where he was going.” Hebrews 11:8

Apply: Take one concrete step this week in obedience to something God has long been asking of you.

2. A blessing for all the families of the earth

  • Five blessings.
  • Altars along the way.
  • Fulfilled in Christ.
“I will bless those who bless you, and the one who curses you I will curse; and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” Genesis 12:3
“And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, declared the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, “All the Gentiles shall be blessed in you.”” Galatians 3:8

Apply: Be a blessing to one family outside your own this week, by a visit, a meal or a word of encouragement.

3. The log and the speck

  • The measure you give.
  • A carpenter's picture.
  • Honest with ourselves, merciful to others.
“for the Lord does not see as mortals see; they look on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart.” 1 Samuel 16:7
“You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor's eye.” Matthew 7:5

Apply: Make a careful examination of conscience before your next confession, looking at your own faults rather than at those of others.

Abraham went at God's word, and through him God began to bless all the families of the earth.

Jesus asks us to judge ourselves honestly and others mercifully; St Aloysius shows us how, strict with himself and gentle with all.

Take-home resolution: Take one step of obedience to God this week and refuse one harsh judgment each day.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the first reading takes us to one of the great turning points of the Bible. After the stories of sin and division, of the flood and the tower of Babel, God begins again with one man. Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you (Genesis 12:1). No map, no address, only a promise.

And the promise is enormous: I will bless you, and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed (Genesis 12:2–3). At Babel the families of the earth were scattered. Through Abraham God wants to bless them all. Christians believe this blessing reached its fullness in Jesus Christ, the son of Abraham, who died for all. Abram's answer is very short: So Abram went, as the Lord had told him (Genesis 12:4). He was seventy-five years old. It is never too late to obey God.

In the Gospel Jesus gives us a word for daily life: Do not judge, so that you may not be judged (Matthew 7:1). He does not mean that we cannot say what is right and wrong. He means that we must not condemn others while ignoring our own sins. He uses a funny picture: a man with a log in his eye trying to take a speck out of his brother's eye. First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly (Matthew 7:5).

St Aloysius Gonzaga lived both readings. Like Abraham he left his father's house: he gave up a princely inheritance to follow Christ as a Jesuit. He was strict with himself and gentle with others. And he died at twenty-three caring for the sick of Rome. In these weeks when schools reopen, let us entrust our children and young people to him, and let us ask for two graces: to go when God calls, and to look at the log in our own eye before we look at the speck in our neighbour's. Amen.

For children

Children, God once said to a man called Abram: pack your things and go to a new place that I will show you. Abram did not know where he was going, but he trusted God and went! Today we also remember St Aloysius, a young prince who left his palace to follow Jesus and to help sick people. You are starting a new school year. Ask Jesus to go with you, and ask St Aloysius to help you study well and be kind to everyone.

Prayer

O God, giver of heavenly gifts, who in Saint Aloysius Gonzaga joined penitence to a wonderful innocence of life, grant, through his merits and intercession, that, though we have failed to follow him in innocence, we may imitate him in penitence. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, you called Abraham to leave his father's house and go to the land you would show him. Give us his trust, so that we set out when you call; and give us the humility to look first at our own faults, so that we may help our brothers and sisters with mercy. Bless all students beginning a new year, and keep our young people pure, generous and brave. Amen.
