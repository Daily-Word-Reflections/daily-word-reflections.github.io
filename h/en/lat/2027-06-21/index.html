Word for the day

“Now the Lord said to Abram, “Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.”” Genesis 12:1

Reflection

This week the first readings take us back to the beginning of the story of salvation: the call of Abraham. The chapters before it are sad ones. Adam and Eve are sent out of the garden, Cain kills Abel, the flood covers the earth, and at Babel people try to build a tower to heaven and end up scattered and unable to understand one another. Humanity has broken into pieces. And then, quietly, God begins again with one man: “Now the LORD said to Abram, ‘Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.’” Three things to leave, in rising order of cost: the country, the clan, the father's house. And no address for the new land, only “the land that I will show you.” Then come the promises, five blessings in a row: “I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you, and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and the one who curses you I will curse; and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” Babel scattered the families of the earth; Abraham's call is God's plan to bless them all. St Paul will say that this was the Gospel preached in advance (Galatians 3:8), fulfilled in Christ, the son of Abraham. The answer is told in a single line: “So Abram went, as the LORD had told him.” He is seventy-five years old. He goes with Sarai his wife and Lot his nephew, and wherever he stops, at Shechem by the oak of Moreh and on the hill east of Bethel, he builds an altar and calls on the name of the Lord. Abraham lives in a tent but marks the land with altars: a pilgrim who worships. The Letter to the Hebrews sums it up: “he set out, not knowing where he was going” (Hebrews 11:8). Jews, Christians and Muslims all look to Abraham; the Church calls him “our father in faith,” and the Second Vatican Council reminds us that the Church is spiritually bound to the stock of Abraham, the Jewish people, to whom the promises first belong (Nostra Aetate 4). The Psalm sings of that people: “Happy is the nation whose God is the LORD, the people whom he has chosen as his heritage.”

The Gospel continues the Sermon on the Mount with a word that touches every family, parish and office: “Do not judge, so that you may not be judged. For with the judgment you make you will be judged, and the measure you give will be the measure you get.” Jesus is not telling us to stop telling right from wrong; a few verses later he will tell us to beware of false prophets and to know a tree by its fruits. What he forbids is the harsh verdict on another person's heart, the condemning spirit that sees every fault in others and none in ourselves. And he paints it with a carpenter's humour: a man with a beam of wood sticking out of his eye, leaning over his brother to remove a speck of sawdust. “You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor's eye.” Notice that Jesus does not say the speck does not matter. He says that only a person who has first dealt honestly with his own sin can help his brother with mercy. St Aloysius Gonzaga, whose memory we keep today, is a good teacher of both readings. Born in 1568, the eldest son of a marquis in northern Italy, he was raised to be a soldier and a ruler, and served as a page at the courts of Florence and Madrid. Like Abraham he left his father's house: at seventeen, after a long struggle with his father, he gave up his right to inherit in favour of his younger brother and entered the Society of Jesus in Rome. He was very strict with himself, and his companions remembered him as gentle and humble with others. In 1591, when plague and famine struck Rome, he begged in the streets for the sick and carried them to the hospital himself; he caught the disease and died on 21 June 1591, at twenty-three. The Church gives him to young people as their patron. In June, when schools and colleges reopen across Kerala, he is a good friend for every student.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the first reading takes us to one of the great turning points of the Bible. After the stories of sin and division, of the flood and the tower of Babel, God begins again with one man. Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you (Genesis 12:1). No map, no address, only a promise.

And the promise is enormous: I will bless you, and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed (Genesis 12:2–3). At Babel the families of the earth were scattered. Through Abraham God wants to bless them all. Christians believe this blessing reached its fullness in Jesus Christ, the son of Abraham, who died for all. Abram's answer is very short: So Abram went, as the Lord had told him (Genesis 12:4). He was seventy-five years old. It is never too late to obey God.

In the Gospel Jesus gives us a word for daily life: Do not judge, so that you may not be judged (Matthew 7:1). He does not mean that we cannot say what is right and wrong. He means that we must not condemn others while ignoring our own sins. He uses a funny picture: a man with a log in his eye trying to take a speck out of his brother's eye. First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly (Matthew 7:5).

St Aloysius Gonzaga lived both readings. Like Abraham he left his father's house: he gave up a princely inheritance to follow Christ as a Jesuit. He was strict with himself and gentle with others. And he died at twenty-three caring for the sick of Rome. In these weeks when schools reopen, let us entrust our children and young people to him, and let us ask for two graces: to go when God calls, and to look at the log in our own eye before we look at the speck in our neighbour's. Amen.

For children

Children, God once said to a man called Abram: pack your things and go to a new place that I will show you. Abram did not know where he was going, but he trusted God and went! Today we also remember St Aloysius, a young prince who left his palace to follow Jesus and to help sick people. You are starting a new school year. Ask Jesus to go with you, and ask St Aloysius to help you study well and be kind to everyone.

Prayer

O God, giver of heavenly gifts, who in Saint Aloysius Gonzaga joined penitence to a wonderful innocence of life, grant, through his merits and intercession, that, though we have failed to follow him in innocence, we may imitate him in penitence. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, you called Abraham to leave his father's house and go to the land you would show him. Give us his trust, so that we set out when you call; and give us the humility to look first at our own faults, so that we may help our brothers and sisters with mercy. Bless all students beginning a new year, and keep our young people pure, generous and brave. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Through these days the first reading follows the story of Abraham. Today God calls Abram to leave his country, his kindred and his father’s house, and promises that in him all the families of the earth shall be blessed; Abram goes, building altars as he travels. In the Gospel Jesus warns against judging others and tells us to take the log out of our own eye first. On the memorial of St Aloysius Gonzaga, a young man who left a prince’s inheritance to follow Christ, Psalm 33 sings: “Happy is the nation whose God is the Lord, the people whom he has chosen as his heritage.”

Genesis 12:1–9 · Go to the land that I will show you

Setting.

The first eleven chapters of Genesis end with the tower of Babel, where humanity is scattered and divided. Then comes the family of Terah. Abram, with his wife Sarai, who has no child (11:30), has moved with his father from Ur of the Chaldeans to Haran in northern Mesopotamia (11:31). Here God speaks to him. With this call the history of salvation takes a new turn: God begins to heal the whole human family through one man and his descendants.

Abram’s route runs south through Canaan, through places that will matter greatly in Israel’s story: Shechem, Bethel and the Negeb, the dry south. At each stop he builds an altar. He owns nothing in the land yet; he claims it only by worship.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: God’s call and promise: land, a great nation, blessing, a great name, and blessing for all the families of the earth.
  • vv. 4–5: Abram goes, at seventy-five, with Sarai, Lot and all they have.
  • vv. 6–7: at Shechem the Lord appears and promises the land to his offspring; an altar.
  • vv. 8–9: near Bethel another altar; Abram calls on the name of the Lord and journeys on toward the Negeb.

Key words.

  • lek-lĕkā, go: Literally “go for yourself” or “go by yourself”. The call asks Abram to leave, in three widening circles, his land, his clan and his father’s household. He is told where to leave, but not yet where he will arrive: “the land that I will show you”.
  • bĕrākâ, blessing: Words from the root “bless” come five times in vv. 2–3. Some commentators note that the story of sin in chapters 3–11 is marked by curses; now the story of salvation begins with blessing, and it is meant for “all the families of the earth”.
  • zeraʿ, offspring, seed: The land is promised to Abram’s “offspring” (v. 7). St Paul notes that the word is singular, and reads it of Christ, in whom the promise is fulfilled (Galatians 3:16).

Verse by verse.

“Go from your country and your kindred and your father’s house to the land that I will show you.” (12:1)

Faith begins with a word from God and a road whose end is not yet seen.

“and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” (12:3)

Abram is chosen not for himself alone but for the sake of all peoples. Paul calls this verse the gospel proclaimed beforehand to Abraham (Galatians 3:8).

“So Abram went, as the Lord had told him;” (12:4)

No speech, no question: only obedience. The Letter to the Hebrews sums it up: “By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called… not knowing where he was going” (Hebrews 11:8).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that, in order to gather together scattered humanity, God calls Abram from his country, his kindred and his father’s house, and makes him Abraham, the father of a multitude of nations (CCC 59). The people descended from him were to be the trustee of the promise (CCC 60). The Letter to the Hebrews lays special emphasis on Abraham’s faith (CCC 145), and the Church calls him our father in faith. Christians share this root with the Jewish people, to whom the promises first belong and whose calling God does not revoke (Romans 11:29).

Matthew 7:1–5 · First take the log out of your own eye

Setting.

The Sermon on the Mount now turns to how disciples treat one another. Jesus has taught the Lord’s Prayer, with its petition to forgive as we are forgiven (6:12). Here he forbids the judging that condemns. He does not forbid all judgment: a few verses later he asks his disciples to recognise false prophets by their fruits (7:15–20). What he forbids is the harsh, self-righteous verdict on another person’s heart. A saying of Hillel in the Mishnah teaches something similar: do not judge your neighbour until you have stood in his place.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: do not judge; the measure you give will be the measure you get.
  • vv. 3–4: the speck in your neighbour’s eye and the log in your own.
  • v. 5: “You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye.”

Key words.

  • krinō, to judge: Here it means to pass sentence, to condemn. “That you may not be judged” is a way of speaking of God’s judgment without naming God.
  • karphos, dokos, speck, log: A speck of chaff or sawdust against a roof beam. Jesus, the carpenter’s son, uses a deliberately comic picture: a man with a beam in his eye offering to do eye surgery on his brother.
  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: In Greek the word meant an actor, one who plays a part behind a mask. The one who judges harshly is playing the part of the righteous man.

Verse by verse.

“Do not judge, so that you may not be judged.” (7:1)

Not a ban on telling right from wrong, but on taking God’s place as judge of another person.

“and the measure you give will be the measure you get.” (7:2)

The picture is of a market measure for grain. The mercy we show others is the measure we ask God to use for us.

“You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor’s eye.” (7:5)

Jesus does not say we should never help our brother. He says: begin with yourself. Only a humble eye sees clearly enough to help.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names rash judgment as a sin against truth and the good name of others: assuming a neighbour’s moral fault without enough foundation (CCC 2477). To avoid it, it quotes St Ignatius of Loyola: every good Christian ought to be more ready to give a favourable interpretation to another’s statement than to condemn it (CCC 2478, in substance). Examination of conscience and the sacrament of penance are the Church’s way of taking the log out of our own eye.

Questions people ask

If we must not judge, how can parents or the Church correct anyone?

Jesus forbids condemning another person’s heart, not caring about right and wrong. Correction given in love is a work of mercy; Jesus himself teaches how to correct a brother (Matthew 18:15). The difference lies in the eye and the heart: the one who corrects well has first looked at his own faults, speaks humbly, and wants his brother’s good, not his own victory.

For the Latin reader

St Aloysius Gonzaga (1568–1591) was the eldest son of the Marquis of Castiglione in northern Italy, and was raised to be a soldier and a prince. While still a boy he resolved to give himself to God. Against his father’s strong opposition he renounced his inheritance in favour of his brother and entered the Society of Jesus in Rome in 1585. His spiritual director was St Robert Bellarmine. In 1591 plague struck Rome, and Aloysius nursed the sick in the hospitals, carrying the dying in his own arms. He caught the disease and died on 21 June, at the age of twenty-three. The Church honours him as a patron of young people. Like Abram, he left his father’s house for a land God would show him.

  • Whom have I judged harshly in recent days? What might the “log” in my own eye be?
