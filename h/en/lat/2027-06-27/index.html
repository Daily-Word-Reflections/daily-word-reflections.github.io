Word for the day

“He took her by the hand and said to her, “Talitha cum,” which means, “Little girl, get up!” And immediately the girl got up and began to walk about” Mark 5:41–42

Reflection

Today's readings stand face to face with the oldest enemy of the human race: death. The first reading comes from the Book of Wisdom, written in Greek by a devout Jew, probably in Alexandria in Egypt, in the last century before Christ. Around him were philosophies that said death is simply the end, so eat and drink while you can. The sacred author answers with a bold sentence: “God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living. For he created all things so that they might exist.” Creation is not a trap; “the generative forces of the world are wholesome, and there is no destructive poison in them.” Then he tells us where we come from and where death comes from: “for God created us for incorruption, and made us in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his company experience it.” Here, reading Genesis 3, Scripture names for the first time the serpent of Eden as the devil, and the Catechism quotes these very lines (Catechism 391, 413). Death is real, but it was not God's plan; it is an intruder. And God is on the side of life. The Psalm is the song of someone who has been to the edge of the grave and come back: “O LORD, you brought up my soul from Sheol, restored me to life from among those gone down to the Pit.” It contains one of the most consoling lines in the whole Psalter: “Weeping may linger for the night, but joy comes with the morning.” And its last word is pure Easter: “You have turned my mourning into dancing.”

In the second reading St Paul is organising a collection. The Christians of Jerusalem are poor, and Paul asks the Gentile churches of Greece to help them. He does not shame the Corinthians or order them; he reminds them of Christ: “For you know the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich.” The Greek word for “generous act” is charis, grace. All Christian giving begins there: we give because the Son of God gave himself. Then Paul speaks of “a fair balance”: your abundance now supplies their need, and one day their abundance may supply yours. He quotes the story of the manna in the desert: “The one who had much did not have too much, and the one who had little did not have too little” (Exodus 16:18). In the Church nobody should be crushed by want while others have more than they need. It is fitting that on this Sunday many dioceses take up Peter's Pence, the collection through which the successor of Peter helps the poor and the suffering Churches of the world in our name.

The Gospel is one of the great stories of St Mark, and he tells it in his favourite way, one story wrapped inside another. Jairus, “one of the leaders of the synagogue,” a respected man, falls at Jesus' feet: “My little daughter is at the point of death. Come and lay your hands on her, so that she may be made well, and live.” Jesus goes with him, and the crowd presses in. In that crowd is a woman with no name and no standing. For twelve years she has suffered from bleeding; she “had endured much under many physicians, and had spent all that she had; and she was no better, but rather grew worse.” Her illness made her ritually unclean (Leviticus 15:25), so she should not even be there. She thinks, “If I but touch his clothes, I will be made well,” and she touches, and is healed. Jesus stops: “Who touched my clothes?” The disciples find the question absurd; everyone is touching him. But there is a difference between pressing against Jesus and touching him with faith. Jesus wants to meet her face to face. She comes trembling and tells “the whole truth,” and he gives her more than health: he calls her “Daughter,” the only time in Mark that he calls anyone so, and says, “your faith has made you well; go in peace.” Meanwhile the worst news arrives for Jairus: “Your daughter is dead. Why trouble the teacher any further?” Jesus overhears and says the words this Gospel was written for: “Do not fear, only believe.” At the house the mourners are already wailing; when Jesus says, “The child is not dead but sleeping,” they laugh at him. Then, with only the parents and Peter, James and John, he takes the girl by the hand and speaks in Aramaic, words Mark keeps exactly as Jesus said them: “Talitha cum,” “Little girl, get up!” She gets up and walks; she is twelve years old, as old as the woman's suffering. And Jesus, practical and tender, tells them to give her something to eat. In one afternoon Jesus touches the two things that made a person unclean, blood and death, and they do not defile him; his life overcomes them. What Wisdom said, God did not make death, Jesus shows. He has not yet abolished death; that will happen on Easter morning. But he shows that for him death is like sleep, from which he can wake us with a word. This is why Christians call their burial ground a cemetery, from the Greek for “sleeping place.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, every one of us has stood beside a sickbed or a coffin. Every one of us knows the fear of a phone call at night. Today's readings speak straight into that fear. The Book of Wisdom says: God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living (Wisdom 1:13). God created us for incorruption, in the image of his own eternity. Death was not his plan. It entered through the devil's envy. God is on the side of life.

In the Gospel two people come to Jesus. One is important: Jairus, a leader of the synagogue. He falls at Jesus' feet: my little daughter is at the point of death. The other is nobody: a woman who has been bleeding for twelve years, has spent all her money on doctors and has only grown worse. Her illness made her unclean, so she should not even be in the crowd. She does not dare to speak. She only thinks: if I but touch his clothes, I will be made well (Mark 5:28).

Many people are pressing against Jesus. But only one touches him with faith. And Jesus stops everything: who touched my clothes? He will not let her slip away healed in body but still hiding in shame. He wants to look at her. And when she tells him the whole truth, he calls her Daughter. Your faith has made you well; go in peace. Dear friends, we come to Mass every Sunday, we press around Jesus. But do we touch him? At Communion today, let us touch him with faith.

While Jesus is still speaking, the terrible news comes for Jairus: your daughter is dead. Why trouble the teacher any further? It is too late. And Jesus says to that father the words he says to every one of us: do not fear, only believe (Mark 5:36). He goes into the house where people are already wailing. He says: the child is not dead but sleeping, and they laugh at him. Then he takes the girl by the hand and says: Talitha cum, little girl, get up. And she gets up and walks.

Jesus has not yet abolished death in this story; the girl will one day die again. But he shows us that for him death is like sleep, and he can wake us with a word. That is why on Easter morning he rose, and why we bury our dead in the hope of the resurrection. And until then he takes our sick by the hand through the Church, in the Anointing of the Sick and in Holy Communion, the medicine of immortality.

St Paul adds one more thing. You know the generous act of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor (2 Corinthians 8:9). The one who raises us to life asks us to lift others up: the poor, the sick, the grieving. Today, as many parishes collect Peter's Pence for the Holy Father's works of charity, let us give generously. And whatever darkness we carry this week, let us hear his voice: do not fear, only believe. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Thirteenth Sunday of Year B is about life stronger than death. The Book of Wisdom says plainly that God did not make death: he created all things to exist, and made us for incorruption. In the Gospel Mark weaves two stories into one: a woman who has bled for twelve years touches Jesus’ cloak and is healed, and the twelve-year-old daughter of Jairus, already dead, hears Jesus say, “Talitha cum,” and gets up. Psalm 30 gives thanks: “O Lord, you brought up my soul from Sheol.”

Between them St Paul asks the Corinthians to give generously to the poor church in Jerusalem, because Christ, though rich, became poor for our sake. The link is the same: the God of life gives himself, and those who receive life are called to share it.

Wisdom 1:13–15; 2:23–24 · God did not make death

Setting.

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, probably in Alexandria in Egypt, during the century before Christ. Its author speaks in the person of Solomon, the wise king, but he writes for Jews living among Greeks, who were tempted to give up their faith. It is one of the latest books of the Old Testament, and it is part of the Catholic canon of Scripture.

Chapters 1–5 contrast the righteous and the ungodly. In chapter 2 the ungodly say that life is short, death is the end, so let us enjoy ourselves and crush the righteous man who rebukes us. The author answers that they are wrong about God and about death. Today’s verses come from the opening and from the answer.

Structure.

  • 1:13: God did not make death and does not delight in it.
  • 1:14: he created all things to exist; creation is wholesome; Hades has no rule on earth.
  • 1:15: righteousness is immortal.
  • 2:23: God created us for incorruption, in the image of his own eternity.
  • 2:24: death entered through the devil’s envy, and those who belong to him experience it.

Key words.

  • aphtharsia, incorruption: Not decaying, not perishing. The word is used by St Paul for the resurrection body: “what is sown is perishable, what is raised is imperishable” (1 Corinthians 15:42).
  • athanatos, immortal: “Righteousness is immortal” (1:15). Wisdom teaches that the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God (3:1). Life with God does not end at death.
  • phthonos, envy: The book says that death entered the world through the devil’s envy (2:24). Envy cannot bear another’s good. Many interpreters see here the serpent of Genesis 3, envious of the human beings made in God’s image.

Verse by verse.

“because God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living.” (1:13)

A strong and simple statement. Death in its full sense, the death that separates from God, is not God’s work. Ezekiel had said the same: God has no pleasure in the death of anyone (Ezekiel 18:32).

“For he created all things so that they might exist;” (1:14)

God’s will in creating is that things should be. Creation is good, as Genesis 1 repeats.

“for God created us for incorruption, and made us in the image of his own eternity,” (2:23)

An echo of Genesis 1:27. Some Greek manuscripts read “image of his own nature” instead of “eternity”; both readings say that we are made to share God’s life.

“but through the devil’s envy death entered the world,” (2:24)

This is one of the first texts in Scripture to link the serpent of Genesis with the devil. The Book of Revelation will call him “that ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan” (Revelation 12:9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses: “God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living… It was through the devil’s envy that death entered the world” (CCC 413). It teaches that death is a consequence of sin, and that God had destined man not to die (CCC 1008). It also teaches that behind the disobedience of our first parents lurks a seductive voice opposed to God, the fallen angel called Satan or the devil (CCC 391). The Church does not deny that the body dies according to nature; she proclaims that Christ has conquered death, and that the resurrection of the body is the fulfilment of the incorruption for which we were made.

2 Corinthians 8:7, 9, 13–15 · Rich, yet for your sakes he became poor

Setting.

Paul is organising a collection among his Gentile churches for the poor Christians of Jerusalem (compare Romans 15:25–27; Galatians 2:10). For him it is more than charity: it is a sign of the unity of Jewish and Gentile believers in one Church. The Corinthians had begun eagerly a year before but had not finished.

In chapters 8–9 Paul encourages them. He points to the churches of Macedonia, poor themselves, who gave beyond their means (8:1–5). Then he points to Christ.

Structure.

  • v. 7: you excel in everything; excel also in this generous undertaking.
  • v. 9: the generous act of the Lord Jesus Christ: rich, he became poor, to make you rich.
  • vv. 13–14: not relief for others and pressure on you, but a fair balance.
  • v. 15: the manna: “The one who had much did not have too much, and the one who had little did not have too little.”

Key words.

  • charis, grace, generous act: Paul plays on this word through the chapter. The collection is a charis (v. 7, “generous undertaking”), and Christ’s self-giving is the charis of the Lord (v. 9, “generous act”). Our giving is a share in his grace.
  • eptōcheusen, he became poor: He became a beggar, one who has nothing. Paul thinks of the whole mystery of the Son becoming man, as in Philippians 2:6–8.
  • isotēs, fair balance: Equality, fairness. Paul does not ask the Corinthians to become poor so that others may be rich; he asks for a balance in which abundance meets need.

Verse by verse.

“so we want you to excel also in this generous undertaking.” (8:7)

Paul does not command; he invites. Generosity is to be one more of the gifts in which the community shines.

“though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich.” (8:9)

One of the great Christ verses of the New Testament. The riches are the divine life; the poverty is his birth, his life and his Cross. The exchange is all to our advantage.

“your present abundance and their need,” (8:14)

Today one community has more, tomorrow another. The Church is one body in which goods circulate.

“The one who had much did not have too much, and the one who had little did not have too little.” (8:15)

Paul quotes Exodus 16:18. In the desert God gave manna to each according to need. Sharing in the Church is meant to reflect that divine providence.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that the whole life of Christ is a mystery of redemption: by becoming poor he enriches us with his poverty (CCC 517). In its teaching on love for the poor it quotes St John Chrysostom: not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life; the goods we possess are not ours, but theirs (CCC 2446). The collection at Mass, and the gifts given for the poor and for needy dioceses, continue Paul’s collection. They are not a tax but a share in the grace of Christ.

Mark 5:21–43 (or shorter: 5:21–24, 35b–43) · Do not fear, only believe

Setting.

Jesus has just crossed back from the Gentile side of the lake, where he healed the man with the legion of demons (5:1–20). In chapters 4–5 Mark shows his power over the storm, the demons, sickness and death.

Mark often places one story inside another, so that each explains the other. Here the story of Jairus is interrupted by the woman with the haemorrhage. Both are called “daughter”, both involve the number twelve, and both turn on faith and touch. The shorter form of the reading keeps only Jairus’ story.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–24: Jairus, a leader of the synagogue, begs Jesus to come to his dying daughter.
  • vv. 25–29: the woman who had suffered twelve years touches his cloak and is healed.
  • vv. 30–34: “Who touched my clothes?” She tells the whole truth. “Daughter, your faith has made you well.”
  • vv. 35–36: news of the girl’s death; “Do not fear, only believe.”
  • vv. 37–40: with Peter, James and John to the house; the mourners laugh.
  • vv. 41–43: “Talitha cum”; the girl gets up; give her something to eat.

Key words.

  • archisynagōgos, leader of the synagogue: A respected lay leader who arranged the services. Jairus is an important man, yet he falls at Jesus’ feet. Grief and love make him humble.
  • sōzō, to save, make well: Mark uses the same verb for healing and for salvation: Jairus asks that his daughter be “made well” (v. 23); the woman thinks “I will be made well” (v. 28); Jesus says “your faith has made you well” (v. 34). Bodily healing points to the salvation of the whole person.
  • ṭalyĕtā qûm, Talitha cum: Aramaic, the language Jesus spoke, kept by Mark and translated for his Greek readers: “Little girl, get up!” Many later manuscripts have the longer form koumi; the oldest have koum.

Verse by verse.

“My little daughter is at the point of death. Come and lay your hands on her, so that she may be made well, and live.” (5:23)

A father’s prayer, urgent and full of faith. He believes that Jesus’ touch can give life.

“If I but touch his clothes, I will be made well.” (5:28)

The law of Leviticus 15 meant that her bleeding made her ritually unclean, and for twelve years she had lived with that and with her illness. She comes from behind, quietly. Her faith is real, though still timid.

“Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease.” (5:34)

Jesus will not let her slip away unseen. He calls her “daughter”, gives her peace, and confirms her healing before everyone. She is healed in body and restored in dignity.

“Do not fear, only believe.” (5:36)

The word to Jairus is the word to every Christian in the face of death. Faith is asked for precisely when there seems nothing more to hope.

“Talitha cum,” (5:41)

Jesus takes her by the hand. The verb “get up” is the same family of words used for the resurrection. The early Church heard in this a sign of the resurrection of all who belong to Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists the raising of Jairus’ daughter with that of the young man of Nain and of Lazarus as signs announcing Christ’s own Resurrection (CCC 994). It notes that Jesus hears the prayer of faith expressed in deeds, such as the woman who touches his clothes (CCC 2616), and that the sick try to touch him, for power comes from him and heals (CCC 1504). In the sacraments Christ continues to touch us: “power came forth from him” then, and it comes forth now in Baptism, the Eucharist and the Anointing of the Sick. The command to give the girl something to eat shows his care for ordinary needs, and the Church has often heard in it an echo of the food of life she gives her children.

Questions people ask

If God did not make death, why do we die?

The Church teaches that death entered as a consequence of sin (CCC 1008), not as part of God’s first plan. The body dies according to nature, but for those who are in Christ death has been changed: it becomes a passage to life with God. Christ did not take death away from this world; he went through it and opened the way to the resurrection.

Was the woman superstitious to think that touching his clothes would heal her?

Her faith was imperfect, perhaps mixed with ideas of magic, but Jesus accepted it and led it further. He calls her forward so that her trust in his clothes becomes a personal meeting with him. The Church honours relics and holy objects in the same spirit: never as magic, always as a way to the person of Christ and the saints.

Why does Paul ask for money in a letter that is Scripture?

Because love for the poor belongs to the heart of the Gospel. Paul’s collection was also a sign of unity between Gentile and Jewish Christians. He asks gently, without command, and he roots it in Christ’s own self-giving. Giving for the poor is part of worship, not a separate matter.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman Rite the Thirteenth Sunday of Year B joins healing, resurrection and charity. Peter’s Pence, the gift of the faithful to the Holy Father for his works of charity, is traditionally linked with the solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul on Tuesday. Today is also a good day to pray for the sick of the parish and to bring them Holy Communion.

For study and teaching

  • Wisdom says “God did not make death.” How does this change the way I think about illness, ageing and the death of those I love?
  • Paul says Christ became poor to make us rich. In what concrete way can my family share our “abundance” with someone in need this week?
  • The woman came from behind; Jesus called her forward. Is there something I bring to God quietly that he wants me to bring openly, perhaps in confession?
  • Jesus said to Jairus, “Do not fear, only believe.” Where do I need to hear that word today?
