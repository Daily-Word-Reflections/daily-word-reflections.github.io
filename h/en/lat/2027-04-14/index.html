Word for the day

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.” John 6:40

Reflection

The day Stephen died, “a severe persecution began against the church in Jerusalem, and all except the apostles were scattered throughout the countryside of Judea and Samaria.” It looks like the end of the young Church. Devout men bury Stephen “and made loud lamentation over him”; Christian mourning is real mourning, even when it is full of hope. Saul goes from house to house, dragging off men and women to prison. And then St Luke writes one of the most hopeful sentences in the Acts of the Apostles: “Now those who were scattered went from place to place, proclaiming the word.” The persecution that was meant to stamp out the Church scatters it like seed. Before his Ascension the risen Lord had said: “you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth” (Acts 1:8). The disciples had stayed in Jerusalem; now, through suffering, the word moves into Judea and Samaria exactly as he said. Tertullian would later write, in substance, that the blood of Christians is seed. Philip, one of the Seven like Stephen, goes down to a city of Samaria. Jews and Samaritans had kept apart for centuries (John 4:9), yet here “the crowds with one accord listened eagerly” to Philip; unclean spirits come out, the paralyzed and the lame are healed, “so there was great joy in that city.” The Psalm becomes the song of Samaria: “Make a joyful noise to God, all the earth… Come and see what God has done.” Kerala knows this story well. Malayali Catholics scattered by work and need across the Gulf, Europe, America and every corner of India have often carried the faith with them and planted it where they went: a rosary group in a labour camp, a Malayalam Mass in a borrowed chapel, a family that keeps praying in a foreign city.

In the Gospel Jesus repeats the great word of yesterday, “I am the bread of life,” and then opens his heart about the will of his Father. “Everything that the Father gives me will come to me, and anyone who comes to me I will never drive away.” No one who comes to him is turned away: not the sinner, not the doubter, not the one who comes late. Then he speaks of his own obedience: “I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me.” Here we glimpse the mystery St Martin I defended: the Son made man has a true human will, and he freely and lovingly makes it one with the Father's will (Catechism 475). And what is that will? “That I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day.” Four times in this chapter Jesus says “I will raise them up on the last day.” The Easter faith is not a vague survival of the soul; it is the resurrection of the body, our own bodies raised and glorified like his. And the Bread of Life is bound to that promise: those who receive the Eucharist receive, as the Catechism says, a pledge of future glory (Catechism 1402–1405). This is the hope with which the devout men buried Stephen, and the hope with which the Church buries her children still: the Lord who feeds us will not lose us.

Full homily

Dear friends, after Stephen's death it seemed that everything was falling apart. A great persecution began, and believers fled from Jerusalem in every direction. But St Luke says: those who were scattered went from place to place, proclaiming the word (Acts 8:4). The wind that was meant to blow out the fire only carried its sparks further.

Philip went to Samaria, a people the Jews had kept apart from for centuries. He proclaimed Christ, the sick were healed, and so there was great joy in that city (Acts 8:8). God's word does not stay within our walls. Many of you know what it is to be scattered: for work, for study, for family. Wherever you go, you take the word with you.

In the Gospel Jesus says something every one of us needs to hear: anyone who comes to me I will never drive away (John 6:37). Not the one who has failed, not the one who has been away for years. And he tells us the Father's will: that he should lose nothing of what the Father has given him, but raise it up on the last day.

That is our Easter hope. Not only that our souls live on, but that our bodies will be raised. Every time we receive the Bread of Life, we receive the pledge of that resurrection. So let us come to him without fear, and carry his joy wherever life scatters us. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: I will never send away anyone who comes to me. That means you can always come to Jesus: when you are happy, when you are sad, even when you have been naughty! He always opens his arms. Let us run to him today and say: Jesus, here I am!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, bread of life, you turned the scattering of your Church into the spreading of your word, and you fill whole cities with joy. Receive all who come to you, lose none of those the Father has given you, and raise us up on the last day to see your face. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Stephen’s death does not silence the word; it scatters it. The reading from Acts begins in the same verse where Tuesday’s ended: a persecution, in which Saul took a leading part, drives the believers out of Jerusalem, and Philip brings the Gospel to Samaria, where “there was great joy in that city”. In the Gospel the discourse at Capernaum continues: the will of the Father is that nothing given to the Son should be lost. Psalm 66 calls all the earth to cry out to God with joy.

Acts 8:1b–8 · Those who were scattered proclaimed the word

Setting.

Devout men bury Stephen with loud lamentation, and a severe persecution falls on the church in Jerusalem. Many scholars think it struck above all the Greek-speaking believers of Stephen’s circle, since the apostles were able to stay in the city. Philip, named next after Stephen in the list of the Seven (6:5), is not the apostle Philip.

Acts follows the plan announced by the risen Lord: “in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth” (1:8). With this reading the Gospel reaches Samaria. Jews and Samaritans had long been divided over worship and the Temple (John 4:9, 20), and the Samaritans too awaited one who was to come (John 4:25).

Structure.

  • vv. 1b–3: persecution; Stephen’s burial; Saul ravages the church.
  • v. 4: the scattered believers proclaim the word.
  • vv. 5–8: Philip in Samaria: preaching, signs, healing and joy.

Key words.

  • diesparēsan, they were scattered: The verb is built on speirō, “to sow”, the root of our word “diaspora”. What was meant to destroy the community sows it across the land.
  • elymaineto, was ravaging: A strong word, used of wild animals mauling or of an army laying waste. Paul himself later admits that he was violently persecuting the church of God (Galatians 1:13).
  • chara, joy: A mark of Luke’s writing, from the angels at Bethlehem (Luke 2:10) to the end of Acts. Here the joy belongs to a whole city of Samaritans.

Verse by verse.

“Now those who were scattered went from place to place, proclaiming the word.” (8:4)

Not the apostles but ordinary believers, refugees, are the first missionaries beyond Jerusalem.

“Philip went down to the city of Samaria and proclaimed the Messiah to them.” (8:5)

Some ancient manuscripts read “the city”, others “a city”, and translations differ. If “the city” is meant, it may be the old capital, rebuilt by Herod the Great as Sebaste. Philip proclaims “the Messiah”: the Christ Samaria too was waiting for.

“So there was great joy in that city.” (8:8)

The last words of the reading. Healing of body and spirit, together with the good news, bring joy.

How the Church reads it.

Tertullian wrote at the end of the second century that the blood of Christians is seed (in substance). Acts already shows the pattern: suffering does not end the mission; it carries it further. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the pilgrim Church is missionary by its very nature (Ad Gentes 2). Philip, counted by tradition among the first deacons and later called “the evangelist” (21:8), shows that the work of proclaiming belongs to more than the apostles. The Catechism adds that the Church on mission walks the road Christ walked, the way of poverty and obedience, of service and self-sacrifice even to death (CCC 852).

John 6:35–40 · I will never drive away anyone who comes to me

Setting.

The discourse at Capernaum continues. Verse 35 repeats the last line of Tuesday’s Gospel, “I am the bread of life”, and Jesus now speaks of the will of his Father. The crowd has seen him and yet does not believe (v. 36). Jesus answers, not with a new sign, but with a promise about all who come to him.

Structure.

  • v. 35: the bread of life.
  • v. 36: seeing without believing.
  • vv. 37–38: all that the Father gives will come; I will never drive them away; I came to do the will of the one who sent me.
  • vv. 39–40: the Father’s will: lose nothing, eternal life, “I will raise them up on the last day”.

Key words.

  • thelēma, will: The word comes four times in vv. 38–40. Jesus said that his food is to do the will of the one who sent him (4:34); here we learn what that will is: that none be lost.
  • ou mē ekbalō exō, I will never drive away: A strong double negative in Greek. In chapter 9 the man born blind will be driven out (9:34); whoever comes to Jesus is never driven out.
  • anastēsō, I will raise up: “I will raise them up on the last day” comes four times in this chapter (vv. 39, 40, 44, 54). The bread of life leads to the resurrection of the body.

Verse by verse.

“Everything that the Father gives me will come to me, and anyone who comes to me I will never drive away;” (6:37)

God’s gift and our coming are held together. No one who comes is turned back, however late or however wounded.

“for I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me.” (6:38)

The Catechism quotes this verse in speaking of the whole life of Jesus as an offering to the Father, which leads him to the cross (CCC 606).

“This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life; and I will raise them up on the last day.”” (6:40)

Eternal life begins now, in believing; it is completed in the resurrection.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus links faith in the resurrection to his own person: it is he who on the last day will raise up those who have believed in him and who have eaten his body and drunk his blood (CCC 994). John 6:37–40 is one of the Gospels the Church offers for funeral Masses: at the grave the Church leans on the promise that the Son loses nothing the Father has given him.

For study and teaching

  • When has a loss or a hardship in my life become, like the scattering in Acts, an opening for the Gospel?
  • Jesus says he will never drive away anyone who comes to him. Is there someone I keep at a distance whom he would welcome?
