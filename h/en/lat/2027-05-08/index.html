Word for the day

“For the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God.” John 16:27

Reflection

Today's short reading opens Paul's third great journey. After a visit to Antioch, his home church, “he departed and went from place to place through the region of Galatia and Phrygia, strengthening all the disciples.” Luke gives only one line to hundreds of kilometres on foot, but the purpose is clear: a missionary does not only found churches; he goes back to strengthen them. Then the scene moves to Ephesus, where Priscilla and Aquila have settled, and a new figure appears: Apollos, a Jew from Alexandria in Egypt, the great city of books and scholars. Luke paints him warmly: “an eloquent man, well-versed in the scriptures,” instructed in the Way of the Lord, speaking “with burning enthusiasm” and teaching “accurately the things concerning Jesus, though he knew only the baptism of John.” He had a gift, a fire, and a gap.

What happens next is a quiet model of Christian correction. “When Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained the Way of God to him more accurately.” They do not interrupt him in the synagogue or denounce him to the apostles. They take him aside, probably into their home and workshop, and teach him. A tentmaking couple instruct a brilliant preacher, and he is humble enough to learn. Notice too that Luke again names Priscilla first: a woman and her husband together hand on the faith. Then the believers write a letter of recommendation, Apollos crosses to Achaia, and there “he greatly helped those who through grace had become believers.” Paul will later sum up the partnership: “I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth” (1 Corinthians 3:6). No one owns the Gospel; each hands on what was received.

In the Gospel Jesus, at the Last Supper, speaks of a new way of praying: “if you ask anything of the Father in my name, he will give it to you … Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.” To ask in his name is not to add a formula at the end of a prayer; it is to pray as one who belongs to him, wanting what he wants. And then the most tender sentence: “the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God.” We do not need Jesus to persuade an unwilling Father; the Father already loves us. Jesus sums up his whole life in one line: “I came from the Father and have come into the world; again, I am leaving the world and am going to the Father.” That is the movement the Church celebrates tomorrow in India, the Ascension. On this Saturday, a day dedicated to Our Lady, we remember that the apostles waited for the Spirit with Mary, the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14). She, who asked so simply at Cana, teaches us to ask in his name.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we meet a brilliant young preacher, Apollos from Alexandria. He was eloquent, he knew the Scriptures well, and he spoke about Jesus with burning enthusiasm. But there was something missing: he knew only the baptism of John.

Who noticed? Not a bishop, not an apostle, but a tentmaking couple, Priscilla and Aquila. And what did they do? They did not stand up in the synagogue and shout: you are wrong! They took him aside and explained the Way of God to him more accurately (Acts 18:26). Probably at their own table, in their own home.

This is how Christians correct one another: privately, kindly, with the wish to help, not to win. And see the humility of Apollos: a learned man from the great city of Alexandria, he accepted teaching from two workers. Because he was humble, he became an even greater blessing to the Church.

In the Gospel Jesus teaches us to pray in his name: if you ask anything of the Father in my name, he will give it to you (John 16:23). And then he says: the Father himself loves you (John 16:27). Many of us pray as if God were an angry officer who must be persuaded. Jesus says no. The Father already loves you.

Jesus also says: I came from the Father and have come into the world; again, I am leaving the world and am going to the Father. Tomorrow, in India, we celebrate that return to the Father, the Ascension of the Lord.

Today is Saturday, a day dedicated to Mary. After the Ascension the apostles waited for the Holy Spirit, praying with Mary the mother of Jesus. Let us do the same: ask the Father, in Jesus' name, with Mary beside us, for the Holy Spirit. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a clever preacher named Apollos did not know everything about Jesus. So Aquila and Priscilla invited him home and taught him kindly. We can help our friends kindly too!

Jesus says: the Father loves you! When you pray, you are talking to someone who loves you very much. Say: Father, I love you too!

Prayer

Father, you love us because we have loved your Son and believed that he came from you. In his name we come to you, trusting that you hear us, and we ask above all for the gift of your Holy Spirit. Make us like Paul, strengthening the disciples; like Priscilla and Aquila, teaching the Way gently and truly; like Apollos, burning with zeal and humble enough to learn. With Mary, who prayed with the apostles while they waited, we wait for the Spirit and for the joy you promised to make complete. As your Son goes to you, let our hearts follow him. Through Christ our Lord. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Apollos, a gifted preacher from Alexandria, teaches accurately about Jesus but knows only John’s baptism, and a married couple, Priscilla and Aquila, take him aside and explain the Way more accurately. In the Gospel Jesus teaches the disciples to ask the Father in his name and tells them that the Father himself loves them. He ends: “I am leaving the world and am going to the Father.” Psalm 47 sings of God who has gone up with a shout, and it prepares the Ascension, which the Church in India keeps this Sunday.

Acts 18:23–28 · They explained the Way of God more accurately

Setting.

Today’s reading begins the third great journey of Paul. After a short visit to Antioch in Syria, he sets out again through Galatia and Phrygia, the region of his earlier churches, strengthening the disciples (v. 23). Luke then turns to Ephesus, where Priscilla and Aquila had stayed when Paul passed through (18:19), and introduces a new figure.

Background. Alexandria in Egypt had a large and learned Jewish community. The Jewish Scriptures had been translated into Greek there (the Septuagint), and the Jewish philosopher Philo taught there in the time of Jesus. An Alexandrian Jew “well-versed in the scriptures” would have known how to read them carefully and eloquently.

Structure.

  • v. 23: Paul strengthens the disciples of Galatia and Phrygia.
  • vv. 24–25: Apollos at Ephesus: eloquent, fervent, but knowing only John’s baptism.
  • v. 26: Priscilla and Aquila take him aside and teach him.
  • vv. 27–28: Apollos goes to Achaia and helps the believers there.

Key words.

  • hodos, the Way: An early name for the Christian faith and community in Acts (9:2; 19:9, 23; 24:14). Faith is not only a set of ideas but a road to walk, the way of the Lord.
  • zeōn tō pneumati, burning in spirit: The NRSV has “with burning enthusiasm”. The words can mean his own fervent spirit, or the Holy Spirit; Paul uses the same phrase in Romans 12:11. Zeal and learning are both gifts, and both need to be guided.
  • akribōs / akribesteron, accurately / more accurately: Apollos taught “accurately” (v. 25), yet Priscilla and Aquila explained the Way to him “more accurately” (v. 26). It is the same word Luke uses in the prologue of his Gospel (Luke 1:3). Good teaching can always grow more complete.

Verse by verse.

“He was an eloquent man, well-versed in the scriptures.” (18:24)

Apollos became well known in Corinth. Some there even boasted “I belong to Apollos” (1 Corinthians 1:12). Paul does not blame Apollos for this: “I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth” (1 Corinthians 3:6).

“though he knew only the baptism of John.” (18:25)

He knew the preaching of repentance and the facts about Jesus, but perhaps not yet the full gift of the Spirit and the baptism in the name of Jesus. Monday’s reading will show a group at Ephesus in the same position.

“they took him aside and explained the Way of God to him more accurately.” (18:26)

They did not correct him in public. They took him aside, probably into their home. Priscilla is named first here, as in several other places; she was clearly a teacher of the faith alongside her husband.

“showing by the scriptures that the Messiah is Jesus.” (18:28)

This was a debate among Jews about how to read their own Scriptures. The early Church’s preaching always began here: the Law and the Prophets point to Christ (Luke 24:27).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Priscilla and Aquila a model of the lay apostolate and of the Christian family as a place of formation. Paul calls them his co-workers who risked their necks for him, and greets “the church in their house” (Romans 16:3–5). The Second Vatican Council, in its decree on the apostolate of the laity, names Aquila and Priscilla among the men and women who helped Paul in spreading the Gospel (Apostolicam Actuositatem 10). Apollos, for his part, shows the humility of a great preacher who is willing to be taught.

John 16:23b–28 · The Father himself loves you

Setting.

The farewell discourse at the Last Supper draws to a close. Jesus has spoken of his going away and of the disciples’ sorrow turned into joy. Now he speaks of what will change on “that day”, the day of his resurrection and of the gift of the Spirit: they will pray to the Father in his name, and they will know the Father’s love directly.

Structure.

  • vv. 23b–24: ask the Father in my name, and your joy will be complete.
  • v. 25: from figures of speech to plain speech about the Father.
  • vv. 26–27: the Father himself loves you.
  • v. 28: from the Father into the world, and from the world to the Father.

Key words.

  • en tō onomati mou, in my name: To ask in his name is not a formula added at the end of a prayer. It is to pray as one united with him, in his person, for what he wills.
  • paroimia, figure of speech: A veiled saying or parable, like the shepherd (10:6) and the woman in labour. After the resurrection the disciples will understand plainly, through the Spirit who teaches them.
  • phileō, to love: In v. 27 John uses the word for the love of friends: the Father loves them as friends because they have loved the Son. Jesus has already called them friends (15:15).

Verse by verse.

“Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.” (16:24)

The purpose of prayer is not only to obtain things but to have complete joy, which is communion with the Father.

“I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf;” (16:26)

This does not deny that Christ intercedes for us; Hebrews 7:25 says that he always lives to make intercession. It means that the disciples are not kept at a distance. United with the Son, they come to the Father themselves.

“for the Father himself loves you,” (16:27)

The Father does not have to be persuaded to love us by the Son. The Son came because the Father already loved the world (3:16).

“I came from the Father and have come into the world; again, I am leaving the world and am going to the Father.” (16:28)

The whole mystery of Christ in one sentence: from the Father, into the world, and back to the Father. It is the road of the Ascension, which the Church celebrates on Sunday.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that prayer in the name of Jesus is new with the resurrection, and quotes v. 24 on asking in his name (CCC 2614–2615). This is why the prayers of the Mass end “through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son”. St Augustine teaches that we ask in the name of the Saviour when we ask for what belongs to our salvation (in substance). Verse 28 is a short creed of the Incarnation and the Ascension: the Son who came down returns to the Father, and he takes our human nature with him.

Questions people ask

Why does God not always give what I ask in Jesus’ name?

To ask in his name is to ask as he would ask, for what truly serves salvation. The Catechism says that God, who knows what we need, sometimes delays or gives something better, and that we must persevere in trusting prayer (CCC 2735–2737). The promise is sure: the Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask him (Luke 11:13).

For study and teaching

  • Who has taken me aside, as Priscilla and Aquila did for Apollos, to help me know the faith more accurately? Whom could I help in the same way?
  • When I pray, do I come to God as a stranger who needs to persuade him, or as one whom the Father himself loves?
