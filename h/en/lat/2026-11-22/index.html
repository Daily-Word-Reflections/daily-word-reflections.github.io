Word for the day

“And the king will answer them, ‘Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.’” Matthew 25:40

Reflection

The Church year ends with a crown. Pope Pius XI instituted this solemnity in 1925 (Quas Primas), as nations were trying to organise life without God; since 1969 it closes the liturgical year, so that all history is seen moving toward Christ. But what kind of King is he? Ezekiel answers first: “I myself will search for my sheep… I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep.” God's kingship is shepherding: seeking the lost, binding up the injured, strengthening the weak.

The Gospel shows the same King at the end of time, seated on the throne of glory with all nations before him. The surprise is the standard of judgement. He does not ask about rank, wealth or even religious achievement, but about the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick and the prisoner: “just as you did it to one of the least of these… you did it to me.” Christ identifies himself with the poor. The Church draws from this the corporal works of mercy (CCC 2447) and teaches that we cannot be united with God unless we freely choose to love him, and we cannot love God if we fail to love our neighbour (CCC 1033).

Paul gives the cosmic scope: “Christ has been raised from the dead, the first fruits of those who have died”; he must reign “until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy to be destroyed is death.” Then he hands the kingdom to the Father, “so that God may be all in all”. His reign is not imposed by force; it grows wherever love is chosen. Each act of mercy is a small coronation of Christ the King.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today we end the Church year by crowning Christ as King. But what kind of King is he?

The prophet Ezekiel tells us: God says, “I myself will search for my sheep… I will seek the lost, bring back the strayed, bind up the injured, strengthen the weak.” Our King is a Shepherd. He does not rule by fear; he rules by care.

In the Gospel we see this King at the end of time, seated on his glorious throne, with all the nations before him. And what will he ask us? Not how much money we earned, not how many titles we held, not even how many devotions we attended. He will ask: “I was hungry, did you feed me? I was a stranger, did you welcome me? I was sick, did you visit me? I was in prison, did you come to me?”

And both groups are surprised: “Lord, when did we see you?” The King answers: “Just as you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me.” Christ hides himself in the poor. St John Chrysostom said: do not honour Christ in church with silk while you neglect him outside, naked and cold.

St Paul tells us that Christ must reign until every enemy is defeated, and the last enemy is death. Then God will be all in all. That is where history is going. Every act of mercy, every plate of food shared, every visit to a lonely person, is a step in that direction: a small crowning of Christ.

So this last week of the year, let us choose one work of mercy each day. And when we say in the Our Father, “Thy Kingdom come,” let us mean it with our hands as well as our lips. Long live Christ the King!

Dear brothers and sisters, on this last Sunday of the liturgical year we proclaim Christ as King of the universe. We sing, we crown His image, we carry Him in procession. But let us remember what kind of crown He wore on earth: a crown of thorns.

When Pilate asked Jesus whether He was a king, Jesus answered: my kingdom is not from this world. He came to testify to the truth, and everyone who belongs to the truth listens to His voice (John 18:36–37). Pilate had soldiers, a palace and the power of Rome. Jesus had nothing but truth and love. Yet two thousand years later Pilate is remembered only because his name is in our Creed, and Jesus reigns in the hearts of millions.

Above the Cross the soldiers nailed an inscription: this is the King of the Jews (Luke 23:38). They meant it as a mockery, but it was the truth. Christ reigns from the Cross. His power is not to take life but to give it. And the first one to recognise His kingship was a dying criminal who said: Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom. Jesus answered: today you will be with me in Paradise (Luke 23:42–43).

So the real question today is not whether Christ is King of the universe. He is, whether the world accepts it or not. The question is whether He is King of my heart. Does He reign over my tongue, my money, my phone, my family, my work, my politics? Or have I kept some area of my life closed to Him, a little kingdom of my own?

As we end this liturgical year, let us open every door to Christ. Let us serve as He served, especially the least of His brothers and sisters. And when we receive Him in this Holy Mass, let us say with the good thief: Jesus, remember me in your kingdom. That prayer is enough to save a whole life.

Dear brothers and sisters, this feast is younger than many of us think. Pope Pius XI established it in 1925, in the encyclical Quas Primas. Europe had just come out of a terrible war, ideologies were rising that put the State or the race in the place of God, and many nations were living as if God did not exist. The Pope proclaimed: Christ is King, of individuals, of families and of societies.

Why is Christ King? First, by His very nature: He is the eternal Son of God, through whom and for whom all things were created (Colossians 1:15–17). Second, by conquest: He redeemed us, not with silver or gold, but with His precious blood (1 Peter 1:18–19). Third, because the Father has given Him all authority in heaven and on earth (Matthew 28:18). Daniel saw one like a son of man receiving an everlasting dominion (Daniel 7:13–14).

His Kingdom is already present and not yet complete. The Second Vatican Council says that the Church is the Kingdom of Christ already present in mystery (Lumen Gentium 3). It grows quietly like a mustard seed. But it will be fulfilled only when Christ comes again in glory, when He hands over the Kingdom to the Father and God will be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:24–28; CCC 671). Until then we pray every day: Thy kingdom come.

Does this feast have anything to say to society? Yes. The Catechism teaches that Christians have the duty to bring the spirit of the Gospel into the life of society, its customs, laws and structures (CCC 2105). That means defending the dignity of every human life from conception to natural death, working for justice and peace, caring for the poor and the migrant. It does not mean forcing faith on anyone; the Church respects freedom of conscience. We propose Christ by our witness.

Through Baptism we share in Christ's royal office, which means to serve (CCC 786). The King washed feet. So let us end this liturgical year by renewing our allegiance to Christ, and let us show it by serving the least of His brothers and sisters. Then His Kingdom will come, a little more, in our homes and in our land.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the last Sunday of the Church's year the readings show us what kind of king Christ is. In Ezekiel, God himself promises to be the shepherd of his scattered flock, seeking the lost and healing the injured, and also judging between sheep and sheep. The psalm answers with the prayer every Christian knows: “The Lord is my shepherd.” In First Corinthians, Christ the risen one reigns until every enemy is overcome, the last being death, and then hands the kingdom to the Father so that God may be all in all. In Matthew, the Son of Man sits on his throne as king and shepherd, and judges the nations by one measure: what they did to the least of his brothers and sisters.

The line runs from shepherd to king to judge, and the three are one person. The King of the universe does not rule from a distance. He looks for the lost sheep, he goes down into death to destroy it, and at the end he is found hidden in the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner. The key words are seeking, reigning and recognising.

Ezekiel 34:11–12, 15–17 · I myself will be the shepherd

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest taken to Babylon with the first group of exiles in 597 BC. He spoke to the exiles before and after the fall of Jerusalem in 587/586 BC. Chapter 34 comes just after the news reaches him that the city has fallen (33:21). The question in everyone's mind is why the nation collapsed, and whether God has abandoned his people.

In the ancient Near East kings were commonly called shepherds; Hammurabi of Babylon calls himself a shepherd in the prologue to his laws. So when Ezekiel speaks against “the shepherds of Israel” (34:2), he means the kings and leaders who fed themselves and not the flock. Today's reading begins at the turning point: since the human shepherds have failed, God will shepherd his sheep himself.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–10 (not read): the charge against the shepherds who fed themselves, and God's sentence on them.
  • vv. 11–12: God himself searches for his scattered sheep and rescues them from the “day of clouds and thick darkness”.
  • vv. 13–14 (omitted today): he gathers them from the nations and feeds them on the mountains of Israel.
  • vv. 15–16: God shepherds, gives rest, seeks, brings back, binds up and strengthens.
  • v. 17: he judges between sheep and sheep.
  • ¶ The chapter goes on to promise “one shepherd, my servant David” (34:23). God himself will shepherd, and yet a son of David will shepherd. The two promises meet only in Christ, who is both God and the Son of David.

Key words.

  • rō‘eh, shepherd: From the verb rā‘āh, to pasture, to feed. A shepherd in the Bible is the one who feeds. The failed shepherds fed themselves (34:2); God feeds the flock (34:14–15).
  • dāraš, to seek, to search out: The same verb is used for seeking God in prayer. Here it is turned around: it is God who goes looking for his people.
  • mišpāṭ, justice: “I will feed them with justice” (v. 16). God's care is not soft indulgence. It sets things right, and so it includes judgment (v. 17).

Verse by verse.

“I myself will search for my sheep, and will seek them out.” (34:11)

“I myself” is emphatic in Hebrew. After the failure of the kings, God does not send a better official; he comes himself. This is the seed of the Incarnation.

“I will rescue them from all the places to which they have been scattered on a day of clouds and thick darkness.” (34:12)

“A day of clouds and thick darkness” is the language of the day of the Lord (Joel 2:2; Zephaniah 1:15). Here it describes the disaster of 587. God goes into the darkness to bring his sheep out.

“I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak, but the fat and the strong I will destroy. I will feed them with justice.” (34:16)

Each verb answers a charge in 34:4, where the bad shepherds did none of these things. A note on the text: the Hebrew has “I will destroy” the fat and the strong, which the NRSV follows. The Greek Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate read “I will watch over” them; the two Hebrew words look very alike. The Malayalam POC follows this second reading (സംരക്ഷിക്കും). The Hebrew fits v. 17, where the strong sheep that push the weak aside are judged (34:20–21).

“I shall judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and goats:” (34:17)

Judgment does not fall only on the shepherds. Inside the flock too, some sheep trample the pasture and foul the water for others (34:18–19). This verse prepares directly for today's Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

Jesus takes this chapter to himself when he says, “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11), and when he tells of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to look for the one lost sheep (Luke 15:4–7). The Catechism lists the sheepfold among the images of the Church, a flock of which God himself foretold that he would be the shepherd (CCC 754, citing Ezekiel 34). St Augustine's sermon “On the Shepherds” is a long commentary on this chapter: those who lead in the Church must feed the flock and not themselves, and all of them are sheep of the one Shepherd (in substance).

1 Corinthians 15:20–26, 28 · He must reign until death is destroyed

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Church he had founded at Corinth from Ephesus, around AD 54. Chapter 15 answers some at Corinth who said “there is no resurrection of the dead” (15:12). They may have believed in a survival of the soul, but found a bodily resurrection foolish. Paul first recalls the creed he received, that Christ died, was buried, was raised and appeared (15:3–8), and then shows that if Christ is raised, the dead will be raised too.

Structure.

  • v. 20: Christ is raised as the first fruits.
  • vv. 21–22: one man brought death, one man brings resurrection: Adam and Christ.
  • v. 23: the order: Christ first, then at his coming those who belong to him.
  • vv. 24–26: the end: Christ hands over the kingdom after destroying every hostile power; the last enemy is death.
  • v. 27 (omitted): Psalm 8:6, “all things under his feet”.
  • v. 28: the Son himself will be subjected to the Father, so that God may be all in all.

Key words.

  • aparchē, first fruits: The first sheaf of the harvest, offered to God as a pledge that the whole harvest belongs to him (Leviticus 23:10–11). Christ's resurrection is not an isolated event; it is the first part of a harvest that includes us.
  • parousia, coming: The word for the official visit of a king or emperor to a city. Paul uses it for Christ's return in glory.
  • katargeō, to destroy, to make powerless: Used of every “ruler and authority and power” and of death. In v. 26 the verb is in the present tense; many read this as saying that death is already being overcome.
  • panta en pasin, all in all: The goal of all history: God's life filling everything and everyone, with nothing left outside it.

Verse by verse.

“But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the first fruits of those who have died.” (15:20)

After imagining a world without the resurrection (15:12–19), Paul turns with “But in fact”. The whole Christian hope rests on this fact.

“for as all die in Adam, so all will be made alive in Christ.” (15:22)

Adam and Christ are two heads of humanity. We belong to Adam by birth and to Christ by faith and baptism. Paul develops the same contrast in Romans 5:12–21.

“For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet.” (15:25)

An echo of Psalm 110:1, the psalm the New Testament quotes more often than any other. Christ's reign is not only future; it has begun with his resurrection and is now at work.

“The last enemy to be destroyed is death.” (15:26)

Death is called an enemy, not a friend or a natural release. Christ did not come to make us comfortable with death but to destroy it.

“so that God may be all in all.” (15:28)

How the Church reads it.

Verse 28 was used by the Arians to argue that the Son is less than the Father. The Fathers answered that the Son is subject as man and as head of his body the Church; in his divinity he is equal to the Father. St Gregory of Nazianzus explains that Christ makes our subjection his own, as he made our sin and our curse his own (in substance). When Marcellus of Ancyra taught that Christ's kingdom would one day end, the Church added to the Creed at Constantinople in 381 the words we still say at Mass: “and his kingdom will have no end”.

The Catechism teaches that Christ's reign is already present in the Church but still to be fulfilled with power and great glory (CCC 671), and that at the end of time the Kingdom will come in its fullness and God will be “all in all” (CCC 1060).

Matthew 25:31–46 · You did it to me

Setting.

This is the last scene of Jesus' last discourse in Matthew (chapters 24–25), and the last teaching before the Passion. Immediately after it Jesus says, “after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be handed over to be crucified” (26:2). The king who will judge the nations is about to be judged himself, stripped, thirsty and a prisoner.

It is not a parable in the strict sense but a great picture of the judgment, with one comparison inside it, the shepherd separating sheep from goats. In Palestine sheep and goats grazed together in mixed flocks; it is often explained that they were separated at evening, because goats need more warmth at night. The right hand is the place of honour.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–33: the Son of Man comes in glory, the nations are gathered, sheep to the right and goats to the left.
  • vv. 34–40: the king speaks to those on his right; six works of mercy; their surprise; “you did it to me”.
  • vv. 41–45: the same pattern for those on his left: the same six needs, the same surprise, “you did not do it to me”.
  • v. 46: two ways out: eternal punishment, eternal life.
  • ¶ The list of six needs is repeated four times. Matthew wants it learned by heart.

Key words.

  • basileus, king: The Son of Man of v. 31 becomes “the king” in v. 34. The one who sits on the throne of glory calls God “my Father”. This is the Gospel of the solemnity of Christ the King.
  • panta ta ethnē, all the nations: Every people is gathered, not Israel alone and not the Church alone. The Church reads the scene as the universal judgment of all humanity.
  • elachistoi, the least: The superlative of “small”. In Matthew the “little ones” are often the disciples (10:42; 18:6), but the Church has always read the passage as including every person in need, with whom Christ identifies himself.

Verse by verse.

“When the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on the throne of his glory.” (25:31)

The language is from Daniel 7:13–14 and Zechariah 14:5. The poor rabbi from Nazareth speaks of himself as the judge of the world.

“Come, you that are blessed by my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world;” (25:34)

The kingdom was prepared “from the foundation of the world”. God's plan from the beginning was not judgment but blessing; the fire, by contrast, was prepared “for the devil and his angels” (v. 41), not for human beings.

“Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food,” (25:37)

Both groups are surprised. The righteous did not help the poor in order to earn a reward from Christ; they simply helped. And those on the left would gladly have served Christ if they had recognised him.

“Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” (25:40)

The NRSV's “members of my family” renders the Greek “my brothers”. The king does not say “it is as if you did it to me” but “you did it to me”. Christ truly identifies himself with the poor and is met in them.

“And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life.” (25:46)

The same adjective, “eternal”, describes both. The Gospel takes our choices with full seriousness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus identifies himself with the poor of every kind and makes active love toward them the condition for entering his kingdom (CCC 544). It quotes this passage when it teaches that we cannot be united with God if we fail to meet the serious needs of the poor and the little ones who are his brethren (CCC 1033). It also gives St John of the Cross's saying: at the evening of life we shall be judged on our love (CCC 1022).

St John Chrysostom warned his people not to honour Christ's body in church with silk vestments while neglecting him outside, cold and naked (in substance). Sulpicius Severus tells that after St Martin of Tours cut his cloak in two for a beggar at Amiens, Christ appeared to him in a dream wearing that half-cloak.

Questions people ask

Does this Gospel mean we are saved by good works, not by faith?

No. The works here are the fruit of a love that comes from God's grace. Faith that is real “works through love” (Galatians 5:6), and the Catechism teaches that grace comes first and our merit is itself God's gift (CCC 2008, 2011). Matthew shows what living faith looks like from the outside.

Is hell really eternal?

The Church teaches that hell exists and is eternal, and that its chief pain is eternal separation from God (CCC 1035). It is the result of a person's own deliberate and final refusal of love (CCC 1033). God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). The Gospel is a warning given in time, while there is still time.

Why are the goats on the left? Are goats bad?

No. In the picture, sheep and goats belong to the same shepherd and graze together. The comparison is only about separation. Goats were valuable animals, used for sacrifice like sheep.

For the Latin reader

Pope Pius XI established the feast of Christ the King in 1925 with the encyclical Quas Primas, at a time when many nations were pushing God out of public life. He placed it on the last Sunday of October. In the reform after the Second Vatican Council, Pope Paul VI moved it to the last Sunday of the liturgical year and gave it its full title, “Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe”, so that the whole year now ends looking at Christ's coming in glory. Pius XI also asked that the consecration of the human race to the Sacred Heart of Jesus be renewed every year on this feast.

The Preface of the Mass describes the kingdom Christ will hand to the Father: a kingdom of truth and life, a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace. Since 2021 this Sunday is also the diocesan celebration of World Youth Day. Next Sunday Advent begins, and the readings move from Matthew to Mark (Year B).
