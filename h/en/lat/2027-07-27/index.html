Word for the day

“The Lord passed before him, and proclaimed, “The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness” Exodus 34:6

Reflection

After the golden calf the Lord said he would not go in the midst of this people (Exodus 33:3), and so Moses does something quiet and brave: he takes the tent, pitches it “outside the camp, far off from the camp,” and calls it the tent of meeting. “Everyone who sought the Lord would go out to the tent.” It is a place open to anyone who wants to pray. When Moses enters, the pillar of cloud comes down at the entrance, and the whole people, each at the door of their own tent, rise and bow down. Then comes the sentence that is at the heart of today's reading: “Thus the Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as one speaks to a friend.” Moses returns to the camp, but young Joshua “would not leave the tent.” Some people cannot bear to leave the place where God speaks. In chapter 34 Moses goes up the mountain a second time, and the Lord comes down in the cloud and “proclaimed the name”: “The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, keeping steadfast love for the thousandth generation, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin.” This is the great self-portrait of God in the Old Testament, repeated by the Psalms, the prophets and Jonah. It holds mercy and justice together (“yet by no means clearing the guilty”), but notice the proportions: judgment reaches “the third and the fourth generation,” while steadfast love reaches “the thousandth.” Moses bows quickly and prays, and his prayer is bold: “Although this is a stiff-necked people, pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us for your inheritance.” He uses their stubbornness as his argument for God to come with them. Then he spends “forty days and forty nights” with the Lord, neither eating nor drinking, and writes the words of the covenant. The Psalm sings it back: “The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. As far as the east is from the west, so far he removes our transgressions from us.”

In the Gospel, away from the crowds and “in the house,” the disciples ask Jesus: “Explain to us the parable of the weeds of the field.” He gives them a key: the one who sows is the Son of Man; the field is the world; the good seed are “the children of the kingdom”; the weeds are “the children of the evil one”; the enemy is the devil; the harvest is the end of the age and the reapers are angels. We should read the explanation together with the parable itself. When the servants asked whether they should pull up the weeds, the householder said: “No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them. Let both of them grow together until the harvest.” That is God's patience, the “slow to anger” of Exodus 34. We are not the reapers. We are told to sow, to weed our own hearts and to leave the final sorting to the Lord, because we cannot always tell wheat from weeds, and a person who looks like a weed today may be wheat tomorrow. The saints began as sinners. But the parable is serious too. There will be a harvest; “all causes of sin and all evildoers” will be gathered, and the end is described in terrible words. The Church teaches that Christ will come to judge the living and the dead (Catechism 678–679) and she does not soften the warning; yet she does not name anyone as lost. And the parable ends in light: “Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father,” echoing the prophet Daniel: “those who lead many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever.” The Church herself is a field where wheat and weeds grow, “holy and always in need of purification” (Catechism 827), and the patience of God is what gives each of us time to turn. St Peter says it plainly: the Lord “is patient with you, not wanting any to perish, but all to come to repentance” (2 Peter 3:9).

Full homily

Dear friends, after the sin of the golden calf, Moses does a beautiful thing. He pitches a tent outside the camp, far from the noise, and calls it the tent of meeting. And anyone who wants to seek the Lord can go there. When Moses goes in, the cloud comes down, and the people stand at the doors of their tents and bow. And the Bible says: the Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as one speaks to a friend (Exodus 33:11).

Do we speak to God like that? We say many prayers, but do we ever just talk? Moses returns to the camp, but Joshua, the young man, would not leave the tent. Some people cannot leave the place where God speaks. Do we have a quiet place, a corner of the house, a church, a chapel, where we can sit with the Lord, who now waits for us in the tabernacle?

And what does God say about himself when he passes before Moses? The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness (Exodus 34:6). Notice: slow to anger. Moses bows to the ground, and he prays: although this is a stiff-necked people, pardon our iniquity and our sin. He knows he is asking for a lot, but he knows to whom he is asking.

The Gospel shows the same patience. The farmer sowed good seed, and an enemy sowed weeds. The servants want to tear them out at once. But the master says: no, you would uproot the wheat along with them. Let both grow until the harvest. Brothers and sisters, God is waiting for the weeds in us to turn. We are not the judges. We are the ones who have been given time. Let us use it: weed our own hearts, confess our sins, forgive those who wrong us, and be patient with others. And at the harvest, the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. Amen.

For children

Children, Moses had a special tent where he went to talk to God. God spoke to him like a friend. We can talk to God like a friend too, at home, in church and on the way to school. And Jesus told a story about a farmer who found weeds growing among the wheat. He did not pull them up at once, because he might hurt the good plants. God is patient with us too. Let us thank him today, and let us be patient with our brothers and sisters!

Prayer

Merciful and gracious God, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, you spoke to Moses face to face as to a friend, and in your Son you call us your friends. Give us the courage to pitch our tent of prayer outside the noise of the camp, and to stay near it like Joshua. Teach us your patience, that we may not pull up the wheat while we judge the weeds, but leave the harvest to you. Make us shine like the sun in the kingdom of our Father. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After the sin of the golden calf, the first reading shows what remained between God and Moses: a tent outside the camp where the Lord spoke to him “face to face, as one speaks to a friend”, and a mountain where the Lord passed before him and proclaimed his name, merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love. Moses’ prayer, “pardon our iniquity and our sin”, is answered with a renewed covenant. In the Gospel the disciples ask Jesus to explain the parable of the weeds, and he describes the harvest at the end of the age, when the righteous will shine like the sun. In both readings the patience of God is the ground of hope, and his justice is not forgotten.

Exodus 33:7–11; 34:5b–9, 28 · The Lord used to speak to Moses face to face

Setting.

The two passages come from after the golden calf (Exodus 32). God has said that his angel, but not he himself, will go with the people (33:2–3), and the people mourn. The “tent of meeting” described in 33:7–11 is not the grand tabernacle, which has not yet been built; it is a simple tent that Moses pitches outside the camp, a place of prayer and of seeking the Lord’s will.

Chapter 34 tells of the renewal: Moses cuts two new tablets and goes up the mountain again, and the Lord comes down in the cloud. The words proclaimed in verses 6–7 are among the most quoted in the Old Testament, repeated in Numbers 14:18, Nehemiah 9:17, Psalm 103:8, Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2.

Structure.

  • 33:7–8: Moses’ tent outside the camp; the people watch him go.
  • 33:9–11: the pillar of cloud, the Lord speaking with Moses face to face; Joshua stays.
  • 34:5–7: the Lord descends, proclaims his name and his merciful character.
  • 34:8–9: Moses bows and prays for forgiveness and for the Lord to go with them.
  • 34:28: forty days and forty nights; the words of the covenant written on the tablets.

Key words.

  • ʾōhel môʿēd, tent of meeting: A place where God and his people meet. Because it is outside the camp, the people must go out from their own tents to seek the Lord, and everyone who seeks him may go (33:7).
  • pānîm ʾel-pānîm, face to face: A Hebrew idiom for direct and friendly speech. The Lord says a little later in the same chapter that no one can see his face and live (33:20), so “face to face” describes closeness, not sight of God’s being.
  • raḥûm wĕḥannûn, merciful and gracious: The first two of the attributes of the Lord that the Jewish tradition calls the thirteen attributes of mercy. “Merciful” is from the word for womb, reḥem: the tenderness of a mother.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love: Faithful, covenant love that does not give up. It is the quality in God that most often makes the covenant possible after the people’s failure.

Verse by verse.

“Thus the Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as one speaks to a friend.” (33:11)

This is the phrase that gives Moses his place among the prophets. In Numbers 12:8 the Lord says of Moses that he speaks with him directly, not in riddles.

“The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness,” (34:6)

God defines himself. He begins with mercy, not with power. This description is the foundation of the whole Old Testament’s hope in forgiveness.

“keeping steadfast love for the thousandth generation,” (34:7)

God’s love reaches to a thousand generations, while his visiting of sin reaches only to three or four (v. 7). The balance is on the side of mercy.

“Although this is a stiff-necked people, pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us for your inheritance.” (34:9)

God had called them a stiff-necked people (33:3, 5), and Moses uses the same word as a reason for God to go with them: because they are so weak they need God more. He includes himself in the sin: “our iniquity”.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads God’s self-description in Exodus 34 as the root of everything she says about divine mercy. The Catechism presents Moses, who spoke to the Lord face to face as to a friend, as a model both of contemplative prayer and of intercession (CCC 2576–2577). It treats this same self-revelation of the Lord as “merciful and gracious” when it speaks of God’s mercy and love (CCC 210–211).

Matthew 13:36–43 · Explain to us the parable of the weeds of the field

Setting.

Jesus has told the parable of the weeds sown among the wheat (13:24–30): the householder’s servants want to pull up the weeds, but he says to let both grow together until the harvest. Now Jesus leaves the crowds, goes into the house, and explains it privately to his disciples. This is the second parable in the chapter with an explanation, after the sower (13:18–23).

The weeds were probably darnel, a plant that looks like wheat until it ripens. The parable answers a question early Christians asked and we still ask: if the kingdom has come, why is there still evil in the field?

Structure.

  • v. 36: the disciples ask for an explanation.
  • vv. 37–39: the interpretation of each figure: the sower, the field, the good seed, the weeds, the enemy, the harvest and the reapers.
  • vv. 40–42: the end of the age: the Son of Man sends angels to collect the causes of sin and the evildoers.
  • v. 43: the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father.

Key words.

  • zizania, weeds, darnel: A weed that looks like wheat. Pulling it up early would destroy the wheat. The point is patience, not indifference to evil.
  • skandala, causes of sin: Literally “stumbling blocks”. They are removed from the kingdom, not individual weak people, but the things that make others fall.
  • synteleia tou aiōnos, end of the age: The close of the present age, when God’s purpose is completed. The expression is characteristic of Matthew (13:39, 40, 49; 24:3; 28:20).

Verse by verse.

“the field is the world,” (13:38)

The field is the world, not only the Church. Jesus sows his Word in the whole world, and the good seed is “the children of the kingdom”.

“Just as the weeds are collected and burned up with fire, so will it be at the end of the age.” (13:40)

Judgment belongs to God and comes at the end. Until then the disciples’ task is not to uproot the weeds but to grow as good wheat.

“Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father.” (13:43)

An echo of Daniel 12:3, where those who lead many to righteousness shine like the stars. The end of the story is light, and the Father’s kingdom is home.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in every member of the Church, the weeds of sin are still mixed with the good wheat of the Gospel until the end of time (CCC 827). It adds that the Church is therefore holy, yet always in need of purification, and follows the way of penance and renewal (CCC 827). The Church’s teaching on the last things quotes the same Gospel: Jesus solemnly proclaims that he will send his angels, and they will gather all who do evil (CCC 1034). St Augustine used the parable to teach that in this age the good and the bad are mixed in the Church and in the world (in substance); only God can sort them, and so we are to judge no one beforehand.

Questions people ask

Does God punish children for the sins of their parents (Exodus 34:7)?

No. The Bible itself corrects this reading. Ezekiel 18 says plainly that the child will not bear the guilt of the parent, and Deuteronomy 24:16 forbids it in the courts. Exodus 34:7 means that sin has long consequences in families and communities, as hatred, violence and addiction pass from one generation to the next. It also says that God’s love reaches a thousand generations, far beyond the reach of sin.

For study and teaching

  • God describes himself first as “merciful and gracious”. When I think of God, do I begin with mercy or with fear?
  • Jesus teaches patience with the weeds. Where in my family or parish am I tempted to “pull up the weeds” instead of praying and waiting?
