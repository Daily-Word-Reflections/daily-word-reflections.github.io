Word for the day

“Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. I do not give to you as the world gives. Do not let your hearts be troubled, and do not let them be afraid.” John 14:27

Reflection

Yesterday the crowd at Lystra wanted to worship Paul; today, stirred up by opponents who have followed him from Antioch and Iconium, the same crowd stones him, drags him out of the city and leaves him for dead. Luke tells the next moment very simply: “when the disciples surrounded him, he got up and went into the city.” The next day he walks on to Derbe. Then comes the surprising part. Instead of taking the short road home, Paul and Barnabas turn back through Lystra, Iconium and Antioch, the very towns where they had been threatened and beaten, to strengthen the new disciples. Their message is honest: “It is through many persecutions that we must enter the kingdom of God.” They appoint elders in each church and, “with prayer and fasting,” entrust them “to the Lord in whom they had come to believe.” Here we see the Church taking shape: local communities with their own pastors, handed over to the Lord in prayer. Years later Paul would remind Timothy, himself a young man of Lystra (Acts 16:1), of “what persecutions I endured” there, “Yet the Lord rescued me from all of them” (2 Timothy 3:11). Back in Syrian Antioch the missionaries gather the church and tell “all that God had done with them, and how he had opened a door of faith for the Gentiles.” They do not report their own sufferings first; they report what God has done. Note too that the opposition came from some who were stirred up, while Paul and Barnabas were themselves Jews, and Jews were among their first converts; Luke's story is never a charge against a whole people.

In the Gospel Jesus, at the Supper, speaks of a peace that can survive stones: “Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. I do not give to you as the world gives.” The world's peace is the absence of trouble, and it lasts only until the next trouble comes. The peace of Christ is his own presence, given on the night before he dies, and it can live in a heart surrounded by trouble. He knows what is coming: “the ruler of this world is coming. He has no power over me; but I do as the Father has commanded me, so that the world may know that I love the Father.” And he ends with words of quiet resolve: “Rise, let us be on our way.” The risen Lord's first word to the apostles on Easter evening will be this same gift: “Peace be with you.” Paul, rising from the stones and walking back into the city, shows us what that peace looks like in a human life.

Full homily

Dear friends, yesterday the crowd at Lystra wanted to worship Paul as a god. Today, stirred up by others, the same crowd stones him and drags him out of the city, thinking he is dead. Crowds change quickly. Praise and stones can come from the same hands within a few days.

But listen to what happens next: when the disciples surrounded him, he got up and went into the city (Acts 14:20). Not out of the city, into it. And soon Paul and Barnabas go back through every town where they had suffered, to strengthen the new believers: it is through many persecutions that we must enter the kingdom of God (Acts 14:22). They do not promise an easy road. They promise that the road leads to the kingdom.

Where did Paul find such strength? In the gift Jesus left on the night before he died: peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. I do not give to you as the world gives (John 14:27). The world's peace means no trouble. Christ's peace means his presence in the middle of the trouble. That is why Paul could rise from the stones.

Each of us has been hit by stones of some kind: criticism, illness, failure, betrayal. The risen Lord does not always stop the stones. He gives us his peace and says, as he said at the Supper: rise, let us be on our way (John 14:31). Let us receive his peace today, and carry it to someone whose heart is troubled. Amen.

For children

Children, some people threw stones at Paul and he fell down. Everyone thought he was dead! But his friends stood around him, and Paul got up and kept telling people about Jesus. Jesus gives us a special present called peace. It is like a calm, warm feeling inside that Jesus is with you, even when something is hard. Tonight, before you sleep, say: Jesus, give me your peace.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on the night before you died you left us your peace, a peace the world cannot give and cannot take away. When stones are thrown at us, help us to rise again as Paul did and walk back into the city with courage. Strengthen all who suffer for the faith, bless the pastors of your Church, and keep open the door of faith that you have opened for all nations. Who live and reign for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul is stoned and left for dead at Lystra, gets up, and goes on. The journey ends where it began, at Antioch, with a report: God had opened a door of faith for the Gentiles. In the Gospel Jesus leaves his peace with men who are about to be badly shaken. Psalm 145 lets the friends of the Lord speak of the glory of his kingdom, the kingdom that Paul says we enter through many persecutions.

Acts 14:19–28 · A door of faith for the Gentiles

Setting.

The crowd that wanted to worship Paul yesterday is turned against him today. Opponents from Antioch of Pisidia and Iconium, where Paul had already met resistance, arrive and stir up the people. Luke means particular opponents from those cities; at Iconium a great number of Jews had in fact believed (14:1). Paul himself later recalls, “Once I received a stoning” (2 Corinthians 11:25), and 2 Timothy 3:11 recalls his sufferings at Antioch, Iconium and Lystra. Timothy came from Lystra (16:1).

After Derbe the apostles do not take the short road home through Tarsus. They go back the way they came, through the very towns that had driven them out, to strengthen the new believers. Then they sail home to Antioch in Syria, the church that had sent them out (13:1–3).

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: Paul is stoned, rises, and goes to Derbe.
  • vv. 21–23: the return journey: strengthening, encouraging, appointing elders.
  • vv. 24–26: the way back to Antioch.
  • vv. 27–28: the report to the church.

Key words.

  • thlipsis, persecution, affliction: Literally pressure, being pressed hard. Paul does not promise new Christians an easy road (v. 22). Suffering is not a sign that God has failed; it is part of the way into his kingdom.
  • cheirotoneō, to appoint: Originally to stretch out the hand, as in voting; then simply to appoint. The apostles leave behind a recognised ministry in each church, with prayer and fasting (v. 23).
  • thyra tēs pisteōs, door of faith: God opened it; the apostles only walked through it. Paul uses the same picture of an open door for the Gospel (1 Corinthians 16:9).

Verse by verse.

“Then they stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing that he was dead.” (14:19)

Saul once guarded the coats of those who stoned Stephen (7:58). Now he knows the stones himself.

““It is through many persecutions that we must enter the kingdom of God.”” (14:22)

This is not gloom. It is honest care for new believers, said by a man still bruised from Lystra. They will not be surprised when trouble comes.

“And after they had appointed elders for them in each church, with prayer and fasting they entrusted them to the Lord in whom they had come to believe.” (14:23)

The ministry of the Church was still taking shape, and the titles were not yet fixed as they are today. What is clear already is that the apostles did not leave communities without shepherds, and that they handed them over not to themselves but to the Lord.

“how he had opened a door of faith for the Gentiles.” (14:27)

The report is about what God has done, not about the missionaries’ hardships. This verse prepares tomorrow’s question: on what terms do the Gentiles come in?

How the Church reads it.

Pope Benedict XVI took the title of his letter announcing the Year of Faith, Porta Fidei (2011), from this verse: the door of faith is always open for us. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the apostles took care to appoint successors so that the mission entrusted to them would continue (Lumen Gentium 20). Paul’s return to the towns that had stoned him shows the heart of a shepherd: the new believers mattered more to him than his own safety.

John 14:27–31a · My peace I give to you

Setting.

The Last Supper discourse continues. Peace, shalom, was the ordinary greeting and farewell among Jews. Jesus takes the everyday word and fills it: his peace is the wholeness that comes from God, and he gives it on the night he is to be betrayed. After the resurrection his first word to the disciples will be “Peace be with you” (20:19).

Structure.

  • v. 27: the gift of peace; do not be troubled.
  • v. 28: “I am going to the Father”.
  • v. 29: he tells them beforehand so that they may believe.
  • vv. 30–31a: the ruler of this world has no power over him; he does what the Father commands.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: The Greek word carries the Hebrew shalom: not only the absence of war but wholeness, harmony with God and others.
  • tarassō, to trouble, stir up: Used of water stirred up. John uses it of Jesus himself, troubled at the tomb of Lazarus (11:33) and at the approach of his hour (12:27; 13:21). He who was troubled for us gives us his peace.
  • ho tou kosmou archōn, the ruler of this world: John’s name for the devil (12:31; 16:11). He is coming in the betrayal and the arrest, but he has no hold on Jesus, who goes to the Cross out of love and obedience.

Verse by verse.

“I do not give to you as the world gives.” (14:27)

The world’s peace often depends on circumstances, or on keeping trouble away. His peace remains even in trouble, because it comes from him.

“because the Father is greater than I.” (14:28)

See the question below. Jesus wants the disciples to rejoice that he is going home to the Father, not to cling to him for themselves.

“He has no power over me;” (14:30)

Literally “in me he has nothing”. There is nothing in Jesus that belongs to the evil one. His death will not be a defeat but an offering.

How the Church reads it.

In the Roman Mass, before the sign of peace, the priest prays: “Lord Jesus Christ, who said to your Apostles: Peace I leave you, my peace I give you”. So this verse is heard at every Mass, and the peace we give one another flows from the Lord’s own gift, not from our feelings. St Augustine teaches that the Father is greater than the Son in the “form of a servant” that the Son took, while in the form of God the Son is equal to the Father (in substance).

Questions people ask

If Jesus is God, how can he say “the Father is greater than I”?

The Church confesses the Son as one in being with the Father. Fathers such as St Augustine read this verse of the Son’s human nature: as man, he is less than the Father. Others also saw in it the Father as the source from whom the Son eternally receives all that he is, without being any less God. Either way, the verse speaks of the Son’s love and obedience, not of a lesser divinity.

For study and teaching

  • When trouble comes, do I take it as a sign that God has abandoned me, or as part of the road Paul spoke of?
  • Next time I give the sign of peace at Mass, whom do I most need to give it to in my heart?
