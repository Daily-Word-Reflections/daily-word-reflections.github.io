Word for the day

“Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” John 6:68

Reflection

After the storm, a calm. St Luke pauses to give a summary of the young Church: “Meanwhile the church throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria had peace and was built up. Living in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, it increased in numbers.” Saul has been converted; the persecution has eased; the Church breathes. Then Luke follows Peter as he goes “here and there among all the believers,” a shepherd visiting his flock. At Lydda, on the coastal plain, Peter finds Aeneas, “who had been bedridden for eight years, for he was paralyzed.” His words are brief and clear about who is acting: “Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you; get up and make your bed!” Peter claims nothing for himself. The man gets up, and the whole region of Lydda and Sharon turns to the Lord. In nearby Joppa a disciple named Tabitha, in Greek Dorcas, both names meaning “gazelle,” has died. Luke describes her in one beautiful line: she “was devoted to good works and acts of charity.” When Peter arrives, the widows stand around him weeping, “showing tunics and other clothing that Dorcas had made while she was with them.” Her charity speaks for her; the work of her hands is her eulogy. Peter sends everyone out, kneels and prays, then turns to the body: “Tabitha, get up.” In Aramaic his words are almost exactly the words Peter had heard Jesus say in the house of Jairus, “Talitha cum,” little girl, get up (Mark 5:41). Peter had been in that room; now he does what he saw his Master do. Tabitha opens her eyes and sits up; Peter gives her his hand. The Psalm sings our gratitude: “What shall I return to the LORD for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation,” and it reminds us that “precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his faithful ones.” The cup of salvation is lifted at every Mass.

In the Gospel the long discourse on the Bread of Life comes to its painful end. Many of the disciples say: “This teaching is difficult; who can accept it?” Jesus does not chase after them with an easier explanation. He asks: “Does this offend you? Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?” And he adds: “It is the spirit that gives life; the flesh is useless. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.” Jesus is not taking back what he said about his flesh as true food; he is saying that merely human reasoning, “the flesh” in that sense, cannot grasp this gift. Only the Spirit gives the faith to receive it. The Catechism notes that the first announcement of the Eucharist divided the disciples, just as the announcement of the Passion scandalized them: the Eucharist and the Cross are stumbling blocks (Catechism 1336). “Because of this many of his disciples turned back and no longer went about with him.” Jesus lets them go rather than water down the truth. Then he turns to the Twelve with a question full of sorrow and freedom: “Do you also wish to go away?” And Simon Peter answers for all who have ever wanted to leave and stayed: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God.” Peter does not say: we understand everything. He says: we know you. Faith is not first a solved problem but a trusted person. That same Peter, in today's first reading, raises the sick and the dead in the name of the Lord he refused to leave. Mary, the Mother of the Lord, is the first and most faithful of those who stayed; in this Easter season we greet her each day with joy: Queen of Heaven, rejoice, alleluia.

Full homily

Dear friends, after the persecution the Church had peace and grew in the comfort of the Holy Spirit (Acts 9:31). Peter went from place to place visiting the believers. At Lydda he met Aeneas, paralyzed for eight years, and said: Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you; get up (Acts 9:34). Not I heal you. Jesus Christ heals you.

At Joppa a good woman, Tabitha, had died. The widows showed Peter the clothes she had made for them. That was her whole life story: good works and acts of charity. Peter knelt and prayed and said: Tabitha, get up. And she opened her eyes (Acts 9:40). The risen Lord is still raising the dead to life.

In the Gospel many disciples said about the Eucharist: this teaching is difficult; who can accept it? (John 6:60). And they left. Jesus did not run after them to make it easier. He turned to the Twelve and asked: do you also wish to go away?

Peter answered with words we can make our own on the hardest days: Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life (John 6:68). He did not say: we understand everything. He said: we know you. When faith is hard, let us not walk away. Let us stay with him, receive him, and ask him to give us the Spirit who gives life. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Tabitha loved to sew clothes for poor people. Everyone loved her! When she died, Peter prayed and said: Tabitha, get up! And God gave her life again. Some people stopped following Jesus because his words were hard. But Peter said: Jesus, we will stay with you! Let us say it too: Jesus, I will always stay with you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you healed Aeneas and raised Tabitha through the faith and prayer of Peter. When your words seem hard to us, keep us from turning back, give us your Spirit who gives life, and let us say with Peter and with all your faithful: Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The week closes with two scenes of Peter. In Acts he goes about among the believers of a Church that now has peace, and through his word the Lord raises up a paralysed man at Lydda and a beloved disciple at Joppa. In John, when many disciples leave after the hard words about his flesh and blood, Peter answers for the Twelve: “Lord, to whom can we go?” Psalm 116 gives the grateful question: “How shall I make a return to the Lord for all the good he has done for me?”

Acts 9:31–42 · Tabitha, get up

Setting.

After Saul’s conversion Luke pauses with a summary: the church in Judea, Galilee and Samaria has peace and grows (v. 31). Then he returns to Peter, who visits the believers on the coastal plain. Lydda (today Lod) lies inland from Joppa (today Jaffa), the old port of Jerusalem. These visits lead to Peter’s stay in Joppa (9:43) and to his call to the house of Cornelius in the next chapter.

Structure.

  • v. 31: summary: the church at peace and growing.
  • vv. 32–35: Aeneas healed at Lydda; many turn to the Lord.
  • vv. 36–39: Tabitha dies at Joppa; the disciples send for Peter; the widows weep.
  • vv. 40–42: Peter prays; “Tabitha, get up”; many believe.

Key words.

  • ekklēsia, church: Verse 31 uses the word in the singular for the believers of three regions together: already one Church in many places. Some later manuscripts have the plural “churches”.
  • iatai, heals: “Jesus Christ heals you.” Peter does not say “I heal you”. As at the Temple gate (3:6), the name of Jesus does the work.
  • mathētria, woman disciple: The feminine form of “disciple” occurs only here in the New Testament. Tabitha is the Aramaic word for a gazelle; Dorcas is the same word in Greek.

Verse by verse.

““Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you; get up and make your bed!” And immediately he got up.” (9:34)

As Jesus told a paralysed man to take up his bed (Luke 5:24), so Peter speaks in his name.

“She was devoted to good works and acts of charity.” (9:36)

Literally “full of good works and of almsgiving”. Her holiness was made of needles and cloth.

“All the widows stood beside him, weeping and showing tunics and other clothing that Dorcas had made while she was with them.” (9:39)

Widows were among the poorest in that society. Their grief is her best eulogy: they show what she made for them.

“Peter put all of them outside, and then he knelt down and prayed. He turned to the body and said, “Tabitha, get up.”” (9:40)

The scene recalls the raising of Jairus’ daughter, where Jesus put the mourners outside and said, “Talitha cum”, “Little girl, get up!” (Mark 5:40–41). In Greek the two words differ by a single letter. But unlike Jesus, Peter first kneels and prays: the power is not his own.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Tabitha a model of charity done with the hands, and many sewing circles for the poor have taken the name of Dorcas. The Catechism counts clothing the naked among the corporal works of mercy (CCC 2447). The raising of Tabitha also shows what Easter means for the Church: the risen Lord goes on working through his apostles and confirms their word by signs (Mark 16:20).

John 6:60–69 · Lord, to whom can we go?

Setting.

The discourse is over, and now the disciples react. Not only the crowd but “many of his disciples” find the teaching too hard. John records the first great falling away, and the first confession of the Twelve in his Gospel. It is his counterpart to Peter’s confession at Caesarea Philippi in the other Gospels (Mark 8:29).

Structure.

  • vv. 60–61: “This teaching is difficult.”
  • vv. 62–65: the Son of Man ascending; the spirit gives life; no one comes unless it is granted by the Father.
  • v. 66: many turn back.
  • vv. 67–69: “Do you also wish to go away?” Peter’s answer.

Key words.

  • sklēros, difficult, hard: Not hard to understand but hard to accept. They understood well enough that he meant what he said.
  • hē sarx ouk ōphelei ouden, the flesh is useless: “Flesh” here means human nature left to itself, judging by its own lights, as in 3:6. It does not cancel what Jesus said of his own flesh in vv. 51–56. His words are “spirit and life”.
  • ho hagios tou theou, the Holy One of God: A rare title (Mark 1:24). Some later manuscripts change it to “the Christ, the Son of the living God”, under the influence of Matthew 16:16; most scholars hold the shorter reading to be the original.

Verse by verse.

“When many of his disciples heard it, they said, “This teaching is difficult; who can accept it?”” (6:60)

The Catechism notes that the first announcement of the Eucharist divided the disciples, just as the announcement of the Passion scandalized them (CCC 1336).

“It is the spirit that gives life; the flesh is useless. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.” (6:63)

St Augustine explains that the hearers took the flesh as meat cut up in the market, and that this was the useless way to understand him; the flesh of Christ, joined to the Spirit, gives life (in substance).

“Because of this many of his disciples turned back and no longer went about with him.” (6:66)

Jesus lets them go. He does not change the teaching to keep the crowd.

“Simon Peter answered him, “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” (6:68)

Peter does not claim to understand everything. He has found no one else with words like these. His faith rests on a person.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism takes up the question of verse 67: it echoes through the ages as a loving invitation to discover that only he has the words of eternal life, and that to receive the gift of the Eucharist in faith is to receive the Lord himself (CCC 1336). Peter’s answer has become the prayer of every Christian who struggles with the faith and yet stays.

Questions people ask

What should I do when a teaching of the Church seems too hard to accept?

Do what Peter did: stay close to Jesus, and say honestly that you are still learning. Ask, read, pray, and talk with a priest or catechist. The Catechism, quoting St John Henry Newman, reminds us that ten thousand difficulties do not make one doubt (CCC 157).

For study and teaching

  • Tabitha’s holiness was seen in the clothes she made for widows. What ordinary work of my hands could become a work of mercy this week?
  • When have I been tempted to “go away”, and what has kept me, like Peter, with the Lord?
