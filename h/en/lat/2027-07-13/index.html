Word for the day

“When the child grew up, she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and she took him as her son. She named him Moses, “because,” she said, “I drew him out of the water.”” Exodus 2:10

Reflection

The book of Exodus opens with a people under pressure. Pharaoh has ordered every Hebrew boy to be thrown into the Nile, and today's reading begins with one family's answer. A man and a woman of the house of Levi have a son. His mother sees that he is “a fine baby” and hides him for three months. When she can hide him no longer she does a remarkable thing: she obeys Pharaoh's order, and defeats it. She does put the child in the Nile, but in a “papyrus basket” plastered with bitumen and pitch. The Hebrew word for this basket, tebah, is the word used for Noah's ark; the Bible is quietly telling us that another great rescue through water has begun. The child's sister, who is later named Miriam, stands at a distance and watches. Three women save him: the mother who lets go, the sister who keeps watch, and the daughter of Pharaoh who “took pity on him” although she knows at once that he is “one of the Hebrews’ children.” The tyrant's own house shelters the one who will break the tyrant's power, and the boy's mother is even paid wages to nurse her own son. Then comes the name: “She named him Moses, ‘because,’ she said, ‘I drew him out of the water.’” The name echoes the Hebrew for “to draw out.” The one drawn out of the water will draw a whole people out of the sea. The reading then jumps forward. Moses, grown up, sees an Egyptian beating one of his kinsfolk and kills him, and the next day one of his own people asks: “Who made you a ruler and judge over us?” Moses flees to Midian and sits down by a well. St Stephen will later say that Moses thought his people would understand that God was rescuing them through him, “but they did not understand,” and yet this very Moses was the one “whom God now sent as both ruler and liberator” (Acts 7:25, 35). The psalm is the prayer of a person in deep water: “I have come into deep waters, and the flood sweeps over me,” and yet “the Lord hears the needy.”

In the Gospel Jesus speaks hard words, and they have to be heard as words of grief. “Then he began to reproach the cities in which most of his deeds of power had been done, because they did not repent.” The cities are Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, small towns around the Sea of Galilee. Capernaum was the base of his ministry, and Bethsaida was the town of Peter, Andrew and Philip (John 1:44). The cry “Woe to you!” is the cry of the prophets over a city they love; it is closer to a mourner's “Alas for you!” than to a curse. The reason is not that these towns were worse than others but that they had been given more. They had seen the deeds of power, the healings and the teaching, and had not repented, which in the Bible means turning round and changing direction. Jesus compares them with Tyre and Sidon, pagan harbour cities that the prophets had condemned, and even with Sodom, the byword for sin. If those cities had seen what you saw, he says, they would have repented “long ago in sackcloth and ashes.” On the day of judgment it will be “more tolerable” for them: grace that is received and not used becomes a weight. Jesus speaks here as a son of Israel to his own neighbours in Galilee, in the manner of the prophets, and the Gospels show that many from these very towns did follow him; the first disciples came from them. The words are a family's lament, not a verdict against a people. The two readings meet in one question. Moses was drawn out of the water, and his people did not at first know their deliverer. Jesus was among his people, and many of his towns had seen and not yet turned. What have we done with what we have been given: our baptism, the Word, the Mass? Nothing in the Gospel is said to make us anxious; it is said to wake us up while there is still time to turn.

Full homily

Dear friends, picture a river and a basket among the reeds. In the basket is a three-month-old baby. His mother has done everything she can; now she has to trust God. And God does what he so often does: he uses ordinary people. A mother who lets go, a sister who stands and watches, and a princess who does not look away from a crying baby. Look at how many small hands it takes to save one child. The woman who draws him out of the river names him Moses: because I drew him out of the water (Exodus 2:10). He will draw out a whole people.

We too have been drawn out of the water. In baptism, God lifted us out of the old life and gave us a name. Who were the hands that held you? A mother, a grandmother, a teacher, a priest, a neighbour. Today is a good day to remember them and to thank God for them, and to ask: whom am I holding up?

The story goes on. Moses grows up, sees his people suffering, and tries to help in his own way, with violence, and it goes badly. His own people say: Who made you a ruler and judge over us? (Exodus 2:14). He runs away. It looks like the end, but it is only the beginning: forty years later God will send this same Moses from a burning bush. God's time is longer than ours. When our own good plans fail, God has not stopped.

In the Gospel Jesus grieves over three towns. Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! (Matthew 11:21). It sounds harsh, but listen to the heart behind it: it is the cry of a friend who has done everything and sees his friends still not turning. They saw the most miracles and changed the least. How much have we seen? The Mass every Sunday, the Bible in our own language, the sacraments, the saints. St Henry, whom we remember today, was an emperor who knew that much had been given to him, and so much would be asked. Let us turn back with gratitude today, and let us not wait. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago a baby was in great danger. His mother made a little basket, put him inside it and placed it by the river. His big sister watched to see what would happen. Then a king's daughter came, found the baby and loved him. She called him Moses, which means “drawn out of the water.” Moses grew up and became a great helper of God's people. God uses many people to look after one child: a mother, a sister and a princess. Who looked after you? Let us say thank you to them today, and let us look after someone smaller too.

Prayer

Lord God, you saw the cry of your people and drew Moses out of the water to be their deliverer. Draw us out of every flood that threatens to carry us away, and teach us to thank those who held us up when we could not hold ourselves. Lord Jesus, you grieved over the cities that saw your works and did not turn: give us hearts that are quick to repent and quick to be grateful. Through the intercession of St Henry, make all who hold power servants of the poor and makers of peace. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church tells two stories of towns and people who were given a great gift. In Exodus a baby is drawn out of the Nile by an Egyptian princess, and grows up to be the one who will draw Israel out of Egypt. In the Gospel Jesus speaks sorrowfully to the Galilean towns of Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, where he did most of his mighty works, because they did not change their hearts. The first reading shows how God quietly saves; the Gospel shows how much is asked of those who have seen his work. Today the Church also remembers St Henry, the emperor who ruled in the early eleventh century and died in 1024; the Mass of the weekday is kept.

Exodus 2:1–15a · Drawn out of the water

Setting.

The book of Exodus opens with the Israelites multiplying in Egypt and with a king who feared them. Pharaoh set them to forced labor (1:11–14) and then ordered that every Hebrew boy be thrown into the Nile (1:22). Chapter 2 begins with a family from the tribe of Levi who will not obey that decree. Their names are not given here; the Bible names them later as Amram and Jochebed (6:20).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: a Levite mother hides her son for three months, then places him in a basket among the reeds, with his sister watching.
  • vv. 5–10: Pharaoh’s daughter finds him, takes pity, and unknowingly hires his own mother as his nurse; she names him Moses.
  • vv. 11–15a: the grown Moses sees the forced labor of his people, kills an Egyptian, and flees from Pharaoh to Midian.

Key words.

  • tēbâ, basket: The same rare word is used for Noah’s ark (Genesis 6:14). In both stories a vessel of wood or reed carries life safely through the waters of death.
  • ṭôb, fine, good: The mother “saw that he was a fine baby”. The Hebrew is the word of Genesis 1, “God saw that it was good”. A quiet echo of creation: a new life, a new beginning.
  • Mōšeh, Moses: The name sounds like the Hebrew verb māšâ, “to draw out”, which the princess uses in her explanation. Many scholars note that the name may also be Egyptian, meaning “born of” as in the name Thutmose. Either way, the story says that this child is the one who will draw out a people.

Verse by verse.

“when she saw that he was a fine baby, she hid him three months.” (2:2)

The parents act in faith. The Letter to the Hebrews says that it was by faith that Moses was hidden by his parents, because they were not afraid of the king’s edict (Hebrews 11:23).

“He was crying, and she took pity on him.” (2:6)

The one who saves the child is not an Israelite but a daughter of the very house that threatens him. God works through the compassion of a woman who disobeys her father’s decree. Her pity opens the way.

“She named him Moses, “because,” she said, “I drew him out of the water.”” (2:10)

Moses grows up in Pharaoh’s palace but is nursed by his own mother, so he knows who he is. God prepares his servant in two worlds.

“Who made you a ruler and judge over us?” (2:14)

Moses acted for his people but by his own anger and his own authority, and they refused him. Stephen later recalls these very words in Acts 7:27 and 35. God’s call will come, but at God’s hour and in his way.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the life of Moses as a shadow of Christ. Matthew tells of the infancy of Jesus in a way that recalls this story: a wicked king orders the death of children, the child is saved, and he is brought out of Egypt (Matthew 2). The Letter to the Hebrews praises the faith of Moses’ parents and says that Moses chose to share the ill-treatment of God’s people rather than enjoy the pleasures of Pharaoh’s house (Hebrews 11:23–25). The water that carries and saves is also a sign the Church has loved: the Catechism recalls that the ark of Noah and the crossing of the Red Sea foreshadow the saving water of Baptism (CCC 1219–1221).

Matthew 11:20–24 · Woe to you, Chorazin!

Setting.

After sending out the Twelve (chapter 10), Jesus answered the question of John the Baptist’s disciples and spoke of John and of “this generation” (11:2–19). Now he turns to the towns where he had done most of his deeds of power. Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum lay close together on the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Capernaum was the town Jesus made his home (4:13; 9:1), and Bethsaida was the home town of Philip, Andrew and Peter (John 1:44). All these hearers were Jews, and Jesus speaks as a prophet to his own people, as Amos and Jeremiah did before him.

Structure.

  • v. 20: the reproach: the towns did not repent.
  • vv. 21–22: Chorazin and Bethsaida are compared with Tyre and Sidon.
  • vv. 23–24: Capernaum is compared with Sodom.

Key words.

  • dynameis, deeds of power: The Gospel’s word for miracles. They are not meant to impress but to call people to turn to God.
  • metanoeō, to repent: Literally to change one’s mind and direction. Repentance is not only sorrow but a new way of living.
  • ouai, woe: The cry of the prophets (Isaiah 5:8–23). It is as much a lament as a threat, like the cry of someone at a funeral: how sad that it should come to this.

Verse by verse.

“Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida!” (11:21)

Tyre and Sidon, pagan cities of the Phoenician coast, had been denounced by the prophets (Isaiah 23; Ezekiel 26–28). Jesus says that even they would have repented if they had seen what Galilee saw.

“on the day of judgment it will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon than for you.” (11:22)

Responsibility is measured by the light received. The more we have been given, the more is asked (compare Luke 12:48).

“will you be exalted to heaven? No, you will be brought down to Hades.” (11:23)

These words echo Isaiah 14:13–15, where the proud king of Babylon wants to climb to heaven and is cast down to the pit. Capernaum, honored by the presence of Jesus, is warned not to make that kind of pride its own.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these words as a call to conversion, not as a curse on any town. The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ warnings about judgment are urgent calls to conversion and that God wishes no one to be lost (CCC 1036; 1037). The Fathers often taught that the more of God’s gifts we receive, in the word and in the sacraments, the more we are accountable for them (in substance). For us the question is whether the Gospel, heard every Sunday, really changes us.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus condemn the people of Capernaum and the other towns for ever?

The passage is a prophetic warning, spoken with sorrow. It does not say that every person in those towns was lost; many of Jesus’ closest followers came from them. Jesus compares the towns with Tyre, Sidon and Sodom to show how serious it is to receive God’s gifts and not respond. The goal of every woe in the Bible is repentance.

For study and teaching

  • Moses’ mother and Pharaoh’s daughter each did a small act of courage and mercy. What small act could I do for a child or a family in danger?
  • Jesus says that more is asked of those who have seen more of God’s works. What have I been given that I have not yet answered?
