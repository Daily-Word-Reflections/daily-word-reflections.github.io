Word for the day

“Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father. And even the hairs of your head are all counted. So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” Matthew 10:29–31

Reflection

The Book of Genesis, which began with the creation of the heavens and the earth, ends with a coffin. Jacob is dying, and his last concern is where he will be buried: not in Egypt, but with his fathers in the cave at Machpelah, near Mamre, “in the field that Abraham bought from Ephron the Hittite as a burial site. There Abraham and his wife Sarah were buried; there Isaac and his wife Rebekah were buried; and there I buried Leah.” The patriarchs own no land but a grave, and in that grave is their claim on the promise. Then the old man dies, Joseph weeps and has him embalmed, and the family goes up to Canaan to bury him; the Egyptians mourn for seventy days. But once the father is gone, an old fear awakens in the brothers. “Realizing that their father was dead, Joseph's brothers said, ‘What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us and pays us back in full for all the wrong that we did to him?’” They send a message in their father's name, begging Joseph to forgive “the crime of the servants of the God of your father.” Genesis does not say whether Jacob really spoke those words. It tells us that the brothers, after seventeen years of living in Egypt on Joseph's bounty, have still not grasped how completely they are forgiven. “Joseph wept when they spoke to him.” It grieved him that they could think so little of his love. When they fall down before him, he says the sentence that is the summit of the whole book: “Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God? Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good, in order to preserve a numerous people, as he is doing today. So have no fear; I myself will provide for you and your little ones.” He forgives them and does not diminish the evil that was done; he sets it inside a larger story that God is writing. “In this way he reassured them, speaking kindly to them.” Genesis ends with Joseph's own death at one hundred and ten. His last words look beyond Egypt: “I am about to die; but God will surely come to you, and bring you up out of this land to the land that he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.” He makes them swear: “When God comes to you, you shall carry up my bones from here.” The book closes: “he was embalmed and placed in a coffin in Egypt.” It is a coffin with a promise inside it. Moses will carry those bones out of Egypt (Exodus 13:19), and the Letter to the Hebrews will say that Joseph, at the end of his life, “made mention of the exodus of the Israelites” by faith (Hebrews 11:22). The Psalm is the song of such a people: “O give thanks to the Lord, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples,” “Seek the Lord and his strength; seek his presence continually,” and “O offspring of his servant Abraham, children of Jacob, his chosen ones.”

The Gospel takes up the same word that Joseph spoke twice: do not be afraid. Jesus has told his disciples that they will be hated and brought before courts; now he tells them what to do with their fear. First he reminds them who they follow: “A disciple is not above the teacher, nor a slave above the master; it is enough for the disciple to be like the teacher, and the slave like the master. If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more will they malign those of his household!” Beelzebul is a name for the prince of the demons, the very name the enemies of Jesus have already thrown at him (Matthew 9:34). The disciples are not to be surprised if they share his treatment. And then three times comes the command: “So have no fear of them.” The first reason is that truth will come out: “nothing is covered up that will not be uncovered, and nothing secret that will not become known.” What Jesus has taught them in the quiet of the circle of disciples, they must now proclaim: “What I say to you in the dark, tell in the light; and what you hear whispered, proclaim from the housetops,” the flat roofs from which news was shouted across the village. The second reason is that there is a greater fear that drives out the lesser: “Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell.” Jesus is not asking us to be afraid of God as of a tyrant; the next verse shows the same God counting sparrows. The holy fear of the Lord, which the Church counts among the gifts of the Holy Spirit (Catechism 1831), is the fear of losing God and the life he gives, and it is the beginning of wisdom. The third reason is the tenderness of the Father. “Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father. And even the hairs of your head are all counted. So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” The sparrow was the cheapest bird sold for food in the market, the poor man's meat. If the Father knows each of them, how much more his children. The passage ends with the choice of every disciple: “Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven; but whoever denies me before others, I also will deny before my Father in heaven.” Joseph's brothers feared a master who had loved them all along; we are asked to trust a Father who has loved us all along.

Homily outline

Theme: “Do not be afraid.”

Joseph forgives the brothers who still fear him, Genesis ends with a coffin and a promise, and Jesus says three times: have no fear.

1. The fear of the forgiven

  • Jacob is buried at Machpelah.
  • What if Joseph still bears a grudge?
  • Seventeen years of kindness, and still afraid.
“Realizing that their father was dead, Joseph's brothers said, “What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us and pays us back in full for all the wrong that we did to him?”” Genesis 50:15
“But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him.” Luke 15:20

Apply: Make a good confession if you need it, and then let the matter rest: you are forgiven.

2. Am I in the place of God?

  • Joseph weeps.
  • God intended it for good.
  • He spoke kindly to them.
“Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God? Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good, in order to preserve a numerous people, as he is doing today.” Genesis 50:19–20
“Bear with one another and, if anyone has a complaint against another, forgive each other; just as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive.” Colossians 3:13

Apply: Say to one person, in clear and kind words, that you have forgiven them.

3. A coffin with a promise

  • Carry up my bones.
  • God will surely come.
  • Moses and the bones of Joseph.
“I am about to die; but God will surely come to you, and bring you up out of this land to the land that he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.” Genesis 50:24
“By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave instructions about his burial.” Hebrews 11:22

Apply: Think of one promise of God you are waiting for, and say aloud: God will surely come.

4. Do not be afraid

  • The truth will come out.
  • Holy fear, not fright.
  • Sparrows and the hairs of your head.
“But now thus says the Lord, he who created you, O Jacob, he who formed you, O Israel: Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.” Isaiah 43:1
“So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” Matthew 10:31

Apply: Choose one fear and hand it to the Father in prayer each morning this week.

Joseph forgives his brothers and sees the larger story God is writing; Genesis ends with his coffin and God's promise to come.

Jesus tells us three times not to be afraid, for the Father knows the sparrows and the hairs of our heads.

Take-home resolution: Hand one old fear to God, and tell one person that you have forgiven them.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we read the last pages of the Book of Genesis. Jacob has died. Seventeen years have passed since the family came down to Egypt, and Joseph has looked after them all. But when their father dies, the brothers are afraid again. What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us? (Genesis 50:15). It is a very human fear. They have been forgiven, and they have not believed it. How many of us have gone to confession, and still carry the old guilt, as though God had not meant what he said?

Joseph wept when they spoke to him. It hurt him that they could think so little of his love. And then he says one of the great sentences of the Bible: Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God? Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good (Genesis 50:19–20). He does not say the evil was good. He says that God was greater than the evil. And he comforts them, speaking kindly to them. That is how God speaks to the penitent.

In the Gospel Jesus says it three times: do not be afraid. He knows that his disciples will meet opposition. But the Father who counts the sparrows, the cheapest birds in the market, also counts the hairs of your head. You are of more value than many sparrows (Matthew 10:31). The only fear we need is the holy fear of losing God, and that fear leads us to him.

Genesis ends with a coffin. But Joseph said: God will surely come to you, and carry up my bones. His body was a promise. Our graves too are waiting rooms. Let us hand our fears to God today, forgive without counting the cost, and speak up for Jesus when it matters. And let us ask Mary, who stood without fear at the Cross, to pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Joseph's brothers were frightened again after their father died. They thought Joseph would punish them. But Joseph cried, and said: do not be afraid! God turned the bad thing into a good one. In the Gospel Jesus says: do not be afraid. God looks after even the smallest sparrow, and he knows how many hairs you have on your head! You are very, very precious to him. So when you are scared in the dark, or about school, or about anything, say: Jesus, I trust you.

Prayer

Lord God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, you wrote the story of Joseph and his brothers and brought good out of the evil that was done. When we are afraid of those we have wronged, and when we cannot believe that we are forgiven, teach us to trust in your mercy. Lord Jesus, you tell us not to be afraid of those who can kill the body, because the Father counts the sparrows and numbers the hairs of our heads. Give us courage to speak in the light what you tell us in the dark, and to acknowledge you before others. Holy Mary, who stood by the Cross without fear, pray for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Book of Genesis ends, and the Gospel of the week keeps its theme: do not be afraid. After Jacob’s death the brothers fear that Joseph will take his revenge, and Joseph answers, “Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God? You intended to do harm to me, but God intended it for good.” In the Gospel Jesus tells the Twelve three times not to fear: not the opposition, not those who kill the body, for the Father counts every sparrow and every hair of your head. Psalm 105 calls the people to seek the Lord and his strength, and to remember the wonderful works he has done. Where it is kept, the Saturday memorial of the Blessed Virgin Mary may be celebrated; the readings are those of the weekday.

Genesis 49:29–32; 50:15–26a · God intended it for good

Setting.

Genesis 49 gives the last words of Jacob, who blesses each of his twelve sons. Today’s reading begins with his final charge: to be buried with his fathers in the cave at Machpelah. Chapter 50 tells of the great funeral in Canaan (50:1–14). When the brothers return to Egypt, the fear they had put aside comes back.

Background. Joseph had forgiven his brothers seventeen years earlier (45:5; 47:28). But the brothers cannot believe forgiveness is real once their protector is gone. The reading ends the book with the death of Joseph in Egypt, in a coffin, and with a promise: God will come and lead the people up to the land he swore to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Genesis ends in a coffin and in hope. The Lectionary leaves out 49:33 and 50:1–14, the death and burial of Jacob.

Structure.

  • 49:29–32: Jacob’s charge: bury me with my ancestors at Machpelah, the field Abraham bought.
  • 50:15–18: the brothers fear that Joseph will repay them; they send a message in Jacob’s name and fall down before him.
  • 50:19–21: Joseph weeps, says “Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God?” and promises to provide for them.
  • 50:22–26: Joseph lives one hundred ten years; he foretells that God will come, makes the Israelites swear to carry up his bones, and dies.

Key words.

  • ḥāšab, to plan, to intend: The same verb is used twice in v. 20: you “intended” evil, God “intended” it for good. The same event has two authors: human sin and divine providence, and God’s purpose wins.
  • pāqad, to visit, to come to: “God will surely come to you” (v. 24). The verb is used again in Exodus 3:16, when God visits his people in their slavery. It means a coming with power to save.
  • dibber ʿal-lēb, to speak to the heart: The Hebrew of v. 21 says Joseph “spoke to their hearts”. It is the language of tenderness (Hosea 2:14). Forgiveness here is not only a verdict but a tender word.

Verse by verse.

“There Abraham and his wife Sarah were buried; there Isaac and his wife Rebekah were buried; and there I buried Leah” (49:31)

Jacob lists the graves of his family. The only piece of the promised land the patriarchs own is a tomb, and Jacob wants to lie there in hope that the land will one day be his people’s.

“What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us” (50:15)

Guilt does not believe in forgiveness easily. The brothers judge Joseph by their own hearts.

“Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God?” (50:19)

Joseph refuses to take the place of the judge. Vengeance belongs to God alone (Deuteronomy 32:35).

“Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good, in order to preserve a numerous people, as he is doing today.” (50:20)

The summit of the Joseph story. It does not say that the wrong was right. It says that God is greater than evil and can bring good out of it.

“In this way he reassured them, speaking kindly to them.” (50:21)

Forgiveness is shown by both providing and speaking tenderly. Joseph promises food and he comforts.

“you shall carry up my bones from here.” (50:25)

Joseph’s hope looks beyond Egypt. His bones were carried out at the Exodus (Exodus 13:19) and buried at Shechem (Joshua 24:32). The Letter to the Hebrews names this as an act of faith (Hebrews 11:22).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses the words of Joseph to teach that God can bring good out of evil: it is one of the great examples in Scripture (CCC 312, quoting Genesis 50:20). The Church also reads the end of Genesis as a sign of hope against the grave. Joseph, who forgave and fed those who had harmed him, has long been seen as a figure of Christ, who prayed for those who crucified him and who gives himself as bread. Christians, as the Catechism says of forgiveness, ask for the grace to do what Joseph did (CCC 2843). The reading is also, first of all, the Jewish people’s own story of the end of the patriarchs and the beginning of the people of Israel.

Matthew 10:24–33 · You are of more value than many sparrows

Setting.

The mission discourse of chapter 10 now comes to its centre: three times in vv. 26, 28 and 31 Jesus says “do not fear”. The disciples will be maligned as their master was. Matthew places this teaching just after the warning of persecution, to show that fear is answered by trust in the Father.

Background. In Jewish households a slave or pupil was bound to his master or teacher. “Beelzebul” in v. 25 is the name the opponents gave to the prince of demons when they accused Jesus (12:24; compare 9:34). To call the master of the house this is to call his whole household the same. A sparrow was the cheapest food in the market, sold for a small coin; in Luke the price is five sparrows for two coins (Luke 12:6).

Structure.

  • vv. 24–25: the disciple is like the teacher; if they malign the master, they will malign his household.
  • vv. 26–27: do not fear: what is hidden will be revealed; proclaim from the housetops what you hear in secret.
  • vv. 28–31: do not fear those who kill the body; the Father knows each sparrow and counts each hair; you are of more value.
  • vv. 32–33: whoever acknowledges Jesus before others, Jesus acknowledges before the Father; whoever denies him, he denies.

Key words.

  • Beelzeboul, Beelzebul: Perhaps “Baal the prince”, from the name of a pagan god (the origin of the name is debated); the Gospels use it for the prince of demons (12:24). The opponents of Jesus used it to belittle his power over evil spirits.
  • phobeisthe, you must fear: The verb appears with and without “not” in vv. 26–31. The Bible distinguishes the fear that paralyses from the reverent fear of God, which is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 1:7) and delivers us from every other fear.
  • homologeō, to acknowledge, to confess: Literally “to say the same thing”: to speak out for Jesus, as he speaks for us. The word later becomes the word for a confession of faith.

Verse by verse.

“A disciple is not above the teacher, nor a slave above the master;” (10:24)

Discipleship means sharing the master’s way, including its cost. The disciple is not exempt from what the Master meets.

“What I say to you in the dark, tell in the light; and what you hear whispered, proclaim from the housetops.” (10:27)

Jesus taught his disciples in private, but the teaching is for all. The flat roofs of Palestine were a natural place for a public announcement.

“Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell.” (10:28)

Most Fathers and commentators understand “him who can destroy” as God, the true judge (compare Luke 12:5). The sense is that nothing human is to frighten the disciple more than the loss of fellowship with God.

“Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father.” (10:29)

God’s care reaches the smallest of creatures. Not “without his knowledge” only, but within the knowledge and love of the Father.

“And even the hairs of your head are all counted.” (10:30)

A picture of the attention of a father’s love. There is nothing about you that is overlooked.

“So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” (10:31)

The argument from lesser to greater: if the Father cares for sparrows, how much more for his children.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes vv. 29–31 when it teaches about divine providence: Jesus asks for a childlike abandonment to the care of our heavenly Father, who looks after the smallest needs of his children (CCC 305). Verses 32–33 are the root of the Christian duty to confess the faith openly; the Catechism teaches that the Christian must not be ashamed to bear witness to the Lord (CCC 2471). The Church also teaches that the one who confesses Christ before others is acknowledged by Christ before the Father, and the one who denies him is denied; this is a call to fidelity and not a threat to the weak, for Peter who denied was forgiven (John 21:15–17).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus tell us to fear God, or not to fear?

Both, but in two different senses. We are never to fear in the sense of terror at a God who wants our harm: “do not be afraid” runs through the whole passage, and the Father numbers the hairs of our head. But the Bible keeps a holy fear of God, a reverent awe and a horror of losing him through sin, which is the beginning of wisdom. The Catechism teaches that the fear of the Lord is a gift of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1831).

For study and teaching

  • The brothers could not quite believe in Joseph’s forgiveness. Where do I find it hard to believe that God has really forgiven me?
  • Which of my fears is the Father’s word “you are of more value than many sparrows” speaking to today?
