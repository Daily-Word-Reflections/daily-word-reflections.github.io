Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, you will weep and mourn, but the world will rejoice; you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” John 16:20

Reflection

Paul has left Athens, where only a handful of people believed, and has come to Corinth. It was a very different city: a busy Roman colony on the narrow neck of land between two seas, with a harbour on each side, full of sailors, traders, slaves, soldiers and pilgrims to its temples. There Paul finds a Jewish couple, Aquila and Priscilla, “who had recently come from Italy … because Claudius had ordered all Jews to leave Rome.” The Roman historian Suetonius also mentions that expulsion, around the year 49. Paul goes to see them, “and, because he was of the same trade, he stayed with them.” They were tentmakers, workers in leather and cloth. The great apostle of the nations earns his bread with a needle and a knife in a workshop, and on the sabbath he goes to the synagogue “and would try to convince Jews and Greeks.” When Silas and Timothy arrive from Macedonia, perhaps bringing a gift from the Philippians, Paul is able to give himself entirely to “proclaiming the word, testifying to the Jews that the Messiah was Jesus.”

Not everyone welcomes the message. Some in the synagogue oppose Paul, and he makes a solemn gesture: he shakes out his clothes and declares that he is now going to the Gentiles. But notice where he goes: next door, to the house of Titius Justus, “a worshiper of God,” whose house stands right beside the synagogue. Paul does not walk away from his people in anger; he stays close. And the very next line tells us that “Crispus, the official of the synagogue, became a believer in the Lord, together with all his household,” and that “many of the Corinthians who heard Paul became believers and were baptized.” Years later Paul will remember that he baptized Crispus with his own hands (1 Corinthians 1:14). The reading is not a story of Jews against Christians: it is a story of one Jewish community in which some believed and some did not, and of a Jewish apostle, working beside Jewish friends, who opened the door of the Messiah to everyone. The Psalm sings the meaning of it: “All the ends of the earth have seen the victory of our God.”

The Gospel takes us back to the Last Supper. Jesus says something the disciples cannot understand: “A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.” They whisper among themselves: “What does he mean by this ‘a little while’? We do not know what he is talking about.” Jesus does not explain the timetable. He tells them what the waiting will feel like: “you will weep and mourn, but the world will rejoice; you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” He does not say that the pain will be replaced by joy, as if it were thrown away; he says it will turn into joy, as the cross itself became the sign of victory. The first “little while” was the three days of the Passion. But the Fathers heard a second meaning too: the whole time of the Church, between the Ascension and the Lord's return, is also “a little while.” Today, in Rome and in many places, the Church celebrates the Ascension, the day the disciples stopped seeing Jesus with their eyes. In India we shall celebrate it on Sunday. Either way, we are the people of the little while: we do not yet see him, and we are learning, in work and witness like Paul's, to let our pains be turned into joy.

Homily outline

Theme: “Your pain will turn into joy.”

Paul the tentmaker, a door kept open next to the synagogue, and the little while in which the Church waits for her Lord.

1. Because he was of the same trade

  • A Jewish couple expelled from Rome.
  • A workshop that became a chapel.
  • Work that does not burden others.
“The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it.” Genesis 2:15
“You remember our labor and toil, brothers and sisters; we worked night and day, so that we might not burden any of you while we proclaimed to you the gospel of God.” 1 Thessalonians 2:9

Apply: Before starting work each day this week, make the sign of the cross and offer the work to Christ by name.

2. Next door to the synagogue

  • Opposition and a solemn gesture.
  • Not walking away, but staying near.
  • Crispus and his household believe.
“I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” Isaiah 49:6
“Crispus, the official of the synagogue, became a believer in the Lord, together with all his household; and many of the Corinthians who heard Paul became believers and were baptized.” Acts 18:8

Apply: Pray every day this week for one person who has rejected your faith, and do them one quiet kindness.

3. A little while

  • The puzzled disciples.
  • Weeping while the world rejoices.
  • Pain that is turned, not thrown away.
“For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you.” Isaiah 54:7
“Very truly, I tell you, you will weep and mourn, but the world will rejoice; you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” John 16:20

Apply: Bring one present sorrow to the Lord in the Blessed Sacrament this week and leave it with him.

Full homily

Dear friends, when we think of St Paul we imagine a great preacher in a crowded city square. Today's reading shows us something else: a man sitting in a workshop in Corinth, stitching leather for tents. Because he was of the same trade, he stayed with Aquila and Priscilla, and they worked together (Acts 18:3).

Paul could have asked the Christians to support him. Often he refused. He wanted no one to think the Gospel was a business. So he worked with his hands by day and preached by night and on the sabbath. His workshop became a chapel, and his customers heard about Jesus while they waited for their tents.

Not everyone listened. Some in the synagogue opposed him. Paul shook out his clothes, a sign that he had done his part. But see where he went: to the house next door. He did not slam the door; he stayed close. And the official of the synagogue himself, Crispus, believed with his whole household (Acts 18:8). Never give up on anyone.

In the Gospel Jesus prepares his friends for a time of sorrow: a little while, and you will no longer see me. You will weep and mourn, but your pain will turn into joy (John 16:20). Not replaced by joy; turned into joy. God does not throw away our tears. He changes them, as he changed Good Friday into Easter.

Today, in many countries, Christians celebrate the Ascension, the day the apostles stopped seeing Jesus with their eyes. In India we will celebrate it on Sunday. But we are all living in that little while now: we do not see him, yet we believe, we work, we wait.

So take your work today, your kitchen, your field, your office, your studies, and offer it to Christ. Take your sorrows and give them to him too. In his time, every pain offered to him will turn into joy, a joy no one can take from you. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, St Paul had a job! He made tents with his friends Aquila and Priscilla. While he worked, he told people about Jesus.

Jesus said: you will be sad for a little while, but then your sadness will turn into joy. When you feel sad, tell Jesus. He can turn your sad face into a smiling face!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you said to your friends: a little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me. We live in that little while. When we weep and mourn, stay near us; when the world laughs at our faith, keep us steady; and turn our pain into joy, as you turned the cross into the tree of life. Bless the work of our hands, as you blessed Paul and Aquila and Priscilla in their workshop. Let our labour be a witness, our homes be open, and our hearts be patient until we see you face to face. You live and reign with the Father in the unity of the Holy Spirit, for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.
