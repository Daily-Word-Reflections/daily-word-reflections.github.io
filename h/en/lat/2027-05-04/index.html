Word for the day

“Nevertheless I tell you the truth: it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you.” John 16:7

Reflection

Paul and Silas have barely arrived in Philippi, the first city of Macedonia where the Gospel is preached, when trouble begins. They have driven a spirit of fortune-telling out of a slave girl, and her owners, who made money from her, drag them before the magistrates. The crowd joins in; the magistrates have them stripped and beaten with rods, and they are thrown into the innermost cell with their feet fastened in the stocks. Then comes one of the most beautiful sentences in the Acts of the Apostles: “About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them.” Bleeding, chained, in the dark, they sing. Suddenly an earthquake shakes the foundations, the doors fly open and everyone's chains are unfastened. The jailer wakes, sees the open doors, and draws his sword to kill himself, for a Roman jailer who lost his prisoners could pay with his life. “But Paul shouted in a loud voice, ‘Do not harm yourself, for we are all here.’” The prisoners have not fled. The jailer falls trembling before them and asks the question of every human heart: “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?” The answer is simple: “Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household.” That same night the jailer washes their wounds, and he and his family are baptized. He brings them into his house, sets food before them, and “he and his entire household rejoiced that he had become a believer in God.” The night that began with beatings ends with Baptism and a meal of joy.

The Psalm is the song of Paul and Silas at midnight: “I give you thanks, O Lord, with my whole heart.” “On the day I called, you answered me, you increased my strength of soul.” And even in the middle of trouble: “you stretch out your hand, and your right hand delivers me.” In the Gospel Jesus prepares the disciples for his going away. They are full of sorrow; they cannot imagine life without him. But he tells them something surprising: “it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you.” The Ascension is not a loss but the condition of a greater gift. While Jesus walked in Galilee he could be in only one place at a time; through the Holy Spirit he will be present in every disciple, in every prison cell and every home. And the Spirit will “prove the world wrong about sin and righteousness and judgment”: he will show that the real sin is refusing to believe in Jesus, that the one condemned on the cross was the righteous one now gone to the Father, and that the ruler of this world, not Jesus, has been judged. The same Spirit sang in Paul and Silas at midnight, shook the prison, and turned a jailer into a brother. In these days before the Ascension, the Church learns to trust that the Lord who goes away is not leaving us alone.

Full homily

Dear friends, picture the scene. Paul and Silas have been stripped and beaten with rods in Philippi. They are in the innermost cell, their feet locked in the stocks. It is dark, it is cold, every bone aches. And about midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them (Acts 16:25).

The other prisoners were listening. People are always listening to how Christians suffer. When we complain, curse and despair, they hear. When we pray and praise God in the middle of pain, they hear that too. A Christian singing at midnight is one of the strongest sermons in the world.

Then the earthquake, the open doors, the jailer with his sword. And Paul's cry: do not harm yourself, for we are all here (Acts 16:28). The prisoners could have run away; they stayed, and their staying saved a life. The jailer asks: what must I do to be saved? Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household (Acts 16:31). By morning there is a baptized family and a joyful meal.

In the Gospel Jesus says something hard: it is to your advantage that I go away (John 16:7). The disciples could not understand. How could losing him be good? But if he does not go, the Advocate will not come. In a few days we will celebrate his Ascension, and then Pentecost. He goes so that his Spirit can be with each of us, everywhere, even in a prison at midnight. Let us trust him in our losses, praise him in our darkness, and wait for the Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, Paul and Silas were in jail, with their feet locked up. Did they cry? No, they sang songs to God in the middle of the night! Then God sent an earthquake and the doors opened. The jailer was so amazed that he and his family came to know Jesus. When you are sad or scared, try singing a little song to Jesus. He hears you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you went away to the Father so that the Advocate might come to us. As Paul and Silas sang to you at midnight, teach us to praise you in our darkest hours. Stretch out your right hand and deliver all who are imprisoned, in body or in spirit. Send us your Holy Spirit, to convince the world of the truth and to make us messengers of salvation to everyone who asks, what must I do to be saved? Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

At Philippi Paul and Silas are stripped, beaten and fastened in the stocks, and at midnight they are singing hymns. An earthquake opens the doors, but the real liberation is the jailer’s: before morning he and his household are baptised and rejoicing at table. In the Gospel Jesus tells his grieving disciples that his going away is for their good, because the Advocate will come. Psalm 138 gives thanks to the Lord whose right hand delivers.

Acts 16:22–34 · What must I do to be saved?

Setting.

Philippi was a Roman colony in Macedonia, proud of its Roman law and its settled veterans. There Paul had baptised Lydia and her household (16:14–15). Then he freed a slave girl from a spirit of divination, and her owners, who had made money from her, dragged Paul and Silas before the magistrates (16:16–21). Today’s reading begins with the punishment. Later in the chapter Paul reveals that he and Silas are Roman citizens, who should never have been beaten without a trial (16:37).

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: beaten and thrown into the innermost cell.
  • vv. 25–26: hymns at midnight, and the earthquake.
  • vv. 27–30: the jailer’s despair and his question.
  • vv. 31–34: the word, the washing of wounds, baptism and the meal.

Key words.

  • sōzō, to save: The jailer’s question (v. 30) may at first have meant “How do I get out of this?” Paul answers it at its deepest level: salvation through faith in the Lord Jesus.
  • oikos, household: “You and your household” (v. 31). In the ancient world a household took in the family, the children and the servants. In Acts whole households come to faith together (16:15; 18:8).
  • hymneō, to sing hymns: Paul and Silas pray and sing (v. 25) with their backs torn and their feet in the stocks. Praise in the dark becomes a witness: the other prisoners were listening.

Verse by verse.

“About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them.” (16:25)

Their praise does not depend on their circumstances. Many prisoners for Christ since then, in many lands, have done the same.

“But Paul shouted in a loud voice, “Do not harm yourself, for we are all here.”” (16:28)

A guard who lost his prisoners could expect to pay with his own life. The apostles could have walked away. They stay, and by staying they save a life before they save a soul.

““Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household.”” (16:31)

Faith is the door. It does not stand against baptism: in the next verses the whole household hears the word of the Lord and is baptised (vv. 32–33).

“At the same hour of the night he took them and washed their wounds; then he and his entire family were baptized without delay.” (16:33)

Two washings in one hour: he washes their wounds, and he himself is washed in baptism. New faith shows itself at once in mercy.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to the household baptisms of Acts when it explains that infant baptism is an immemorial tradition of the Church: there is explicit testimony from the second century on, and it is quite possible that when whole households received baptism, infants were baptised too (CCC 1252). The jailer’s question, “What must I do to be saved?”, is the question the Church answers in every preparation for baptism: hear the word, believe, be baptised, and live in joy.

John 16:5–11 · It is to your advantage that I go away

Setting.

The farewell discourse continues. Jesus has warned the disciples that hard times are coming for them (16:1–4). Now he speaks of his going, and they are filled with sorrow. Earlier Peter and Thomas had asked where he was going (13:36; 14:5). Now no one asks; grief has silenced them.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–6: I am going, and sorrow fills your hearts.
  • v. 7: it is for your good; the Advocate will come.
  • vv. 8–11: the Spirit will prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment.

Key words.

  • paraklētos, Advocate: One called to stand beside another: a counsel for the defence, a helper, a comforter (v. 7). In the Gospels only John uses this title, always of the Spirit (14:16, 26; 15:26; 16:7); his first letter uses it of Jesus himself (1 John 2:1).
  • elenchō, to prove wrong, convince: A word of the law court: to expose, to bring to light, to convince (v. 8). In the trial between Jesus and the world, the world thought it had judged him. The Spirit shows that the verdict was the other way round.
  • sympherei, it is to your advantage: The same word Caiaphas used when he said that it was better for one man to die for the people (11:50). He spoke more truly than he knew. Jesus’ going brings good to all.

Verse by verse.

“But because I have said these things to you, sorrow has filled your hearts.” (16:6)

Jesus does not scold their sorrow. He names it, and then gives them a reason for hope.

“it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you;” (16:7)

In his earthly body he was in one place at a time. Through the Spirit he will be present everywhere, and within every disciple.

“about sin, because they do not believe in me;” (16:9)

For John the root sin is refusing the light that has come into the world (3:19). The Spirit brings it to light, not to crush, but so that the world may turn and believe.

How the Church reads it.

St John Paul II made this passage the centre of his encyclical on the Holy Spirit, Dominum et Vivificantem (1986), dwelling on the Spirit who convinces the world concerning sin. He teaches that the Spirit convinces of sin in order to save: the conviction opens the way to conversion and to the forgiveness that flows from the Cross (in substance). The Catechism, too, speaks of the Spirit who reveals sin and at the same time gives the grace of repentance (CCC 1433).

Questions people ask

How can it be better for the disciples that Jesus goes away?

He does not leave them alone. He goes to the Father and sends the Spirit, who makes him present in a new and deeper way: not beside them, but within them, and in the word and sacraments of the Church. The Ascension is not an absence but a new kind of presence.

For study and teaching

  • Paul and Silas sang at midnight. What helps me to pray when things are dark?
  • What sorrow in me might Jesus be turning toward the gift of his Spirit?
