Word for the day

“The kingdom of God is as if someone would scatter seed on the ground, and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how.” Mark 4:26–27

Reflection

The green Sundays have returned. After the great feasts of Easter, Pentecost, the Trinity and Corpus Christi, the Church puts on the colour of growing things, and today's readings are almost all about growth: a sprig of cedar, a palm tree, seed scattered on the ground, a mustard bush full of birds. The first reading comes from Ezekiel, a priest who was taken to Babylon with the first exiles in 597 before Christ. In chapter 17 he tells a riddle about two great eagles and a cedar of Lebanon: the kings of the nations break off the top of Israel's royal tree and carry it away. The house of David looks finished. Then God himself speaks: “Thus says the Lord God: I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out. I will break off a tender one from the topmost of its young twigs; I myself will plant it on a high and lofty mountain.” Notice the word “I myself,” repeated. What the great powers could only break, God alone can plant. And the tender sprig will become something no one expected: “On the mountain height of Israel I will plant it, in order that it may produce boughs and bear fruit, and become a noble cedar. Under it every kind of bird will live; in the shade of its branches will nest winged creatures of every kind.” Then comes the law of God's way of working: “I bring low the high tree, I make high the low tree; I dry up the green tree and make the dry tree flourish. I the Lord have spoken; I will accomplish it.” The Church has always heard in this tender shoot a promise of the Messiah from David's line, and in the birds of every kind the nations who will find shelter in his kingdom.

The Psalm takes up the same picture and gives it to every faithful person: “The righteous flourish like the palm tree, and grow like a cedar in Lebanon. They are planted in the house of the Lord; they flourish in the courts of our God. In old age they still produce fruit; they are always green and full of sap.” Here is a beautiful promise for the elderly in our parishes: age is not the end of fruitfulness. The grandmother who prays the rosary for her scattered children, the old man who still comes to the first Mass every morning, are “always green.” And the Psalm begins where all growth must begin, with thanksgiving: “It is good to give thanks to the Lord, to sing praises to your name, O Most High; to declare your steadfast love in the morning, and your faithfulness by night.”

St Paul, in the Second Letter to the Corinthians, speaks of a growth we cannot see: the hidden ripening of our lives towards God. “So we are always confident; even though we know that while we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord, for we walk by faith, not by sight.” Paul does not despise this life in the body, but he knows it is not the whole story: “we would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord.” So whatever happens, near or far, “we make it our aim to please him.” And then a sober line: “For all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive recompense for what has been done in the body, whether good or evil.” A seed is judged by its harvest. What we sow in the body, in our families, our work and our prayer, will one day be gathered in.

In the Gospel Jesus tells two short parables, found together only in Mark. The first is the seed that grows by itself: a farmer scatters seed, “and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how. The earth produces of itself, first the stalk, then the head, then the full grain in the head.” The farmer does his part: he sows, and at harvest he goes in “with his sickle.” But the growing is not his work. Every farmer in Kerala who has sown paddy with the first monsoon rains knows this patience: you cannot pull the shoots up to make them grow faster. The kingdom of God works like that in a parish, in a family, in a soul: quietly, by night as well as by day, by a power that is not ours. The second parable is the mustard seed, “the smallest of all the seeds on earth,” which grows into “the greatest of all shrubs, and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.” Jesus is echoing Ezekiel's cedar, but he chooses a humble garden plant instead of a mighty tree. The kingdom begins small: a carpenter from Nazareth, twelve fishermen and tax collectors, a few women who followed him, a handful of bread on an altar. Mark adds that Jesus spoke “the word to them, as they were able to hear it,” and explained everything in private to his disciples. God does not force growth; he sows, waits and explains, patiently, as we are able to hear.

Full homily

Dear friends, in these weeks of June the monsoon arrives in Kerala, and anyone who has lived in a village knows what happens after the first rains. The dry red earth turns green almost overnight. Seeds that lay hidden in the soil for months suddenly push up their small shoots. Nobody pulled them up; nobody told them to grow. Today's readings are full of this mystery of growth.

In the first reading Ezekiel speaks to a people in exile. Their king has been taken to Babylon, the royal tree of David seems cut down. And God says: I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out (Ezekiel 17:22). What the great empires broke, God replants. And the small, tender shoot becomes a noble cedar where every kind of bird finds shelter. God's way is always the same: I bring low the high tree, I make high the low tree (Ezekiel 17:24). It is the song of Mary in the Magnificat: he has brought down the powerful and lifted up the lowly.

In the Gospel Jesus tells of a farmer who scatters seed and then goes to sleep. He sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how (Mark 4:27). This is a great comfort for parents, teachers, catechists and priests. We sow, and often we see nothing. A child seems to forget everything we taught. A husband does not come back to the sacraments. A parish seems to sleep. Jesus says: do your part, and trust the seed. The power of growth is not in you; it is in the word of God.

Then he speaks of the mustard seed, the smallest of all the seeds on earth (Mark 4:31). The kingdom of God did not begin with armies or palaces. It began in a stable in Bethlehem, in a carpenter's house in Nazareth, with twelve ordinary men. It began, in our own land, with a few families on the coast who received the faith long ago. Today it is a great tree. Never despise small beginnings: a small prayer group, a small act of kindness, a small Sunday school class.

St Paul tells us what kind of life this growth asks of us: we walk by faith, not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7). We cannot see the growth, but we make it our aim to please him. And Paul reminds us that there will be a harvest: all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10). What we sow now, we will reap then.

Today is also the day of St Antony of Padua, though this year the Sunday takes its place. Many of you will go to his shrine or keep his Tuesday devotions. He was a great sower of the word, and he loved the poor. Let us ask his prayers, and let us go home today to sow one small seed: a word of faith, an act of forgiveness, a gift to someone in need. Then let us go to sleep in peace. God will give the growth. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever planted a seed? You put it in the soil, you water it, and then you wait. You cannot see anything happening! But one day a little green shoot comes up. Jesus says God's kingdom is like that. When you pray, when you share, when you say sorry, you are planting little seeds. You cannot see them grow, but God makes them grow.

Jesus also talked about the mustard seed. It is so tiny, smaller than a grain of rice! But it grows into a big plant, and birds come and make their nests in it. So never think: I am too small to do anything for Jesus. Small seeds become big trees. Today, plant one seed of love at home: help your mother, share your toys, or say a prayer for someone who is sad.

Prayer

Lord God, you take the tender sprig and plant it on the mountain, you bring low the high tree and make the low tree flourish. We thank you for the seed of your word sown in our hearts, and for the growth you give by night and by day, when we do not know how. Lord Jesus, your kingdom is like a mustard seed: make our small faith grow, so that our families and our parish may be a shelter for the poor, the lonely and the stranger. Holy Spirit, keep us green and full of sap even in old age, and help us to walk by faith, not by sight, making it our aim to please you, until we stand before the judgment seat of Christ and are gathered into the harvest of your kingdom. Mary, Mother of the Church, who kept the seed of the Word in your heart, pray for us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After the great feasts of Easter, Pentecost, the Trinity and Corpus Christi, the green Sundays of Ordinary Time return, and the readings of this Eleventh Sunday of Year B are about growth. In Ezekiel God himself takes a tender sprig from the top of a cedar and plants it on a high mountain, where it becomes a noble cedar with birds of every kind in its branches. In the Gospel Jesus tells two parables of the kingdom: the seed that grows while the farmer sleeps, and the mustard seed that becomes the greatest of shrubs, with birds nesting in its shade. Both readings say the same thing: God plants, God gives the growth, and what begins small becomes a shelter for many.

Psalm 92 gives the picture to every faithful person: “The righteous flourish like the palm tree, and grow like a cedar in Lebanon.” St Paul, in the Second Letter to the Corinthians, speaks of the growth we cannot yet see: “we walk by faith, not by sight”, until the harvest when all appear before the judgment seat of Christ. This year 13 June, the day of St Antony of Padua, falls on the Sunday, so his memorial gives way to the Lord’s Day.

Ezekiel 17:22–24 · I myself will plant it

Setting.

Ezekiel was a priest of Jerusalem who was taken to Babylon with King Jehoiachin and the first exiles in 597 before Christ. There, by the river Chebar, he received his call (1:1–3). For years he warned the exiles that Jerusalem would fall; after it fell in 587 he became the prophet of hope and restoration.

Background. Chapter 17 is a riddle or allegory. A great eagle breaks off the top of a cedar of Lebanon and carries it to a land of trade (vv. 3–4): this is Nebuchadnezzar taking King Jehoiachin, the top of the royal tree of David, to Babylon (v. 12). A vine planted in his place turns to another eagle, Egypt: this is King Zedekiah, who broke his oath and sought Egypt’s help (vv. 15–21). The house of David seems finished. Then, in our three verses, God speaks in his own name and promises to plant again.

Structure.

  • v. 22: “I myself” take a tender sprig from the top of the cedar and plant it on a high and lofty mountain.
  • v. 23: on the mountain height of Israel it becomes a noble cedar; birds of every kind live under it.
  • v. 24: all the trees of the field shall know the Lord, who brings low the high tree and makes the low tree high.
  • ¶ The passage answers the riddle. The eagles of the nations broke off the top of the cedar; only God can plant it again, and he does it with the most tender shoot.

Key words.

  • ʾerez, cedar: The cedar of Lebanon was the noblest tree of the ancient Near East; Solomon built the Temple and his palace with it (1 Kings 5:6). In the prophets it stands for kings and kingdoms, sometimes proud and cut down (Isaiah 2:13), here a sign of God’s own planting.
  • rak, tender: The young, soft shoot at the very top. God does not choose a strong branch. The royal hope will begin again from something weak and small, as Isaiah also says of the shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1).
  • wəlāqaḥtî ʾānî, and I myself will take: The Hebrew adds the pronoun “I” to the verb for emphasis, and repeats it in v. 22. What the great powers could only break, God alone will plant.

Verse by verse.

“I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out.” (17:22)

The sprig comes from the same cedar, the line of David. God does not start with a new family; he keeps his promise to David (2 Samuel 7:16).

“On the mountain height of Israel I will plant it,” (17:23)

The mountain is Zion, the place of the Temple. Ezekiel sees the restored kingdom bound up with the worship of God.

“Under it every kind of bird will live; in the shade of its branches will nest winged creatures of every kind.” (17:23)

In the Old Testament birds sheltering in a great tree are a picture of nations finding protection under a kingdom (Ezekiel 31:6; Daniel 4:12). God’s planting will be a home for many peoples.

“I bring low the high tree, I make high the low tree; I dry up the green tree and make the dry tree flourish.” (17:24)

This is the law of God’s way of acting, sung again by Hannah (1 Samuel 2:7–8) and by Mary in the Magnificat (Luke 1:52).

How the Church reads it.

The Church has read this tender sprig as a promise of the Messiah from David’s line. Jesus himself is the shoot that seemed small and weak, born in Bethlehem, raised in Nazareth, cut down on the Cross, and raised by God to be a home for all nations. The birds of every kind are the peoples who come to the Church. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church, the kingdom of Christ already present in mystery, grows visibly in the world through the power of God (Lumen Gentium 3).

2 Corinthians 5:6–10 · We walk by faith, not by sight

Setting.

Paul founded the church in Corinth, a busy port city of Greece, around the year 50, and wrote to it several times. The Second Letter to the Corinthians is the most personal of his letters. In chapters 4 and 5 he speaks of the treasure of the Gospel carried “in clay jars” (4:7), of the outer nature wasting away while the inner nature is renewed day by day (4:16), and of the earthly tent of the body that will be exchanged for a building from God (5:1).

Our passage follows. Paul is not afraid of death, but he does not despise life in the body either. He lives between the two, confident, and with one aim: to please the Lord.

Structure.

  • vv. 6–8: always confident: at home in the body we are away from the Lord; we walk by faith, not by sight; we would rather be at home with the Lord.
  • v. 9: whether at home or away, our aim is to please him.
  • v. 10: all must appear before the judgment seat of Christ.

Key words.

  • tharreō, to be confident: To be of good courage. Paul uses it twice (vv. 6, 8). His confidence does not come from his strength but from the Spirit, whom God has given as a guarantee (5:5).
  • eidos, sight: Literally what is seen, the visible form. We do not yet see the Lord face to face; we live by trust in his word (compare 1 Corinthians 13:12).
  • bēma, judgment seat: The raised platform from which a magistrate gave judgment. In the forum of Corinth Paul had himself stood before the bēma of the proconsul Gallio (Acts 18:12). Now he speaks of the judgment seat of Christ.

Verse by verse.

“while we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord” (5:6)

Paul uses the picture of people living in their own country or away from it. Life in the body is good, but it is not yet the homeland.

“for we walk by faith, not by sight.” (5:7)

“Walk” in the Bible means to live, to conduct one’s life. Faith is not a feeling for special moments but the way we move through every day.

“So whether we are at home or away, we make it our aim to please him.” (5:9)

Whether he lives or dies, Paul’s aim is the same. This releases the Christian from fear of death and from making comfort the goal of life.

“so that each may receive recompense for what has been done in the body, whether good or evil.” (5:10)

What we do in the body matters. Paul, the apostle of grace, still speaks of judgment according to deeds. Grace does not make our deeds unimportant; it makes them able to bear fruit for God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 7 when it teaches that faith is a foretaste of the vision we shall have: now “we walk by faith, not by sight” (CCC 164). It also quotes v. 8 on Christian death: to be away from the body and at home with the Lord (CCC 1005). The Church teaches that each person receives his eternal recompense in his immortal soul at the moment of death, in a particular judgment (CCC 1022), and that at the end Christ will judge the living and the dead (CCC 1038–1039). This is not meant to frighten but to give weight to each day, lived by grace, with the aim of pleasing the Lord.

Mark 4:26–34 · The seed grows, he does not know how

Setting.

Mark 4 is Mark’s collection of parables, told by the Sea of Galilee from a boat (4:1). It begins with the sower (4:1–20) and the lamp (4:21–25). Then come today’s two parables: the seed growing secretly, which only Mark records, and the mustard seed, which Matthew and Luke also tell.

Background. In Jesus’ ministry the kingdom looked very small: a teacher from Nazareth, a few fishermen and tax collectors, some women who followed him, crowds who came and went, and opposition growing (3:6). The parables answer the question: can this really be the kingdom of God?

Structure.

  • vv. 26–29: the seed growing by itself: the farmer sows, sleeps and rises; the earth produces of itself; at harvest he puts in the sickle.
  • vv. 30–32: the mustard seed: the smallest of seeds becomes the greatest of shrubs, and birds nest in its shade.
  • vv. 33–34: Jesus spoke the word in parables as they were able to hear, and explained everything to his disciples in private.

Key words.

  • automatē, of itself: The word from which “automatic” comes. It does not mean that the kingdom grows without God, but that it grows without the farmer’s control. The power of growth is in the seed and the soil that God has made.
  • kokkos sinapeōs, a mustard seed: Black mustard grows around the Lake of Galilee. Its seed is tiny, yet in one season the plant can grow taller than a man. “Small as a mustard seed” was a common saying for the smallest thing.
  • parabolē, parable: From a Greek word meaning to set side by side. A parable sets an everyday scene beside the kingdom and invites the hearer to think. Behind it stands the Hebrew māšāl, which covers proverbs, riddles and stories, like Ezekiel’s riddle of the cedar.

Verse by verse.

“and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how.” (4:27)

The farmer does his part, but the growth is not his work. Every farmer in Kerala who sows paddy with the monsoon knows this: you cannot pull the shoots up to make them grow faster.

“first the stalk, then the head, then the full grain in the head.” (4:28)

Growth has its stages and its time. The kingdom ripens patiently, in a soul, a family or a parish, by night as well as by day.

“at once he goes in with his sickle, because the harvest has come.” (4:29)

These words echo Joel 3:13, “Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe.” The harvest is God’s final judgment, which Paul also speaks of in the second reading.

“It is like a mustard seed, which, when sown upon the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth;” (4:31)

Jesus does not choose a mighty cedar like Ezekiel, but a humble garden plant. The kingdom begins in lowliness.

“so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.” (4:32)

His hearers would have caught the echo of Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4:12. The small beginning will become a shelter for the nations.

“With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it;” (4:33)

Jesus is patient with his hearers. He does not force the truth on them; he gives it as they can receive it.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council, quoting this chapter, teaches that the word of the Lord is compared to a seed sown in a field; those who hear it with faith and are numbered among the little flock of Christ have received the kingdom itself; then, by its own power, the seed sprouts and grows until harvest time (Lumen Gentium 5; CCC 764). The Catechism says that the kingdom is already present in mystery in the Church, its seed and beginning (CCC 669; Lumen Gentium 3, 5).

St Ambrose saw Christ himself in the mustard seed: small and despised when buried in the earth like a grain, but when he rose he grew into a tree whose branches shelter the whole world (in substance, Commentary on Luke). St Thérèse of Lisieux, who chose the “little way”, is a modern witness that God does great things with small seeds.

Questions people ask

Is the mustard seed really the smallest of all seeds?

Jesus speaks as a Galilean to Galileans, using a well-known saying about the smallest seed they sowed. There are smaller seeds in the world, such as orchid seeds, but the point of the parable is not botany. It is the contrast between a tiny beginning and a great result, which every listener could see in his own field.

If the seed grows “of itself”, does it matter what we do?

Yes. The farmer sows and the farmer reaps; without sowing there is no harvest. But he cannot make the seed grow. The parable guards us against two errors: laziness, which does nothing, and anxiety, which thinks everything depends on us. The Church teaches that God works in us and with us, and that our cooperation is itself his gift (CCC 2001, 2008).

What does “judgment seat of Christ” mean for a Christian who trusts in grace?

Paul knows that we are saved by grace (Ephesians 2:8), and he also says we will receive recompense for what we did in the body. The two belong together. Grace is not a reason to do nothing; it is the power that makes our works good. The Catechism teaches that our merits are themselves gifts of God’s goodness (CCC 2009, 2011). So we look to the judgment with hope, not with fear.

For the Latin reader

On the Sundays of Ordinary Time the Latin Church reads Mark’s Gospel in Year B, and today begins a series from Mark 4 and 5. This year the feast day of St Antony of Padua falls on the Sunday and is not celebrated at Mass. Antony was born in Lisbon about 1195, became a Franciscan, was a great preacher, and died near Padua on 13 June 1231. He was canonised within a year, and Pope Pius XII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1946. In many parishes in Kerala and across India, the Tuesday devotions, the novenas and the distribution of St Antony’s bread to the poor may go on outside the Mass. Antony himself was a tender sprig: a little-known friar whose preaching became, after his death, a shelter for countless people.

For study and teaching

  • What “seed” have I sown in my family or my work that I must now leave to God to grow?
  • Paul says “we walk by faith, not by sight”. In what situation this week do I most need to walk by faith?
  • Psalm 92 says the righteous “in old age still produce fruit”. How can our parish honour and use the gifts of its elderly?
