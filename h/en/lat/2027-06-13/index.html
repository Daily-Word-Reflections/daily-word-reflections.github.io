Word for the day

“The kingdom of God is as if someone would scatter seed on the ground, and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how.” Mark 4:26–27

Reflection

The green Sundays have returned. After the great feasts of Easter, Pentecost, the Trinity and Corpus Christi, the Church puts on the colour of growing things, and today's readings are almost all about growth: a sprig of cedar, a palm tree, seed scattered on the ground, a mustard bush full of birds. The first reading comes from Ezekiel, a priest who was taken to Babylon with the first exiles in 597 before Christ. In chapter 17 he tells a riddle about two great eagles and a cedar of Lebanon: the kings of the nations break off the top of Israel's royal tree and carry it away. The house of David looks finished. Then God himself speaks: “Thus says the Lord God: I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out. I will break off a tender one from the topmost of its young twigs; I myself will plant it on a high and lofty mountain.” Notice the word “I myself,” repeated. What the great powers could only break, God alone can plant. And the tender sprig will become something no one expected: “On the mountain height of Israel I will plant it, in order that it may produce boughs and bear fruit, and become a noble cedar. Under it every kind of bird will live; in the shade of its branches will nest winged creatures of every kind.” Then comes the law of God's way of working: “I bring low the high tree, I make high the low tree; I dry up the green tree and make the dry tree flourish. I the Lord have spoken; I will accomplish it.” The Church has always heard in this tender shoot a promise of the Messiah from David's line, and in the birds of every kind the nations who will find shelter in his kingdom.

The Psalm takes up the same picture and gives it to every faithful person: “The righteous flourish like the palm tree, and grow like a cedar in Lebanon. They are planted in the house of the Lord; they flourish in the courts of our God. In old age they still produce fruit; they are always green and full of sap.” Here is a beautiful promise for the elderly in our parishes: age is not the end of fruitfulness. The grandmother who prays the rosary for her scattered children, the old man who still comes to the first Mass every morning, are “always green.” And the Psalm begins where all growth must begin, with thanksgiving: “It is good to give thanks to the Lord, to sing praises to your name, O Most High; to declare your steadfast love in the morning, and your faithfulness by night.”

St Paul, in the Second Letter to the Corinthians, speaks of a growth we cannot see: the hidden ripening of our lives towards God. “So we are always confident; even though we know that while we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord, for we walk by faith, not by sight.” Paul does not despise this life in the body, but he knows it is not the whole story: “we would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord.” So whatever happens, near or far, “we make it our aim to please him.” And then a sober line: “For all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive recompense for what has been done in the body, whether good or evil.” A seed is judged by its harvest. What we sow in the body, in our families, our work and our prayer, will one day be gathered in.

In the Gospel Jesus tells two short parables, found together only in Mark. The first is the seed that grows by itself: a farmer scatters seed, “and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how. The earth produces of itself, first the stalk, then the head, then the full grain in the head.” The farmer does his part: he sows, and at harvest he goes in “with his sickle.” But the growing is not his work. Every farmer in Kerala who has sown paddy with the first monsoon rains knows this patience: you cannot pull the shoots up to make them grow faster. The kingdom of God works like that in a parish, in a family, in a soul: quietly, by night as well as by day, by a power that is not ours. The second parable is the mustard seed, “the smallest of all the seeds on earth,” which grows into “the greatest of all shrubs, and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.” Jesus is echoing Ezekiel's cedar, but he chooses a humble garden plant instead of a mighty tree. The kingdom begins small: a carpenter from Nazareth, twelve fishermen and tax collectors, a few women who followed him, a handful of bread on an altar. Mark adds that Jesus spoke “the word to them, as they were able to hear it,” and explained everything in private to his disciples. God does not force growth; he sows, waits and explains, patiently, as we are able to hear.

Homily outline

Theme: “The seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how.”

The cedar God himself replants, the seed that grows by itself, the smallest of all seeds, and the life of faith that waits for the harvest.

1. I myself will plant it

  • A broken royal tree.
  • What empires break, God replants.
  • The low tree made high.
“I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out. I will break off a tender one from the topmost of its young twigs; I myself will plant it on a high and lofty mountain.” Ezekiel 17:22
“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. So neither the one who plants nor the one who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth.” 1 Corinthians 3:6–7

Apply: Bring to God in prayer one part of your life that seems broken, and ask him to plant something new there.

2. He does not know how

  • Sowing and sleeping.
  • The earth produces of itself.
  • Patience with hidden growth.
“For as the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and do not return there until they have watered the earth, making it bring forth and sprout, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater, so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty,” Isaiah 55:10–11
“The kingdom of God is as if someone would scatter seed on the ground, and would sleep and rise night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how.” Mark 4:26–27

Apply: Keep praying for someone in whom you see no change, and thank God each day for the growth you cannot see.

3. The smallest of all the seeds

  • A garden plant, not a mighty cedar.
  • Small beginnings of the kingdom.
  • Shelter for every bird.
“For whoever has despised the day of small things shall rejoice,” Zechariah 4:10
“It is like a mustard seed, which, when sown upon the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth; yet when it is sown it grows up and becomes the greatest of all shrubs, and puts forth large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade.” Mark 4:31–32

Apply: Do one small thing for the kingdom this week that no one will notice: a visit, a gift, a hidden prayer.

4. We walk by faith

  • At home in the body, away from the Lord.
  • Our aim: to please him.
  • The harvest before the judgment seat.
“The righteous flourish like the palm tree, and grow like a cedar in Lebanon. They are planted in the house of the Lord; they flourish in the courts of our God. In old age they still produce fruit; they are always green and full of sap,” Psalm 92:12–14
“for we walk by faith, not by sight.” 2 Corinthians 5:7

Apply: Each night this week, before sleeping, ask: what did I sow today that will please the Lord at the harvest?

God plants, the seed grows by his power, and the kingdom begins small but shelters all.

Our part is to sow in faith, to wait in patience and to live so as to please the Lord until the harvest.

Take-home resolution: Sow one seed of faith or kindness each day this week, and leave the growth to God.

Full homily

Dear friends, in these weeks of June the monsoon arrives in Kerala, and anyone who has lived in a village knows what happens after the first rains. The dry red earth turns green almost overnight. Seeds that lay hidden in the soil for months suddenly push up their small shoots. Nobody pulled them up; nobody told them to grow. Today's readings are full of this mystery of growth.

In the first reading Ezekiel speaks to a people in exile. Their king has been taken to Babylon, the royal tree of David seems cut down. And God says: I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of a cedar; I will set it out (Ezekiel 17:22). What the great empires broke, God replants. And the small, tender shoot becomes a noble cedar where every kind of bird finds shelter. God's way is always the same: I bring low the high tree, I make high the low tree (Ezekiel 17:24). It is the song of Mary in the Magnificat: he has brought down the powerful and lifted up the lowly.

In the Gospel Jesus tells of a farmer who scatters seed and then goes to sleep. He sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how (Mark 4:27). This is a great comfort for parents, teachers, catechists and priests. We sow, and often we see nothing. A child seems to forget everything we taught. A husband does not come back to the sacraments. A parish seems to sleep. Jesus says: do your part, and trust the seed. The power of growth is not in you; it is in the word of God.

Then he speaks of the mustard seed, the smallest of all the seeds on earth (Mark 4:31). The kingdom of God did not begin with armies or palaces. It began in a stable in Bethlehem, in a carpenter's house in Nazareth, with twelve ordinary men. It began, in our own land, with a few families on the coast who received the faith long ago. Today it is a great tree. Never despise small beginnings: a small prayer group, a small act of kindness, a small Sunday school class.

St Paul tells us what kind of life this growth asks of us: we walk by faith, not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7). We cannot see the growth, but we make it our aim to please him. And Paul reminds us that there will be a harvest: all of us must appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10). What we sow now, we will reap then.

Today is also the day of St Antony of Padua, though this year the Sunday takes its place. Many of you will go to his shrine or keep his Tuesday devotions. He was a great sower of the word, and he loved the poor. Let us ask his prayers, and let us go home today to sow one small seed: a word of faith, an act of forgiveness, a gift to someone in need. Then let us go to sleep in peace. God will give the growth. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever planted a seed? You put it in the soil, you water it, and then you wait. You cannot see anything happening! But one day a little green shoot comes up. Jesus says God's kingdom is like that. When you pray, when you share, when you say sorry, you are planting little seeds. You cannot see them grow, but God makes them grow.

Jesus also talked about the mustard seed. It is so tiny, smaller than a grain of rice! But it grows into a big plant, and birds come and make their nests in it. So never think: I am too small to do anything for Jesus. Small seeds become big trees. Today, plant one seed of love at home: help your mother, share your toys, or say a prayer for someone who is sad.

Prayer

Lord God, you take the tender sprig and plant it on the mountain, you bring low the high tree and make the low tree flourish. We thank you for the seed of your word sown in our hearts, and for the growth you give by night and by day, when we do not know how. Lord Jesus, your kingdom is like a mustard seed: make our small faith grow, so that our families and our parish may be a shelter for the poor, the lonely and the stranger. Holy Spirit, keep us green and full of sap even in old age, and help us to walk by faith, not by sight, making it our aim to please you, until we stand before the judgment seat of Christ and are gathered into the harvest of your kingdom. Mary, Mother of the Church, who kept the seed of the Word in your heart, pray for us. Amen.
