Word for the day

“I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” Isaiah 49:6

Reflection

Today the second Song of the Servant lets the Servant himself speak. “The LORD called me before I was born, while I was in my mother's womb he named me.” God has shaped him like a sharp sword and a polished arrow, hidden in his quiver until the right moment. Yet the Servant also knows the taste of failure: “I have labored in vain, I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity; yet surely my cause is with the LORD.” God's answer is astonishing. Instead of reducing the mission, he enlarges it: “It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob… I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” On Tuesday of Holy Week we hear this as the prayer of Jesus. To human eyes his work in Jerusalem is collapsing; one of the Twelve is about to sell him, another will deny him, the rest will run. Yet through that apparent failure the light will reach the whole world, even to the shores of India. The Psalm is the prayer of one who has trusted God from the womb: “Upon you I have leaned from my birth.”

The Gospel takes us into the Upper Room. Jesus has just washed the feet of the disciples, and now he is “troubled in spirit” and says: “Very truly, I tell you, one of you will betray me.” The disciples look at one another. The beloved disciple, reclining next to Jesus, asks: “Lord, who is it?” Jesus dips a piece of bread and gives it to Judas. In the East, to give a morsel from the dish to a guest was a sign of honour and friendship. Even at this moment Jesus offers Judas love. But Judas takes the bread and goes out. St John adds four words that fall like a stone: “And it was night.” Not only the night outside, but the night of a heart that refuses the light. And yet Jesus says: “Now the Son of Man has been glorified.” The hour of betrayal is also the hour of glory, because love is about to be shown to the end.

Then Peter speaks with all his warm, impulsive heart: “Lord, why can I not follow you now? I will lay down my life for you.” Jesus answers gently and truly: “Will you lay down your life for me? Very truly, I tell you, before the cock crows, you will have denied me three times.” Two disciples fail the Lord on the same night. The difference between them is not the size of their sin, but what they do afterwards. Judas goes out into the night and despairs. Peter will meet the gaze of Jesus, weep bitterly, and return (Luke 22:61–62). Jesus had said, “Where I am going, you cannot follow me now; but you will follow afterward”; and Peter did follow, to Rome and to his own cross. Holy Week is the time to be honest about our betrayals and denials, and to choose Peter's tears instead of Judas's night. No one is too far gone for the mercy of the One who gave the morsel even to his betrayer.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's first reading the Servant speaks: the LORD called me before I was born, while I was in my mother's womb he named me (Isaiah 49:1). And then he admits: I have labored in vain, I have spent my strength for nothing (Isaiah 49:4). But God answers: it is too small a thing to restore only Israel; I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth (Isaiah 49:6).

In the Upper Room this light seems to be going out. Jesus is troubled in spirit and says: one of you will betray me. He gives a piece of bread, a sign of friendship, to Judas. Judas takes it and goes out. And it was night (John 13:30).

Then Peter says: I will lay down my life for you. Jesus answers: before the cock crows, you will have denied me three times (John 13:38). Two apostles fail him that night. Judas despairs; Peter weeps and returns. The difference is not that Peter's sin was small, but that Peter trusted the mercy of Jesus.

This Holy Week, do not go out into the night. Whatever you have done, come back to the light. Go to confession. Let Jesus look at you as he looked at Peter. From that weak, forgiven fisherman the light of Christ reached to the end of the earth, and it reaches you today. Amen.

For children

Children, Peter said he would never leave Jesus, but he got scared and said he did not know him. Then he was very sorry and cried, and Jesus forgave him and made him the leader of the apostles! When we do wrong, let us say sorry quickly and come back to Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you were called from the womb to be a light to the nations, and in the night of betrayal you went on loving to the end. When we fail you, do not let us go out into the night like Judas, but look at us as you looked at Peter, so that we may weep, return and follow you. Make your salvation reach to the ends of the earth. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s two readings are both about a calling that seems to have failed. The Servant cries, “I have labored in vain”, and God answers by making his mission wider. At the supper Jesus is troubled, Judas goes out into the night, and Peter promises more than he can give. Yet at that very moment Jesus says, “Now the Son of Man has been glorified”. Psalm 71 prays from the womb onward, as the Servant does: “Upon you I have leaned from my birth”.

Isaiah 49:1–6 · A light to the nations

Setting.

This is the second Servant Song. In the first, God spoke about the Servant. Now the Servant speaks for himself, and he addresses the coastlands and peoples far away. In Babylon the exiles felt forgotten (49:14). The song answers that weariness from within: even God’s chosen one has felt he has achieved nothing.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the call from the womb; mouth like a sword; “You are my servant”.
  • v. 4: the Servant’s discouragement and his trust.
  • vv. 5–6: God’s answer: the mission grows to the end of the earth.

Key words.

  • ḥēṣ bārûr, a polished arrow: A polished arrow is kept hidden in the quiver until the moment it is needed. Long hiddenness is part of the Servant’s preparation, as it was in the thirty years at Nazareth.
  • tōhû, nothing, emptiness: In v. 4 the Servant says he has spent his strength for tōhû, the word used for the formless earth in Genesis 1:2. The word beside it, hebel, is the “vanity” of Ecclesiastes. He holds nothing back from God about how it feels.
  • ’ôr gôyim, light of the nations: See also 42:6. The Latin form of this phrase gives the Second Vatican Council’s Constitution on the Church its opening words, Lumen gentium, and the Council says at once that the light is Christ.

Verse by verse.

“The Lord called me before I was born, while I was in my mother’s womb he named me.” (49:1)

St Paul describes his own call in these words (Galatians 1:15; compare Jeremiah 1:5). A vocation begins earlier than any human decision.

““You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified.”” (49:3)

“Israel” stands in nearly all manuscripts, yet in vv. 5–6 this servant has a mission to Israel. Here is the song’s riddle: the Servant both embodies the people and serves them. Christians see it resolved in Jesus, the faithful Israelite who gathers Israel and the nations. The people of Israel remain God’s own, whose gifts and calling are not withdrawn (Romans 11:29).

“I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” (49:6)

God answers a complaint of failure not by making the work smaller but by making it larger.

How the Church reads it.

Old Simeon, holding the child, calls him “a light for revelation to the Gentiles” (Luke 2:32). Paul and Barnabas take 49:6 as their own charter in Antioch of Pisidia (Acts 13:47). So the song belongs first to Christ, and then to those who share his mission. The Catechism lists it among the Songs that proclaim the meaning of the Passion (CCC 713). Those who feel their work has come to nothing can pray v. 4 honestly, including its second half: “yet surely my cause is with the Lord”.

John 13:21–33, 36–38 · And it was night

Setting.

We are at the supper, after the washing of the feet (13:1–20). John’s reader already knows the devil’s work in Judas (13:2). The Mass reading passes over vv. 34–35, the new commandment. The verses left in show three disciples around Jesus: the one who betrays, the one who rests close to him, and the one who boasts and will fall.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–26: “One of you will betray me”; the disciple Jesus loved asks who; the piece of bread.
  • vv. 27–30: Satan enters; Judas goes out; night.
  • vv. 31–33: “Now the Son of Man has been glorified”; “Little children”.
  • vv. 36–38: Peter’s promise and the warning about the cock.

Key words.

  • etarachthē, he was troubled: The same verb is used at the tomb of Lazarus (11:33) and when his hour comes near (12:27). The Jesus who goes so firmly to death in John is not untouched by what is coming. Betrayal by a friend hurts him.
  • psōmion, piece of bread: A morsel dipped in the shared dish. Many commentators think that to be offered it by the host was a mark of friendship. If so, the last thing Jesus does for Judas is to hold out his hand to him.
  • doxazō, glorify: In John the glory of Jesus is not reached after the cross as though it were a mere reward. The cross is itself the place where God’s love is shown in full, and so where he is glorified.

Verse by verse.

“the one whom Jesus loved…was reclining next to him;” (13:23)

Greek has “on the bosom of Jesus”, the word used of the Son who is “close to the Father’s heart” (1:18). As the Son rests in the Father, so this disciple rests in the Son. The Gospel does not name him; ancient tradition sees him as John the son of Zebedee, while some modern scholars keep the question open.

“So, after receiving the piece of bread, he immediately went out. And it was night.” (13:30)

A time of day and a state of soul. Jesus has said that whoever walks at night stumbles (11:10). St Augustine remarks that Judas was not harmed by something evil in the bread: he took a good thing badly (in substance).

““Lord, why can I not follow you now? I will lay down my life for you.”” (13:37)

Peter uses the very words of the Good Shepherd (10:11). He means them sincerely, but he has the order reversed: Jesus lays down his life for Peter first. After the Resurrection Peter will be called three times to love and will truly follow (21:15–19).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the two failures of this night together. Judas and Peter both fail their Lord. The difference shows itself after the fall, in Peter’s return. No one should say of any person that he is beyond mercy. The words “It was night” are followed at once by “Now the Son of Man has been glorified”: the darkest deed of the week is taken up into the plan of love.

Questions people ask

If Jesus foretold the betrayal, did Judas have any choice?

Yes. To know beforehand is not to force. The Catechism says that God sees all moments of time in his eternal present, so his plan includes each person’s response, and he permits the acts that flow from human blindness in order to bring about his plan of salvation (CCC 600). Judas acted, and answered for it.

For study and teaching

  • Where have I felt, like the Servant, that I “labored in vain”? Can I also say the second half of his verse?
  • Where am I closest to Peter this week, promising much out of love but relying on my own strength?
