Word for the day

“for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith. And a great many people were brought to the Lord.” Acts 11:24

Reflection

The Acts of the Apostles first introduces Barnabas as a generous man: a Levite from Cyprus named Joseph, “to whom the apostles gave the name Barnabas (which means ‘son of encouragement’),” who sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet (Acts 4:36–37). The name fits him perfectly. When Saul, the former persecutor, came to Jerusalem and the disciples were all afraid of him, it was Barnabas who “took him, brought him to the apostles,” and vouched for him (Acts 9:27). Today's reading shows him at his best. After the persecution that followed Stephen's death, believers from Cyprus and Cyrene went to Antioch, the great city of Syria, and began to preach the Lord Jesus to Greeks as well as Jews. “The hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number became believers and turned to the Lord.” The church in Jerusalem sent Barnabas to look into this new thing. He did not come with suspicion or a list of rules: “When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced, and he exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast devotion; for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” Then, seeing that the work was too big for him, he did something humble and wise: “Barnabas went to Tarsus to look for Saul, and when he had found him, he brought him to Antioch.” For a whole year the two taught together, “and it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called ‘Christians.’” The second part of the reading shows the Church of Antioch at prayer: “While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.’ Then after fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off.” The great missionary journeys of the Church begin with worship, fasting and the laying on of hands. The Psalm is the song of that mission: “All the ends of the earth have seen the victory of our God.”

The Gospel is part of the instructions Jesus gave the Twelve when he sent them out for the first time. “As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’ Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons.” And then a principle for every missionary: “You received without payment; give without payment.” The apostle travels light, with no gold or silver or copper in his belt, trusting that “laborers deserve their food.” He brings peace into every house he enters: “If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it.” Barnabas lived this. He gave away his field before he gave away his life. He was big enough to step aside so that Saul, the more gifted preacher, could shine; Acts soon begins to say “Paul and Barnabas” instead of “Barnabas and Saul.” And when Paul refused to take John Mark on a second journey because the young man had left them once before, Barnabas, his cousin, took Mark with him to Cyprus and gave him a second chance (Acts 15:37–39). Years later Paul himself wrote that Mark was useful to him in his ministry (2 Timothy 4:11). The son of encouragement had been right. Tradition says that Barnabas preached in Cyprus and died a martyr at Salamis; the Church of Cyprus honours him as its founder. Every parish needs a Barnabas: someone who sees the grace of God in others and rejoices, who brings the newcomer in, who gives the one who failed a second chance.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate St Barnabas. His real name was Joseph, but the apostles gave him a nickname: Barnabas, son of encouragement (Acts 4:36). Imagine being known in the whole Church for one thing: you make people stronger. What a beautiful reputation!

Look at what he did at Antioch. Something new was happening there: Greeks, not only Jews, were coming to believe in Jesus. Some people in Jerusalem may have been worried. Barnabas came, and when he saw the grace of God, he rejoiced (Acts 11:23). He did not look first for problems. He looked first for grace. Then he encouraged them to remain faithful. That is the first lesson of Barnabas: look for the good God is doing in others, and rejoice in it.

The second lesson is humility. The work at Antioch grew too big for Barnabas, so he went to Tarsus to look for Saul and brought him to Antioch (Acts 11:25–26). He knew Saul was a brilliant teacher, maybe more gifted than himself. He did not mind. Soon everyone was speaking of Paul and Barnabas, not Barnabas and Paul. Later, when Paul did not want to take young Mark on a journey because Mark had once left them, Barnabas gave Mark a second chance. That Mark became a great helper of the Church.

In the Gospel Jesus says to his apostles: you received without payment; give without payment (Matthew 10:8). Barnabas gave his field, his time, his trust, and finally, tradition says, his life in Cyprus. Today let us ask him for a heart like his. Let us look for the grace of God in others, make room for their gifts, give second chances, and be sons and daughters of encouragement. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what an encourager is? It is someone who says: you can do it! Don't give up! St Barnabas was the best encourager in the Bible. When nobody trusted Saul, Barnabas trusted him, and Saul became St Paul. When young Mark made a mistake, Barnabas gave him another chance, and Mark wrote a Gospel! Today, say an encouraging word to a friend. You might be helping a future saint!

Prayer

Lord God, you chose St Barnabas, a good man full of the Holy Spirit and of faith, to strengthen your Church and to carry the Gospel to the nations. Give us his eyes, to see your grace in others and rejoice; his humility, to make room for gifts greater than our own; and his generous heart, to give without payment what we have received without payment. Bless all missionaries, especially those sent from India to other lands, and make us all sons and daughters of encouragement. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church honours St Barnabas, whom the Acts of the Apostles calls “a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith”. The first reading tells how the church in Jerusalem sent him to Antioch, how he rejoiced to see God’s grace among the Gentile believers, brought Saul from Tarsus, and was finally sent out with him on mission. The Gospel gives Jesus’ instructions to the Twelve as he sends them out: proclaim the kingdom, heal, and give as you have received. Psalm 98 sings that the Lord has revealed his saving power to the nations.

Acts 11:21b–26; 13:1–3 · A good man, full of the Holy Spirit

Setting.

Barnabas first appears in Acts 4:36–37. His name was Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus; the apostles called him Barnabas, which Luke explains as “son of encouragement”. He sold a field and laid the money at the apostles’ feet. Later, when the disciples in Jerusalem were afraid of the newly converted Saul, it was Barnabas who took him and brought him to the apostles (9:27).

After the death of Stephen, believers scattered by persecution came to Antioch in Syria, one of the great cities of the Roman Empire. Some of them, men of Cyprus and Cyrene, began to preach the Lord Jesus to Greeks as well (11:20). This was something new, and the church in Jerusalem sent Barnabas to see. Two chapters later, the church of Antioch sends Barnabas and Saul on what is usually called Paul’s first missionary journey, beginning with Cyprus, Barnabas’ homeland.

Structure.

  • 11:21b–24: many Gentiles turn to the Lord; Barnabas is sent from Jerusalem, rejoices and encourages them.
  • 11:25–26: Barnabas brings Saul from Tarsus; a year of teaching; the disciples are first called Christians at Antioch.
  • 13:1–3: prophets and teachers at Antioch; the Holy Spirit sets apart Barnabas and Saul; fasting, prayer, laying on of hands, sending.

Key words.

  • parakaleō, to exhort, encourage: Barnabas “exhorted them all” (11:23). Luke’s verb echoes the meaning he gives to Barnabas’ name, “son of encouragement” (4:36). He lived up to his name.
  • Christianoi, Christians: The word is formed like a Latin name for the followers of a leader: “Christ’s people”. Many scholars think it was first given by outsiders in Antioch. The believers came to bear it without shame (1 Peter 4:16).
  • leitourgountōn, while they were worshiping: The Greek word gives us “liturgy”. The great decision to begin the mission to the nations was taken during worship and fasting, in obedience to the Holy Spirit.

Verse by verse.

“When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced, and he exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast devotion;” (11:23)

Barnabas could have come to inspect and to find fault. Instead he saw grace and rejoiced. That is the first gift of a good pastor.

“for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” (11:24)

A rare and simple praise in Acts. Goodness, the Spirit and faith go together; his goodness was the fruit of the Spirit.

“Then Barnabas went to Tarsus to look for Saul,” (11:25)

Tarsus was about two hundred kilometres away by land and sea. Barnabas went himself to find the man others had feared. Without Barnabas, Paul might have stayed unknown.

“it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called “Christians.”” (11:26)

A mixed community of Jewish and Gentile believers is the first to bear the name of Christ.

“Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.” (13:2)

The Holy Spirit chooses, the community confirms. Barnabas is named first; later in Acts the order becomes “Paul and Barnabas”, and Barnabas does not seem to mind.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours Barnabas as an apostle, though he was not one of the Twelve, because Luke himself calls him and Paul apostles (Acts 14:14), and because he was sent by the Spirit and the Church to found churches. Tradition holds that he died as a martyr in Cyprus, at Salamis. The laying on of hands with prayer at Antioch is not described as an ordination in the later sense, but the Church sees in it the pattern of every sending: the Spirit calls, the community prays and fasts, and hands are laid on those who are sent (compare CCC 699 on the laying on of hands as a sign of the Spirit). Barnabas also teaches the virtue of encouragement: to see grace in others before seeing their faults, and to give a second chance, as he did again later with his cousin John Mark (Acts 15:37–39; Colossians 4:10).

Matthew 10:7–13 · Give as you have received

Setting.

Chapter 10 is the second of the five great discourses in Matthew, the missionary discourse. Jesus has just called the Twelve by name (10:1–4) and now sends them out to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. After the resurrection, the mission will open to all nations (28:19). The Church reads this Gospel on the feast of Barnabas because he lived exactly this way: poor, generous and on the move.

Structure.

  • v. 7: proclaim that the kingdom of heaven has come near.
  • v. 8: heal, raise, cleanse, cast out; you received without payment, give without payment.
  • vv. 9–10: take no money, bag, spare tunic, sandals or staff; the labourer deserves his food.
  • vv. 11–13: stay with a worthy household; greet the house; your peace will rest on it or return to you.

Key words.

  • ēngiken, has come near: The same message John the Baptist and Jesus proclaimed (3:2; 4:17). The apostles do not bring a new message of their own; they carry Jesus’ message.
  • dōrean, without payment, as a gift: What the apostles received from Christ was a gift; it must be handed on as a gift. From this verse the Church learned to condemn the buying and selling of spiritual things.
  • eirēnē, peace: Behind it stands the Hebrew shalom, the ordinary greeting, which means wholeness and well-being from God. The apostles’ greeting carries real power: it rests on the house or returns to them.

Verse by verse.

“As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’” (10:7)

Proclaiming happens “as you go”, along the way. Mission is not only an event; it is a way of life.

“You received without payment; give without payment.” (10:8)

The rule of every ministry in the Church. Grace cannot be sold.

“for laborers deserve their food.” (10:10)

Giving without payment does not mean the community has no duty to support those who serve it. The same principle appears in 1 Corinthians 9:14 and 1 Timothy 5:18.

“If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you.” (10:13)

The messenger’s peace is never wasted. If it is refused, it returns to the one who gave it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism defines simony, the buying or selling of spiritual things, and quotes this Gospel against it: “You received without pay, give without pay” (CCC 2121). It adds that the minister should ask nothing for the sacraments beyond the offerings set by the competent authority, since the labourer deserves his food (CCC 2122). Barnabas, who sold his field and gave the money to the apostles, shows what this looks like in a life.

Questions people ask

Did Barnabas write the “Letter of Barnabas”?

There is an early Christian writing called the Letter of Barnabas, but almost all scholars agree that it was not written by the Barnabas of Acts. It dates from about the end of the first century or early second, and the name was attached to it later. The true Barnabas is known to us from the Acts of the Apostles and the letters of Paul.

For the Latin reader

The memorial of St Barnabas is kept in the Roman calendar on 11 June, with proper readings. Barnabas reminds the Church that the mission to the nations began in a mixed, joyful community at Antioch, through a man who knew how to encourage. Parishes can honour him by thanking catechists, sponsors and mentors who, like Barnabas, have taken someone by the hand and brought them into the life of the Church.

For study and teaching

  • Whom could I encourage this week, especially someone others have given up on?
  • What have I received “without payment” from God that I can give in the same way?
