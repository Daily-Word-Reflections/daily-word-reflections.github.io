Word for the day

“Jesus answered him, “Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.”” John 14:23

Reflection

Paul and Barnabas are on their first missionary journey, in the hill country of Lycaonia in the middle of what is now Turkey. Threatened in Iconium, they move on to Lystra, and there they meet a man “who could not use his feet and had never walked, for he had been crippled from birth.” He listens to Paul preaching, and Paul, “looking at him intently and seeing that he had faith to be healed,” calls out: “Stand upright on your feet.” The man springs up and walks. Then something unexpected happens. The crowd shouts in their own language, “The gods have come down to us in human form!” They call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes, “because he was the chief speaker,” and the priest of Zeus arrives with oxen and garlands to offer sacrifice. The people of that region told an old story of Zeus and Hermes once visiting in disguise and going unrecognised; perhaps they did not want to make that mistake again. The apostles tear their clothes and rush into the crowd: “Friends, why are you doing this? We are mortals just like you, and we bring you good news, that you should turn from these worthless things to the living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them.” Paul's first sermon to pure pagans does not begin with the Law of Moses but with creation: God “has not left himself without a witness in doing good,” for he gives “rains from heaven and fruitful seasons,” filling people “with food and your hearts with joy.” The Psalm gives the apostles' answer in one line: “Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory.”

In the Gospel we are back at the Last Supper. Jesus has promised to show himself to those who love him, and Judas, “not Iscariot,” asks a fair question: “Lord, how is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world?” Jesus does not answer with a spectacle. He answers with a dwelling: “Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” The people of Lystra wanted gods who would come down for a visit and be honoured with oxen. The true God does far more: the Father and the Son come to stay, in the heart of the one who loves and obeys. And they do not leave us to remember everything by our own strength: “the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you.” St Augustine, looking back on the years he searched for God outside himself, wrote (in substance): you were within me, and I was outside, looking for you there. In these last weeks before Pentecost the Church asks us to look within, where the Holy Trinity has made a home.

Apostles who refuse worship, a Creator who never leaves himself without witness, and a God who comes to stay.

Full homily

Dear friends, at Lystra a man crippled from birth listens to Paul, and Paul sees that he has faith to be healed. Stand upright on your feet! The man springs up and walks (Acts 14:10). And the crowd goes wild: the gods have come down to us in human form! They bring oxen and garlands to worship Paul and Barnabas.

This was a great temptation. Who would not enjoy being treated as a god? But the apostles tear their clothes and run into the crowd: we are mortals just like you (Acts 14:15). They point away from themselves to the living God, who sends rain from heaven and fruitful seasons and fills hearts with joy. The best witness is a finger that points beyond itself.

In the Gospel Jesus tells us what the true God really does. Not a short visit to be honoured with sacrifices, but a dwelling: those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them (John 14:23). The Father and the Son make their home in an ordinary heart that loves and obeys.

And we are not left alone to remember everything. The Holy Spirit, the Advocate, will teach you everything and remind you of all that I have said (John 14:26). In these days of Easter, as Pentecost comes near, let us give every glory back to God, and keep a quiet room in our hearts for the Guest who has come to stay. Amen.

For children

Children, Paul healed a man who had never walked, and the people thought Paul was a god! But Paul said: no, I am just a person like you. Thank God! Jesus says something wonderful: if you love me, my Father and I will come and live in your heart. So your heart can be God's little house. Keep it clean and happy for him!

Prayer

Living God, maker of heaven and earth and sea, you have never left yourself without a witness: you send the rain, you ripen the harvest, you fill our hearts with joy. Not to us, Lord, but to your name be the glory. Lord Jesus, risen from the dead, come with the Father and make your home in us; and send us the Holy Spirit, the Advocate, to teach us everything and remind us of all you have said. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

At Lystra a crowd takes two missionaries for gods, and Paul and Barnabas rush in among the people to stop the sacrifice. In the Gospel Jesus promises that the Father and he will come and make their home with those who love him, and that the Holy Spirit will teach and remind. Psalm 115 answers both: glory belongs not to us but to the Lord’s name, and the idols of silver and gold are only the work of human hands.

Acts 14:5–18 · We are mortals just like you

Setting.

This is the first missionary journey of Paul and Barnabas (chapters 13–14), sent out by the church of Antioch in Syria (13:1–3). After Pisidian Antioch and Iconium, a threat of stoning drives them on to Lystra and Derbe, towns of Lycaonia in the south of present-day Turkey. Augustus had made Lystra a Roman colony, but the ordinary people spoke their own Lycaonian language.

Background. The Roman poet Ovid tells an old tale of Jupiter and Mercury (Zeus and Hermes, in Greek) visiting the neighbouring region of Phrygia in human form, unrecognised by everyone except one old couple. Many commentators think a local story of this kind explains why the crowd was so quick to see gods in the two strangers.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: the plot at Iconium and the flight to Lystra and Derbe.
  • vv. 8–10: a man lame from birth is healed.
  • vv. 11–13: the crowd cries out that the gods have come; the priest of Zeus prepares a sacrifice.
  • vv. 14–18: Barnabas and Paul tear their clothes and speak of the living God.

Key words.

  • Lykaonisti, in the Lycaonian language: The crowd shouts in its local tongue, which Paul and Barnabas probably did not understand. This may be why they react only when the oxen and garlands arrive (v. 14).
  • mataia, worthless things: Empty, vain things. The Greek Old Testament uses this kind of word for idols. Paul calls the people away from what cannot give life to the God who does.
  • homoiopatheis, of like nature: “Mortals just like you” (v. 15): people with the same weakness. James 5:17 uses the same word of Elijah. The apostles will not let a miracle make them more than human.

Verse by verse.

“And Paul, looking at him intently and seeing that he had faith to be healed,” (14:9)

Luke tells this healing much like Peter’s healing of the man lame from birth at the Temple gate, where Peter too “looked intently” at him (3:4). The same Lord works through both apostles.

““Stand upright on your feet.” And the man sprang up and began to walk.” (14:10)

No name is invoked aloud here, but the sermon he had just heard was about Jesus. Faith came by hearing, and healing followed.

“We are mortals just like you, and we bring you good news, that you should turn from these worthless things to the living God,” (14:15)

Tearing one’s clothes was a Jewish sign of horror at blasphemy. The greatest danger for a preacher is to be worshipped. Paul and Barnabas turn every eye away from themselves.

“yet he has not left himself without a witness in doing good” (14:17)

To people who did not know the Scriptures, Paul begins with rain, harvest, food and joy. Creation itself speaks of the Creator.

How the Church reads it.

This is the first speech in Acts to a purely pagan audience, and it begins not from the prophets but from creation, as Paul will do again in Athens (17:22–31). The Church teaches that God can be known with certainty from the created world by the natural light of human reason (CCC 36, from the First Vatican Council). She rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions (Nostra Aetate 2), and like Paul and Barnabas she will never let a creature, however gifted, take the place of the living God.

John 14:21–26 · We will make our home with them

Setting.

We are at the Last Supper. One after another the disciples interrupt with questions: Peter (13:36), Thomas (14:5), Philip (14:8), and now Judas, “not Iscariot”. Luke calls him Judas son of James (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13), and tradition identifies him with Thaddeus (Mark 3:18). The Church honours him with St Simon on 28 October.

Structure.

  • v. 21: love is proved by keeping his commandments, and he will reveal himself.
  • v. 22: Judas asks why to them and not to the world.
  • vv. 23–24: the answer: the Father and the Son will come and dwell.
  • vv. 25–26: the Advocate will teach and remind.

Key words.

  • monē, home, dwelling place: The same word as the “dwelling places” of the Father’s house in 14:2. There, we go to dwell with God; here, God comes to dwell with us. The Father’s house begins now in the heart that loves.
  • paraklētos, Advocate: Literally one called to stand beside someone: a helper, a defender, a comforter. In 1 John 2:1 the same word is used of Jesus himself; in John 14:16 the Spirit is “another Advocate”.
  • hypomimnēskō, to remind: The Spirit will bring back to mind all that Jesus said. The Gospels themselves are fruit of this remembering.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, how is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world?” (14:22)

Judas perhaps expected a public triumph. Jesus answers by speaking of love, not of display.

“Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them.” (14:23)

Not a passing visit but a home. The whole Trinity dwells in the one who loves: the Father, the Son, and, as the next verses say, the Spirit.

“will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you.” (14:26)

The Spirit does not bring a new Gospel. He makes the one Gospel understood and alive in every age.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 23 when it says that the goal of God’s whole plan is that his creatures enter the perfect unity of the Blessed Trinity, who already now come to dwell in us (CCC 260). It explains that Paraclete means “he who is called to one’s side” (CCC 692), and it calls the Holy Spirit the Church’s living memory (CCC 1099).

Questions people ask

Why did the Risen Jesus not show himself to the whole world?

In Acts Peter says that God allowed him to appear not to all the people but to witnesses chosen beforehand (Acts 10:40–41). God does not force himself on anyone. He shows himself to those who love and keep his word, and through their witness he reaches the world.

For study and teaching

  • Where am I tempted to take credit for what God has done, as the crowd at Lystra wanted to give it to Paul and Barnabas?
  • If the Father and the Son make their home in me, what in my daily life would I want to tidy, and what would I want to open?
