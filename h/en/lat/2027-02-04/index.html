Word for the day

“He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” Mark 6:7

Reflection

Hebrews contrasts two mountains. At Sinai Israel met God in “a blazing fire, and darkness, and gloom, and a tempest, and the sound of a trumpet”, so terrifying that “Moses said, ‘I tremble with fear.’” But Christians have come somewhere else: “you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering, and to the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven… and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel.” Abel's blood cried out for vengeance; the blood of Jesus speaks forgiveness. This is what happens at every Mass: we stand with angels and saints at the festival of heaven.

In the Gospel Jesus “called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” They go in pairs, because witness is never solitary. They travel light: “nothing for their journey except a staff; no bread, no bag, no money in their belts.” They depend on God and on the hospitality of those who receive them. Their message is simple, “that all should repent”, and their works are signs of the Kingdom: “They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” The Church sees in that anointing the beginning of the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick. Mission is not a privilege of a few; every baptized Christian is sent.

Full homily

Dear friends, Hebrews tells us we have not come to Sinai with its fire, darkness and trumpet. You have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering (Hebrews 12:22).

We have come to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel (Hebrews 12:24). Every Mass is this festival of heaven.

In the Gospel Jesus called the twelve and began to send them out two by two (Mark 6:7). They took nothing but a staff. They preached repentance, cast out demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them (Mark 6:13).

We too are sent, not alone but together, not loaded with baggage but trusting in God. Let us go from this Mass two by two to bring his healing. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus sent his friends out in pairs to help people and tell them about God. Who can you go with to do something kind this week?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, mediator of the new covenant, you send your disciples two by two with nothing but trust. Free us from the baggage that holds us back, and send us to call others to repentance and to bring your healing. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hebrews describes where the Christian stands at worship: not at a mountain of fire, but in the assembly of angels and saints, in front of Jesus. Mark shows the same Jesus sending twelve men out with almost nothing, to give what they have received. We come in to the feast and we are sent out, and the two go together. Psalm 48, in praise of the city of God, supplies the words for the gathering: “We ponder your steadfast love, O God, in the midst of your temple.”

Hebrews 12:18–19, 21–24 · You have come to Mount Zion

Setting.

This is one of the high points of the letter. The author sets two mountains in front of his tired readers. One is Sinai, as Exodus 19 and Deuteronomy 4–5 describe it: fire, darkness and a voice that frightened the people. The other is Zion, the heavenly city. His argument is pastoral: look at where you have come, and do not turn back. The Lectionary omits v. 20.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19, 21: you have not come to this: the terrors of Sinai.
  • vv. 22–24: you have come to this: a long list, from the city to the blood of Jesus.

Key words.

  • panēgyris, festal gathering: A festival assembly, the word used for a great public celebration. Only here in the New Testament. Where Sinai had dread, Zion has a feast.
  • ekklēsia prōtotokōn, assembly of the firstborn: Ekklēsia is the word for Church. The firstborn were the ones who belonged to God in a special way, as this week's feast of the Presentation recalled. Their names are written in heaven.
  • mesitēs, mediator: One who stands between two parties. Hebrews uses it of Jesus three times (8:6; 9:15; 12:24), always with the new covenant.

Verse by verse.

“Indeed, so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, “I tremble with fear.”” (12:21)

Exodus does not record these words at Sinai. The nearest is Deuteronomy 9:19, when Moses was afraid after the golden calf. Hebrews brings it into this scene.

“But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering,” (12:22)

The tense is striking: “you have come”. The author does not say “you will come one day”. The Christian who worships is already, in hope and in sacrament, in that company.

“and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel.” (12:24)

The blood of Abel cried out to God from the ground (Genesis 4:10). The blood of Jesus also speaks, and it asks for mercy. The sprinkling recalls the blood with which Moses sealed the covenant (Exodus 24:8). The whole list ends here, on the note of mercy.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that in the earthly liturgy we take part in a foretaste of the heavenly liturgy which is celebrated in the holy city of Jerusalem (Sacrosanctum Concilium 8; CCC 1090). This is why every Preface ends by joining the angels and saints in the Holy, Holy, Holy. At every Mass, however small the parish and however few the people, this passage is what is really happening.

Mark 6:7–13 · He began to send them out two by two

Setting.

After the rejection at Nazareth, the work widens. Jesus chose the Twelve “to be with him, and to be sent out” (3:14). They have been with him, and now they are sent. Mark sets their mission around the story of John's death (6:14–29). They return only at 6:30.

Structure.

  • v. 7: two by two, with authority.
  • vv. 8–9: what to take, and it is almost nothing.
  • vv. 10–11: where to stay, and what to do if refused.
  • vv. 12–13: what they did: preached repentance, cast out demons, anointed the sick.

Key words.

  • apostellein, to send: The verb behind the word “apostle”, one who is sent with the authority of the sender. Mark will call them apostles when they return (6:30).
  • exousia, authority: The authority that amazed people in Jesus' own teaching (1:22, 27). He now shares it.
  • ēleiphon elaiō, they anointed with oil: Olive oil was used as a medicine in the ancient world (compare Luke 10:34). Here it is joined to the power of Jesus' mission. Only Mark mentions it in this account.

Verse by verse.

“He called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.” (6:7)

Two witnesses were needed to establish a matter under the Law (Deuteronomy 19:15). Two also means company on the road. No one in the Gospel is sent to be a lone hero.

“He ordered them to take nothing for their journey except a staff; no bread, no bag, no money in their belts;” (6:8)

The poverty of the mission is the message: they depend on God and on the people who receive them. Matthew 10:10 and Luke 9:3 report that even the staff was forbidden. The Gospel writers differ on the detail and agree on the point.

“So they went out and proclaimed that all should repent. They cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick and cured them.” (6:12–13)

They say what John said (1:4) and what Jesus said (1:15). Word and healing go together, as they do in Jesus' own work.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these two verses in speaking of Christ's call to his disciples to share his ministry of compassion and healing (CCC 1506). The Council of Trent taught that the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick is alluded to by Mark and is recommended and promulgated by James (James 5:14–15; CCC 1511). The sending of the Twelve is the root of the apostolic ministry in the Church (CCC 858–859).

Questions people ask

Does Hebrews mean that Sinai was a bad thing, or that God has rejected Israel?

No. Sinai was God's own work, and the Law is holy. Hebrews compares the fear of that first meeting with the access given in Christ, and it does so in order to encourage tired Christians, not to condemn anyone. The Church teaches that the New Testament lies hidden in the Old and the Old is made clear in the New (Dei Verbum 16), and that God's covenant with Israel has never been revoked (CCC 121; Nostra Aetate 4).

For study and teaching

  • When I go to Mass, do I see only the people around me? How would it change my prayer to remember the company that Hebrews lists?
  • What would I find it hardest to leave behind if Jesus sent me out with “nothing for their journey”, as he sent the Twelve? Who is the companion that he has given me for the road?
