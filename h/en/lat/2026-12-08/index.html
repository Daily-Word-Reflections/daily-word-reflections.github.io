Word for the day

“And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”” Luke 1:28

Reflection

Today the Church celebrates that Mary, from the first moment of her conception, was preserved free from all stain of original sin, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the human race. So Pope Pius IX defined it in 1854 (Ineffabilis Deus; CCC 491). This is not about the conception of Jesus (that is the Annunciation) but about Mary's own conception in the womb of her mother, St Anne.

The first reading takes us back to the garden. After the fall God asks, “Where are you?”, and then speaks the first promise of redemption: “I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head.” Christian tradition calls this the Protoevangelium, the first Gospel. The New Eve, whose Son crushes the serpent, was never under the serpent's power.

The angel greets Mary as kecharitōmenē, “full of grace” or “highly favoured”: one who has been and remains filled with God's grace. Mary's sinlessness is not her own achievement; she too was redeemed by Christ, but “in a more exalted fashion” (CCC 492), preserved rather than rescued. Paul says God “chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love”. What God did fully in Mary from the first moment, he wants to do in us through Baptism and a lifetime of grace.

And Mary's freedom shines in her answer: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Grace does not crush freedom; it frees it. Where Eve said no, Mary says yes. The Immaculate Conception in the middle of Advent shows us how God prepares a dwelling for his Son, and how we may prepare too.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today in the middle of Advent we celebrate Mary Immaculate. Some think this feast is about Jesus' conception, but it is about Mary's own conception in her mother's womb. From that first moment God preserved her from original sin.

Why? Because God was preparing a home for his Son. When we expect an important guest, we prepare the house. God prepared the most beautiful home: a soul without any stain of sin.

The first reading takes us to the garden. After the first sin, God calls, “Where are you?” And then he gives the first promise: the woman and her offspring will crush the serpent's head. Mary is that woman. The serpent never had power over her.

But don't think Mary did not need Jesus. She was saved by Jesus too, only in a more wonderful way. We are pulled out of the mud after we fall; Mary was kept from falling. Both are the work of the same Saviour.

And grace did not make Mary a robot. When the angel greeted her, “Full of grace, the Lord is with you,” she asked questions, she thought, and then she freely said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord.” Eve said no to God; Mary said yes. St Irenaeus said: Eve tied the knot; Mary untied it.

What God did in Mary from the first moment, he wants to do in us through Baptism, Confession and the Eucharist. Paul says God chose us “to be holy and blameless before him in love”. So today, let us answer God's question, “Where are you?”, honestly. Let us come to Confession, and with Mary say: “Let it be with me according to your word.”

Dear brothers and sisters, the early Christians did not have the dogma of the Immaculate Conception in its later words, but they had a deep intuition about Mary, drawn from the Scriptures. Let us look at two images that the Fathers loved: Mary as the new Eve, and Mary as the Ark of the Covenant.

In the garden, Eve listened to the serpent and believed his lie. Through her disobedience, sin and death entered the world. But God promised that the offspring of a woman would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). In Nazareth another woman listened, not to a serpent but to an angel, and said: here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word (Luke 1:38). St Irenaeus wrote that the knot of Eve's disobedience was untied by Mary's obedience. Where Eve said no, Mary said yes.

The second image is the Ark of the Covenant. God commanded Moses to make it of acacia wood, covered with pure gold inside and out (Exodus 25:10–11). Inside were the tablets of the law, a jar of manna and Aaron's rod (Hebrews 9:4). When the Ark entered a house, it brought blessing; David danced before it. Mary carried in her womb the Word of God, the true Bread from heaven and the eternal High Priest. When she entered Elizabeth's house, the child leaped for joy (Luke 1:41). If the Ark had to be pure gold, how much more the living Ark of the Son of God.

This is what the Church proclaims today: that from the first moment of her conception Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ. She too was saved by her Son, but in a more exalted way: preserved rather than cleansed.

And what God did perfectly for Mary, He wants to begin in us. In Baptism we were washed; in confession we are cleansed again; in the Holy Mass we receive Christ as Mary did. In these days before Christmas, let us prepare a clean dwelling for Him. Make a good confession before Christmas, and ask Mary Immaculate to teach you to say yes.

Dear brothers and sisters, many Catholics think that today's feast is about the conception of Jesus in Mary's womb. It is not. That we celebrate on 25 March, the Annunciation. Today we celebrate the conception of Mary herself in the womb of her mother, whom tradition names Anne.

On 8 December 1854, Pope Pius IX solemnly defined that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God, and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin (CCC 491). Every word matters. Singular grace: it was God's gift, not Mary's achievement. In view of the merits of Jesus Christ: Mary too was saved by her Son.

Where is this in the Bible? The angel greets Mary as full of grace (Luke 1:28). The Greek word means one who has been, and remains, filled with God's favour. The Church understood that the woman chosen to be the Mother of God was prepared by grace from the beginning. The Catechism says that Mary was redeemed from the moment of her conception, and that by God's grace she remained free of every personal sin her whole life long (CCC 492–493). The Eastern Fathers call her Panagia, the All-Holy.

Four years after the definition, in 1858, Our Lady appeared to a poor girl, Bernadette, at Lourdes. When Bernadette asked her name, she answered: I am the Immaculate Conception. Bernadette did not understand the words, but the parish priest did. Heaven confirmed what the Church had taught.

Why does this matter? Because it shows what God's grace can do. Mary is not a goddess; she is a creature, entirely filled with grace. She shows what the Church is called to become: holy and without blemish (Ephesians 5:27). So let us honour her this Advent with the Rosary, and ask her to help us prepare for her Son at this Holy Mass and at Christmas.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the middle of Advent the Church stops to look at the woman God prepared for the coming of his Son. Today’s solemnity is about Mary’s own beginning: from the first moment of her existence in the womb of her mother, she was kept from all stain of original sin, by a unique grace of God and because of the merits of Christ. It is not about the conception of Jesus, which is the Annunciation, though the Gospel read today is the Annunciation, because that is where Scripture shows us who Mary already is.

The readings go from a garden to a small town. In Genesis the first man and woman hide from God, and God speaks the first promise: there will be enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their offspring. The psalm sings of the victory God has made known. Ephesians says that God chose us in Christ “before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless”. In Luke the angel greets a young woman in Nazareth as one already filled with grace, and she answers, “let it be with me according to your word.” The key words are enmity, chosen and grace.

Genesis 3:9–15, 20 · Where are you?

Setting.

Genesis 2–3 tells of the beginning of sin in the language of a story: a garden, a tree, a serpent. The Catechism says that this account “uses figurative language, but affirms a primeval event” (CCC 390). Our reading begins just after the man and the woman have eaten and have hidden among the trees (3:8). Trust in God has broken, and now fear and shame enter.

The Lectionary leaps from v. 15 to v. 20. It leaves out the consequences for the woman and the man (vv. 16–19) and keeps the promise and the name of Eve, so that the first mother of the living stands beside the promise.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–13: God’s questions: Where are you? Who told you? What have you done? Each person passes the blame to another.
  • vv. 14–15: God’s word to the serpent: a curse, and a lasting enmity with the woman and her offspring.
  • v. 20: the man names his wife Eve, “the mother of all living”.
  • ¶ God asks the man and the woman questions, but he does not question the serpent. He simply pronounces sentence, and inside that sentence is the first hope of the human race.

Key words.

  • ’êbāh, enmity: A word for deep and lasting hostility. It is God who puts it there: “I will put”. The serpent’s friendship with the human race is not allowed to stand.
  • zera‘, offspring, seed: A collective word that can also point to one descendant. The promise looks down the generations, and Christian reading sees it reach its goal in Christ.
  • šûp, to strike: The same Hebrew verb is used for both blows: he will strike your head, you will strike his heel. The blows are not equal: one is to the head, the other to the heel.
  • ḥawwāh, Eve: The name sounds like the Hebrew word for living, ḥay. After the sentence of death, the man gives his wife a name of life.

Verse by verse.

“But the Lord God called to the man, and said to him, “Where are you?”” (3:9)

God knows the answer. The question is an invitation to come out of hiding. It is the first question God asks in the Bible, and he is still asking it.

“I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.” (3:15)

A note on the text. The Hebrew says “he” (hû’) will strike, and so do the Greek Old Testament, the NRSV and the POC. The Latin Vulgate tradition reads “she” (ipsa), which is why Western art so often shows Mary with the serpent under her foot. Both point to the same victory: the Son wins it, and his mother shares in it by his grace.

“The man named his wife Eve, because she was the mother of all living.” (3:20)

The Fathers set Eve and Mary side by side. Comparing the two, they call Mary “the Mother of the living” (CCC 494).

How the Church reads it.

The Church calls Genesis 3:15 the Protoevangelium, the “first Gospel”, the first announcement of the Messiah and of a battle between the serpent and the Woman (CCC 410). Many Fathers and Doctors see in this woman the mother of Christ, the “new Eve” (CCC 411). St Irenaeus wrote that the knot of Eve’s disobedience was untied by Mary’s obedience (quoted in CCC 494; Lumen Gentium 56). The last book of the Bible returns to the same scene, with “that ancient serpent” and a woman who gives birth to the Messiah (Revelation 12).

Psalm 98:1, 2–3ab, 3cd–4 · All the ends of the earth have seen

Setting.

Psalm 98 is one of the hymns that celebrate the Lord as King (Psalms 93–99). It calls for a new song because God has acted, and it sees his saving deed in the sight of all nations. Its language is close to the second part of Isaiah.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the call to sing, and the reason: God’s right hand has won.
  • vv. 2–3: God’s victory is made known, because he has remembered Israel.
  • v. 4: all the earth is invited to shout for joy.

Key words.

  • šîr ḥādāš, a new song: In the Psalms a new song answers a new act of God. Today the new thing is the beginning of the Redeemer’s coming.
  • yešû‘āh, victory, salvation: From the same root as the name Jesus (Yēšûa‘). The NRSV says “victory”; the word also means rescue and salvation.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love: God’s faithful covenant love, which he “has remembered”. For God, to remember is to act.

Verse by verse.

“O sing to the Lord a new song, for he has done marvelous things.” (98:1)
“He has remembered his steadfast love and faithfulness to the house of Israel. All the ends of the earth have seen the victory of our God.” (98:3)

Israel first, then the whole earth. Mary’s own song says the same thing: God has helped Israel “in remembrance of his mercy” (Luke 1:54).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sings this psalm at the Mass of Christmas Day as well as today. The marvel that begins hidden in Mary’s life is shown to all the ends of the earth in her Son. On this solemnity the psalm also reminds us that the praise goes to God: what is celebrated in Mary is his work.

Ephesians 1:3–6, 11–12 · Chosen before the foundation of the world

Setting.

The letter comes to us under Paul’s name and speaks from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think it was written by a disciple carrying on his teaching, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself. After the greeting it opens with a great blessing, on the Jewish model of “Blessed be God”, which runs from v. 3 to v. 14. In the Greek this is a single long sentence.

Structure.

  • v. 3: blessing God who has blessed us in Christ.
  • vv. 4–6: he chose us before creation, to be holy and to be his adopted children.
  • vv. 11–12: in Christ we too have received an inheritance, to live for the praise of his glory.

Key words.

  • exelexato, he chose: God’s choice comes before anything we do. It is made “in Christ”, the phrase that returns again and again in this passage.
  • amōmos, blameless, without blemish: The word used in the Greek Old Testament for a sacrificial animal without defect. The letter uses the same pair of words for the Church, “holy and without blemish” (5:27).
  • echaritōsen, he freely bestowed (grace): From the verb charitoō, to fill with grace, to favour. In the New Testament this verb appears only here and in Luke 1:28, in the angel’s greeting to Mary. Today’s two readings are joined by this one rare word.

Verse by verse.

“just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” (1:4)

The goal is not only a clean record but love, “before him”. The Catechism applies this verse directly to Mary (CCC 492). What God wills for all of us, he gave to her from the first moment.

“He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will,” (1:5)

Adoption (huiothesia) was a legal act in the Roman world that gave a full place in the family. All of this comes “through Jesus Christ”, and Mary is no exception: she too is saved through her Son.

“to the praise of his glorious grace that he freely bestowed on us in the Beloved.” (1:6)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Father blessed Mary more than any other created person “in Christ with every spiritual blessing”, and chose her “in Christ before the foundation of the world” (CCC 492, quoting this passage). The Church looks at Mary and sees what she herself hopes to become: in the Blessed Virgin the Church has already reached the perfection by which she exists without spot or wrinkle, while the rest of us still strive to grow in holiness (CCC 829; Lumen Gentium 65).

Luke 1:26–38 · Greetings, favored one

Setting.

Luke has just told of the angel’s visit to Zechariah in the Temple at Jerusalem, a priest in the sanctuary, who doubts (1:5–25). Now the same angel goes to a village never named in the Old Testament, to a young woman who is engaged and not yet living with her husband. The contrast is deliberate.

Betrothal among the Jews of that time was a binding bond, already close to marriage, which came before the bride moved to her husband’s house. So Joseph, a descendant of David, is legally the one through whom Jesus belongs to the house of David.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: the time, the place, the persons.
  • vv. 28–29: the greeting, and Mary’s perplexity.
  • vv. 30–33: the first message: a son, Jesus, the heir of David.
  • v. 34: Mary’s question.
  • vv. 35–37: the second message: the Holy Spirit, the Son of God, the sign of Elizabeth.
  • v. 38: Mary’s answer.

Key words.

  • chaire, greetings, rejoice: The usual Greek greeting, but it literally means “rejoice”. Many commentators hear in it the prophets’ call to the daughter of Zion to rejoice because the Lord is in her midst (Zephaniah 3:14–15; Zechariah 9:9).
  • kecharitōmenē, favored one, full of grace: A perfect passive participle of charitoō: one who has been graced and remains so. The angel uses it in place of her name, as if it were her title. The Latin gratia plena and the POC “ദൈവകൃപ നിറഞ്ഞവളേ” bring this out. The grammar alone does not prove the dogma, but the Church has come to see in this word the depth of what God had already done in her (CCC 490–491).
  • episkiazein, to overshadow: The word the Greek Old Testament uses for the cloud settling on the tent of meeting when God’s glory filled it (Exodus 40:35). Mary is the new dwelling of God. The same verb returns in the cloud of the Transfiguration (Luke 9:34).
  • doulē, servant: The feminine of the word for slave or servant, a title of honour for those who belong to God. She uses it again in her song (1:48).

Verse by verse.

“And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”” (1:28)

“The Lord is with you” is what God says to those he calls to a great task, like Moses and Gideon (Exodus 3:12; Judges 6:12). Here it comes with no word of Mary’s achievement. Everything begins from grace.

“Mary said to the angel, “How can this be, since I am a virgin?”” (1:34)

Literally, “since I do not know a man”, which is how the POC renders it. This is not the doubt of Zechariah. She does not ask for a sign but asks how it will happen.

“The angel said to her, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” (1:35)

This is the conception of Jesus, by the Holy Spirit. Today’s feast celebrates something earlier: the grace that made Mary ready to receive him.

“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”” (1:38)

The Greek verb genoito expresses a wish, a glad desire. Grace has not removed her freedom; it has made her able to say a whole yes.

How the Church reads it.

Pope Pius IX, in the bull Ineffabilis Deus (1854), defined that Mary, from the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, was preserved from all stain of original sin (CCC 491). Holding on to God’s word with her whole heart, and with no sin to hold her back, she gave herself entirely to the person and work of her Son (CCC 494). St Bernard, preaching on this passage, pictures the whole world waiting for her answer (in substance). St Augustine says that Mary was more blessed for receiving Christ by faith than for conceiving him in the flesh (in substance; CCC 506).

Questions people ask

Is the Immaculate Conception the same as the Virgin Birth?

No, though the two are often confused. The Immaculate Conception concerns Mary’s own beginning in the womb of her mother, whom tradition names Anne: she was preserved from original sin. The virginal conception concerns Jesus: he was conceived in Mary by the Holy Spirit, without a human father (CCC 496–498).

If Mary was without sin, did she need a Saviour?

Yes, and she says so: “my spirit rejoices in God my Savior” (Luke 1:47). She was redeemed “in a more exalted fashion” by the merits of her Son (CCC 492). St Bernard and St Thomas Aquinas had difficulty with the doctrine for this very reason. Blessed John Duns Scotus helped the Church to see that keeping someone from falling is a more perfect rescue than lifting her up after a fall (in substance).

Where is this in the Bible?

Scripture does not state the doctrine in so many words. Ineffabilis Deus points to Genesis 3:15 and Luke 1:28, read within the faith of the Church across the centuries. The Fathers called Mary all-holy (Panagia) (CCC 493), and the Church’s understanding grew until it was solemnly defined.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman Rite this is a solemnity, with white vestments in the middle of violet Advent. The universal law of the Latin Church lists it among the holy days of obligation (Code of Canon Law, can. 1246), though bishops’ conferences may adjust this, so follow the direction of your own diocese. The Collect of the Mass says it all: God prepared a worthy dwelling for his Son and preserved Mary from every stain by virtue of the Death of his Son, which he foresaw (in substance). Four years after the definition, at Lourdes in 1858, the Lady told St Bernadette, “I am the Immaculate Conception.” The doctrine does not rest on that private revelation. In Advent the solemnity teaches us how to wait: God prepares the place where his Son will come.

For study and teaching

  • God’s first question is “Where are you?” Where am I hiding from him at present, and what would it mean to answer him?
  • Ephesians 1:4 says that God chose me “to be holy and blameless before him in love”. How does it change my day to believe that holiness is God’s plan for me and not only my own effort?
  • Mary’s sinlessness did not make her life easy or remove her freedom. What does her “let it be” teach me about my own freedom?
  • How can I explain to someone, simply and correctly, the difference between the Immaculate Conception and the virginal conception of Jesus?
