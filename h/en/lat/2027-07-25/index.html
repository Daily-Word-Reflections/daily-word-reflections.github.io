Word for the day

“Then Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them to those who were seated; so also the fish, as much as they wanted.” John 6:11

Reflection

Elisha is the prophet of the lean years. Just before today's story the Bible says, “there was a famine in the land” (2 Kings 4:38), and now a man arrives from Baal-shalishah with “twenty loaves of barley and fresh ears of grain in his sack.” They are first fruits, the first of the harvest, which the Law set apart for God and his ministers (compare Numbers 18:12–13; Deuteronomy 18:4). In a famine the man could easily have kept them for his own family; he carries them instead to the man of God. Elisha says only: “Give it to the people and let them eat.” His servant does the arithmetic every hungry community knows: “How can I set this before a hundred people?” Elisha repeats the command and gives the reason: “for thus says the Lord, ‘They shall eat and have some left.’” The servant obeys, and it happens “according to the word of the Lord.” Notice what is not asked: nobody is told to pray bread down from the sky. Twenty real loaves are placed in a servant's hands, and the word of the Lord does the rest. The Psalm names the God behind the sign: “The eyes of all look to you, and you give them their food in due season. You open your hand, satisfying the desire of every living thing.”

In the second reading St Paul writes as “the prisoner in the Lord,” and he begs rather than commands: “lead a life worthy of the calling to which you have been called, with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, making every effort to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.” Then comes the great litany of the “ones”: one body and one Spirit, one hope, “one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is above all and through all and in all.” The Church's unity is not something we build; it is a gift to be guarded. And the Gospel shows where that gift is fed. To the Corinthians Paul writes: “Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread” (1 Corinthians 10:17). The many are made one by the one bread.

For five Sundays the Church now reads John 6, and it opens with the sign. Every detail is chosen. “Now the Passover, the festival of the Jews, was near”: we are to think of the Exodus and the manna. Jesus “went up the mountain and sat down there with his disciples,” like a new Moses. He asks Philip, “Where are we to buy bread for these people to eat?” and John adds, “He said this to test him, for he himself knew what he was going to do.” Philip does the sums: “Six months' wages would not buy enough bread for each of them to get a little.” Then Andrew, who in this Gospel is always bringing someone to Jesus, brings a boy with “five barley loaves and two fish,” and asks the honest question: “But what are they among so many people?” Jesus says, “Make the people sit down.” Then comes the action that every Mass repeats: “Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them.” The Greek for “given thanks” is eucharistesas, the word from which we get Eucharist. Nothing is wasted: “Gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost,” and twelve baskets are filled. The crowd, seeing the sign, wants “to take him by force to make him king,” but Jesus “withdrew again to the mountain by himself.” He will be king by giving himself, and in the coming Sundays he will say what the bread is: “I am the bread of life” (John 6:35); “the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh” (John 6:51).

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, it is a hungry crowd. A man brings twenty small barley loaves to the prophet Elisha in a time of famine. Elisha says: give it to the people. And his servant says what we would all say: how can I set this before a hundred people? (2 Kings 4:43). It is the arithmetic of every hungry place. But Elisha says: thus says the Lord, they shall eat and have some left. And they did. The man's first fruits fed a hundred.

In the Gospel there is an even bigger crowd, five thousand, and again Jesus asks a question to test his disciples: where are we to buy bread for these people? Philip does the sums: six months' wages would not be enough. And then Andrew finds a boy. Notice, it is a boy, a child, with five barley loaves and two fish, the poor man's lunch. And Andrew says honestly: but what are they among so many people? (John 6:9). I think every one of us has said that about our own gifts. What is my little time, my little money, my little talent among so many needs?

But look at what Jesus does. He does not ask the crowd for more. He takes what the boy offers, and he gives thanks. Not a complaint that it is too little, but thanks. Then he distributes it, and they all eat as much as they wanted. The boy did not make the miracle. But there would have been no miracle that day without his five loaves. God asks us for our little, and he does the rest.

And then Jesus says: gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost (John 6:12). In God's house nothing is thrown away, no crumb and no person. Twelve baskets are filled. That is how we should look at the Eucharist, at the food on our tables, and at the old and the weak among us, whom the world is tempted to call leftovers. Today is the World Day for Grandparents and the Elderly. They are not fragments to be discarded; they are the baskets of the faith, and they hold what was handed down to us. Let us visit them and listen.

St Paul tells us what this bread is for. There is one body and one Spirit, one Lord, one faith, one baptism. At every Mass Jesus takes, gives thanks, breaks and gives, and from many grains makes one bread, from many persons one Church. So let us leave the altar with humility, gentleness and patience, bearing with one another in love. And when we feel that our gift is too small, let us remember the boy with the barley loaves, and bring it to Jesus. Amen.

For children

Children, a big crowd followed Jesus and they were all hungry. A little boy had five small loaves and two fish. He gave them to Jesus. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Seventeenth Sunday of Year B opens five Sundays in which the Roman Lectionary pauses Mark and reads John 6, the chapter of the bread. Today is the sign itself: Jesus takes five barley loaves and two fish from a boy, gives thanks, and feeds about five thousand, with twelve baskets left over. The first reading gives the pattern in miniature: the prophet Elisha feeds a hundred people with twenty barley loaves, and the Lord says, “They shall eat and have some left.” Psalm 145 sings the same trust: “You open your hand, satisfying the desire of every living thing.”

The second reading, from Ephesians, seems to change the subject, but it names what the bread builds: one body, one Spirit, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all. The bread that Christ gives is not only for each person; it gathers a people into unity.

2 Kings 4:42–44 · They shall eat and have some left

Setting.

The story belongs to the cycle of Elisha, the prophet who took up the work of Elijah in the northern kingdom of Israel in the ninth century before Christ. Just before this scene the sons of the prophets are hungry because of a famine in the land, and Elisha has made a poisoned stew safe to eat (4:38–41). Bread is scarce, and a man brings the first of his harvest to the “man of God”.

The gift comes from Baal-shalishah, a place in the hill country of Ephraim whose exact site is not certain. Under the Law the firstfruits were holy to the Lord and were given to his priests and ministers (Numbers 18:13; Deuteronomy 18:4). Here the man brings them to the prophet, and the prophet gives them away.

Structure.

  • v. 42: a man brings twenty barley loaves and fresh grain from the first fruits; Elisha says, “Give it to the people and let them eat.”
  • v. 43: the servant objects that it cannot feed a hundred; Elisha repeats the command and gives God’s word: “They shall eat and have some left.”
  • v. 44: the servant sets it before them; they eat and have some left, “according to the word of the Lord”.

Key words.

  • bikkûrîm, first fruits: The first and best of the crop, offered to God in thanks. The loaves are therefore holy bread, yet Elisha does not keep them for himself or for the prophets; he gives them to the hungry.
  • ʾākōl wĕhôtēr, eat and have some left: The word of the Lord is a promise of enough and more than enough. The same two verbs close the story, so that the event matches the word exactly.
  • ʾîš hāʾĕlōhîm, man of God: The usual title for a prophet who speaks and acts for God. The miracle is done by God’s word through his messenger, not by the prophet’s own power.

Verse by verse.

“Give it to the people and let them eat.” (4:42)

The command is simple and generous. The firstfruits, given to God, become food for his hungry people.

“How can I set this before a hundred people?” (4:43)

The servant speaks honestly about the numbers. The story gives his doubt its place: the miracle begins where human resources end.

“thus says the Lord, ‘They shall eat and have some left.’” (4:43)

Elisha does not argue about arithmetic; he repeats what God has said. Obedience to the word comes first, and then the loaves are enough.

“He set it before them, they ate, and had some left, according to the word of the Lord.” (4:44)

The story ends with the word fulfilled. John does not name Elisha, but the barley loaves, the doubt about the numbers and the leftovers make the echo hard to miss.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this story as a sign pointing forward to the Gospel, as the Lectionary does by placing it before John 6. The Catechism teaches that the Old Testament prepares what Christ fulfils, and that the miracles of the multiplication of the loaves foretell the greater bread of the Eucharist (CCC 1335). What Elisha does for a hundred, Christ does for five thousand; what Elisha does by a word received, Christ does by his own word, since he is the Word. The Church also keeps the thought of the firstfruits: in the Mass the bread and wine are offered in thanks to God, and returned to his people as the Body and Blood of Christ.

Ephesians 4:1–6 · One Lord, one faith, one baptism

Setting.

The letter to the Ephesians is traditionally ascribed to St Paul, writing as a prisoner. Many scholars think it was written by a close disciple of Paul after his death, in his name and from his thought; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture and as Paul’s teaching either way. The words “in Ephesus” (1:1) are missing from some of the oldest manuscripts, and many think the letter was meant for several churches in the region, not for one community only.

Chapters 1–3 praise what God has done in Christ: Jews and Gentiles, once divided, are made one in Christ’s body. Chapters 4–6 turn to how that unity is to be lived. Our reading opens the second half with the word “therefore”: because of what God has done, live in a way that fits it.

Structure.

  • v. 1: “I… beg you to lead a life worthy of the calling to which you have been called.”
  • v. 2: the attitudes that make unity possible: humility, gentleness, patience, love.
  • v. 3: the effort to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.
  • vv. 4–6: the creed of unity in seven “ones”: body, Spirit, hope, Lord, faith, baptism, God and Father of all.

Key words.

  • axiōs peripatēsai, to walk worthily: Literally “to walk in a way worthy of”. In Scripture the Christian life is a way along which one walks day by day, not only a belief held.
  • spoudazontes, making every effort: The word means eagerness and diligence. Unity is a gift of the Spirit, yet it asks effort from us; Paul says to “maintain” it, not to create it.
  • syndesmos, bond: A ligament, something that binds parts together. The unity is held together by peace, as the body is held together by its ligaments.

Verse by verse.

“I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you” (4:1)

Paul writes from prison and has no earthly power. He begs, and his chains give weight to his words.

“with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love,” (4:2)

Unity is not first an organisation but a way of treating one another. Christians are different from each other, and love means bearing with the difference.

“making every effort to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.” (4:3)

The Spirit gives the unity; Christians must protect it. The goal is not sameness but peace among those who are different.

“one Lord, one faith, one baptism,” (4:5)

Many scholars think Paul, or his disciple, is quoting here a very early summary of the faith: one Lord confessed, one faith professed, one baptism by which believers are joined to him.

“one God and Father of all, who is above all and through all and in all.” (4:6)

The list ends with the Father, source of everything. The Trinity is in the background: one Spirit (v. 4), one Lord (v. 5), one God and Father (v. 6).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the Church is one because of her source, the unity of the Trinity (CCC 813), and that baptism is the foundation of the communion among all Christians (CCC 1271). It teaches that the Eucharist makes the Church: those who share one bread are one body (CCC 1396). The wounds of division among Christians are real, and the Church prays and works for the unity Christ wills. The seven “ones” are also the foundation of ecumenical dialogue: Catholics, Orthodox and many other Christians share one Lord, one baptism and a great part of the one faith.

John 6:1–15 · Jesus took the loaves, gave thanks, and distributed them

Setting.

John calls the miracles of Jesus “signs”, and this is one of the best known. Apart from the Resurrection, it is the only miracle that all four Gospels record. John sets it by the Sea of Galilee, also called the Sea of Tiberias, a name taken from the city Herod Antipas built there and named for the emperor.

John places it near Passover, “the festival of the Jews” (v. 4). He writes for readers who may not know the feast, and he does not mean it as blame: Jesus and his disciples are Jews keeping a Jewish pilgrimage season. The mention of Passover tells the reader that the bread of this chapter will be linked to the new Passover. After the sign Jesus teaches in the synagogue of Capernaum about the bread of life (6:22–59), and the Lectionary reads it across the next Sundays.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the crowd follows him because of his signs; Jesus goes up the mountain; Passover is near.
  • vv. 5–9: Jesus asks Philip where to buy bread, to test him; Philip counts the cost; Andrew finds a boy with five barley loaves and two fish.
  • vv. 10–11: the people sit on the grass; Jesus takes the loaves, gives thanks, and distributes them, and the fish also.
  • vv. 12–13: “Gather up the fragments”; twelve baskets are filled.
  • vv. 14–15: “This is indeed the prophet”; Jesus withdraws from those who would make him king.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: John calls miracles “signs”: acts that show who Jesus is. The multiplication points to the one who is himself the bread of life.
  • eucharistēsas, having given thanks: The verb from which “Eucharist” comes. John uses it of Jesus’ prayer over the loaves (vv. 11 and 23), and the Church has always heard the Mass in it.
  • klasmata, fragments: Broken pieces of bread. The same word appears in the early Christian eucharistic prayer of the Didache, which speaks of the broken bread scattered on the hills and gathered into one.

Verse by verse.

“Where are we to buy bread for these people to eat?” (6:5)

Jesus asks the question himself and does not wait to be asked. John says that he already knew what he would do (v. 6).

“There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish. But what are they among so many people?” (6:9)

Only John mentions the boy and the barley, the bread of the poor. Andrew’s question is honest. Jesus does not dismiss the small gift; he takes it.

“Then Jesus took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them to those who were seated;” (6:11)

The actions, take, give thanks, distribute, are those the Gospels and St Paul record at the Last Supper (compare Luke 22:19; 1 Corinthians 11:23–24). John does not tell the Supper in this way, so he lets the sign carry the meaning here.

“Gather up the fragments left over, so that nothing may be lost.” (6:12)

Jesus is generous and not wasteful. The word “lost” will come again in the discourse: “that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me” (6:39).

“he withdrew again to the mountain by himself.” (6:15)

The people want a king who will feed them. Jesus will be a king, but on the cross, not by force. He withdraws to pray, as in other Gospels.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the miracles of the multiplication of the loaves, when the Lord said the blessing, broke and gave the loaves through his disciples, prefigure the superabundance of the one bread of the Eucharist (CCC 1335). Many of the Fathers loved the details (in substance): they took the twelve baskets as a sign of the twelve apostles, through whom the bread is given to the world, and some heard in the gathered fragments a lesson of reverence for what has been consecrated. The Church has never turned this Gospel into a lesson about food alone: it is about the one who feeds, and about the bread which, in the next verses of the chapter, he will say is his flesh for the life of the world.

The reading also teaches generosity. Jesus used a boy’s small gift; the Church reads in it the call to bring what we have, however little, and to trust that Christ can use it. The Church has always taught that charity toward the hungry is not optional. Those who share the one bread at the altar must not leave others without bread (CCC 1397).

Questions people ask

Was the miracle only a miracle of sharing, as some say?

The Church reads the Gospel as it was written: Jesus multiplied the loaves. John calls it a sign, and signs in his Gospel are real acts that point beyond themselves. Sharing is part of the lesson, since the boy gave and the people received, yet the Gospel does not reduce the miracle to sharing. The one who made bread from five loaves is the one who will give himself as bread.

Why do we read John on the Sundays of Mark’s year?

Mark’s Gospel is short, and in Year B the Lectionary reads it through; but it has no long account of the bread. So for five Sundays, from today to the Twenty-first Sunday, the Church reads John 6. This lets the whole chapter be heard in order: the sign, the discourse on the bread of life, and the response of faith or refusal.

Why does Jesus withdraw when the people want to make him king?

Because they want the wrong kind of king, one who gives food without asking for conversion. Jesus is the Messiah, but his kingdom is not won by force or by giving away bread. The same temptation was put to him in the wilderness (Matthew 4:3–4), and he refused it. His kingship will be shown on the cross.

For the Latin reader

In 2021 Pope Francis established the World Day for Grandparents and the Elderly, to be kept on the Sunday closest to the memorial of Saints Joachim and Anne, whom the Church honours tomorrow as the parents of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Many parishes remember the elderly and the lonely today. The five Sundays of John 6 are also a good time to teach about the Mass: the Eucharistic Prayer follows the same movement as the Gospel, taking bread, giving thanks, breaking and giving. The feast of St James the Apostle, usually kept on this date, gives way to the Sunday.

For study and teaching

  • Elisha trusted God’s word when the arithmetic was against it. Where in my life am I counting only what I have and forgetting what God has said?
  • Paul begs the Ephesians to “maintain the unity of the Spirit”. In my family, parish or workplace, which of the four attitudes, humility, gentleness, patience or love, do I most need to ask for today?
  • Andrew brought a boy with five loaves to Jesus, though it seemed too little. What small gift of time, skill or money can I bring to Christ this week?
  • Jesus gave thanks before he broke the bread. How does thanksgiving shape the way I come to Mass and the way I eat at home?
