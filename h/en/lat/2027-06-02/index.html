Word for the day

“He is God not of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong.” Mark 12:27

Reflection

Yesterday we left Tobit blind and quarrelling with his wife. Today the Book of Tobit shows us two people, far apart, reaching the bottom on the same day. First Tobit: “with much grief and anguish of heart I wept, and with groaning began to pray.” His prayer begins with praise, not complaint: “You are righteous, O Lord, and all your deeds are just; all your ways are mercy and truth.” He confesses his sins and the sins of his people, and then asks something frightening: “command my spirit to be taken from me … For it is better for me to die than to live, because I have had to listen to undeserved insults.” Then the scene moves hundreds of kilometres east, to Ecbatana in Media, where a young woman named Sarah has been married seven times, and each husband died on the wedding night, killed by the demon Asmodeus. A maid taunts her cruelly: “You are the one who kills your husbands!” Sarah goes up to her father's upper room meaning to hang herself. But she thinks of her father: people would mock him, saying his only beloved daughter hanged herself. So instead she prays, “with hands outstretched toward the window”: “Blessed are you, merciful God! Blessed is your name forever.” Two broken people, two honest prayers, both asking to die. And then the most beautiful sentence of the chapter: “At that very moment, the prayers of both of them were heard in the glorious presence of God. So Raphael was sent to heal both of them.” God does not give them what they asked; he gives them what they needed. The answer to both prayers is already on its way, and it will bring the two of them together. The Psalm is their prayer and ours: “To you, O Lord, I lift up my soul … Make me to know your ways, O Lord; teach me your paths.”

In the Gospel, curiously, we meet another woman with seven husbands. The Sadducees, priestly aristocrats who accepted only the books of Moses and denied the resurrection, bring Jesus a case designed to make the resurrection look absurd: seven brothers marry the same woman one after another, each dying without children. “In the resurrection whose wife will she be?” Jesus answers in two steps. First, they misunderstand the life to come: “when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven.” Risen life is not this life continued with the same arrangements; it is transformed. Second, they misread their own Scriptures. At the burning bush God said to Moses, “I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” (Exodus 3:6), not “I was.” Abraham, Isaac and Jacob were long dead in Moses' time, yet God still calls himself their God. And God does not bind himself in covenant to corpses: “He is God not of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong.” The faithfulness of God is stronger than death. Tobit and Sarah asked to die; God answered with life. Sts Marcellinus and Peter, a priest and an exorcist of Rome, were beheaded under Diocletian about 304, in a wood outside the city. Their executioner, who later became a Christian, told the story to a boy who grew up to be Pope Damasus, and he wrote their epitaph. Their names are still read in the first Eucharistic Prayer, because the God of the living keeps them alive.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we hear two prayers prayed on the same day in two different cities. In Nineveh old Tobit, blind and insulted, weeps and prays: command my spirit to be taken from me; it is better for me to die than to live (Tobit 3:6). In Ecbatana young Sarah, mocked because seven husbands have died, goes upstairs meaning to hang herself. But she thinks of her father's grief, and instead she stretches out her hands toward the window and prays.

Notice how both of them pray. They do not hide their pain. They tell God exactly how they feel. But they also begin with praise: you are righteous, O Lord; blessed are you, merciful God. Even at the bottom they still call God merciful. That is real faith. Faith is not pretending to be happy; it is turning to God even in the dark.

And then the Bible says: at that very moment, the prayers of both of them were heard in the glorious presence of God, and Raphael was sent to heal both of them (Tobit 3:16–17). God did not give them death. He gave them healing, a marriage, a future. Sometimes God says no to what we ask because he is preparing something far better.

In the Gospel Jesus tells the Sadducees that God is the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the God not of the dead but of the living (Mark 12:27). Death does not have the last word. If you are carrying a heavy sorrow today, lift up your soul to the Lord, as the Psalm says. He hears you at this very moment. And pray for your beloved dead, because for God they are alive. Amen.

For children

Children, did you know that God hears every prayer, even a prayer whispered with tears? Long ago a blind old man called Tobit and a sad young woman called Sarah lived in different cities. On the same day both prayed. And God heard both of them at the very same moment and sent an angel to help! So when you are sad, tell God. He is listening right now.

Prayer

Lord God, you hear the prayer of the broken-hearted at the very moment they pray. To you we lift up our souls: make us know your ways and teach us your paths. When we are tempted to despair, send us your help as you sent Raphael to Tobit and Sarah. You are the God of the living; keep in your life all our beloved dead, and through the prayers of Sts Marcellinus and Peter make us faithful witnesses to the resurrection. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the same day, far apart, two people pray from the depth of their sorrow: blind Tobit in Nineveh and young Sarah in Ecbatana, insulted and grieving her seven dead husbands. Both ask to die. At that very moment, says the book, both prayers are heard, and Raphael is sent to heal them. In the Gospel the Sadducees mock the hope of resurrection with the story of a woman married to seven brothers. Jesus answers that God is not God of the dead, but of the living. Psalm 25 gives the prayer of the day: “To you, O Lord, I lift up my soul.”

Tobit 3:1–11a, 16–17a · Two prayers heard at the same moment

Setting.

Stung by Anna’s rebuke, Tobit turns to God. Then the scene moves to Ecbatana, a city in Media far to the east, where Sarah, the only daughter of Raguel, is suffering in a different way. Seven times she was married, and each husband was killed on the wedding night by the demon Asmodeus. A servant mocks her, and she goes up to her father’s room meaning to hang herself.

The author tells the two stories side by side, like two threads that God will soon weave together. The Lectionary leaves out the rest of Sarah’s prayer (vv. 11b–15) and the last lines of v. 17.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–6: Tobit’s prayer: God is just; Israel has sinned; let me die rather than hear insults.
  • vv. 7–10: Sarah, mocked by a maid, thinks of her father and turns from despair to prayer.
  • v. 11a: Sarah prays with hands outstretched toward the window.
  • vv. 16–17a: both prayers are heard; Raphael is sent to heal them both.

Key words.

  • Asmodaios, Asmodeus: The name of the demon, probably from a Persian word meaning “demon of wrath”. In the story he stands for the power of evil that destroys life and love.
  • Raphaēl, Raphael: A Hebrew name meaning “God heals”. The angel’s name tells his task: to heal Tobit’s eyes and to deliver Sarah. He will later say that he is one of the seven angels who stand before the Lord (12:15).

Verse by verse.

“You are righteous, O Lord, and all your deeds are just; all your ways are mercy and truth;” (3:2)

Tobit begins not with his complaint but with praise. Even in darkness, he confesses that God is just and merciful.

“But she thought it over and said, “Never shall they reproach my father,” (3:10)

Love for her father turns Sarah back from death. She chooses prayer instead of despair.

“Blessed are you, merciful God! Blessed is your name forever;” (3:11)

Like Tobit, Sarah begins with blessing. Jewish prayer to this day begins in the same way: “Blessed are you, Lord.”

“At that very moment, the prayers of both of them were heard in the glorious presence of God.” (3:16)

Neither of them knows about the other. God hears both and answers both with one plan.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Tobit and Sarah a school of prayer in suffering. Their honest cry, even the wish to die, is not condemned; it is brought to God, and God answers better than they asked. The Catechism teaches that God’s providence works through the actions of creatures and that he can draw good even from evil (CCC 306–308, 312). The angel Raphael has given Christians a lasting image of the guardian angel, a companion on the road (CCC 336).

Mark 12:18–27 · Not God of the dead, but of the living

Setting.

The questions in the Temple continue. Now it is the Sadducees, a group closely linked with the priestly families. They accepted the five books of Moses as their chief authority and did not believe in the resurrection of the dead, which they found nowhere in the Law. The Pharisees, and many ordinary Jews, did believe in it (compare Daniel 12:2; 2 Maccabees 7).

Background. The law of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–10) required a man to marry his brother’s widow if the brother died childless, so that his name would not be lost. The Sadducees build an absurd case on it to make resurrection look foolish. Readers have noticed that, like Sarah in Tobit, the woman in the story has seven husbands.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–23: the Sadducees’ case: seven brothers, one wife; whose wife will she be?
  • v. 24: “you know neither the scriptures nor the power of God”.
  • v. 25: the risen do not marry; they are like angels.
  • vv. 26–27: the burning bush: “I am the God of Abraham …”; he is God of the living.

Key words.

  • anastasis, resurrection: Literally “standing up again”. Not the return to the same earthly life, but a new life given by God to the whole person, body and soul.
  • hōs angeloi, like angels: Jesus does not say that the risen become angels, but that, like them, they no longer marry or die. Life in the age to come is not a copy of this one.
  • batos, the bush: Jesus refers to the passage “about the bush” (Exodus 3), the way passages were then named before there were chapter numbers.

Verse by verse.

“Is not this the reason you are wrong, that you know neither the scriptures nor the power of God?” (12:24)

Two mistakes: they read the Scriptures too narrowly, and they imagine God’s power too small, as if he could only repeat the present world.

“For when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven.” (12:25)

Earthly marriage serves this life and the handing on of life. In the resurrection love is not lost but fulfilled in God.

“I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” (12:26)

Jesus argues from the Law the Sadducees accept. God speaks of the patriarchs long dead as his own. A bond with the living God cannot be broken by death.

“He is God not of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong.” (12:27)

The friendship God offers is faithful. Those he loves live to him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this Gospel when it teaches that Jesus firmly upheld faith in the resurrection of the dead against the Sadducees and that faith in the resurrection rests on faith in God, who is the God of the living (CCC 993). At every Mass, in the Creed, we say: “I look forward to the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come.”

For the Latin reader

Today the Church may remember Sts Marcellinus, a priest, and Peter, an exorcist, martyred in Rome about the year 304 under Diocletian. Pope St Damasus later wrote their epitaph, saying that he had heard the story of their death as a boy from the executioner himself. Their names are in the Roman Canon, the First Eucharistic Prayer of the Mass.

For study and teaching

  • Tobit and Sarah both begin their prayer with praise. How do I begin my prayer in difficult times?
  • Do I imagine heaven as a copy of this life? What does “God of the living” teach me about those I have lost?
