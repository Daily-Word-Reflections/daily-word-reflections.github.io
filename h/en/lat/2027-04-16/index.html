Word for the day

“Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” John 6:56

Reflection

Saul last appeared holding the coats of the men who stoned Stephen and dragging believers from their homes. Today he is “still breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord.” He asks the high priest for letters to the synagogues of Damascus, so that he may bring to Jerusalem in chains anyone “who belonged to the Way,” the earliest name for the followers of Jesus. Near Damascus “a light from heaven flashed around him. He fell to the ground and heard a voice saying to him, ‘Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?’” Saul asks: “Who are you, Lord?” And the answer changes his life and should change ours: “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” Saul had never laid a hand on Jesus; he had persecuted his disciples. But the risen Lord says: you are persecuting me. Christ and his Church are one, as Head and Body (Catechism 795); what is done to the least of his members is done to him. Saul gets up blind. For three days he neither eats nor drinks, as if he too must pass through a kind of death and burial before rising. Then the Lord calls Ananias, an ordinary disciple of Damascus, who is understandably afraid: “Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he has done to your saints.” But the Lord has his own plans: “he is an instrument whom I have chosen to bring my name before Gentiles and kings and before the people of Israel.” Ananias goes, lays his hands on the man who came to arrest him, and his first word is: “Brother Saul.” Something like scales falls from Saul's eyes; he is baptized, he eats, and at once he proclaims Jesus in the synagogues: “He is the Son of God.” Saul does not leave the God of Israel; he meets the risen Messiah of Israel's God, and spends the rest of his life proclaiming him. St Paul himself retells this story twice in Acts (22 and 26) and in his letters (Galatians 1:15–16; 1 Corinthians 15:8–10). The Psalm, the shortest in the Bible, sums up his new mission: “Praise the LORD, all you nations!”

In the Gospel the Bread of Life discourse reaches its most realistic words. When Jesus says that the bread he will give is his flesh, his hearers in the synagogue of Capernaum dispute among themselves: “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” St John calls them “the Jews”; they are Jesus' own people, as he and all his disciples are, honestly puzzled by a hard saying, and the Church does not read such words as blame on the Jewish people (Catechism 597). What matters is that Jesus does not soften what he has said. He makes it stronger: “Very truly, I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.” In verse 54 St John even uses a Greek verb that means to chew or munch, as if to close every escape into mere symbolism. “For my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink.” The fruit of this food is communion: “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” And it is a sharing in the very life of the Trinity: “Just as the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever eats me will live because of me.” This is why the Church believes that in the Most Holy Eucharist “the body and blood, together with the soul and divinity, of our Lord Jesus Christ and, therefore, the whole Christ is truly, really, and substantially contained” (Catechism 1374). The two readings belong together. On the road to Damascus Jesus said: I am the one you are persecuting, because his disciples are his body. At the altar he says: this is my body, and he makes us his body by feeding us with it. St Augustine told the newly baptized, in substance: be what you see, and receive what you are.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saul was on his way to arrest Christians. Suddenly a light from heaven, and a voice: Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me? Saul asks: who are you, Lord? And the answer: I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting (Acts 9:4–5). Not my disciples. Me.

Jesus is so united with his people that what is done to them is done to him. When we hurt one another, we hurt Christ. When we serve the poor, we serve Christ. The Church is not a club of his admirers; it is his body.

Then comes Ananias, a simple disciple who is afraid of Saul, and rightly so. But he obeys. He goes into the house, lays his hands on the man who came to arrest people like him, and says: Brother Saul (Acts 9:17). With that one word the persecutor becomes family. Who is the Saul in your life to whom you need to say: brother, sister?

In the Gospel Jesus says the strongest words about the Eucharist: my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink. Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them (John 6:55–56). He does not say: this is like my body. He gives himself. So let us receive him with faith, and then go out to be his body for others. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Saul was mean to Christians. But Jesus loved him and spoke to him with a great light. Saul changed and became St Paul, one of Jesus' greatest friends! Jesus can change anyone's heart. And in Holy Communion Jesus gives us his own Body, so that he lives in us and we live in him.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you stopped Saul on the road to Damascus, opened his eyes and made the persecutor an apostle. Turn our hearts to you, give us the courage of Ananias to call our enemies brothers and sisters, and let us so receive your true Body and Blood that we may abide in you and you in us, now and for ever. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Friday brings the turning point of Saul’s life on the Damascus road, and the most direct words of the discourse at Capernaum: “my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink”. The two readings meet in one truth. Christ so joins himself to his own that to persecute them is to persecute him, and to eat his flesh is to abide in him. Psalm 117, the shortest psalm, sends us out: “Go out to all the world and tell the Good News”.

Acts 9:1–20 · Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?

Setting.

Saul of Tarsus was a Jew of the dispersion and a Pharisee, educated in Jerusalem (22:3; Philippians 3:5). He approved of Stephen’s death (8:1) and now asks the high priest for letters to the synagogues of Damascus, so as to bring back bound any who belong to “the Way”. Acts tells his conversion three times (here, 22:6–16 and 26:12–18), a sign of how much it mattered to Luke. Paul himself speaks of it briefly: God “was pleased to reveal his Son to me” (Galatians 1:15–16).

Many scholars prefer to speak of a call rather than a conversion, because Paul describes it in words that recall the call of Jeremiah (Galatians 1:15; Jeremiah 1:5). He did not leave the God of Israel; he came to know Jesus as Israel’s Messiah and Lord.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Saul sets out for Damascus.
  • vv. 3–9: light from heaven; “Saul, Saul”; three days without sight, food or drink.
  • vv. 10–16: the Lord sends Ananias, who protests and is answered.
  • vv. 17–20: “Brother Saul”; sight, baptism, food, and preaching that Jesus is the Son of God.

Key words.

  • hodos, the Way: One of the earliest names for the Christian movement in Acts (9:2; 19:9, 23; 22:4; 24:14, 22). Jesus had called himself the way (John 14:6).
  • skeuos eklogēs, an instrument whom I have chosen: Literally “a vessel of choice”, a chosen container or tool. Paul later says that we have this treasure in clay jars (2 Corinthians 4:7). The vessel is chosen for what it will carry: the name of Jesus.
  • adelphe, brother: Ananias’ greeting to the persecutor. Before the scales fall from Saul’s eyes, he has been received into a family.

Verse by verse.

“He fell to the ground and heard a voice saying to him, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?”” (9:4)

Not “my disciples”, but “me”. The risen Christ is one with his followers.

“He asked, “Who are you, Lord?” The reply came, “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” (9:5)

Paul’s whole later teaching on the Church as the body of Christ is folded into this answer.

“But Ananias answered, “Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he has done to your saints in Jerusalem;” (9:13)

Ananias is honest with the Lord. Obedience does not need to hide fear; it needs to go anyway.

“But the Lord said to him, “Go, for he is an instrument whom I have chosen to bring my name before Gentiles and kings and before the people of Israel;” (9:15)

The plan of Paul’s mission as Acts will tell it: Gentiles, kings and governors (Felix, Festus, Agrippa), and his own people, to whom he usually went first.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, preaching on the Ascension, says that Christ in heaven cried out on behalf of his body on earth: he did not say “why do you persecute my saints?” but “why do you persecute me?” (in substance). The Catechism teaches that Christ and his Church together make up the “whole Christ” (CCC 795). The Roman calendar keeps the Conversion of St Paul as a feast on 25 January. Ananias, of whom we know almost nothing else, reminds us that the Lord often works through simple disciples who are willing to knock on a dangerous door.

John 6:52–59 · My flesh is true food

Setting.

The hearers now argue among themselves: “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” The question is serious, and Jesus does not soften his words; he repeats them more strongly. John closes the discourse by naming its place: the synagogue at Capernaum (v. 59). In this chapter “the Jews” are the Galileans in that synagogue, not the Jewish people as such.

Structure.

  • v. 52: the dispute.
  • vv. 53–54: unless you eat and drink, you have no life; whoever eats has eternal life and will be raised.
  • vv. 55–57: true food and true drink; abiding; life from the Father, through the Son, to the one who eats.
  • vv. 58–59: this bread and the manna; the place.

Key words.

  • trōgōn, eating: From verse 54 John changes from the usual verb for eating to trōgō, a more physical word, used for chewing or munching. Many commentators see in this a refusal to let the words be taken as a mere figure.
  • alēthēs brōsis, true food: Not a picture of food but real food. Jesus answers the question “how can this man give us his flesh?” by saying that he will.
  • menei, abides: A favourite word of John. In the Eucharist Christ and the believer dwell in one another, as the branch abides in the vine (15:4).

Verse by verse.

““Very truly, I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.” (6:53)

For Jewish hearers the drinking of blood was forbidden (Leviticus 17:10–14), so these words were truly shocking. Jesus does not explain them away.

“for my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink.” (6:55)

The Church reads this verse as one of the plainest witnesses to the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist (CCC 1374, 1406).

“Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” (6:56)

The Catechism quotes this verse: Holy Communion deepens our union with Christ (CCC 1391).

“Just as the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever eats me will live because of me.” (6:57)

A chain of life: from the Father to the Son, from the Son to us.

How the Church reads it.

Early in the second century St Ignatius of Antioch called the Eucharist “the medicine of immortality” (Letter to the Ephesians 20), and wrote that some kept away from it because they would not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ (Letter to the Smyrnaeans 7). The Catechism places the words of John 6 at the centre of its teaching on the Eucharist (CCC 1338, 1406). Following St Paul VI, it explains that this presence is called “real” not to deny Christ’s other ways of being present, but because it is presence in the fullest sense (CCC 1374).

Questions people ask

Did Jesus mean his words only as a symbol?

If he had, it would have been easy to say so when many disciples left him (6:66). Instead he repeated them more strongly. From the earliest Fathers the Church has believed that the Eucharist is truly his Body and Blood, while the manner of this presence is a mystery that the senses cannot grasp and faith receives (CCC 1376, 1381).

For study and teaching

  • Jesus says to Saul, “why do you persecute me?” How does it change the way I treat others to know that Christ is one with them?
  • How can I prepare more carefully for Holy Communion this Sunday, knowing that in it I abide in Christ and he in me?
