Word for the day

“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”” Luke 1:38

Reflection

On the day after the Octave of Easter the Church goes back, as it were, nine months before Christmas, to the little house in Nazareth. This year the feast has waited for us. The Annunciation fell on Holy Thursday, and the Church does not let the joy of the Incarnation compete with the Supper, the Cross and the empty tomb; so she keeps it now, in the white light of Easter. The move is not an accident of the calendar but a lesson. The child announced by Gabriel is the same Lord who died and rose. The flesh he received from the Virgin Mary is the flesh that hung on the cross and walked out of the tomb on the third day. In the first reading King Ahaz, frightened by two enemy kings, refuses the sign that God offers him; it sounds pious, “I will not put the LORD to the test,” but it is really fear dressed up as piety. Isaiah answers: “the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel.” The Hebrew word, almah, means a young woman of marriageable age; the ancient Greek translation made by Jewish scholars rendered it parthenos, a virgin, and this is the text St Matthew quotes. The Church, with the whole tradition, reads the promise as fulfilled to the letter in the Virgin of Nazareth, and so the Lectionary proclaims it today: “the virgin shall conceive.” The reading ends with the name itself: “God is with us.”

The Letter to the Hebrews lets us hear what the Son said as he entered the world. It quotes our psalm: “Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body you have prepared for me… See, God, I have come to do your will.” The Annunciation has two yeses. Before Mary says “Here am I,” the eternal Son has said “See, I have come.” The Father prepares a body for him in the womb of the Virgin, and that body will be offered once for all on the cross: “it is by God's will that we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.” The Incarnation is already turned toward the Passover. That is why this feast sits so naturally in the Easter season: the Son took our flesh in order to offer it, and he offered it in order to raise it up. And every time we kneel at Mass and the priest says “This is my Body,” we are before the same body that began to exist in Mary's womb on this day.

Then the Gospel, which Christians have prayed in the Angelus three times a day for centuries. The angel Gabriel is sent “to a town in Galilee called Nazareth,” a village nobody had heard of, “to a virgin engaged to a man whose name was Joseph, of the house of David.” He greets her: “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.” The Greek word, kecharitomene, means one who has been and remains filled with grace; the Church sees here the grace that preserved Mary from all stain of sin from her conception. Mary is troubled and ponders; she does not doubt, she asks: “How can this be, since I am a virgin?” The answer reveals the whole Trinity: the Most High is the Father, the Son of the Most High is the child, and “the Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you.” That word overshadow is the word used for the cloud of God's glory that covered the tent of meeting. Mary becomes the new Ark, the place where God dwells. Gabriel adds a sign, her old relative Elizabeth expecting a child, “for nothing will be impossible with God.” And then the yes on which our salvation hung: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” In that moment, says the Creed, he “was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man.” That is why we kneel today when we say those words. The Easter Church kneels before the beginning of everything she celebrates.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today the Church does something unusual. In the middle of the Easter season she takes us back to Nazareth, to a quiet house and a young woman at prayer. The Annunciation fell on Holy Thursday this year, so it was moved to today. And how fitting it is: the child announced by the angel is the Lord who has just risen from the dead.

In the first reading King Ahaz is afraid. God offers him a sign, and he refuses with pious words. But God gives a sign anyway: the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall name him Emmanuel, God is with us (Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23). God does not wait for our perfect faith before he comes near.

The Letter to the Hebrews tells us what the Son said when he came into the world: a body you have prepared for me; see, God, I have come to do your will (Hebrews 10:5, 7). Before Mary said yes, the Son said yes. The body prepared in Mary's womb would one day be offered on the cross for us, and that same body rose on Easter morning.

Then the angel comes to Mary: Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you. Mary does not run away and does not pretend. She asks an honest question: how can this be? And the angel answers: the Holy Spirit will come upon you; nothing will be impossible with God. Then Mary says the most important yes in history: here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word (Luke 1:38).

Today, when we say the Creed, we will kneel at the words: and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man. We kneel because God became small for us. The great God who made the stars became a tiny child, hidden for nine months. And at this altar he becomes small again, hidden in the bread that is his Body.

So let us ask Mary today for her faith. Ahaz said no out of fear; Mary said yes out of love. Whatever God is asking of you this Easter, a forgiveness, a vocation, a child, a sacrifice, say with her: let it be with me according to your word. Holy Mary, rejoice, for your Son has risen. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, a long time ago an angel came to a girl named Mary. He said: God wants you to be the mother of his Son, Jesus. Mary said: yes, I am God's servant. And Jesus began to grow inside her, like every baby grows inside its mother.

Today at Mass, when we say “and became man,” we kneel down to say thank you to Jesus for becoming a baby for us. Let us say yes to God like Mary!

Prayer

O God, who willed that your Word should take on the reality of human flesh in the womb of the Virgin Mary, grant, we pray, that we, who confess our Redeemer to be God and man, may merit to become partakers even in his divine nature. Who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, Word made flesh, you entered our world through the yes of your Mother and rose from the dead in the same flesh you took from her. Give us her faith, that we may hear your word and keep it, and her courage, that we may say each day: let it be with me according to your word. Holy Mary, Mother of God, rejoice, for your Son has risen. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This year 25 March fell on Holy Thursday. When the Annunciation falls in Holy Week or the Easter Octave, the Roman calendar moves it to the Monday after the Second Sunday of Easter, and so the Church celebrates it today, in the white of Easter. It is a solemnity of the Lord: the day the Word became flesh in the womb of the Virgin Mary. The Gloria is sung, and in the Creed all kneel at the words “and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man”.

The readings are built on two answers of “Here I am”. Isaiah promises King Ahaz a sign he did not want: a young woman who will bear a son called Immanuel, God with us. Psalm 40 sings, “Here I am; in the scroll of the book it is written of me”, and the Letter to the Hebrews puts those words on the lips of the Son entering the world: “See, I have come to do your will.” In the Gospel Mary answers the angel: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord.” The Son's yes to the Father and Mary's yes to God meet in one moment. The key words are sign, body and yes.

Isaiah 7:10–14; 8:10 · God is with us

Setting.

About the year 734 BC the kings of Aram (Damascus) and of the northern kingdom of Israel marched against Jerusalem to force Judah into their alliance against Assyria. King Ahaz of Judah, a descendant of David, was terrified: “his heart and the heart of his people shook as the trees of the forest shake before the wind” (7:2). Isaiah went to meet him with God's word: stand firm and do not fear. Ahaz had in fact already decided to buy the protection of the king of Assyria (2 Kings 16:7–8).

The Lectionary adds a single verse from the next chapter, 8:10, which ends with the meaning of the child's name: “for God is with us”.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: God offers Ahaz a sign, as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven.
  • v. 12: Ahaz refuses, with pious words.
  • v. 13: Isaiah's rebuke to the house of David.
  • v. 14: the Lord himself will give a sign: a young woman, a son, Immanuel.
  • 8:10: the plans of the nations will fail, for God is with us.

Key words.

  • ’ôt, sign: In Isaiah a sign is a visible pledge that God will keep his word. Ahaz is offered any sign he likes, and refuses, because he does not want to depend on God.
  • ‘almâ, young woman: The Hebrew word means a young woman of marriageable age; it does not in itself say “virgin”. The Greek Old Testament, made by Jewish translators about two centuries before Christ, rendered it parthenos, “virgin”, and Matthew quotes that form (Matthew 1:23). The Lectionary follows it, and the NRSV prints the Hebrew sense.
  • ‘immānû ’ēl, Immanuel, God is with us: The name is a short confession of faith. In Isaiah it promises God's presence with the house of David in danger. In Christ, God is with us in person.

Verse by verse.

“Ask a sign of the Lord your God; let it be deep as Sheol or high as heaven.” (7:11)

God's offer is limitless: from the depths of the underworld to the heights of the sky. St Irenaeus saw in this range a hint of the Son who would come down from heaven and go down even to the dead (in substance).

“But Ahaz said, I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test.” (7:12)

The words sound devout, and they quote the law (Deuteronomy 6:16). But Ahaz is not being humble. He has already put his trust in Assyria, and he does not want God to interfere.

“Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel.” (7:14)

Many scholars think that in Isaiah's own day the sign was the birth of a royal child, perhaps the future king Hezekiah, which showed that God had not abandoned David's line. The Church, following Matthew, sees the full meaning of the prophecy in the virgin birth of Jesus, the son of David who is truly God with us.

“speak a word, but it will not stand, for God is with us.” (8:10)

The nations make plans, and God's presence brings them to nothing. The verse is the name Immanuel spelled out.

How the Church reads it.

St Matthew sees this prophecy fulfilled when Jesus is conceived of the Virgin Mary by the Holy Spirit (Matthew 1:22–23). The Catechism teaches that the virginal conception is a divine work beyond human understanding and possibility, and cites Matthew's reading of Isaiah (CCC 497). The Second Vatican Council names this text among the Old Testament passages that, read in the light of the fuller revelation, bring the figure of the Mother of the Redeemer gradually into clearer light (Lumen Gentium 55). The king refused the sign; the Virgin, as the Gospel shows, received it.

Psalm 40:7–8a, 8b–9, 10, 11 (NRSV 40:6–10) · Here I am; I come to do your will

Setting.

Psalm 40 is the song of someone whom God has lifted out of “the desolate pit” (v. 2). Having been rescued, he asks what offering he should bring, and learns that God wants more than animals on the altar. He wants a person who listens and obeys. This teaching is found in the prophets too (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6). Liturgical books count the psalm's title as verse 1, so verses 7–11 there are verses 6–10 in the NRSV.

Structure.

  • v. 6: sacrifice and offering you do not desire, but you have given me an open ear.
  • vv. 7–8: “Here I am”; the scroll; your law is within my heart.
  • vv. 9–10: I have told the glad news in the great congregation; I have not hidden your saving help.

Key words.

  • ’oznayim kārîtā lî, you have given me an open ear: Literally “ears you have dug for me”: you have opened a channel for your word. The Greek Old Testament, followed by Hebrews 10:5, has “a body you have prepared for me”. The ear that hears and the body that offers itself are two sides of the same obedience.
  • hinnēh-bā’tî, here I am (I have come): The word of a servant presenting himself for duty. Abraham, Moses, Samuel and Isaiah answer God with the same readiness.

Verse by verse.

“Sacrifice and offering you do not desire, but you have given me an open ear.” (40:6)

The psalm does not condemn Israel's worship. It says what all worship is for: a listening heart.

“Then I said, “Here I am; in the scroll of the book it is written of me.” (40:7)

The psalmist finds his life already written in God's word. The Church hears Christ say this of himself, for the whole of Scripture speaks of him (Luke 24:44).

“I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart.”” (40:8)

The law written on the heart is the promise of the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:33). Obedience here is not a burden but a delight.

How the Church reads it.

The Letter to the Hebrews reads these verses as the words of Christ at the moment of the Incarnation (Hebrews 10:5–7), and the Catechism takes them up in the same way (CCC 462). On this feast the psalm becomes the prayer that unites the Son's obedience and Mary's: the response “Here I am, Lord; I come to do your will” is sung by the whole Church.

Hebrews 10:4–10 · A body you have prepared for me

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews was written to Christians, very likely of Jewish background, who were tempted to look back to the Temple and its sacrifices. Its author is unknown; the early Church often linked it with St Paul, though its style is very different from his letters. Chapters 8 to 10 compare the yearly sacrifices of the Day of Atonement with the one offering of Christ. Here the author quotes Psalm 40 in its Greek form and hears it as Christ's own prayer on coming into the world.

Structure.

  • v. 4: the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins.
  • vv. 5–7: Christ's words on coming into the world: a body you have prepared for me; I have come to do your will.
  • vv. 8–9: the first is taken away so that the second may stand.
  • v. 10: by that will we are sanctified through the offering of Jesus' body once for all.

Key words.

  • sōma, body: The Greek of Psalm 40 has “a body” where the Hebrew has “ears”. The author sees in it the Incarnation: the Son takes a body in order to offer it.
  • thelēma, will: The word appears three times in these verses. The heart of Christ's sacrifice is not the suffering in itself but loving obedience to the Father's will.
  • ephapax, once for all: Christ's offering is unique and complete. It does not need to be repeated, and nothing can be added to it.

Verse by verse.

“For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.” (10:4)

The sacrifices of the law were given by God and were truly holy. But they pointed beyond themselves to the offering that would do what they could only promise.

“Consequently, when Christ came into the world, he said, “Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body you have prepared for me;” (10:5)

The Annunciation is the moment of these words. In Mary's womb the Son receives the body he will offer on the Cross.

“then he added, “See, I have come to do your will.” He abolishes the first in order to establish the second.” (10:9)

“The first” is the order of animal sacrifices; “the second” is the doing of God's will by the Son. Israel's worship is not despised. It reaches its goal.

“And it is by God’s will that we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.” (10:10)

The body that began in Nazareth is the body offered on Calvary and given in the Eucharist. From the first moment to the last, Christ's life is one offering.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage when it teaches why the Word became flesh (CCC 462), and when it speaks of Christ's whole life as an offering to the Father, from his entry into the world to the Cross (CCC 606, 614). The Mass is not another sacrifice added to this one. It makes present the one sacrifice of the Cross, offered once for all (CCC 1366–1367). On this feast the Church sees the beginning and the end of Christ's offering together: “See, I have come” in Nazareth, and “It is finished” on Golgotha.

Luke 1:26–38 · Here am I, the servant of the Lord

Setting.

Luke tells two annunciations side by side. Six months earlier the angel Gabriel appeared to Zechariah, a priest, in the Temple of Jerusalem, at the hour of incense (1:5–25). Now he is sent to a young woman in Nazareth, an unknown village in Galilee. Zechariah doubted and was struck silent; Mary asks a question and believes. The parallel is deliberate.

Mary is betrothed to Joseph. In Jewish custom of the time betrothal was a binding agreement, already a kind of marriage, though the bride did not yet live in her husband's house. Joseph is “of the house of David”, and so the child will legally belong to David's line.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: the sixth month; Gabriel; Nazareth; a virgin betrothed to Joseph; her name was Mary.
  • vv. 28–29: “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you”; she is perplexed.
  • vv. 30–33: do not be afraid; you will conceive and bear a son, Jesus; the throne of David; a kingdom without end.
  • vv. 34–35: “How can this be?”; the Holy Spirit, the power of the Most High; Son of God.
  • vv. 36–37: Elizabeth; nothing is impossible with God.
  • v. 38: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”

Key words.

  • kecharitōmenē, favored one, full of grace: A perfect passive participle of the verb “to grace”: one who has been graced and remains so. It stands in place of her name. The Church sees in it the fullness of grace given to Mary from the first moment of her existence (CCC 490–491).
  • episkiasei, will overshadow: The same verb the Greek Old Testament uses for the cloud of God's glory that settled on the tent of meeting (Exodus 40:35), and Luke uses it again of the cloud at the Transfiguration (9:34). Mary becomes the dwelling of God's presence.
  • doulē, servant: The feminine of the word for slave or servant. Mary takes the title of Israel's great servants of God. Her true liberty is to belong wholly to God.

Verse by verse.

“And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”” (1:28)

The Greek greeting chaire means literally “rejoice”. Many scholars hear in it the prophets' call to Daughter Zion: “Rejoice … the Lord is in your midst” (Zephaniah 3:14–17). The Lord who was promised to dwell in Zion is about to dwell in her.

“He will be great, and will be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God will give to him the throne of his ancestor David.” (1:32)

The words take up God's promise to David through the prophet Nathan (2 Samuel 7:12–16). What Ahaz would not believe is now fulfilled: the house of David has its everlasting king.

“Mary said to the angel, “How can this be, since I am a virgin?”” (1:34)

Mary does not doubt the promise. She asks how it will happen. Many Fathers, among them St Augustine, read in her question an intention to remain a virgin (in substance). The angel's answer shows that this child will come from God alone.

“The angel said to her, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” (1:35)

The Spirit who hovered over the waters at creation now comes upon Mary for the new creation. The conception of Jesus is the work of the Holy Spirit, not of any man.

“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Then the angel departed from her.” (1:38)

Her yes is given in faith, without knowing what it will cost. The Greek verb genoito expresses a wish: not resignation, but glad desire.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that at the Annunciation Mary gave the consent of faith, and that by this yes she became the Mother of God and gave herself entirely to the person and work of her Son (CCC 494–495). It quotes St Irenaeus: the knot of Eve's disobedience was untied by Mary's obedience (CCC 494; Lumen Gentium 56). It also teaches the virginal conception as the work of the Spirit (CCC 484–486, 497), and sees in Mary's “let it be with me” at the Annunciation the heart of Christian prayer (CCC 2617). St Bernard, in a homily on this Gospel, pictures the whole world waiting on Mary's answer and begging her to reply (in substance). The Church repeats this mystery three times a day in the Angelus, which in Eastertide gives way to the Regina caeli.

Questions people ask

Why does the NRSV say “young woman” in Isaiah 7:14 when the Lectionary says “virgin”?

The Hebrew word ‘almâ means a young woman of marriageable age. The Greek translation made by Jews before Christ rendered it “virgin” (parthenos), and Matthew quotes it in that form. The NRSV translates the Hebrew; the Lectionary follows the Greek and Matthew. The Church's faith in the virginal conception rests on the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, which say plainly that Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit without a human father (CCC 496–497). Isaiah's sign finds its fullest meaning there.

Why is the Annunciation a feast of the Lord rather than of Mary?

Because it celebrates the Incarnation: the Word made flesh. Mary is at the centre of the Gospel scene, and her yes is honoured, but the solemnity is the beginning of our salvation in Christ. Since the reform of the calendar in 1969 it has been called the Annunciation of the Lord. Christmas, nine months later, completes it.

Does “full of grace” mean Mary did not need a Saviour?

No. Mary was redeemed by Christ, in a more exalted way: she was preserved from all stain of original sin from the first moment of her conception, by the merits of her Son (Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854; CCC 491–492). Mary herself calls God “my Saviour” (Luke 1:47).

For the Latin reader

Because this solemnity falls in Eastertide this year, the double Alleluia continues at the dismissal and the paschal candle stands lit beside the altar or ambo. Today, at the words of the Creed “and by the Holy Spirit was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man”, the people kneel, as they do on Christmas Day. In the Roman tradition this feast also turns our thoughts to the dignity of human life from its beginning, for the Son of God was himself an unborn child. Families might pray the Regina caeli together today and remember that the Easter joy began in a quiet house in Nazareth.

For study and teaching

  • Ahaz refused a sign because he had already made his own plans. Where do I avoid asking God, so that he will not interfere?
  • Mary asked, “How can this be?” and then said yes. How do I bring my honest questions to God without closing my heart?
  • Christ's first words on entering the world were “I have come to do your will”. What would change if I began each day with those words?
  • The Word became flesh in an unborn child. How does this feast shape the way I treat the unborn, the poor and the forgotten?
