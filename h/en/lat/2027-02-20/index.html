Word for the day

“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,” Matthew 5:44

Reflection

Moses, at the end of his long journey with Israel, describes the covenant as a solemn exchange of promises made “this very day”. “Today you have obtained the LORD's agreement: to be your God; and for you to walk in his ways… Today the LORD has obtained your agreement: to be his treasured people.” It sounds almost like a marriage vow. God binds himself to Israel, and Israel binds itself to God. The word “treasured” translates the Hebrew segullah, the private treasure a king keeps for himself apart from the state treasury. Israel is God's personal treasure, set “high above all nations”, not for pride but to be “a people holy to the LORD your God”. Every Christian has made this same exchange of promises at baptism, and every Mass renews it. In Lent we are invited to say again, “today”, that we belong to him and will walk in his ways.

Jesus now shows how far the ways of God reach. “You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.’ But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the righteous and on the unrighteous.” The sun does not choose whom to warm. If we love only those who love us, Jesus says, we are doing nothing more than tax collectors and pagans. Then comes the summit of the Sermon: “Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect.” This perfection is not flawlessness but completeness of love: a love that, like the Father's, excludes no one. It is impossible on our own; it is the work of grace in God's treasured people. As the first week of Lent ends, we bring to God the one person we find hardest to love, and we pray for them by name.

Full homily

Dear friends, Moses tells Israel: today you have obtained the LORD's agreement: to be your God… Today the LORD has obtained your agreement: to be his treasured people (Deuteronomy 26:17–18). It is like a marriage vow between God and his people.

At baptism we made the same covenant. We are God's treasured people. Lent is the time to say again, today: I am yours, and I will walk in your ways.

Jesus shows us how far God's ways reach: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good (Matthew 5:44–45).

Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect (Matthew 5:48). Not flawless, but complete in love. As this first week of Lent ends, let us pray by name for the one person we find hardest to love. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: love even people who are unkind to you, and pray for them. The sun shines on everyone! Tonight, pray for someone who was not nice to you.

Prayer

Father in heaven, you make your sun rise on the evil and on the good, and you have chosen us as your treasured people. Renew in us today the promises of our baptism. Give us a heart like yours, that we may love our enemies, pray for those who persecute us, and grow towards the perfection of your love. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The first week of Lent ends with a covenant and a family likeness. In Deuteronomy God and Israel bind themselves to each other “today”, and Israel becomes his treasured people. In Matthew Jesus shows what the children of such a Father look like: they love even their enemies, because that is what he does. The key words are today, treasured and perfect.

Deuteronomy 26:16–19 · A people holy to the Lord your God

Setting.

Deuteronomy is presented as Moses’ last address to Israel on the plains of Moab, before they cross into the land. Chapters 12–26 give the long code of laws. Today’s four verses are its closing lines. After all the statutes, Moses reminds the people what they mean: a relationship between God and his people.

Structure.

  • v. 16: the command: observe with all your heart and soul.
  • v. 17: what Israel has declared about God.
  • vv. 18–19: what God has declared about Israel.
  • ¶ It is laid out like a treaty, with each side making a solemn statement of what it will be for the other.

Key words.

  • hayyôm, today: A favourite word of Deuteronomy, and it comes three times here. Every generation that hears these words stands at the covenant “today”. Psalm 95 and Hebrews 3–4 take it up in the same way.
  • he’ĕmartā, you have obtained the agreement: A rare verb form, found in this sense only here (vv. 17–18), and its exact sense is debated. The NRSV’s “obtained the agreement” and the POC’s “declared” (പ്രഖ്യാപിച്ചിരിക്കുന്നു) are both attempts to render a solemn exchange of promises.
  • segullâ, treasured people: A personal treasure, such as a king’s private wealth (1 Chronicles 29:3). It is used for Israel in Exodus 19:5, Deuteronomy 7:6 and Malachi 3:17.

Verse by verse.

“so observe them diligently with all your heart and with all your soul.” (26:16)

These are the words of the great commandment (Deuteronomy 6:5). The laws are to be kept not as an outward task but with one’s whole self.

“Today the Lord has obtained your agreement: to be his treasured people, as he promised you, and to keep his commandments;” (26:18)

Being treasured comes first, and it comes as a promise. The commandments are how the treasured live.

“and for you to be a people holy to the Lord your God, as he promised.” (26:19)

Holiness here is a promise and not only a demand: “as he promised”. And the honor Israel receives among the nations (v. 19) belongs with this holiness: it is a people set high because it belongs to God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the commandments express the implications of belonging to God through the covenant, and that moral existence is a response to the Lord’s loving initiative (CCC 2062). The Second Vatican Council, speaking of the People of God, begins from God’s choice of Israel, and applies to the Church the words of 1 Peter 2:9, “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God’s own people” (Lumen Gentium 9; CCC 781–782). The covenant with Israel is not thereby revoked (CCC 121, 839).

Matthew 5:43–48 · Children of your Father in heaven

Setting.

This is the last of the six “You have heard… But I say to you” sayings, and the peak of them all. Love of neighbor is commanded in Leviticus 19:18. The words “hate your enemy” are not found in the Law. Jesus seems to be quoting a popular reading of the command, which took “neighbor” to imply its opposite. Many scholars also point to a text from Qumran that speaks of hating the sons of darkness.

Jewish Scripture itself had already pointed in Jesus’ direction: help your enemy’s stray animal (Exodus 23:4–5), and feed your enemy when he is hungry (Proverbs 25:21–22, quoted in Romans 12:20).

Structure.

  • v. 43: the saying they have heard.
  • v. 44: the command: love and pray.
  • v. 45: the reason: the Father’s sun and rain.
  • vv. 46–47: two questions: what more are you doing?
  • v. 48: the summary.

Key words.

  • agapate, love: Not a feeling of liking, which cannot be ordered. It is a will for the other’s good, shown in action. Jesus does not ask us to feel warmly about those who harm us.
  • echthroi, enemies: This is not only personal rivals. In the setting of v. 44, it includes those who persecute the disciples.
  • teleios, perfect: Not flawless, but whole, complete, undivided. Behind it may stand the Hebrew tāmîm, used in Deuteronomy 18:13. Luke’s parallel has “merciful” (Luke 6:36), and that tells us which perfection is meant.

Verse by verse.

“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,” (5:44)

Prayer is where this love begins, and where it may have to stay for a long time. Christians suffer for their faith in many lands. The Lord’s command is to pray for them and for those who wrong them. We can pray for someone whom we cannot yet feel any warmth toward.

“so that you may be children of your Father in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good,” (5:45)

The reason is a family likeness. Children of this Father look like him. The sun is the great daily sermon on the mercy of God.

“For if you love those who love you, what reward do you have? Do not even the tax collectors do the same?” (5:46)

Jesus uses the people his hearers looked down on, and he himself ate with them (Matthew 9:10–13). The question is simple: what is different about you?

“Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect.” (5:48)

Compare “You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy” (Leviticus 19:2). The command is impossible by human strength, and that is why the Sermon also teaches us to ask.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christ died for us while we were still enemies (Romans 5:10), and that the Lord asks us to love as he does, even our enemies (CCC 1825). The “as” of Matthew 5:48 cannot mean imitating God from outside, but only sharing, in the depths of the heart, in the holiness and mercy of God (CCC 2842). The Council teaches that this perfection is for all the baptized (Lumen Gentium 40; CCC 2013). The first martyr followed his Lord’s example: “Lord, do not hold this sin against them” (Acts 7:60).

Questions people ask

Can anyone really be perfect? Is Jesus setting us up to fail?

He is setting a direction and a likeness, not a score to reach by ourselves. Perfection in Matthew means the wholeness of a love with no one left out. It grows through grace all through a life, and we always need the petition “forgive us our debts”. The saints did not think they had arrived, and that is part of why we call them saints.

For study and teaching

  • Moses tells Israel that it is God’s “treasured people”. How would my obedience change if I began from that?
  • Name one person who is hard for me to love. Can I pray for that person by name every day for the rest of Lent?
