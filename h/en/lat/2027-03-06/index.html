Readings

  • First Reading Hosea 6:1–6 · I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings
  • Responsorial Psalm Psalm 51:1–2, 16–17, 18–19 · It is mercy I desire, and not sacrifice
  • Gospel Luke 18:9–14 · The tax collector went home justified rather than the Pharisee

Word for the day

“But the tax collector, standing far off, would not even look up to heaven, but was beating his breast and saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’” Luke 18:13

Reflection

The first reading opens with a beautiful song of repentance: “Come, let us return to the LORD; for it is he who has torn, and he will heal us… After two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up.” The Fathers of the Church heard in these words a hidden promise of the Resurrection on the third day. But God's reply is sobering: “What shall I do with you, O Ephraim?… Your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes away early.” The people's repentance is sincere for an hour and then evaporates in the sun. God wants something deeper and more lasting: “For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.” The Hebrew word for steadfast love, hesed, means faithful, covenant love, the kind that does not dry up by noon. Jesus himself quoted this verse twice when he was criticized for eating with sinners: “Go and learn what this means, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’” The Psalm, David's great prayer of repentance, gives us the words: “The sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise.”

In the Gospel two men go up to the Temple to pray. The Pharisee stands by himself and gives thanks, but only for himself: “God, I thank you that I am not like other people: thieves, rogues, adulterers, or even like this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of all my income.” Everything he says is true. He really fasts and really gives. But he has come to God to be admired, and he looks down on his neighbour. The tax collector, a man despised as a collaborator with Rome, stands far off, will not lift his eyes, beats his breast, and says only: “God, be merciful to me, a sinner!” Jesus says this man “went down to his home justified rather than the other.” The parable is told, Luke says, “to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt.” It is a warning to every devout person, especially in Lent, when our fasting and almsgiving can quietly become a mirror. The tax collector's prayer has become one of the treasures of the Church: the Jesus Prayer of the East, “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner,” grew from it, and at every Mass we strike our breast with him at the Confiteor. On this Saturday before Laetare Sunday, it is a good day to go to confession and to pray his prayer.

Full homily

Dear friends, the people in Hosea sing: come, let us return to the LORD; for it is he who has torn, and he will heal us (Hosea 6:1). It sounds like a perfect repentance. But God answers: your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes away early (Hosea 6:4). Their conversion lasted an hour.

God wants more: for I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings (Hosea 6:6). Faithful love, not religious display. Jesus repeated these words when people complained that he ate with sinners.

In the Gospel two men pray in the Temple. The Pharisee says: God, I thank you that I am not like other people… I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of all my income (Luke 18:11–12). What he says is true, but he prays to a mirror. The tax collector stands far off, beats his breast, and says: God, be merciful to me, a sinner! (Luke 18:13).

Jesus says the tax collector went down to his home justified, for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted (Luke 18:14). This weekend, let us bring that prayer to the confessional. God does not despise a broken and contrite heart. Amen.

For children

Children, two men prayed in church. One said: God, I am better than everybody! The other said: God, I am sorry, please be kind to me. God loved the humble prayer. Let us say tonight: Jesus, I am sorry, please be kind to me.

Prayer

Merciful God, you desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, and you do not despise a broken and contrite heart. Free us from pride and from contempt for others; make our love faithful and not like the morning dew; and let us go down to our homes justified, praying with the tax collector: God, be merciful to me, a sinner. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.
