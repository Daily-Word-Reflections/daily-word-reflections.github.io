Word for the day

“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” Matthew 5:16

Reflection

Paul had promised the Corinthians a visit and then changed his plans. Some of them said at once: you see, he is a man who says yes and no in the same breath; how can we trust his gospel? Paul answers the accusation, but he lifts it to a much higher level. His own travel plans may change; God's word does not. “As surely as God is faithful, our word to you has not been ‘Yes and No.’ For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, whom we proclaimed among you, Silvanus and Timothy and I, was not ‘Yes and No’; but in him it is always ‘Yes.’” Then comes one of the great sentences of the New Testament: “For in him every one of God's promises is a ‘Yes.’ For this reason it is through him that we say the ‘Amen,’ to the glory of God.” Every promise God made to Abraham, to Moses, to David and to the prophets finds its fulfilment in Jesus. He is God's Yes to the world. And when the Church answers at the end of the Eucharistic Prayer with the great Amen, she is saying her own yes through him. Then Paul uses words that the Church has always heard as a description of Baptism and Confirmation: “But it is God who establishes us with you in Christ and has anointed us, by putting his seal on us and giving us his Spirit in our hearts as a first installment.” Anointed, sealed, given the Spirit as a first installment, a pledge of the full inheritance to come. The Catechism (1296) quotes exactly these words when it explains the seal of Confirmation. The Psalm, a verse from the long praise of God's law, prays like a disciple who has tasted that faithfulness: “The unfolding of your words gives light; it imparts understanding to the simple,” and “Make your face shine upon your servant.”

Yesterday Jesus spoke the Beatitudes. Today he tells the same people, the poor, the meek, the persecuted, who they are: “You are the salt of the earth.” “You are the light of the world.” He does not say: try to become salt, or one day you may be light. He says: you are. It is a gift before it is a task. Salt in the ancient world preserved food from going bad and gave it taste; it was also used with sacrifices and in making covenants. A little salt changes a whole dish. But “if salt has lost its taste, how can its saltiness be restored? It is no longer good for anything.” Light is useless if it is hidden: “No one after lighting a lamp puts it under the bushel basket, but on the lampstand, and it gives light to all in the house.” Every Kerala home knows this. When the monsoon storms cut the electricity, someone lights the nilavilakku or a kerosene lamp and sets it in the middle of the room, not under the cot. Jesus' last word explains the purpose of the light: “so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” Our good works are not meant to make people admire us but to make them praise God. The disciple is like the moon, which has no light of its own but reflects the sun. Paul's “Yes” in Christ and Jesus' salt and light belong together: the Christian sealed by the Spirit is God's Yes made visible in a village, an office, a classroom.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul had told the Corinthians he would visit them, and then he changed his plans. Some people immediately said: this man cannot be trusted; he says yes and no at the same time. Paul answers: my travel plans may change, but the message I preached never changes, because it is Jesus Christ, and in him it is always Yes (2 Corinthians 1:19).

For in him every one of God's promises is a Yes (2 Corinthians 1:20). Think of all God's promises in the Old Testament: a land, a descendant, a king, a new covenant, a new heart. In Jesus they are all fulfilled. He is God's great Yes to humanity. And at every Mass, when the priest lifts up the Body and Blood of Christ, we answer Amen. That Amen is our yes to God's Yes.

Paul also says that God has anointed us, put his seal on us and given us his Spirit in our hearts (2 Corinthians 1:21–22). At Baptism and Confirmation we were anointed with holy chrism and sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit. A seal is a mark of ownership. We belong to God. The Spirit in us is like an advance payment, a first installment of the joy of heaven.

And Jesus tells us what that means in daily life: you are the salt of the earth; you are the light of the world (Matthew 5:13–14). Not: you should become. You are. A Christian who hides his faith is like a lamp under a basket. In these monsoon nights, when the power goes off, we know how precious one small lamp is. Let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven (Matthew 5:16). Amen.

For children

Children, what happens if you forget to put salt in the curry? It tastes flat! And what happens when the electricity goes off at night? Everyone looks for a lamp. Jesus said to his friends: you are salt, and you are light. That means a Christian child makes things better, like salt in food, and helps others see God's goodness, like a lamp in the dark. When you share, help, and speak kindly, you are shining Jesus' light!

Prayer

Faithful God, in your Son Jesus Christ every one of your promises is Yes. We thank you for anointing us, for setting your seal upon us and giving us your Spirit in our hearts. Keep us from being people of yes and no, who promise and do not keep their word. Lord Jesus, you call us the salt of the earth and the light of the world; do not let us lose our taste or hide our light. Let our good works shine, so that all who see them may give glory to our Father in heaven. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul had changed his travel plans, and some in Corinth said he was a man of “Yes and No”, unreliable. He answers that his word is not double, because the Son of God he preached is God’s great “Yes” to all his promises. In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples who they are: salt of the earth, light of the world. Both readings speak of a life that is reliable and visible. Psalm 119 prays: let your face shine on your servant.

2 Corinthians 1:18–22 · In him it is always “Yes”

Setting.

Paul had promised a double visit to Corinth, on the way to Macedonia and back again (1:15–16). He changed his plan, to spare them another painful visit (1:23). His critics used the change to say that he could not be trusted. Paul replies not only with an explanation but with theology: the faithfulness of God stands behind the preacher and his message.

Structure.

  • v. 18: God is faithful; our word to you has not been “Yes and No”.
  • vv. 19–20: Jesus Christ, whom Paul, Silvanus and Timothy preached, is God’s “Yes”; through him we say “Amen”.
  • vv. 21–22: God establishes us in Christ, anoints us, seals us and gives us the Spirit as a first installment.

Key words.

  • Amēn, Amen: A Hebrew word meaning firm, sure, reliable. To say Amen is to lean one’s whole weight on God’s word. Christ is God’s Yes; the Church answers Amen.
  • chrisas, having anointed: Paul plays on words: God establishes us “in Christ” (Christos, the Anointed One) and has “anointed” us (chrisas). Christians share in the anointing of the Messiah.
  • arrabōn, first installment, pledge: A word from trade, borrowed from the Semitic languages: a down payment that guarantees the full price will follow. The Spirit given now is the pledge of the glory to come.

Verse by verse.

“As surely as God is faithful, our word to you has not been “Yes and No.”” (1:18)

Paul swears, in effect, by God’s faithfulness. The reliability of the messenger matters because he carries the word of a faithful God.

“For in him every one of God’s promises is a “Yes.”” (1:20)

All the promises made to Abraham, to David and through the prophets find their fulfilment in Christ. God has not changed his mind about any of them.

“For this reason it is through him that we say the “Amen,” to the glory of God.” (1:20)

Probably a reference to the Amen of the community at worship. When the people answer “Amen” at the end of the Eucharistic Prayer, they say this same word through Christ to the Father.

“by putting his seal on us and giving us his Spirit in our hearts as a first installment.” (1:22)

A seal marked ownership and protection. Paul does not name a rite here, but the Church has always heard in these words Baptism and the anointing with the Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it explains the seal of Confirmation: Christians are marked with the seal of the Spirit, who establishes them in Christ (CCC 1296). It also teaches that Jesus Christ himself is the “Amen” (Revelation 3:14), the definitive Amen of the Father’s love for us (CCC 1065). So the simple Amen said at Mass, and especially before receiving Holy Communion, is a small act of faith with a long history: a Yes to the One who is God’s Yes.

Matthew 5:13–16 · Salt of the earth, light of the world

Setting.

Right after the Beatitudes Jesus turns to the disciples and says “you are”. He does not say “try to become” salt and light. Those who live the Beatitudes already are salt and light, and they must not lose what they are. Salt and light were ordinary things in every Galilean house: salt to preserve and to season, an oil lamp to light the one room where the family lived.

Structure.

  • v. 13: you are the salt of the earth; salt that loses its taste is thrown out.
  • v. 14: you are the light of the world; a city on a hill cannot be hidden.
  • vv. 15–16: a lamp is put on a lampstand; let your light shine so that others give glory to the Father.

Key words.

  • halas, salt: Salt preserved food from decay and gave it flavour. In Israel’s worship every offering was seasoned with “the salt of the covenant” (Leviticus 2:13).
  • mōranthē, has lost its taste: The Greek verb literally means “become foolish”. The salt of the Dead Sea region, mixed with other minerals, could lose its saltiness. A disciple who loses the wisdom of the Gospel becomes useless in the same way.
  • modios, bushel basket: A grain measure holding about nine litres. Putting it over a lamp would put the lamp out.

Verse by verse.

“You are the salt of the earth;” (5:13)

A small group of fishermen and ordinary people are told that they matter for the whole earth. The mission is universal from the start.

“You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” (5:14)

Jesus calls himself the light of the world in John 8:12; here he shares the title with his disciples. Their light is his light reflected. Many commentators also hear Isaiah’s vision of Zion on the mountain, to which the nations come (Isaiah 2:2–3).

“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” (5:16)

The aim is not that people praise us, but that they praise the Father. Later in the Sermon Jesus warns against doing good in order to be seen (6:1); the difference is where the glory goes.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council took its great document on the Church from this image: Christ is the light of the nations, Lumen Gentium, and that light shines on the face of the Church. The Catechism, describing the People of God, says that its mission is to be salt of the earth and light of the world (CCC 782). The Council also teaches that lay people make Christ known to others above all by the witness of their lives (Lumen Gentium 31). A Christian home, an honest office, a kind classroom are lamps on the lampstand.

Questions people ask

Does “let your light shine” contradict Jesus’ warning not to practise our piety before others (Matthew 6:1)?

No. Both sayings ask the same question: for whose glory? Good works are to be seen so that people give glory to the Father. Piety done in order to be admired by others has already received its reward. The disciple neither hides the light nor puts himself in the spotlight.

For study and teaching

  • Where do I need to keep my word more faithfully, so that my “Yes” means yes?
  • In what place of my daily life is God asking me to be salt or light?
