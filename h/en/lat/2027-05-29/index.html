Word for the day

“More to be desired are they than gold, even much fine gold; sweeter also than honey, and drippings of the honeycomb.” Psalm 19:10

Reflection

The Book of Sirach ends with a short autobiography in the form of a poem. The old teacher looks back and tells his students how he found wisdom. It began with thanksgiving: he blesses the name of the Lord who rescued him in time of trouble. Then he remembers his youth: “While I was still young, before I went on my travels, I sought wisdom openly in my prayer. Before the temple I asked for her, and I will search for her until the end.” Wisdom is not first a set of clever ideas; it is something asked for in prayer, before God's house. He uses the image of a vine: “From the first blossom to the ripening grape my heart delighted in her; my foot walked on the straight path; from my youth I followed her steps.” Growth was slow but steady: “I inclined my ear a little and received her, and I found for myself much instruction. I made progress in her; to him who gives wisdom I will give glory.” It also took effort: “My soul grappled with wisdom, and in my conduct I was strict.” And the fruit was purity of heart: “I directed my soul to her, and in purity I found her.” The Psalm praises God's law in the same spirit: “the precepts of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart; the commandment of the LORD is clear, enlightening the eyes,” “sweeter also than honey, and drippings of the honeycomb.” As schools in Kerala and across India prepare to reopen, these lines are a prayer for every student: seek wisdom on your knees, and keep seeking her to the end.

In the Gospel Jesus is walking in the Temple the day after he drove out the traders, and the chief priests, the scribes and the elders come with a sharp question: “By what authority are you doing these things? Who gave you this authority to do them?” It is a fair question for the guardians of the Temple to ask. Jesus answers with a question of his own: “Did the baptism of John come from heaven, or was it of human origin? Answer me.” The leaders discuss it among themselves. If they say from heaven, he will ask why they did not believe John; if they say of human origin, they fear the crowd, “for all regarded John as truly a prophet.” So they answer, “We do not know.” And Jesus says, “Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things.” Jesus is not playing a clever game. John had pointed to Jesus; anyone who accepted John's witness would already know where Jesus' authority came from. But the question cannot be answered by calculation, by asking what is safe to say. It needs a heart that seeks the truth, as Ben Sira sought wisdom. This is not a judgment on a whole people; these were particular men in a particular moment, and the same temptation to measure the truth by what others will think lives in every one of us. Today the Church may remember St Paul VI, the pope who guided the Second Vatican Council to its end. He knew the weight of authority, and he used it as a servant: in 1964 he laid the papal tiara on the altar of St Peter's as a gift for the poor, and in the same year he came to Bombay for the International Eucharistic Congress, the first pope ever to visit India. He wrote that people today listen more willingly to witnesses than to teachers (Evangelii Nuntiandi 41, in substance). Authority in the Church is credible when it is a witness to the truth.

Full homily

Dear friends, at the end of his long book Ben Sira tells us his own story. While I was still young, before I went on my travels, I sought wisdom openly in my prayer. Before the temple I asked for her, and I will search for her until the end (Sirach 51:13–14). He did not find wisdom first in books. He found her on his knees.

He also says that wisdom grew in him slowly, like grapes on a vine, from the first blossom to the ripening grape. And it took effort: my soul grappled with wisdom. And it needed a clean heart: in purity I found her (Sirach 51:20). Many children will soon go back to school. Let us pray that they become not only clever but wise, and that they learn, like Ben Sira, to ask God for wisdom.

In the Gospel the leaders ask Jesus: by what authority are you doing these things? Jesus asks them about John the Baptist: was his baptism from heaven or of human origin? They do not ask what is true. They ask what is safe. If we say this, he will say that; if we say that, the crowd will be angry. So they answer: we do not know (Mark 11:33). The same temptation lives in us: to decide what is true by what others will think.

Jesus' authority comes from his Father, and we receive it with an open heart. St Paul VI, whom we may remember today, held great authority as pope. But he laid down the papal crown for the poor and came to India to honour Jesus in the Eucharist. He taught that people listen to witnesses more than to teachers. Let us ask today for wisdom, for an honest heart, and for the courage to be witnesses of the truth. Amen.

For children

Children, a wise man in the Bible says: when I was young, I prayed and asked God to make me wise. Being wise means knowing what is good and choosing it. The psalm says God's words are sweeter than honey! Soon school will begin. Every morning, before you study, say a little prayer: Jesus, help me to learn, and make me wise and good.

Prayer

Lord God, giver of wisdom, from our youth teach us to seek you in prayer and to follow wisdom's steps until the end. Make your precepts sweeter to us than honey, and let your commandment enlighten our eyes. Lord Jesus, your authority comes from the Father; give us hearts that seek the truth and do not measure it by the fear of others. Through the prayers of St Paul VI, may all who hold authority in the Church be humble servants and faithful witnesses. Amen.
