Word for the day

“See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” Matthew 10:16

Reflection

Jacob is an old man, and the journey he now takes is the hardest of his life. Joseph is alive and has sent wagons, and the whole family sets out for Egypt. But Egypt is not the land of the promise; it is a place where the patriarchs went in times of famine, and Isaac had once been forbidden to go there (Genesis 26:2). So at Beer-sheba, on the southern edge of Canaan, Jacob stops. He does not simply go. “When Israel set out on his journey with all that he had and came to Beer-sheba, he offered sacrifices to the God of his father Isaac.” And God answers: “God spoke to Israel in visions of the night, and said, ‘Jacob, Jacob.’ And he said, ‘Here I am.’” The double call is the call of the great vocations, the call of Abraham on Mount Moriah and of Moses at the burning bush. Then come three promises. “I am God, the God of your father; do not be afraid to go down to Egypt, for I will make of you a great nation there.” That is the first: the promise of a people. “I myself will go down with you to Egypt.” That is the second: God himself will travel with him into the foreign land. “And I will also bring you up again; and Joseph's own hand shall close your eyes.” The third is the promise of a return, and of a gentle death in the arms of a son. So the whole house of Jacob goes down, sons, grandsons, daughters, little ones, flocks, in the wagons that Pharaoh sent; the chapter counts seventy persons, the seed of the people of God. At the end of the road comes the meeting. Israel sends Judah ahead to Goshen, and it is the man who once offered himself in place of Benjamin who leads the way. Then “Joseph made ready his chariot and went up to meet his father Israel in Goshen. He presented himself to him, fell on his neck, and wept on his neck a good while.” The father's first words are among the most moving in all the Bible: “I can die now, having seen for myself that you are still alive.” For more than twenty years he had grieved for a son he thought dead. Now he asks nothing more of life. The Psalm is the song of an old believer who has seen it all: “Trust in the Lord, and do good,” “Take delight in the Lord, and he will give you the desires of your heart,” and the sweet promise that “in the days of famine they have abundance.” It ends: “The salvation of the righteous is from the Lord; he is their refuge in the time of trouble.”

In the Gospel the mission discourse takes a darker turn. Jesus does not promise his messengers an easy road: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” The disciples must be shrewd, not naive, and pure, not cunning. They will be handed over to councils and flogged in the synagogues; they will be dragged before governors and kings “as a testimony to them and the Gentiles.” Even this is mission: a trial becomes a pulpit. Jesus promises help for exactly those moments: “When they hand you over, do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say; for what you are to say will be given to you at that time; for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” The words about family betrayal are harsh: “Brother will betray brother to death, and a father his child.” In every age, in lands where the Church is persecuted, they have been true. “You will be hated by all because of my name. But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” Jesus also allows the prudent step: “When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next.” The martyr does not go looking for death; he goes on bearing witness, and if it comes, he does not run from it. The saying that closes the passage, “you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes,” is a difficult one, read in several ways in the tradition of the Church; it tells at least that the work of the mission will not be finished before the Lord comes. The Church may keep today the memorial of St Augustine Zhao Rong and his companions, the 120 martyrs of China whom Pope John Paul II canonised in the Jubilee Year 2000. Bishops, priests, religious and lay women and men, Chinese and foreign, they died for the faith in the centuries from 1648 to 1930. Augustine Zhao Rong was a Chinese priest who, according to the usual account, had first been a soldier guarding a captive missionary and was so moved by his faith that he asked for baptism; he died in prison in 1815. Closer to home, India too has her martyrs; St Devasahayam, a layman of the Tamil country who was killed in 1752 for his faith, was canonised in 2022. They show how Jacob's three promises hold good: God gives a people, he goes down with them into every foreign land, and he brings them up again.

Full homily

Dear friends, Jacob is old, and he is afraid. He has to leave the land of the promise and go down to Egypt, a foreign country and the land of Pharaoh. And what does he do? He does what believers always do: before he sets out, he stops at Beer-sheba and offers sacrifice. And in the night God calls him: Jacob, Jacob. Here I am. And God says: do not be afraid to go down to Egypt, for I will make of you a great nation there. I myself will go down with you (Genesis 46:3–4).

I myself will go down with you. That is one of the most beautiful sentences in the Bible. God does not wave from the shore. God goes down. Later God will go down in the Incarnation, in the womb of Mary, and down to the grave, and in the Mass he goes down on the altar. Wherever you go, whatever you fear, God goes down with you. And then the meeting of father and son: Joseph wept on his neck a good while, and old Jacob said: I can die now, having seen for myself that you are still alive (Genesis 46:30). A life that has seen God's faithfulness can be ended in peace.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks plainly to the Twelve: I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves (Matthew 10:16). He does not hide it. Many of his followers have been put to death. Today the Church remembers St Augustine Zhao Rong and his companions, the martyrs of China. We think too of St Devasahayam, who gave his life for Christ in the south of our own land. How could they do it? Jesus had promised: it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you (Matthew 10:20).

Few of us will be asked to die for the faith. But all of us are asked to witness: at work, at home, in the family. To be wise as serpents and innocent as doves. To say a word for the Lord when it is not easy. Do not worry about how you are to speak. Go, trusting the God who said to Jacob: I myself will go down with you. Amen.

For children

Children, old Jacob was going to a new country, and he was afraid. But at night God called: Jacob, Jacob! And God said: do not be afraid, I will go with you. Later Jacob saw his son Joseph again, and he cried with joy. In the Gospel Jesus tells his friends that it will not always be easy to follow him, but the Holy Spirit will help them to find the right words. Some brave Christians, like the martyrs of China, died because they loved Jesus so much. When you feel afraid, say: Jesus, you are with me.

Prayer

God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, you called Jacob by name at Beer-sheba and promised to go down with him into Egypt and bring him up again. Be with all who set out into the unknown this day, and with all who are afraid. Lord Jesus, you sent your disciples out like sheep among wolves and promised them the Spirit of your Father. Give us the wisdom of serpents and the innocence of doves; give us courage when we are brought before the powerful, and words from the Holy Spirit when we have none. Receive into your glory the martyrs of every land, St Augustine Zhao Rong and his companions, and the martyrs of India, and by their prayers keep us faithful to the end. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The family of Jacob goes down into Egypt. At Beer-sheba, on the edge of the promised land, the old patriarch is afraid, and God speaks to him in the night: “Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt … I myself will go down with you.” In the Gospel Jesus sends the Twelve out “like sheep into the midst of wolves”, and promises that the Spirit of their Father will speak through them. Both readings are about being sent into a place that is not safe, with God going along. Psalm 37 gives the quiet confidence: the salvation of the righteous is from the Lord, their refuge in time of trouble. Some places keep today the optional memorial of St Augustine Zhao Rong and his companions, the martyrs of China; the readings are those of the weekday.

Genesis 46:1–7, 28–30 · I will go down with you to Egypt

Setting.

Joseph has revealed himself and Pharaoh has invited Jacob’s whole household to come and live in Egypt (45:16–20). Jacob, now called Israel, sets out with all that he has. In this chapter the text moves between his two names: Jacob, the name he was born with, and Israel, the name given after the struggle at the Jabbok (32:28).

Background. Beer-sheba is the southernmost place of the promised land, where Abraham planted a tree and called on the name of the Lord, and Isaac built an altar (21:33; 26:25). To go further is to leave the land God promised. Isaac had been told not to go down to Egypt in a famine (26:2), so Jacob’s hesitation is understandable. The words of God in v. 3 answer his fear. The reading leaves out the list of the seventy persons of Jacob’s house who came into Egypt (46:8–27).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: at Beer-sheba Israel offers sacrifices, and God speaks in the night: do not be afraid; I will go down with you and bring you up again.
  • vv. 5–7: Jacob’s sons carry him, their families and their livestock into Egypt in Pharaoh’s wagons.
  • vv. 28–30: Judah leads the way to Goshen; Joseph meets his father, weeps on his neck, and Israel says he can die now.

Key words.

  • ʾal-tîrāʾ, do not be afraid: The word of God to the patriarchs and the prophets at moments of great decision (15:1; 26:24; Isaiah 41:10). Faith begins where fear is answered by a promise.
  • gôy gādôl, great nation: The promise first made to Abraham (12:2) is renewed in a place that seems the opposite of promise. Israel will become a people in Egypt, not in Canaan.
  • ʿālâ, to go up, to bring up: God promises that he will “bring you up” again. In the Bible this is the language of the Exodus and of the pilgrimage to Jerusalem. The descent to Egypt already carries the hope of a return.

Verse by verse.

“God spoke to Israel in visions of the night, and said, “Jacob, Jacob.”” (46:2)

God calls by name, twice, as he did to Abraham (22:11) and to Moses (Exodus 3:4). The old man hears the name of his beginning.

“do not be afraid to go down to Egypt, for I will make of you a great nation there.” (46:3)

God does not avoid Egypt; he goes into it. The place of danger becomes the place of growth.

“I myself will go down with you to Egypt, and I will also bring you up again;” (46:4)

Two promises: presence and return. The same God who goes down with Jacob will later go down to see Israel’s affliction in Egypt (Exodus 3:7–8).

“Joseph’s own hand shall close your eyes.” (46:4)

A tender promise: Jacob will die in the presence of the son he thought dead.

“I can die now, having seen for myself that you are still alive.” (46:30)

Jacob’s words recall Simeon in the Temple, who was ready to depart in peace after seeing the child (Luke 2:29–30). A long grief ends in joy.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the descent of Israel into Egypt in the light of the Exodus and of the flight of the Holy Family into Egypt, where Matthew sees Hosea’s words fulfilled: “Out of Egypt I called my son” (Matthew 2:15). The word of God to Jacob, “I myself will go down with you”, is read as a promise of God’s nearness. The Catechism teaches that the Son of God came down from heaven for us and for our salvation (CCC 456), so that God has gone down to share our condition. When the Church prays for migrants and refugees, this text gives a warm reminder: God does not leave those who are sent into another land.

Matthew 10:16–23 · Sheep in the midst of wolves

Setting.

The mission discourse now turns from the instructions to the cost. After the details of the journey, Jesus is open with the Twelve: they will meet hostility. Matthew writes for a community, probably Jewish-Christian in origin, that already knew rejection and persecution, and the words of Jesus are placed where his readers could recognize their own experience.

Background. The first disciples were all Jews. When Jesus warns that they will be handed over to councils and flogged in the synagogues (v. 17), he speaks of conflicts inside the Jewish world of the first century, between a Jewish teacher’s followers and other Jewish authorities. This must never be turned into a charge against the Jewish people. The warnings also reach to governors and kings and to the Gentiles (v. 18), and to families (v. 21).

Structure.

  • v. 16: sheep among wolves; be wise as serpents, innocent as doves.
  • vv. 17–18: they will be handed over to councils, flogged in synagogues and brought before governors and kings, as a testimony.
  • vv. 19–20: do not worry about what to say; the Spirit of your Father will speak through you.
  • vv. 21–23: betrayal even within families; hatred for the sake of Jesus’ name; the one who endures will be saved; flee to the next town.

Key words.

  • phronimoi, wise, prudent: The word for practical wisdom, the sense of the one who builds his house on rock (7:24). The serpent’s cleverness in the story of Genesis is here turned to good use.
  • akeraioi, innocent, unmixed: Literally unmixed, as of pure metal or undiluted wine. The disciple is to be simple and without double dealing. Prudence without simplicity becomes cunning; simplicity without prudence becomes naivety.
  • hypomeinas, the one who endures: Not only to put up with, but to remain under: the staying power of those who keep faith under pressure.

Verse by verse.

“See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves;” (10:16)

The sender is Jesus, “I am sending you”. The disciples are not sent to be safe but to be faithful, and the Shepherd goes with the sheep.

“so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” (10:16)

Both together. Jesus does not call for naivety, and he does not call for cleverness that lies.

“do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say;” (10:19)

The promise is not that there will be no trial but that the Spirit will give the word at the hour. It does not forbid preparation for teaching; it speaks of the hour of testimony.

“it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” (10:20)

The martyrs have often recalled this. The words of witness are a gift of God, and so the witness is not a matter of eloquence.

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” (10:22)

Salvation is God’s gift and also asks for perseverance. The Church speaks of final perseverance as a grace that we ask for (CCC 1821).

“When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next;” (10:23)

Flight is not cowardice. Jesus himself withdrew when his hour had not yet come, and the Church has never required anyone to seek martyrdom.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this Gospel the pattern of all Christian witness. The Catechism teaches that the disciple must not only keep the faith but profess it and bear witness to it with confidence (CCC 1816), and that martyrdom is the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473). The last words of v. 23, “before the Son of Man comes”, have been understood in several ways, as referring to the Resurrection and Pentecost, to the fall of Jerusalem in the year 70, or to the continuing mission to Israel until the end; the Church does not settle the question by a definition. The Church’s own history confirms the warning: in every century Christians in many lands have been hated for the name of Jesus, and she prays today for all who suffer for the faith, and for those who wrong them.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus want his disciples to run away from persecution?

He allows it. “When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next” shows that prudence is part of discipleship. The Church honours the martyrs who stood firm when flight was not possible or when faith itself was at stake, but she has never taught that anyone should seek suffering for its own sake.

For the Latin reader

Today the Roman Calendar gives the optional memorial of St Augustine Zhao Rong and his companions, the 120 martyrs of China canonized in the year 2000, who died between the seventeenth and the twentieth century. Zhao Rong was a soldier who guarded a bishop under arrest, was moved by his witness, became a Christian and later a priest, and died in prison in 1815. Their story shows how Jesus’ words, “the Spirit of your Father speaking through you”, have been lived.

For study and teaching

  • Where in my life do I feel “like a sheep among wolves”, and how can I be wise and innocent there at the same time?
  • Jacob was afraid to leave the promised land, and God said, “I myself will go down with you.” What fear of mine can I put before God in prayer today?
