Word for the day

“Then Abram said to Lot, “Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herders and my herders; for we are kindred.”” Genesis 13:8

Reflection

Abram has come back from Egypt a rich man: “Now Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold.” His nephew Lot has also grown rich, with “flocks and herds and tents.” And riches bring their usual trouble: “the land could not support both of them living together,” and “there was strife between the herders of Abram's livestock and the herders of Lot's livestock.” Every Kerala family that has quarrelled over a boundary wall, a coconut tree on the dividing line or the share of an ancestral house will recognise the scene. Abram is the elder and the head of the clan; by custom the choice is his. But he speaks first for peace: “Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herders and my herders; for we are kindred.” And then he does something astonishing: he gives the choice to the younger man. “Is not the whole land before you? Separate yourself from me. If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right; or if you take the right hand, then I will go to the left.” Lot looks and sees with the eyes of the market: the plain of the Jordan, “well watered everywhere like the garden of the LORD.” He chooses it, and moves his tent “as far as Sodom.” The narrator adds a quiet warning: “Now the people of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the LORD.” What looks best to the eye is not always best for the soul. Then, when Lot has gone, God speaks to Abram, who has kept the poorer hill country: “Raise your eyes now, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward; for all the land that you see I will give to you and to your offspring forever.” The man who let go receives everything. Abram moves to the oaks of Mamre at Hebron and, as always, builds an altar. The Psalm draws the portrait of such a man: “Those who walk blamelessly, and do what is right, and speak the truth from their heart,” who “stand by their oath even to their hurt” and “do not take a bribe against the innocent.” “Those who do these things shall never be moved.”

The Gospel gathers three sayings from the end of the Sermon on the Mount. The first is about reverence: “Do not give what is holy to dogs; and do not throw your pearls before swine.” Holy things must be treated as holy; the early Christians applied this word to the Eucharist, which, as the Didache says, is only for the baptised. The second is the saying the world calls the Golden Rule: “In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” Jewish teachers before Jesus had taught the same wisdom in its negative form, do not do to others what you hate (Tobit 4:15); Jesus puts it positively, and so turns it from a limit into a programme: do not only avoid harming, but go and do the good you would hope for. Abram did exactly this with Lot. The third saying is the hardest: “Enter through the narrow gate; for the gate is wide and the road is easy that leads to destruction, and there are many who take it. For the gate is narrow and the road is hard that leads to life, and there are few who find it.” Today's optional memorials show three men who found the narrow gate. St Paulinus of Nola, a rich Roman senator and governor in the fourth century, was baptised, and with his wife Therasia gave away his great wealth to the poor; he settled at Nola near Naples beside the shrine of the martyr St Felix, became a priest and then bishop, and wrote Christian poetry and letters to friends such as Augustine and Jerome. St John Fisher, bishop of Rochester, and St Thomas More, Lord Chancellor of England, were the two most respected men of their country when King Henry VIII demanded an oath that went against their conscience and their communion with the Pope. Under great pressure, almost everyone swore. They stood by the truth “even to their hurt.” Fisher was beheaded on 22 June 1535, More on 6 July. More is reported to have said on the scaffold that he died the king's good servant, but God's first.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's first reading could have happened in any village in Kerala. Abram and his nephew Lot have become rich, and their herdsmen start fighting over the grass and the water. How many families have been broken by fights over land, a boundary or an inheritance? Brothers who do not speak, cases that go on for twenty years.

Abram, the elder, could have taken the best land for himself. Instead he says: let there be no strife between you and me, for we are kindred (Genesis 13:8). And he lets Lot choose first. Lot chooses the green, well-watered plain, near Sodom. It looks good, but it leads him into trouble. Abram takes what is left. And then God says to him: raise your eyes and look in every direction; all the land that you see I will give to you (Genesis 13:14–15). The one who let go for the sake of peace receives everything from God.

In the Gospel Jesus gives the rule that Abram lived: in everything do to others as you would have them do to you (Matthew 7:12). It is simple to say and hard to do. And he warns that the road to life is narrow: the gate is narrow and the road is hard that leads to life, and there are few who find it (Matthew 7:14).

St John Fisher and St Thomas More, whom we may remember today, chose that narrow gate. When the king demanded an oath against their conscience and against unity with the Pope, almost everyone took it. They refused, and gave their lives. Today, let us ask for their courage, and for Abram's generosity. If there is a quarrel in our family over land or money, let us be the first to say: let there be no strife between us, for we are kindred. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever fought with your brother or sister over a toy, or the best seat, or the biggest piece of cake? Abram and his nephew Lot had a fight between their shepherds. Abram said: let us not fight, we are family. You choose first! Jesus gave us a golden rule: do to others what you would like them to do to you. Today, try to let someone else choose first, and see how happy it makes them, and Jesus.

Prayer

O God, who in martyrdom have brought true faith to its highest expression, graciously grant that, strengthened through the intercession of Saint John Fisher and Saint Thomas More, we may confirm by the witness of our life the faith we profess with our lips. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, you blessed Abram when he chose peace with his kinsman over the best land. Heal the quarrels in our families over property and inheritance; teach us to do to others as we would have them do to us; and give us courage, like St Paulinus, St John Fisher and St Thomas More, to enter through the narrow gate that leads to life. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Abram and his nephew Lot have grown so rich that the land cannot hold them both, and their herdsmen quarrel. Abram, the elder, lets Lot choose first, and God then renews the promise to Abram: all the land he can see will be his. In the Gospel Jesus gives the rule that sums up the law and the prophets: “In everything do to others as you would have them do to you,” and he calls his disciples to the narrow gate. Psalm 15 describes the one who may dwell with God: one who walks blamelessly and does no evil to a friend. Today the Church may remember St Paulinus of Nola, and Sts John Fisher and Thomas More, martyrs.

Genesis 13:2, 5–18 · Let there be no strife between us

Setting.

Abram has returned from Egypt to the Negeb, where he went during a famine (12:10–13:1). He comes back to Bethel, to the altar he built at the beginning (13:3–4). He and Lot now have large flocks, and pasture and water are limited in the hill country. The quarrel of the herdsmen threatens to divide the family.

The Lectionary leaves out vv. 3–4, the return to Bethel. The verse about Sodom (v. 13) prepares for the stories of chapters 18–19.

Structure.

  • vv. 2, 5–7: Abram and Lot are rich; the land cannot support both; strife among the herdsmen.
  • vv. 8–9: Abram’s offer: “we are kindred”; let Lot choose left or right.
  • vv. 10–13: Lot looks, chooses the plain of the Jordan, and settles near Sodom.
  • vv. 14–17: the Lord tells Abram to raise his eyes: all the land he sees is his and his offspring’s.
  • v. 18: Abram settles by the oaks of Mamre at Hebron and builds an altar.

Key words.

  • rîb, mĕrîbâ, strife, quarrel: The same word family names the place where Israel later quarrelled with God in the desert, Meribah (Exodus 17:7). Abram refuses to let a quarrel over goods break a family bond.
  • ʾaḥîm, kindred, brothers: Literally “brothers”, though Lot is Abram’s nephew. In Hebrew the word covers close relatives. For Abram, being kin matters more than having the better land.
  • nāśāʾ ʿênayim, to lift up the eyes: Lot “looked about him”, literally lifted up his eyes, and chose for himself (v. 10). After Lot has gone, God tells Abram, “Raise your eyes now” (v. 14). Lot looks with his own desire; Abram looks at God’s word, and receives far more.

Verse by verse.

“Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herders and my herders; for we are kindred.” (13:8)

The elder and the one who received the promise gives way to the younger. Peace in the family is worth more than his rights.

“Lot looked about him, and saw that the plain of the Jordan was well watered everywhere like the garden of the Lord,” (13:10)

The plain looks like Eden. But the narrator adds at once that Sodom was nearby and wicked (v. 13). What looks best is not always best.

“Raise your eyes now, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward;” (13:14)

Abram gave up the choice, and God gives him the whole. The four directions mean everything his eyes can see.

How the Church reads it.

The fathers held up Abram as a model of a peacemaker who yields for the sake of concord. St John Chrysostom, preaching on Genesis, praised his humility: though he was the elder and the one to whom God had spoken, he let the younger choose (in substance). The Church reads here the beatitude of the peacemakers (Matthew 5:9), and the teaching of the Catechism that peace is the work of justice and the fruit of charity (CCC 2304). Families who quarrel over land and inheritance can hear Abram’s words as their own: “we are kindred”.

Matthew 7:6, 12–14 · The narrow gate

Setting.

These verses come near the end of the Sermon on the Mount. Verse 6 is a short, hard saying about holy things; vv. 7–11, on asking and receiving, are left out today. Verse 12 gives the summary of the whole Sermon: the “Golden Rule”. Then vv. 13–14 begin the closing call to decision, with its pairs: two gates, two trees, two builders (7:13–27).

Background. The Golden Rule was known in other forms. The Book of Tobit says, “What you hate, do not do to anyone” (Tobit 4:15). The Talmud tells that the great Jewish teacher Hillel summed up the Torah in a similar way: what is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour. Jesus gives the rule in its positive form: not only avoid harm, but do the good you would wish to receive.

Structure.

  • v. 6: do not give what is holy to dogs, nor pearls to swine.
  • v. 12: in everything do to others as you would have them do to you; this is the law and the prophets.
  • vv. 13–14: the narrow gate and hard road that lead to life; the wide gate and easy road that lead to destruction.

Key words.

  • to hagion, what is holy: Originally the holy meat of the Temple sacrifices, which could not be thrown to dogs (compare Exodus 29:33). The Didache, an early Christian writing, applies this saying to the Eucharist: only the baptised may receive it (Didache 9).
  • ho nomos kai hoi prophētai, the law and the prophets: A way of naming the whole of the Scriptures of Israel. Jesus said at the start of the Sermon that he came not to abolish the law and the prophets but to fulfil them (5:17). Now the Sermon closes the circle: love of neighbour sums them up (compare 22:40).
  • stenē pylē, narrow gate: The gate is narrow, and the road is literally “pressed” or “afflicted” (tethlimmenē), from the same family as the word for tribulation. The way of life is not crowded and comfortable; it asks for decision and effort, sustained by grace.

Verse by verse.

“Do not give what is holy to dogs; and do not throw your pearls before swine,” (7:6)

Coming just after the warning not to judge, this saying asks for discernment. The holy things of God are to be treated with reverence.

“In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” (7:12)

“In everything” leaves no exceptions: family, work, money, speech. The rule asks me to imagine myself in the other’s place.

“For the gate is narrow and the road is hard that leads to life, and there are few who find it.” (7:14)

Jesus does not tell us how many will be saved; when someone asks him, he answers, “Strive to enter through the narrow door” (Luke 13:24). The saying is a call, not a census.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the law of the Gospel requires us to choose between the two ways, and that it is summed up in the Golden Rule (CCC 1970). It also lists the Golden Rule among the rules that apply in every case of conscience (CCC 1789). The words on the narrow gate appear where the Catechism speaks of the last things: the teachings on hell are a call to responsibility and to conversion (CCC 1036). God wants all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and his grace makes the narrow way possible.

Questions people ask

What does “do not give what is holy to dogs” mean? Is Jesus calling people dogs?

Jesus uses a proverb of his time about holy things and unclean animals. It is not an insult to any people. Its point is reverence: sacred things must not be handled carelessly or given to those who would trample them. The early Church applied it to the Eucharist, which is given to the baptised who are prepared to receive it (Didache 9; compare 1 Corinthians 11:27–29).

For the Latin reader

St Paulinus of Nola (about 354–431) was born at Bordeaux in Gaul, became a senator and governor in Campania, and then, with his wife Therasia, gave away his great wealth to the poor. He settled at Nola, near the tomb of St Felix, was ordained priest and later became bishop of Nola. He was a poet and the friend by letter of Augustine and others. Sts John Fisher (1469–1535), bishop of Rochester and cardinal, and Thomas More (1478–1535), Lord Chancellor of England, refused to accept King Henry VIII as supreme head of the Church in England and were beheaded: Fisher on 22 June, More on 6 July 1535. More is reported to have said on the scaffold that he died the king’s good servant, but God’s first. Pope St John Paul II named him patron of statesmen and politicians in 2000. They walked the narrow gate.

For study and teaching

  • Abram said, “Let there be no strife between you and me.” Is there a quarrel in my family, perhaps over property or inheritance, where I could give way for the sake of peace?
  • Choose one person I will meet tomorrow. How would I want to be treated in that person’s place?
