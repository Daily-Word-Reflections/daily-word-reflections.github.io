The Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ (Corpus Christi)

Word for the day

“While they were eating, he took a loaf of bread, and after blessing it he broke it, gave it to them, and said, “Take; this is my body.” Then he took a cup, and after giving thanks he gave it to them, and all of them drank from it.” Mark 14:22–23

Reflection

On this Sunday the Church kneels before the gift that Jesus left on the night before he died: his own Body and Blood. In Holy Week we remembered the Last Supper in the shadow of the Passion. Today, in the light of Easter and Pentecost, we celebrate the same gift with open joy, carry it through our streets and adore it. The readings of Year B have one thread running through all of them: blood and covenant. The first reading takes us to the foot of Mount Sinai. Moses has come down and “told the people all the words of the LORD and all the ordinances; and all the people answered with one voice, and said, ‘All the words that the LORD has spoken we will do.’” Then he builds an altar with twelve pillars, one for each tribe. Young men offer sacrifices, and Moses does something that seems strange to us: he takes half of the blood and dashes it against the altar, which stands for God, and after reading the book of the covenant aloud and hearing the people promise again, he dashes the other half on the people, saying: “See the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words.” For the people of the Bible, blood is life. The same life now touches both the altar and the people: God and Israel are bound together like blood relatives, one family. That is what a covenant means. The Psalm is the song of one who has been rescued and asks how to give thanks: “What shall I return to the LORD for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD.” Christians have always heard in it the voice of the Church at every Mass.

The Letter to the Hebrews explains what Moses' ceremony was pointing to. The high priest of the old covenant entered the Holy of Holies once a year with the blood of animals. “But when Christ came as a high priest of the good things that have come, then through the greater and perfect tent (not made with hands, that is, not of this creation), he entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood, thus obtaining eternal redemption.” The author reasons from the lesser to the greater: if the blood of animals could make people outwardly clean for worship, “how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to worship the living God!” Notice that the whole Trinity is at work: Christ offers himself, through the eternal Spirit, to God the Father. And the result is a new covenant: “For this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance.” Moses sprinkled blood on the outside of the people. Christ's blood reaches the conscience, the inner heart, and makes us able to worship the living God.

The Gospel brings us into the upper room. Jesus sends two disciples into the city: “a man carrying a jar of water will meet you; follow him,” and he will show them “a large room upstairs, furnished and ready.” The Lord himself prepares the place of the meal. That evening, while they are eating the Passover, “he took a loaf of bread, and after blessing it he broke it, gave it to them, and said, ‘Take; this is my body.’ Then he took a cup, and after giving thanks he gave it to them, and all of them drank from it. He said to them, ‘This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.’” The disciples, good Jews who knew Exodus 24, could not miss the echo: the blood of the covenant. What Moses sprinkled from a basin, Jesus gives to drink, and it is his own blood. He does not say: this is a sign of my body; he says: this is my body. The Church has always taken him at his word. By the power of the Holy Spirit and the words of Christ spoken by the priest, the whole substance of the bread becomes the Body of Christ and the whole substance of the wine becomes his Blood, while the appearances of bread and wine remain; the Church calls this change transubstantiation (Council of Trent; Catechism 1376). Christ is present in the Eucharist truly, really and substantially, Body and Blood, soul and divinity. And because the Eucharist makes present the one sacrifice of the cross, the Mass is not only a meal but the sacrifice of the new covenant. The Gospel ends simply: “When they had sung the hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.” The hymn was probably the Hallel psalms, which include today's Psalm 116. From the table Jesus goes out to give his body and pour out his blood. Today we too go out from the altar in procession, carrying him through our towns and villages, so that the whole world may see the One who gave his life for many.

Homily outline

Theme: “Take; this is my body.”

The blood of the covenant at Sinai, the blood of Christ that purifies the conscience, and the gift of the upper room carried into our streets.

1. The blood of the covenant

  • The people's promise.
  • Twelve pillars and an altar.
  • Blood on altar and people: one family.
“Moses took the blood and dashed it on the people, and said, “See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you in accordance with all these words.”” Exodus 24:8
“He said to them, “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.”” Mark 14:24

Apply: At the consecration this week, when the chalice is raised, say inwardly: Lord, by your blood I belong to your family.

2. How much more the blood of Christ

  • The high priest and the Holy of Holies.
  • Once for all, with his own blood.
  • A purified conscience.
“For the life of the flesh is in the blood; and I have given it to you for making atonement for your lives on the altar;” Leviticus 17:11
“how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to worship the living God!” Hebrews 9:14

Apply: Make a good confession before you next receive Communion if you are aware of serious sin, so that the Lord may purify your conscience.

3. This is my body

  • The room made ready.
  • Bread blessed and broken.
  • Truly, really, substantially present.
“What shall I return to the Lord for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the Lord,” Psalm 116:12–13
“The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ?” 1 Corinthians 10:16

Apply: Stay at least two minutes in silent thanksgiving after every Holy Communion this month.

4. They went out

  • The hymn and the Mount of Olives.
  • The procession through our streets.
  • Becoming what we receive.
“I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all his people,” Psalm 116:18
“Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.” 1 Corinthians 10:17

Apply: After the procession, share a meal or some food this week with a family or person in need, so that the Bread of Life becomes bread shared.

At Sinai the blood of the covenant made Israel God's family; on the cross and at the altar the blood of Christ seals the new and eternal covenant.

In the Eucharist Jesus gives us his true Body and Blood; we adore him, receive him, and go out to become his body for the world.

Take-home resolution: Make one visit to the Blessed Sacrament this week and stay in thanksgiving after every Communion.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today the Church kneels in wonder before the greatest gift Jesus left us: his own Body and Blood. In many of our parishes the Blessed Sacrament will be carried through the streets this evening, with candles, flowers, songs and children in white. We do this because we believe that the small white Host is not a thing but a Person: Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

In the first reading Moses stands at the foot of Mount Sinai. The people promise: all that the Lord has spoken we will do. Moses takes the blood of the sacrifice, dashes half of it on the altar and half on the people, and says: see the blood of the covenant (Exodus 24:8). For the people of the Bible, blood is life. The same life touches God's altar and God's people. Now they are one family. That is a covenant.

But that covenant was written in the blood of animals, and the people did not keep their promise. So God made a new covenant. The Letter to the Hebrews says: Christ entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood (Hebrews 9:12). And his blood does what animal blood could never do: it purifies our conscience from dead works to worship the living God (Hebrews 9:14). It washes not our skin but our heart.

Then the Gospel. In the upper room Jesus takes bread and says: take; this is my body. He takes the cup and says: this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many (Mark 14:22, 24). The apostles remembered Moses. What Moses sprinkled, Jesus gives to drink, and it is his own blood. He did not say: this is a picture of my body. He said: this is my body. And the Church believes him. The bread becomes his Body, the wine becomes his Blood. It still looks like bread, it still tastes like wine, but it is the Lord.

If we truly believe this, it will change how we come to Mass. We will come on time. We will prepare our hearts, and go to confession when we have sinned seriously. We will receive him with reverence, and say Amen with faith. And we will not run away after Communion, but stay a few moments to thank him. As the Psalm says: what shall I return to the Lord for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation.

Finally the Gospel says: when they had sung the hymn, they went out (Mark 14:26). Jesus went out to give his life. Today we go out in procession, carrying him through our streets, so that our neighbours may see the One who loves them. And when we go home, we carry him too, in our hearts. Let us become what we receive: the Body of Christ, broken and shared for the world. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, today's Gospel begins not with the Last Supper itself but with the preparations. On the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb is sacrificed, his disciples said to him: where do you want us to go and make the preparations for you to eat the Passover? (Mark 14:12). They asked. Jesus had already chosen the place.

The Book of Proverbs pictures Wisdom building her house and setting her table, and calling: come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed (Proverbs 9:5). Jesus is the Wisdom of God. He has prepared the table long before we arrive. But he wants us to prepare too. Each Sunday the question of the disciples is ours: Lord, where do you want me to meet you?

Jesus gives a strange sign: go into the city, and a man carrying a jar of water will meet you; follow him (Mark 14:13). In those days men rarely carried water jars; it was the work of women. The disciples had to obey before they understood. Isaiah had called: everyone who thirsts, come to the waters (Isaiah 55:1). Sometimes the way to the Eucharist is through simple obedience: to the Sunday obligation, to the one-hour fast, to confession when we have sinned seriously.

Then: he will show you a large room upstairs, furnished and ready. Make preparations for us there (Mark 14:15). The room was ready, but the disciples still had to prepare the meal. The psalm says: you prepare a table before me (Psalm 23:5). God prepares; we prepare. How do we prepare our hearts? With a short prayer before Mass, by arriving early, by an act of contrition, by keeping the Eucharistic fast, by a moment of silent thanks after Communion.

So the disciples set out and went to the city, and found everything as he had told them (Mark 14:16). If we prepare, we will find everything as he promised: the Lord himself, truly present, waiting for us. Let us prepare our churches beautifully, our homes for the procession, and above all our hearts for every Mass. Amen.

The Eucharist makes us dwell in Christ and Christ in us.

Whoever adores him at the altar must serve him in the poor.

Take-home resolution: This week I will spend time in adoration and serve Christ in one poor person.

Dear friends, today we carry the Blessed Sacrament through our streets. The procession is a picture of the whole Christian life: we begin at the altar and we go out to the street. Let us reflect on that journey.

It begins with communion. Jesus said: those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them (John 6:56). To abide means to stay, to make a home. Jesus does not come to us as a visitor who leaves. He wants to make his home in us. The psalm says: O taste and see that the LORD is good (Psalm 34:8). In adoration before the Blessed Sacrament, we learn to stay with him as he stays with us.

The second reading calls Christ the mediator of a new covenant (Hebrews 9:15). Jeremiah had promised: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). The old covenant was written on stone; the new covenant is written on hearts, and the Eucharist is how God writes. Each Communion, received with faith, changes us a little: we become more patient, more pure, more generous.

And that change must show in the street. St Paul warned that those who eat and drink without discerning the body bring judgment on themselves. St John Chrysostom told his people: do you want to honour the body of Christ? Do not ignore him when he is naked; do not honour him here in church with silk and then neglect him outside, where he is cold and naked. Jesus said: just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me (Matthew 25:40). Moses commanded: open your hand to the poor and needy neighbor in your land (Deuteronomy 15:11).

So let us adore him at the altar and serve him in the street. Spend time in adoration this week. Ask him after Communion to write his law on your heart. And let every Mass end in one act of charity for someone in need. Then the procession will not end today; it will continue every day of our lives. Amen.

For children

Children, at the Last Supper Jesus took bread and said: this is my body. Then he took the cup and said: this is my blood. Jesus loves us so much that he wants to come right into our hearts! At Mass the bread and wine become Jesus himself. It still looks like bread, but it is really Jesus.

So when you see the Host lifted up at Mass, or carried in the procession today, say quietly in your heart: My Lord and my God! And if you have made your First Holy Communion, when you receive Jesus, say thank you to him with all your heart.

Prayer

O God, who in this wonderful Sacrament have left us a memorial of your Passion, grant us, we pray, so to revere the sacred mysteries of your Body and Blood that we may always experience in ourselves the fruits of your redemption. Who live and reign with God the Father in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, true God and true man, hidden under the appearance of bread and wine, we adore you. You sealed the new covenant with your own blood; purify our conscience to worship the living God. What shall we return to you for all your goodness? We will lift up the cup of salvation and call on your name. Walk with us through our streets today, bless our homes, and make us one body in you. Amen.
