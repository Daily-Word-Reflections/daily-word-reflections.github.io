Word for the day

“While they were eating, he took a loaf of bread, and after blessing it he broke it, gave it to them, and said, “Take; this is my body.” Then he took a cup, and after giving thanks he gave it to them, and all of them drank from it.” Mark 14:22–23

Reflection

On this Sunday the Church kneels before the gift that Jesus left on the night before he died: his own Body and Blood. In Holy Week we remembered the Last Supper in the shadow of the Passion. Today, in the light of Easter and Pentecost, we celebrate the same gift with open joy, carry it through our streets and adore it. The readings of Year B have one thread running through all of them: blood and covenant. The first reading takes us to the foot of Mount Sinai. Moses has come down and “told the people all the words of the LORD and all the ordinances; and all the people answered with one voice, and said, ‘All the words that the LORD has spoken we will do.’” Then he builds an altar with twelve pillars, one for each tribe. Young men offer sacrifices, and Moses does something that seems strange to us: he takes half of the blood and dashes it against the altar, which stands for God, and after reading the book of the covenant aloud and hearing the people promise again, he dashes the other half on the people, saying: “See the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words.” For the people of the Bible, blood is life. The same life now touches both the altar and the people: God and Israel are bound together like blood relatives, one family. That is what a covenant means. The Psalm is the song of one who has been rescued and asks how to give thanks: “What shall I return to the LORD for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD.” Christians have always heard in it the voice of the Church at every Mass.

The Letter to the Hebrews explains what Moses' ceremony was pointing to. The high priest of the old covenant entered the Holy of Holies once a year with the blood of animals. “But when Christ came as a high priest of the good things that have come, then through the greater and perfect tent (not made with hands, that is, not of this creation), he entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood, thus obtaining eternal redemption.” The author reasons from the lesser to the greater: if the blood of animals could make people outwardly clean for worship, “how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to worship the living God!” Notice that the whole Trinity is at work: Christ offers himself, through the eternal Spirit, to God the Father. And the result is a new covenant: “For this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance.” Moses sprinkled blood on the outside of the people. Christ's blood reaches the conscience, the inner heart, and makes us able to worship the living God.

The Gospel brings us into the upper room. Jesus sends two disciples into the city: “a man carrying a jar of water will meet you; follow him,” and he will show them “a large room upstairs, furnished and ready.” The Lord himself prepares the place of the meal. That evening, while they are eating the Passover, “he took a loaf of bread, and after blessing it he broke it, gave it to them, and said, ‘Take; this is my body.’ Then he took a cup, and after giving thanks he gave it to them, and all of them drank from it. He said to them, ‘This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.’” The disciples, good Jews who knew Exodus 24, could not miss the echo: the blood of the covenant. What Moses sprinkled from a basin, Jesus gives to drink, and it is his own blood. He does not say: this is a sign of my body; he says: this is my body. The Church has always taken him at his word. By the power of the Holy Spirit and the words of Christ spoken by the priest, the whole substance of the bread becomes the Body of Christ and the whole substance of the wine becomes his Blood, while the appearances of bread and wine remain; the Church calls this change transubstantiation (Council of Trent; Catechism 1376). Christ is present in the Eucharist truly, really and substantially, Body and Blood, soul and divinity. And because the Eucharist makes present the one sacrifice of the cross, the Mass is not only a meal but the sacrifice of the new covenant. The Gospel ends simply: “When they had sung the hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.” The hymn was probably the Hallel psalms, which include today's Psalm 116. From the table Jesus goes out to give his body and pour out his blood. Today we too go out from the altar in procession, carrying him through our towns and villages, so that the whole world may see the One who gave his life for many.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today the Church kneels in wonder before the greatest gift Jesus left us: his own Body and Blood. In many of our parishes the Blessed Sacrament will be carried through the streets this evening, with candles, flowers, songs and children in white. We do this because we believe that the small white Host is not a thing but a Person: Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

In the first reading Moses stands at the foot of Mount Sinai. The people promise: all that the Lord has spoken we will do. Moses takes the blood of the sacrifice, dashes half of it on the altar and half on the people, and says: see the blood of the covenant (Exodus 24:8). For the people of the Bible, blood is life. The same life touches God's altar and God's people. Now they are one family. That is a covenant.

But that covenant was written in the blood of animals, and the people did not keep their promise. So God made a new covenant. The Letter to the Hebrews says: Christ entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood (Hebrews 9:12). And his blood does what animal blood could never do: it purifies our conscience from dead works to worship the living God (Hebrews 9:14). It washes not our skin but our heart.

Then the Gospel. In the upper room Jesus takes bread and says: take; this is my body. He takes the cup and says: this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many (Mark 14:22, 24). The apostles remembered Moses. What Moses sprinkled, Jesus gives to drink, and it is his own blood. He did not say: this is a picture of my body. He said: this is my body. And the Church believes him. The bread becomes his Body, the wine becomes his Blood. It still looks like bread, it still tastes like wine, but it is the Lord.

If we truly believe this, it will change how we come to Mass. We will come on time. We will prepare our hearts, and go to confession when we have sinned seriously. We will receive him with reverence, and say Amen with faith. And we will not run away after Communion, but stay a few moments to thank him. As the Psalm says: what shall I return to the Lord for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation.

Finally the Gospel says: when they had sung the hymn, they went out (Mark 14:26). Jesus went out to give his life. Today we go out in procession, carrying him through our streets, so that our neighbours may see the One who loves them. And when we go home, we carry him too, in our hearts. Let us become what we receive: the Body of Christ, broken and shared for the world. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, today's Gospel begins not with the Last Supper itself but with the preparations. On the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb is sacrificed, his disciples said to him: where do you want us to go and make the preparations for you to eat the Passover? (Mark 14:12). They asked. Jesus had already chosen the place.

The Book of Proverbs pictures Wisdom building her house and setting her table, and calling: come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed (Proverbs 9:5). Jesus is the Wisdom of God. He has prepared the table long before we arrive. But he wants us to prepare too. Each Sunday the question of the disciples is ours: Lord, where do you want me to meet you?

Jesus gives a strange sign: go into the city, and a man carrying a jar of water will meet you; follow him (Mark 14:13). In those days men rarely carried water jars; it was the work of women. The disciples had to obey before they understood. Isaiah had called: everyone who thirsts, come to the waters (Isaiah 55:1). Sometimes the way to the Eucharist is through simple obedience: to the Sunday obligation, to the one-hour fast, to confession when we have sinned seriously.

Then: he will show you a large room upstairs, furnished and ready. Make preparations for us there (Mark 14:15). The room was ready, but the disciples still had to prepare the meal. The psalm says: you prepare a table before me (Psalm 23:5). God prepares; we prepare. How do we prepare our hearts? With a short prayer before Mass, by arriving early, by an act of contrition, by keeping the Eucharistic fast, by a moment of silent thanks after Communion.

So the disciples set out and went to the city, and found everything as he had told them (Mark 14:16). If we prepare, we will find everything as he promised: the Lord himself, truly present, waiting for us. Let us prepare our churches beautifully, our homes for the procession, and above all our hearts for every Mass. Amen.

Dear friends, today we carry the Blessed Sacrament through our streets. The procession is a picture of the whole Christian life: we begin at the altar and we go out to the street. Let us reflect on that journey.

It begins with communion. Jesus said: those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them (John 6:56). To abide means to stay, to make a home. Jesus does not come to us as a visitor who leaves. He wants to make his home in us. The psalm says: O taste and see that the LORD is good (Psalm 34:8). In adoration before the Blessed Sacrament, we learn to stay with him as he stays with us.

The second reading calls Christ the mediator of a new covenant (Hebrews 9:15). Jeremiah had promised: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). The old covenant was written on stone; the new covenant is written on hearts, and the Eucharist is how God writes. Each Communion, received with faith, changes us a little: we become more patient, more pure, more generous.

And that change must show in the street. St Paul warned that those who eat and drink without discerning the body bring judgment on themselves. St John Chrysostom told his people: do you want to honour the body of Christ? Do not ignore him when he is naked; do not honour him here in church with silk and then neglect him outside, where he is cold and naked. Jesus said: just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me (Matthew 25:40). Moses commanded: open your hand to the poor and needy neighbor in your land (Deuteronomy 15:11).

So let us adore him at the altar and serve him in the street. Spend time in adoration this week. Ask him after Communion to write his law on your heart. And let every Mass end in one act of charity for someone in need. Then the procession will not end today; it will continue every day of our lives. Amen.

So when you see the Host lifted up at Mass, or carried in the procession today, say quietly in your heart: My Lord and my God! And if you have made your First Holy Communion, when you receive Jesus, say thank you to him with all your heart.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Solemnity of the Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ the three readings of Year B are held together by one phrase: “the blood of the covenant”. At Sinai Moses reads the book of the covenant, the people promise to obey, and he sprinkles them with the blood of the sacrifice. The Letter to the Hebrews says that Christ, the high priest, has entered the true sanctuary with his own blood and become the mediator of a new covenant. In the Gospel, at the Passover meal, Jesus takes the cup and says, “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.”

Psalm 116, one of the psalms sung at the Passover meal, gives the response: “I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the Lord.” Before the Gospel the Church may sing the sequence Lauda Sion, written by St Thomas Aquinas, which teaches in verse what the Church believes about this sacrament. In India the Latin Church keeps the solemnity on this Sunday, and where possible a procession with the Blessed Sacrament follows the Mass.

Exodus 24:3–8 · The blood of the covenant

Setting.

At Mount Sinai God has spoken the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20) and the laws that follow, which scholars call the “Book of the Covenant” (20:22–23:33). Now the covenant is solemnly sealed. Moses goes between God and the people: he tells them the words of the Lord, writes them down, and then performs a rite that binds both sides together.

Background. In the ancient world, covenants between peoples or between a king and his subjects were often sealed with a sacrifice and a shared meal. Blood was the sign of life (Leviticus 17:11). In this rite the same blood touches both the altar, which stands for God, and the people. Just after our passage Moses and the elders go up the mountain, see the God of Israel, and eat and drink in his presence (24:9–11).

Structure.

  • v. 3: Moses tells the people the words of the Lord; they answer with one voice.
  • v. 4a: Moses writes down the words.
  • vv. 4b–5: the altar and the twelve pillars; burnt offerings and offerings of well-being.
  • v. 6: half the blood dashed against the altar.
  • v. 7: the book of the covenant read; the people promise to obey.
  • v. 8: the rest of the blood dashed on the people: “See the blood of the covenant”.
  • ¶ The structure is a double promise. The people say twice that they will do all the Lord has spoken (vv. 3, 7). Between the two promises stand the word written and the sacrifice offered.

Key words.

  • bərît, covenant: A solemn bond of loyalty, not only a contract. God binds himself to Israel and Israel to God: “I will be your God, and you shall be my people” (compare Leviticus 26:12).
  • dam, blood: For Israel the life is in the blood (Leviticus 17:11), and so blood belongs to God. Sprinkled on altar and people, it makes them in a sense one family, bound by one life.
  • naʿăśeh wənišmāʿ, we will do, and we will be obedient: Literally “we will do and we will hear” (v. 7). Jewish tradition has long admired that the people promised to do before they heard all. The word šāmaʿ, to hear, also means to obey, as in the great prayer “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4).

Verse by verse.

“All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.” (24:3)

The covenant begins with God’s word and the people’s answer. Worship without obedience would be empty.

“set up twelve pillars, corresponding to the twelve tribes of Israel.” (24:4)

The whole people stands around the altar. Jesus will choose twelve apostles, and at the Last Supper the Twelve will be at table with him.

“Then he took the book of the covenant, and read it in the hearing of the people;” (24:7)

The Word is proclaimed before the blood is sprinkled. The Mass has the same order: the Liturgy of the Word, then the Liturgy of the Eucharist.

“See the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you in accordance with all these words.” (24:8)

These are almost the words Jesus will say over the cup (Mark 14:24). The Letter to the Hebrews quotes this verse when it explains the new covenant (Hebrews 9:20).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Sinai in the light of the Last Supper. The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is the memorial of Christ’s Passover, and that in it the sacrifice Christ offered once for all on the Cross remains ever present (CCC 1364). The old covenant was sealed with the blood of animals; the new covenant with the blood of the Son. God’s covenant with Israel has not been revoked (Romans 11:29; CCC 121). The new covenant does not cancel the call of Israel; it brings God’s promises to their fullness in Christ for all peoples.

Hebrews 9:11–15 · How much more the blood of Christ

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews is a long sermon, written for Christians who were tired and tempted to fall back. It has no named author. The Church long linked it with St Paul, and it belongs to the Pauline tradition, but most scholars today think it was written by another teacher, and its date is uncertain. It shows Christ as the high priest of the new covenant.

Background. Chapters 8–10 compare Christ with the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). On that one day each year the high priest entered the Holy of Holies, the inner room of the Temple, with the blood of a bull and a goat, to make atonement for himself and the people. Hebrews says that Christ has done this in reality, once for all, in the true sanctuary of heaven. The “ashes of a heifer” recall another rite of cleansing (Numbers 19).

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: Christ enters the true sanctuary once for all, with his own blood, and obtains eternal redemption.
  • vv. 13–14: if animal blood cleansed the flesh, how much more will Christ’s blood cleanse the conscience.
  • v. 15: he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the eternal inheritance.

Key words.

  • ephapax, once for all: A key word of the letter (7:27; 9:12; 10:10). Christ’s sacrifice does not need to be repeated. Every Mass makes present this one sacrifice; it does not add another.
  • lytrōsis, redemption: Release by a price, the same family of words as the “ransom” of Mark 10:45. Christ obtains an eternal redemption, not one that has to be renewed each year.
  • mesitēs, mediator: One who stands between two parties to make peace. Moses stood between God and the people at Sinai; Christ is mediator of the new covenant (8:6; 9:15), the one promised in Jeremiah 31:31.

Verse by verse.

“he entered once for all into the Holy Place, not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood,” (9:12)

The priest and the victim are the same. Christ does not offer something outside himself; he offers himself.

“how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God,” (9:14)

The whole Trinity is at work: the Son offers himself to the Father through the Spirit. “Without blemish” recalls the lambs of the Passover and the sacrifices, which had to be perfect.

“purify our conscience from dead works to worship the living God!” (9:14)

The old rites cleansed the body. Christ’s blood reaches the conscience, the inner heart, and sets it to worship. “Dead works” are deeds that lead to death, sinful works.

“For this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant,” (9:15)

The letter quotes Jeremiah 31 at length just before (8:8–12). The new covenant is written on the heart.

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent taught that the Mass is a true sacrifice, the same sacrifice as the Cross, offered now in an unbloody manner (Session 22). The Catechism says that the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice: only the manner of offering is different (CCC 1367). That is why Hebrews’ “once for all” is not against the Mass. The Mass is the one sacrifice made present, so that each generation may share in it. The Catechism also calls Christ the “high priest of the good things to come” in the heavenly liturgy (CCC 662).

Mark 14:12–16, 22–26 · This is my body, this is my blood

Setting.

It is the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lambs were sacrificed in the Temple in the afternoon and eaten at night in houses in Jerusalem. The Passover meal remembered the night when God brought Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 12). Families ate the lamb with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, drank cups of wine, retold the story and sang psalms of praise.

The Lectionary leaves out vv. 17–21, where Jesus foretells his betrayal by one of the Twelve. Even so the shadow is there: the meal is the evening before his death.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–16: the preparation: two disciples, a man carrying a jar of water, the large room upstairs.
  • v. 22: the bread: “Take; this is my body.”
  • vv. 23–24: the cup: “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.”
  • v. 25: “I will never again drink of the fruit of the vine until that day…”
  • v. 26: they sing the hymn and go out to the Mount of Olives.

Key words.

  • eulogēsas, eucharistēsas, having blessed, having given thanks: Over the bread Jesus blesses (v. 22); over the cup he gives thanks (v. 23). Both words describe the Jewish blessings said at meals. From eucharistia, thanksgiving, comes the name Eucharist.
  • sōma, body: In biblical thought the body is the whole person in his presence and action. “This is my body” means: this is I myself, given for you.
  • to haima mou tēs diathēkēs, my blood of the covenant: The words echo Exodus 24:8, today’s first reading. Paul and Luke give the form “the new covenant in my blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25; Luke 22:20), recalling Jeremiah 31:31. The blood “poured out for many” recalls the servant of Isaiah 53.

Verse by verse.

“a man carrying a jar of water will meet you; follow him,” (14:13)

Carrying water was usually women’s work, so such a man would be easy to see. Mark shows that Jesus knows and arranges what will happen; he goes to his Passion willingly.

“Take; this is my body.” (14:22)

Jesus does not say “this is like my body” or “this stands for my body”. The Church has always taken his words as they stand.

“This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.” (14:24)

“Poured out” is the language of sacrifice, of blood poured at the altar. Jesus speaks of his death the next day as the sacrifice that seals the new covenant.

“When they had sung the hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.” (14:26)

Most commentators think this was the second part of the Hallel, Psalms 113–118, sung at the end of the Passover meal. Today’s Psalm 116 belongs to it. Jesus went out to Gethsemane with these psalms on his lips.

How the Church reads it.

The Church believes that in the Eucharist the body and blood, together with the soul and divinity, of our Lord Jesus Christ, and therefore the whole Christ, is truly, really and substantially contained (CCC 1374, quoting the Council of Trent). The Council of Trent called the change of the whole substance of bread and wine into the substance of his body and blood transubstantiation (CCC 1376). The Catechism quotes St John Chrysostom: it is not man who makes the gifts become the body and blood of Christ, but Christ himself, who was crucified for us; and St Ambrose: the word of Christ, which could make from nothing what did not exist, can change things that exist into what they were not (CCC 1375, in substance).

The Second Vatican Council calls the Eucharist the source and summit of the whole Christian life (Lumen Gentium 11; CCC 1324). St Augustine told the newly baptised, as they came to receive the body of Christ: be what you see, and receive what you are (in substance, Sermon 272).

Questions people ask

Is the bread at Mass a symbol of Christ’s body?

It is a sign, but not only a sign. The Church teaches that after the consecration what looks like bread and wine is truly the body and blood of Christ, his whole living person (CCC 1374, 1376). This is why we kneel, genuflect before the tabernacle, and fast for an hour before Communion. It is a mystery of faith, not something the senses can prove; as Lauda Sion sings, what you do not understand, a lively faith confirms.

If I receive only the host, do I receive the whole Christ?

Yes. The Catechism teaches that Christ is present whole and entire in each of the species, so that Communion under the species of bread alone makes it possible to receive all the fruit of grace of the Eucharist (CCC 1377, 1390). Communion under both kinds is a fuller sign, and the Church allows it on many occasions, but nothing is lacking to those who receive the host.

Why does the Mass say “for many” and not “for all”?

Because those are the words of Jesus in Mark and Matthew, and the Church keeps them. They do not mean that Christ died only for some. The Catechism explains that “many” is not restrictive: it sets the whole of humanity over against the one person of the Redeemer who gives himself for all (CCC 605). Christ died for all; the many are those who receive what he offers.

For the Latin reader

The feast of Corpus Christi began in the thirteenth century at Liège, in present-day Belgium, inspired by the visions of St Juliana of Cornillon. Pope Urban IV extended it to the whole Church in 1264, and St Thomas Aquinas wrote the texts for its Mass and Office, including Lauda Sion and the hymn Pange lingua, whose last verses, Tantum ergo, are sung at Benediction. In India the solemnity is kept on Sunday, so that the faithful can take part, and in many parishes the Mass is followed by a procession with the Blessed Sacrament through the streets, ending with Benediction. The Church in India also remembers that Pope St Paul VI came to Bombay in 1964 for the International Eucharistic Congress.

For study and teaching

  • The people at Sinai said, “we will do, and we will be obedient”. What does my “Amen” at Communion promise?
  • How do I show, by my body and my manner, that I believe Christ is truly present: in church, before the tabernacle, at Communion?
  • Hebrews says Christ’s blood cleanses our conscience “to worship the living God”. Is there something on my conscience I should bring to the sacrament of reconciliation?
  • Jesus’ body was “given” and his blood “poured out”. How can my family become, like the Eucharist, a life given for others?
