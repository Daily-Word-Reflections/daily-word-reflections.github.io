Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, ‘Show us the Father’?”” John 14:9

Reflection

A week after Paul's sermon in the synagogue of Antioch in Pisidia, “almost the whole city gathered to hear the word of the Lord.” The good news has spread through the whole town, among Jews and Gentiles alike. But success brings conflict. Some of the synagogue's leaders, seeing the crowds, are filled with jealousy and contradict Paul. Paul and Barnabas answer boldly: “It was necessary that the word of God should be spoken first to you.” Notice the word “first.” The apostles never stop going first to the synagogue in every town; God's promises were given to Israel, and Israel keeps its place in God's plan (Romans 11:29). Then they quote the Servant Song of Isaiah, applying it to their own mission: “I have set you to be a light for the Gentiles, so that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth.” What was said of the Servant, and of Christ, is now the task of the Church.

The Gentiles hear this with joy; they “were glad and praised the word of the Lord,” and “the word of the Lord spread throughout the region.” Opposition grows; some of the city's leading men and devout women of high standing are stirred up, and Paul and Barnabas are driven out. Luke is describing a local conflict in one city, not the response of the whole Jewish people, and the Church reads such passages without any hostility to the Jews, our elder brothers in faith (Nostra Aetate 4). The apostles do what Jesus told them: they shake the dust off their feet and move on to Iconium. And then the most surprising line of all: “the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit.” Driven out, and full of joy. That is Easter joy, which does not depend on success. The Psalm sings it: “All the ends of the earth have seen the victory of our God.”

In the Gospel Philip makes a request that comes from the depths of every human heart: “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied.” And Jesus answers with gentle surprise: “Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” In the face of Jesus, in his compassion, his words, his wounds, we see the face of God. Then Jesus makes two astonishing promises: those who believe in him will do “greater works” than his, because he is going to the Father; and “I will do whatever you ask in my name, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son.” Today the Church remembers St Fidelis of Sigmaringen. Mark Roy was a brilliant lawyer in Alsace who was called “the poor man's lawyer” because he defended the poor without payment. Sickened by the dishonesty of his profession, he became a Capuchin friar and took the name Fidelis, “faithful.” He was sent by the newly founded Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith to preach in the Swiss region of Graubünden, in a time of bitter religious and political conflict. On 24 April 1622, after preaching at Seewis, he was attacked and killed. He forgave his attackers as he died. He is honoured as the first martyr of that Congregation. Today Catholics and Protestants, recalling with sorrow the violence of those centuries on all sides, pray and work together as brothers and sisters in Christ.

Full homily

Dear friends, in Antioch of Pisidia almost the whole city comes to hear the word of the Lord. But some are jealous and oppose Paul. And Paul says: it was necessary that the word of God should be spoken first to you. God never forgets his promise to Israel. Then he turns to the Gentiles, saying: I have set you to be a light for the Gentiles (Acts 13:47).

The apostles are driven out of the city. What do they do? They shake off the dust and go on. And Luke says: the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit (Acts 13:52). Driven out, but full of joy!

That is Easter joy. It does not depend on success, on applause, on things going our way. It comes from the risen Lord living in us. Let us not lose our joy when things go wrong.

In the Gospel Philip says: Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied. And Jesus says: have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father (John 14:9). If you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus: his mercy, his patience, his wounds.

Today we remember St Fidelis, a lawyer who helped the poor without payment and then became a Capuchin friar. His name means faithful. He went to preach in Switzerland in a time of great conflict, and he was killed in 1622, forgiving those who attacked him. He was faithful until death.

Let us ask for that faithfulness, and for the joy that no one can take away. And let us look at Jesus, to see the Father. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Philip wanted to see God the Father. Jesus said: if you look at me, you see the Father!

When we look at Jesus, so kind and loving, we see what God is like. Let us say: Jesus, show me the Father!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, in you we see the Father, and in your name we may ask for anything. When the word we bring is welcomed, keep us humble; when it is rejected, keep us full of joy and of the Holy Spirit. Make us a light for the nations, as you sent Paul and Barnabas to be. By the prayers of St Fidelis, who gave up wealth to serve the poor and gave up his life for the faith, make us faithful until death, honest in our work and generous to the needy. Show us the Father, and we will be satisfied. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the next sabbath almost the whole city comes to hear Paul. Opposition follows; Paul and Barnabas turn to the Gentiles in the words of Isaiah, and the disciples, though driven out, are “filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit”. In the Gospel Philip asks, “Lord, show us the Father”, and Jesus answers, “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” Psalm 98 sings that all the ends of the earth have seen the saving power of God. Today is the optional memorial of St Fidelis of Sigmaringen, priest and martyr.

Acts 13:44–52 · A light for the Gentiles

Setting.

Paul’s first sermon had moved many: “many Jews and devout converts to Judaism followed Paul and Barnabas” (13:43). A week later the crowd is so large that some of the synagogue’s members and leaders turn against the preachers. Luke’s phrase “the Jews” here means these opponents in one city, not the Jewish people; many of the first believers in Antioch were themselves Jews. The pattern runs through Acts: the word goes first to the synagogue, and then also to the Gentiles (compare Romans 1:16, “to the Jew first and also to the Greek”).

Structure.

  • vv. 44–45: the whole city gathers; jealousy and contradiction.
  • vv. 46–47: “We are now turning to the Gentiles”; Isaiah 49:6.
  • vv. 48–49: the Gentiles rejoice; the word spreads.
  • vv. 50–52: persecution; dust shaken off; joy and the Spirit.

Key words.

  • zēlos, jealousy, zeal: The same word can mean zeal for God or jealousy. Here it is the sad side: a religious zeal that cannot bear to see others welcomed. It is a temptation for believers in every age.
  • parrēsiazomai, to speak boldly: The word for the apostles’ boldness, which Luke treats as a gift of the Spirit (4:29, 31). Paul and Barnabas do not lose their nerve when opposed; they speak more plainly.
  • tetagmenoi, destined, appointed: “As many as had been destined for eternal life became believers.” Luke stresses that faith is God’s gift and initiative. The Church does not read this as if some were created for damnation: God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). God’s call and the human response work together.

Verse by verse.

“It was necessary that the word of God should be spoken first to you.” (13:46)

“Necessary” because of God’s plan and promise: Israel is the first heir of the word. Turning to the Gentiles in this city does not cancel that; in the very next town Paul goes first to the synagogue again (14:1).

“I have set you to be a light for the Gentiles, so that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth.” (13:47)

Isaiah 49:6, from the song of the Servant. Simeon applied it to the child Jesus (Luke 2:32); Paul applies it to the mission. The light is Christ’s, and his messengers carry it.

“And the disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit.” (13:52)

The chapter ends not in defeat but in joy. Christian joy does not depend on success; it comes from the Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage as the shape of every mission: welcome and rejection, and both met with joy. The Second Vatican Council recalls that the Jews remain most dear to God for the sake of their fathers, for his gifts and call are irrevocable (Nostra Aetate 4; Romans 11:28–29). Paul’s shaking off of the dust, a gesture Jesus had given his disciples (Luke 9:5), is not a curse; it leaves the matter in God’s hands, a sign that the preachers have done their part.

John 14:7–14 · Show us the Father

Setting.

The conversation at the Last Supper continues. Thomas has asked about the way; now Philip, who once told Nathanael, “Come and see” (1:46), asks to see the Father. His request voices the deepest longing of the Old Testament, the prayer of Moses: “Show me your glory, I pray” (Exodus 33:18).

Structure.

  • v. 7: to know Jesus is to know the Father.
  • vv. 8–11: Philip’s request; “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father”; the Father in the Son and the Son in the Father.
  • vv. 12–14: greater works; prayer in his name.

Key words.

  • heōrakōs, has seen: A perfect tense: to have seen and still to see. In Jesus the Father is not glimpsed for a moment but shown to stay.
  • en emoi, in me: “I am in the Father and the Father is in me.” Because of their unity the Father is wholly in the Son and the Son wholly in the Father (CCC 255). What Jesus says and does is the Father’s own speaking and working.
  • onoma, name: To ask “in my name” is not to add a formula but to ask as one united with Jesus, for what he wills, so that the Father may be glorified.

Verse by verse.

“Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me?” (14:9)

A gentle reproach, not a dismissal. One can live close to Jesus for years and still have more of him to discover.

“Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” (14:9)

The center of the passage. Jesus is the image of the invisible God (Colossians 1:15); the Catechism calls the Son the Father’s one, perfect and unsurpassable Word (CCC 65).

“the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these, because I am going to the Father.” (14:12)

Not greater in power but wider in reach: through the Spirit the Church carries the Gospel to the ends of the earth. St Augustine, commenting on this verse, says that to make a sinner righteous is a greater work than to create heaven and earth (in substance; see CCC 1994).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Philip’s request the question of every human heart, and in Jesus’ answer the whole of Christian faith: God is not hidden behind Jesus; he is shown in him. Asking “in his name” is the new thing Jesus gives his disciples on this night (CCC 2614). The prayers of the Mass end “through Christ our Lord”: this is what asking in his name means.

For the Latin reader

St Fidelis of Sigmaringen (1578–1622), a lawyer who became a Capuchin friar, preached in the Swiss region of the Grisons at a time of bitter conflict among Christians. He was killed at Seewis in 1622 and is honored as the first martyr of the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, founded that same year. Like Paul in Antioch of Pisidia, he kept preaching where he was not welcome. The Church remembers him today in a spirit of reconciliation, praying for unity among all who bear the name of Christ.

For study and teaching

  • When I meet opposition for my faith, what would it mean for me to be “filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit” rather than with resentment?
  • Philip had been with Jesus a long time. What have I not yet discovered about Jesus, and so about the Father?
