Word for the day

“All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.” Acts 2:4

Reflection

“When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place.” Pentecost was not a new feast invented by the Church. It was one of the three great pilgrim feasts of Israel, Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, kept fifty days after Passover (the Greek word pentēkostē means “fiftieth”). It began as a harvest festival, when the first loaves of the new wheat were offered to God (Leviticus 23:16); in the time of Jesus many also linked it with the gift of the Law at Sinai, which came about fifty days after the Exodus. That is why Jerusalem is full of “devout Jews from every nation under heaven.” And now, fifty days after the Passover of Jesus' death and resurrection, God gives the first harvest of the new creation and a new law, written not on stone but on hearts. The signs recall Sinai, where “the Lord had descended upon it in fire” with thunder and a trumpet blast: “suddenly from heaven there came a sound like the rush of a violent wind, and it filled the entire house where they were sitting. Divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them.” Wind and breath are one word in Hebrew (ruach) and in Greek (pneuma); John the Baptist had promised one who would baptise “with the Holy Spirit and fire.” Then the gift is named: “All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.”

The crowd that gathers is astonished: “Are not all these who are speaking Galileans? And how is it that we hear, each of us, in our own native language?” Luke lists the nations, from Parthians and Medes in the east to visitors from Rome in the west, Jews and proselytes, Cretans and Arabs. The Fathers of the Church saw here the reversal of Babel. At Babel people tried to build “a tower with its top in the heavens” and “make a name for ourselves,” and they ended unable to understand one another, scattered over the earth (Genesis 11). At Pentecost God comes down as a gift, and the scattered are gathered: not by forcing everyone into one language, but by making the one Gospel heard in every language. The Church is born speaking many tongues. Here, in a land of many languages, we can feel the meaning of this: the same Good News is proclaimed today in Malayalam and Tamil, Hindi and Konkani, Telugu and Kannada and English, and it is one faith. What they all hear is the same: “in our own languages we hear them speaking about God's deeds of power.” The Psalm sings the Spirit's work on all creation: “When you send forth your spirit, they are created; and you renew the face of the ground.” The Lectionary refrain prays it as a petition: Lord, send forth your Spirit, and renew the face of the earth.

St Paul, writing to a quarrelsome community in Corinth, shows us the Spirit's work inside the Church. First, faith itself is his gift: “no one can say ‘Jesus is Lord’ except by the Holy Spirit.” Every act of faith, every prayer, even the simplest “Jesus,” is already the Spirit at work in us. Then he speaks of variety in unity: “there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of services, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of activities, but it is the same God who activates all of them in everyone.” Spirit, Lord, God: Paul names the whole Trinity in one breath. And the purpose of every gift is clear: “To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good.” Gifts are not medals for display but tools for building up others. Finally the image of the body: “For in the one Spirit we were all baptized into one body,” and “we were all made to drink of one Spirit.” Baptism plunged us into the Spirit; Confirmation sealed us with him; the Eucharist makes us one body. If the second reading from Galatians is chosen, Paul lists “the fruit of the Spirit”: “love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.” These are the signs that the Spirit lives in us.

The Gospel takes us back to the evening of Easter Day. The disciples are behind locked doors “for fear of the Jews,” which in John's Gospel means the Temple authorities who had handed Jesus over, not the Jewish people as such; the disciples themselves were Jews. Jesus comes and stands among them: “Peace be with you.” He shows them his hands and his side, the wounds that are now the proof of his love and his victory. Then he sends them: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” And “he breathed on them and said to them, ‘Receive the Holy Spirit.’” The verb John uses for “breathed” is the same word the Greek Bible uses in Genesis, when the Lord God “breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and the man became a living being.” The risen Christ breathes a new creation into his disciples. And the first work of that new creation is forgiveness: “If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.” The Church has always heard in these words the institution of the sacrament of Penance: the Holy Spirit is given for the forgiveness of sins. If the alternative Gospel is read, Jesus promises “the Advocate … the Spirit of truth,” who “will guide you into all the truth.”

Luke tells us who was in the upper room when the Spirit came: the apostles, “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). The one who conceived the Son of God by the power of the Holy Spirit was there when the same Spirit came upon the Church. Mary prayed with the apostles for the gift she had already received in fullness; she is the Mother of the Church at its birth, and tomorrow the Church will honour her with that title. Today the Church prays the ancient Sequence, Veni, Sancte Spiritus, with its tender names for the Spirit: Father of the poor, giver of gifts, light of hearts, best of comforters, sweet guest of the soul, rest in labour, coolness in the heat, comfort in tears. It asks him to wash what is stained, water what is dry, heal what is wounded, bend what is rigid, warm what is cold, and guide what has gone astray. That is the prayer for every Christian today. The Easter season ends not with an ending but with a sending. The candle that stood by the ambo for fifty days goes to the font, because the fire of Easter now burns in the baptised, sent out into the world with the Spirit's peace.

Pentecost: the wind and fire of Acts 2, the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12, and the breath of the risen Christ on Easter evening.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, happy birthday! Today is the birthday of the Church. Fifty days ago we sang the Alleluia of Easter; today we sing it for the last time in this season, and we celebrate the great gift that Easter was for: the Holy Spirit.

Look at the apostles before Pentecost. They had seen the risen Lord, they had eaten with him, they had watched him ascend. And yet they were still in one room, waiting. Then, when the day of Pentecost had come, suddenly from heaven there came a sound like the rush of a violent wind, and tongues as of fire rested on each of them. All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit (Acts 2:2–4). The same frightened men opened the doors and went out into the street to proclaim the deeds of God.

And what happened outside? People from every nation heard them, each in their own native language. At Babel human pride divided languages and scattered people. At Pentecost God's gift gathered them. Notice: the Spirit did not make everyone speak one language. He made the one Gospel understood in many. Here in India we know what that means. In our country the same Mass is offered and the same Gospel proclaimed in many languages, and it is one faith, one Lord, one Spirit.

St Paul tells us that the Spirit is not only for great events. No one can say Jesus is Lord except by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:3). Every time you pray, even a simple Jesus, I love you, the Spirit is praying in you. And he gives gifts to each one: there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:4). The gift of teaching, of caring for the sick, of music, of patience with old parents, of making peace. Each is given for the common good. Nobody in the Church is useless; nobody is without a gift.

In the Gospel we go back to Easter evening. The doors are locked; the disciples are afraid. Jesus comes, shows his wounds, and says: Peace be with you. Then he breathed on them and said, Receive the Holy Spirit (John 20:22). Just as God breathed life into Adam at the beginning, the risen Christ breathes new life into his Church. And the first gift of that breath is forgiveness: if you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them. Every confession is a little Pentecost, a breath of new life.

Finally, remember who was in the upper room: Mary, the Mother of Jesus, praying with the apostles. The Spirit who overshadowed her at Nazareth now fills the whole Church. Let us ask her to pray with us today. What doors are locked in our lives? What fears keep us silent? Where do we need forgiveness, healing, warmth? Let us pray with the whole Church: Come, Holy Spirit. Heal what is wounded, warm what is cold, guide what has gone astray. Send forth your Spirit, and renew the face of the earth. Amen. Alleluia, alleluia!

Homily option 2 · The gifts and the fruit of the Spirit

The Spirit who came at Pentecost comes to each of us in Baptism and Confirmation. He brings gifts that strengthen us and fruit that others can see.

1. The seven gifts

  • Isaiah's promise of the Spirit.
  • Wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude.
  • Knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord.
📖 Isaiah 11:2–3
📖 1 Corinthians 12:7–11

Apply: Each day this week, ask for one of the seven gifts by name.

2. The fruit others can see

  • Love, joy, peace.
  • Patience, kindness, generosity.
  • Faithfulness, gentleness, self-control.
📖 Ezekiel 36:26–27
📖 Galatians 5:22–25

Apply: Ask a family member which fruit of the Spirit you most need.

3. Sealed with the Spirit

  • Peter and John lay on hands.
  • Confirmation completes Baptism.
  • Strength to witness.
📖 Joel 2:28–29
📖 Acts 8:14–17

Apply: Find out the date of your Confirmation and pray for your sponsor.

The Spirit gives gifts to make us strong and fruit to make us like Christ.

What began at Pentecost continues in every Confirmation.

Take-home resolution: This week I will pray daily for the gifts of the Spirit and work on one fruit of the Spirit.

Dear brothers and sisters, at Pentecost the Holy Spirit came upon the apostles with wind and fire. But the Spirit did not come only once, long ago. He comes to every Christian in Baptism and, in a special way, in Confirmation. Today let us look at what he brings: gifts and fruit.

Isaiah foretold the Messiah: the spirit of the LORD shall rest on him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD (Isaiah 11:2). From this passage the Church names seven gifts: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety and fear of the Lord. The Spirit who rested on Christ now rests on us. To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good (1 Corinthians 12:7).

The gifts make us strong. The fruit shows that the Spirit lives in us. Paul writes: the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22–23). God had promised: I will put my spirit within you (Ezekiel 36:27). If someone wants to know whether the Spirit lives in me, they should not look for wonders but for this fruit. Am I patient? Am I kind? Can I control my tongue?

In the Acts of the Apostles, when the people of Samaria had been baptised, Peter and John came and laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:17). That is the root of our sacrament of Confirmation. Joel had promised: I will pour out my spirit on all flesh (Joel 2:28). The bishop's hand and the holy chrism seal us with the Spirit, to make us witnesses of Christ.

So today let us thank God for our Confirmation. If you were never confirmed, speak to the parish priest. Pray each day this week for one of the seven gifts. Ask someone at home which fruit you most need, and work on it. And at this Mass, let us pray: Come, Holy Spirit, fill the hearts of your faithful. Alleluia. Amen.

Homily option 3 · Father of the poor, best of consolers

Before the Gospel today the Church sings the Golden Sequence, Veni, Sancte Spiritus. It calls the Spirit father of the poor and best of consolers. These titles are a whole teaching on the Spirit.

1. Father of the poor

  • The Spirit anointed Jesus for the poor.
  • He comes to the empty-handed.
  • Our poverty is his opening.
📖 Isaiah 61:1
📖 Luke 4:18

Apply: Visit or help one poor person this week in the power of the Spirit.

2. Best of consolers

  • Another Advocate.
  • Rest in labour, comfort in tears.
  • He stays with us for ever.
📖 Isaiah 40:1
📖 John 14:16–17

Apply: Console one grieving or lonely person this week.

3. Without you, nothing

  • Breath taken away, we die.
  • Spirit sent, we are created.
  • He prays in our weakness.
📖 Psalm 104:29–30
📖 Romans 8:26

Apply: Before every task this week, pray: Come, Holy Spirit.

The Holy Spirit comes to the poor, consoles the sorrowful and gives life to all.

Without him we can do nothing; with him all things are made new.

Take-home resolution: This week I will begin every task with Come, Holy Spirit, and console one person who suffers.

Dear friends, today the Church sings one of her most beautiful prayers, the Golden Sequence: Come, Holy Spirit. It gives the Spirit tender names. Let us reflect on three of them.

First, the Sequence calls him father of the poor. In the synagogue at Nazareth Jesus read from Isaiah: the Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor (Luke 4:18; Isaiah 61:1). The Spirit loves to come where hands are empty. The proud and self-sufficient have no room for him. If you feel poor today, in money, in strength, in faith, you are exactly where the Spirit likes to come.

Second, he is called the best of consolers, rest in our labour, coolness in the heat, comfort in our tears. Jesus promised: I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Advocate, to be with you forever (John 14:16). The prophet had heard God say: comfort, O comfort my people (Isaiah 40:1). The Holy Spirit is God's comfort living in us. He does not always take the sorrow away, but he never leaves us alone in it.

Third, the Sequence admits that without the Spirit there is nothing in us, nothing that is not harmful. The psalm says the same: when you take away their breath, they die and return to their dust. When you send forth your spirit, they are created (Psalm 104:29–30). Even our prayer depends on him: the Spirit helps us in our weakness; for we do not know how to pray as we ought (Romans 8:26). Every good thing we do begins with his breath.

So let us make the Sequence our prayer this week. Before every task, say: Come, Holy Spirit. Visit someone poor and console someone in tears, so that through us the Spirit may be father of the poor and consoler of the sorrowful. And at this Mass, let us ask him to send forth his Spirit and renew our hearts. Alleluia, alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, today is the birthday of the Church! Fifty days after Easter, the friends of Jesus were praying with Mary. Suddenly, whoosh, a sound like a strong wind! And little flames like fire came on each of them. It was the Holy Spirit! They were not scared any more, and they told everybody about Jesus.

The Holy Spirit lives in you too, from your Baptism. He helps you pray, be kind and be brave. Let us say together: Come, Holy Spirit, fill my heart with your love!

Prayer

O God, who by the mystery of today's great feast sanctify your whole Church in every people and nation, pour out, we pray, the gifts of the Holy Spirit across the face of the earth and, with the divine grace that was at work when the Gospel was first proclaimed, fill now once more the hearts of believers. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Come, Holy Spirit, fill the hearts of your faithful and kindle in them the fire of your love. Breath of the risen Christ, give us his peace and the joy of forgiveness. Tongue of fire, give us words to speak of God's deeds of power in every language we know. Giver of gifts, make each of us useful for the common good, and make us one body in Christ. With Mary, who waited with the apostles, we pray: send forth your Spirit, and renew the face of the earth. Amen.
