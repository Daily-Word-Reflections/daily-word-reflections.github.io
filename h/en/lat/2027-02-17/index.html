Word for the day

“When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil ways, God changed his mind about the calamity that he had said he would bring upon them; and he did not do it.” Jonah 3:10

Reflection

“The word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time.” That little phrase is already Gospel. Jonah had run away the first time, and God gave him a second chance. Now he walks into Nineveh, the vast capital of Assyria, Israel's cruel enemy, and preaches the shortest sermon in the Bible: “Forty days more, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!” Then something astonishing happens. “The people of Nineveh believed God; they proclaimed a fast, and everyone, great and small, put on sackcloth.” The king rises from his throne, takes off his robe and sits in ashes. Even the animals are made to fast. The royal decree says: “All shall turn from their evil ways and from the violence that is in their hands. Who knows? God may relent.” And God does. The forty days of Nineveh are the forty days of our Lent: a time when a whole people, from the palace to the cattle shed, turns back to God.

In the Gospel the crowds keep asking Jesus for a sign. He answers that they will get only one: “the sign of Jonah. For just as Jonah became a sign to the people of Nineveh, so the Son of Man will be to this generation.” Jonah came to Nineveh as a man who had been three days in the belly of the fish; Jesus will give the sign of his own death and resurrection on the third day. The pagans of Nineveh repented at a stranger's five-word sermon, and the queen of the South crossed the earth to hear Solomon. “See, something greater than Jonah is here!” The danger is not that God gives too few signs, but that we who hear Christ every Sunday stop being moved. Today the Church also remembers seven merchants of Florence who in 1233 left their businesses to live in prayer and penance on Monte Senario, serving Our Lady of Sorrows: the Servites. Like Nineveh, they turned around completely.

Full homily

Dear friends, the word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time (Jonah 3:1). That is good news: God does not give up on us after our first failure.

Jonah enters Nineveh and cries: forty days more, and Nineveh shall be overthrown! (Jonah 3:4). And the people of Nineveh believed God; they proclaimed a fast, and everyone, great and small, put on sackcloth (Jonah 3:5). Even the king stood up from his throne and sat in ashes.

God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil ways, and he did not bring the calamity (Jonah 3:10). Repentance is not just feeling sorry; it is turning, with our hands as well as our hearts.

Jesus says the only sign he will give is the sign of Jonah: his death and resurrection. See, something greater than Jonah is here! (Luke 11:32). If the pagans turned at five words, how much more should we turn at the word of Christ. Amen.

For children

Children, the people of Nineveh were doing many bad things. When Jonah told them to change, they all said sorry to God, even the king! And God forgave them. God always forgives us when we are truly sorry.

Prayer

Merciful God, you sent Jonah to Nineveh a second time and you relented when the people turned to you. Give us a second chance and a contrite heart. May we, who have heard one greater than Jonah, turn from our evil ways and live. Through the prayers of the Seven Holy Founders, help us to stand with Mary at the cross of her Son. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This is the Lenten reading par excellence: a whole foreign city turns to God at the preaching of a reluctant prophet. Jesus takes up that story and turns it toward his hearers: those people listened to Jonah, and someone greater than Jonah is here. The question for Lent is simple. Nineveh needed five Hebrew words; how many have I heard? The key words are turn, sign and greater.

Jonah 3:1–10 · Who knows? God may relent

Setting.

The Book of Jonah is short and unlike the other prophets: it is a story about a prophet, not a collection of his sayings. A prophet named Jonah son of Amittai appears in 2 Kings 14:25, in the eighth century BC. Many Catholic scholars read the book as a teaching story, written later, after the exile. Its message stands in either case.

Nineveh was a capital of Assyria, the empire that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, and it stood in Israel’s memory as a city of cruelty; the prophet Nahum sings of its fall (612 BC). Jonah has already fled from this mission once, and has been swallowed and brought back (chapters 1–2). Now comes the second call. Chapter 4 will show why he ran: he did not want these people forgiven.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3a: the second call, and this time Jonah goes.
  • vv. 3b–4: the great city and the very short sermon.
  • v. 5: the people believe and fast, from the greatest to the least.
  • vv. 6–9: the king and his decree.
  • v. 10: God sees, and does not do what he had said.

Key words.

  • šûb, to turn: The Old Testament’s great word for conversion. It is about direction: you were walking one way, and you turn around. Here it is used of the people (v. 8) and of God (v. 9).
  • nehpāket, overthrown: From a verb meaning to overturn, used of Sodom (Genesis 19:25). But it can also mean to be changed, and many have noticed the double sense: Nineveh was indeed overturned in forty days, by conversion.
  • nāḥam, to relent, change one’s mind: Said of God, this describes in human language his turning from a threat when people turn from their sin. Jeremiah states the rule: “if that nation, concerning which I have spoken, turns from its evil, I will change my mind” (18:8).

Verse by verse.

“The word of the Lord came to Jonah a second time,” (3:1)

The God who gives a second chance to Nineveh has first given one to his own prophet.

“Forty days more, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!” (3:4)

In Hebrew the sermon is five words long. There is no call to repent and no offer of mercy in it. A note on the text. The Hebrew says forty days, while the Greek Old Testament says three.

“And the people of Nineveh believed God;” (3:5)

They respond before the king does, and before anyone tells them what to do. Foreigners do at once what Israel’s prophet had run away from.

“Who knows? God may relent and change his mind;” (3:9)

The king claims nothing. Repentance does not put God under an obligation; it hopes in his mercy. Joel uses the same words (Joel 2:14). Even the animals in sackcloth (v. 8), with their touch of humor, show how complete the turning was.

“When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil ways,” (3:10)

God looks at what they did: not at the sackcloth, but at the turning from evil and violence (v. 8).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ call to conversion does not aim first at outward works, “sackcloth and ashes”, fasting and mortification, but at conversion of the heart; without this such works remain sterile, and true interior conversion in turn seeks expression in visible signs (CCC 1430). Lent is one of the Church’s seasons of penance (CCC 1438). The Syriac Churches, including our Syro-Malabar and Malankara brothers and sisters in Kerala, keep a special three-day fast in memory of the repentance of Nineveh, before Lent begins.

Luke 11:29–32 · Something greater than Jonah is here

Setting.

Luke’s setting explains the saying. Jesus has driven out a demon, some have said he does it by Beelzebul, and “others, to test him, kept demanding from him a sign from heaven” (11:16). Now, with the crowds growing, he answers. He has already given signs. The trouble is what the demand for one more sign hides.

“This generation” in the Gospels means those who refuse to hear, in that place and time. It is a warning to every listener in every age, not a verdict on a people (CCC 597–598).

Structure.

  • v. 29: no sign but the sign of Jonah.
  • v. 30: Jonah was a sign to Nineveh; so the Son of Man will be.
  • v. 31: the queen of the South.
  • v. 32: the people of Nineveh.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: A mark that points beyond itself. A sign demanded as proof is different from a sign received in faith.
  • basilissa notou, queen of the South: The queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10:1–13), who came from far away to test Solomon with hard questions. A foreign woman and a foreign city are the witnesses in this court.
  • pleion, greater, “something greater”: The Greek is neuter: not “someone” but “something greater”. It points to what is happening among them in Jesus, greater than wisdom and greater than prophecy.

Verse by verse.

“For just as Jonah became a sign to the people of Nineveh, so the Son of Man will be to this generation.” (11:30)

In Luke the sign is Jonah himself and his preaching; Luke says nothing here about the sea monster. Matthew draws out the three days and three nights (Matthew 12:40). The two fit together: the one who preaches is the one who will go down into death and rise.

“The people of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it,” (11:32)

The picture is of a court. The foreigners stand as witnesses for the prosecution, not because they were holy, but because they repented.

“because they repented at the proclamation of Jonah, and see, something greater than Jonah is here!” (11:32)

The argument goes from the lesser to the greater. Nineveh had a reluctant prophet and no miracles. We have the Son of Man.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that only the divine identity of Jesus’ person can justify a claim like “something greater than Jonah” and “greater than Solomon” (CCC 590). It also notes that Jesus speaks of the sign of Jonah when he announces his Resurrection on the third day (CCC 994). So the sign that the Lord gives his Church is the Paschal mystery itself, toward which all of Lent is walking.

Questions people ask

Does God change his mind?

Scripture says both things. “God is not a human being, that he should lie, or a mortal, that he should change his mind” (Numbers 23:19), and also that God “changed his mind” about Nineveh (3:10). God’s will to save does not change. The threat was given so that it would never have to be carried out. When we change, we meet that same will as mercy instead of as judgment. Scripture’s human language tells us that God truly responds to his people.

For study and teaching

  • Jonah ran from a mission because he did not want certain people forgiven. Is there anyone I secretly do not want God to forgive?
  • What “sign” am I waiting for before I turn to God in earnest? What has he already given me?
