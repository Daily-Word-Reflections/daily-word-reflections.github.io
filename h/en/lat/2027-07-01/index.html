Word for the day

“When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.”” Matthew 9:2

Reflection

Today's first reading is one of the most demanding chapters of the Bible. “After these things God tested Abraham.” The old man has waited twenty-five years for Isaac, has sent Ishmael away at God's command, and now hears the voice again: “Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering.” Notice how slowly the command tightens: your son, your only son, Isaac, whom you love. Each word presses on the father's heart. The surrounding peoples did sacrifice their children, and the Bible tells this story to show the opposite: the God of Abraham does not want the death of a child. The angel stops the knife, and Israel will never offer her sons. Yet the test is real, and Abraham's answer is a model of faith. He rises early in the morning. He walks for three days. He says to his servants, “we will worship, and then we will come back to you.” He believes, as the Letter to the Hebrews explains, that “God is able even to raise someone from the dead” (Hebrews 11:19). The most moving words are Isaac's: “The fire and the wood are here, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?” and Abraham's reply: “God himself will provide the lamb for a burnt offering, my son.” The Hebrew name he gives the mountain, Yahweh-yireh, means “The Lord will provide.” The Church has always heard in this story a shadow of the Father who “did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us” (Romans 8:32): the beloved son who carries the wood, the mountain in the land of Moriah, the three days, the lamb that God himself provides. Our Jewish elder brothers and sisters read this chapter, the Akedah, with deep reverence at the New Year, and we honour their reading as we offer ours. The Psalm gives the right answer to a test passed: “Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory.”

Matthew's Gospel now brings us home to Capernaum, “his own town.” Some people carry a paralysed man to Jesus on a bed, and the first thing Matthew tells us is that Jesus sees something: “When Jesus saw their faith.” The faith of the friends carries the sick man, and Jesus answers with a sentence that nobody asked for: “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.” The man had come for his legs; Jesus first heals his heart. Some scribes think, “This man is blaspheming,” and from their own point of view they are right: only God can forgive sins. Jesus does not deny it. He reads their thoughts and asks which is easier, to say “Your sins are forgiven” or “Stand up and walk.” Anyone can say the first, since no one can check it; the second can be seen. So he proves the invisible by the visible: “so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins,” he says to the paralytic, “Stand up, take your bed and go to your home.” And the man goes home carrying the bed that carried him. The crowds “glorified God, who had given such authority to human beings.” The last words are in the plural, and the Church has always understood why: on Easter evening Jesus breathed on his apostles and said, “If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them” (John 20:23). This is why we confess our sins to a priest: the one who forgave the paralytic in Capernaum forgives us through the ministry of his Church (Catechism 1441–1442). And the lamb that God provided on Moriah is the One of whom John the Baptist said, “Here is the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the first reading takes us up the mountain with Abraham and Isaac. It is a hard story, and we should not hurry past it. God asks Abraham for his son, his only son, whom he loves. Abraham does not argue. He rises early. He walks for three days. And on the way a boy asks the question every child would ask: where is the lamb? And the father who has no answer except trust says: God himself will provide the lamb, my son (Genesis 22:8).

At the last moment, the angel calls: Abraham, Abraham! Do not lay your hand on the boy (Genesis 22:12). The God of Abraham does not want children sacrificed. He wanted Abraham's heart, and Abraham gave it. And the story does not end with a test passed. It ends with a promise: in your offspring all the nations of the earth will gain blessing (Genesis 22:18). That offspring is Christ. Centuries later another Father would not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us (Romans 8:32), and John would point to Jesus and say: Here is the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). God did provide the lamb.

In the Gospel the lamb is already at work, in a small town by the lake. Friends bring a paralysed man, and Matthew says: when Jesus saw their faith (Matthew 9:2). Think of that. The friends say nothing in the story, and yet Jesus hears them. Their faith carried a man who could not walk. Who in your life has carried you? A mother, a grandmother, a teacher, a friend who prayed? And whom are you carrying?

Then Jesus says the unexpected words: Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven (Matthew 9:2). The paralytic's real burden was not his legs. Jesus heals the heart first, and then, to show that he has the right, he heals the body. And the crowds glorify God who had given such authority to human beings. Dear friends, that is the sacrament of Reconciliation. This month, the month of the Precious Blood, let us make the time. Let the Lamb of God lift the burden off our hearts, and then let us go home, like the paralytic, carrying the bed that carried us.

For children

Children, Abraham loved his son Isaac. One day God asked Abraham to trust him in a very hard way. Abraham got up early and walked to a mountain. Isaac asked: where is the lamb? Abraham said: God will provide the lamb. And God did! He stopped Abraham and gave a ram. God never wants a child to be hurt, and he always keeps his promise. In the Gospel, some friends carried a man who could not walk to Jesus. Jesus said: your sins are forgiven, and he made the man walk! Let us be good friends who help carry others.

Prayer

God of Abraham, you asked everything of your friend and you gave everything back to him. Give us a faith that trusts you on the mountain we do not understand, and teach us that you yourself provide the lamb. Lord Jesus, Lamb of God, who forgave the paralysed man and gave your Church the power to forgive, heal our hearts before our bodies, and make us friends who carry one another to you. Through your Precious Blood, wash away our sins and bring us home. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two stories of trust in God’s provision stand side by side. On Mount Moriah Abraham is asked for the one thing that holds all God’s promises, his son Isaac, and answers Isaac’s question with the words “God himself will provide”. In Capernaum a paralyzed man is carried to Jesus by friends, and Jesus, seeing their faith, offers first what the man did not ask for: the forgiveness of his sins. In both, God gives more than anyone dared to hope. The psalm, Psalm 115, gives the right answer: “Not to us, O Lord, but to your name give glory.”

Genesis 22:1b–19 · God himself will provide

Setting.

Abraham was a hundred years old when Isaac, the child of the promise, was born (Genesis 21:5), after many years of waiting. Only shortly before our passage Abraham had to send away his other son, Ishmael (21:8–21). Now God asks for Isaac. The ancient Jewish tradition calls this chapter the Akedah, “the binding” of Isaac, and it is one of the most reflected-on chapters of the Old Testament.

Background. Moriah is mentioned again only in 2 Chronicles 3:1, where Solomon builds the Temple “on Mount Moriah”, so the story was early linked with Jerusalem. Some neighbouring peoples practised child sacrifice, and the Law of Israel forbids it (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31). Many readers see in the story’s ending a lesson that the God of Israel does not want it. The narrator tells us at the start what the reader must know and Abraham does not: “God tested Abraham”.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: God’s call, “Abraham!”, and the command, with its four slow steps from “your son” to “whom you love”.
  • vv. 3–8: the three-day journey; the wood laid on Isaac; Isaac’s question and Abraham’s answer.
  • vv. 9–10: the altar, the binding, the raised knife.
  • vv. 11–14: the angel of the Lord stops him; the ram; the place is named.
  • vv. 15–19: God’s oath renews the blessing for all the nations.

Key words.

  • nissāh, tested: To test is to bring what is hidden in a person into the open, not to lead into sin. The Letter of James says that God tempts no one to evil (James 1:13). Here the test shows the depth of Abraham’s trust, and teaches Abraham himself.
  • hinnēnî, Here I am: Abraham says this three times: to God (v. 1), to Isaac (v. 7), and to the angel (v. 11). It is the answer of a man wholly available, to God and to his child alike.
  • YHWH yir’eh, The Lord will provide: Literally “the Lord sees” or “sees to it”. The name Abraham gives the place (v. 14) repeats his own answer in verse 8: God sees the need and supplies it.

Verse by verse.

“Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love” (22:2)

Each phrase tightens the cost. In the Hebrew the name Isaac comes last, as if God were holding back the word that hurts most. The command does not explain itself; it asks for trust.

“God himself will provide the lamb for a burnt offering, my son.” (22:8)

Abraham neither lies to Isaac nor claims to understand. It is an answer of faith, and the Church hears in it a promise only Christ fulfils (John 1:29).

“Do not lay your hand on the boy or do anything to him” (22:12)

God stops the sacrifice before it happens. Isaac is not given up to death; he is given back. The test was of Abraham’s heart, and God never willed the child’s death.

“by your offspring shall all the nations of the earth gain blessing for themselves” (22:18)

The promise made at the first call (12:3) is sworn by God’s own oath. Paul reads the “offspring” as finally fulfilled in Christ (Galatians 3:16).

How the Church reads it.

The New Testament reads this chapter as a lesson in faith. The Letter to the Hebrews says that Abraham, offering Isaac, reasoned that God was able even to raise someone from the dead (Hebrews 11:17–19). The Catechism speaks of Abraham’s faith that did not weaken, “God himself will provide the lamb”, and sees him made like the Father who did not spare his own Son (CCC 2572; Romans 8:32). The Fathers saw Isaac carrying the wood as a figure of Christ carrying his Cross; Origen develops this at length (in substance, Homilies on Genesis 8). The first Eucharistic Prayer of the Mass, the Roman Canon, names “the sacrifice of Abraham, our father in faith” among the offerings that God has accepted. The Church does not read the chapter as approval of human sacrifice: God alone is Lord of life (CCC 2258).

Matthew 9:1–8 · Authority to forgive sins

Setting.

Jesus has just crossed the Sea of Galilee after healing two men on the far shore (8:28–34). He comes to “his own town”, which is Capernaum, where he had made his home (4:13). Mark 2:1–12 and Luke 5:17–26 tell the same story with more detail, including the roof opened by the bearers. Matthew tells it briefly, so that attention rests on one question: who has authority to forgive sins?

Background. Many people in the ancient world linked serious illness with sin, and the man’s friends may have shared that view. Jesus never says here that his illness was caused by his sins. The scribes are right in one thing: only God forgives sins. What they do not yet see is that God is at work in Jesus.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2a: arrival in Capernaum; the bed carried to Jesus.
  • v. 2b: Jesus sees their faith and says, “your sins are forgiven”.
  • vv. 3–4: some scribes think “blasphemy”; Jesus knows their thoughts.
  • vv. 5–6a: which is easier? The Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.
  • vv. 6b–8: “Stand up”; the man goes home; the crowds glorify God.

Key words.

  • tharsei, take heart: A word of encouragement, used again in Matthew 9:22 and 14:27. Jesus first calls the man “son”, then strengthens him; the forgiveness comes as a father’s word.
  • blasphēmei, he blasphemes: To speak against God, or to claim what belongs to God alone. By the law, only God can forgive. The scribes reason well from a wrong starting point: they do not recognize who stands before them.
  • exousia, authority: Not only power but the right to act. Matthew uses the word again of Jesus’ teaching (7:29) and at the very end of the Gospel (28:18).

Verse by verse.

“When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Take heart, son; your sins are forgiven.”” (9:2)

“Their faith” is the faith of the friends who carried him, and perhaps his own. Jesus answers the deeper need first; the healing of the body follows as a sign.

“Why do you think evil in your hearts?” (9:4)

Jesus reads hearts, which Scripture attributes to God (1 Samuel 16:7). His question is not hostile; it opens a door to understanding.

“so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins” (9:6)

An unseen forgiveness is proved by a visible cure. The “Son of Man” recalls the figure of Daniel 7:13–14, to whom dominion is given.

“they were filled with awe, and they glorified God, who had given such authority to human beings.” (9:8)

The crowd says “human beings”, in the plural. The Church hears in this a hint of what Jesus will give to his apostles and their successors (John 20:21–23).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only God forgives sins, and that Jesus, as Son of God, says of himself that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive them (CCC 1441). Christ willed that the whole Church, in her prayer, life and work, be the instrument of the forgiveness he won, and he entrusted its exercise to the apostles and their successors (CCC 1442). So the priest in the sacrament of Penance speaks the words of absolution in the name of Christ. The friends who carry the paralyzed man are also a picture of intercession: since Abraham, standing before God for another has marked a heart that shares God’s mercy (CCC 2635).

Questions people ask

Did God really ask Abraham to kill his son?

The text says plainly that God “tested” Abraham, and the story ends with God forbidding the sacrifice. The Church reads it as a trial of faith and trust, not as approval of human sacrifice. God alone is the Lord of life (CCC 2258). The story teaches that Abraham held nothing back from God, and that God does not take what he asks us to be ready to give.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham answered, “Here I am”, to God and to his son. When am I truly available to God and to the people closest to me?
  • The friends carried the paralyzed man to Jesus. Who is someone I can carry to him in prayer this week?
