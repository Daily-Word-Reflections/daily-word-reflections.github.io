Word for the day

“I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” John 10:11

Reflection

Every year, halfway between Easter and Pentecost, the Church sets before us the face of the risen Christ as the Good Shepherd. The first reading shows us one of his under-shepherds at work. Peter and John have been arrested for healing a man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate of the Temple and for preaching the resurrection. The next morning they stand before the rulers, the elders and the scribes, the high priestly family among them. And Peter, “filled with the Holy Spirit,” speaks. Only a few weeks earlier, in the courtyard of the high priest, this same man had been frightened by a servant girl into denying that he knew Jesus. Now he says to the most powerful men in Jerusalem: if you are asking how this man was healed, “let it be known to all of you… that this man is standing before you in good health by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead.” Then he quotes the psalm we sing today: “This Jesus is ‘the stone that was rejected by you, the builders; it has become the cornerstone.’” And he ends with one of the boldest sentences in the New Testament: “There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved.” The Church has never softened this. Jesus Christ is the one Saviour of all (Catechism 432; 846). At the same time she teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know him, but seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will, can be saved by his grace, which reaches them in ways known to God (Catechism 847; Lumen Gentium 16). Peter's words are spoken to the particular leaders before him at that moment, not to the Jewish people as a whole (Catechism 597); he himself is a son of Israel announcing Israel's Messiah.

The Gospel is the heart of the day. “I am the good shepherd.” The Greek word for “good” here, kalos, also means noble and beautiful: Jesus is the true shepherd, the shepherd as a shepherd should be. Israel knew the image well. God was the shepherd of his people (Psalm 23; Psalm 80:1), and through Ezekiel he had condemned the shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock and promised: “I myself will search for my sheep, and will seek them out” (Ezekiel 34:11). When Jesus says “I am the good shepherd,” he claims to be that promised shepherd, God himself come to seek his sheep. He sets himself against the hired hand, who works for wages, “sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and runs away.” The difference is not skill but love: the hired hand “does not care for the sheep.” The Good Shepherd knows his own, and they know him, “just as the Father knows me and I know the Father.” This is not a list of names on a register; it is the intimacy of the Trinity offered to each of us. Five times in these few verses Jesus speaks of laying down his life. He does it freely: “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it up again.” In the Easter season we hear both halves of that sentence: the Cross and the Resurrection are one act of the Shepherd's love (Catechism 609). And his love is wider than any fold: “I have other sheep that do not belong to this fold. I must bring them also… So there will be one flock, one shepherd.” Today we pray for all who do not yet know him, and for the unity of all Christians in his one flock.

The second reading tells us what the Shepherd's sacrifice has made of us: “See what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God; and that is what we are.” Not only called, but made so, in Baptism. And there is more to come: “what we will be has not yet been revealed. What we do know is this: when he is revealed, we will be like him, for we will see him as he is.” That vision of God face to face is the goal of every Christian life (Catechism 1023). This is why the Church prays today for vocations. The Good Shepherd continues to lead his flock through shepherds he calls: priests who stand in his person at the altar and in the confessional, deacons, men and women in consecrated life, missionaries, and also fathers and mothers who are the first shepherds of the domestic church. God promised through Jeremiah: “I will give you shepherds after my own heart” (Jeremiah 3:15). Kerala has received this gift abundantly, and many of her sons and daughters serve the Church across India and the world. Today let each family ask whether God may be calling one of its own, and let each of us pray for our priests by name. May the Mother of the Good Shepherd, the Blessed Virgin Mary, who gave her whole life to God's call, intercede for all who are called.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today is Good Shepherd Sunday. In the middle of the Easter season the Church shows us the risen Jesus as the shepherd who knows us by name and leads us home. And today we pray in a special way for vocations.

In the first reading Peter stands before the most powerful council in Jerusalem. Only weeks before, he had denied Jesus to a servant girl. Now, filled with the Holy Spirit, he says: there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved (Acts 4:12). The Good Shepherd has made a frightened sheep into a fearless shepherd.

In the Gospel Jesus says: I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). He compares himself with the hired hand who runs away when the wolf comes. Why does the hired hand run? Because he does not care for the sheep. The good shepherd stays, because he loves. And Jesus did not only risk his life; he gave it on the cross, freely, and took it up again on Easter morning.

He also says: I know my own and my own know me (John 10:14). You are not a number to him. He knows your name, your story, your wounds. And he has other sheep too, not yet in the fold. He wants one flock and one shepherd. So we pray today for those far from him and for the unity of all Christians.

St John tells us what that love has made of us: see what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God; and that is what we are (1 John 3:1). One day we will see him as he is. That is where the Shepherd is leading us.

And the Shepherd still calls shepherds. Let us pray for our priests, who give their lives for us. Let us thank them. And let every family ask: could Jesus be calling one of ours? Do not be afraid if he is. There is no greater joy than to follow the Good Shepherd wherever he leads. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: I am the good shepherd. A good shepherd knows all his sheep by name. Jesus knows your name too! He loves you so much that he gave his life for you, and he rose again to be with you always.

Jesus also needs helpers to be shepherds for his people: priests and sisters. Maybe one day he will call you! Let us pray: Jesus, Good Shepherd, help me to listen to your voice.

Prayer

Almighty ever-living God, lead us to a share in the joys of heaven, so that the humble flock may reach where the brave Shepherd has gone before. Who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, you laid down your life for us and took it up again. Call many of our young people to follow you as priests and consecrated persons, keep our pastors faithful and joyful, and gather all your sheep into one flock under you, the one Shepherd. Amen. Alleluia.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Fourth Sunday of Easter is Good Shepherd Sunday. Each year its Gospel comes from John 10, and this year Jesus speaks the central sentence: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” Around it the other readings show who this shepherd is. In Acts, Peter on trial before the council says that there is salvation in no other name. Psalm 118 sings of the stone the builders rejected, the verse Peter has just quoted. The First Letter of John tells us what the shepherd’s self-giving has made of us: “we are God’s children now”.

The key words today are name, stone, children and lay down. The shepherd lays down his life of his own accord and takes it up again; that is why this Sunday belongs to Easter.

Acts 4:8–12 · No other name

Setting.

Peter and John had healed a man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate of the Temple “in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” (3:2–6). A crowd gathered and Peter preached. Then the priests, the captain of the Temple and the Sadducees, who did not accept the resurrection of the dead, arrested them (4:1–3). The next day they stand before the council, with Annas and Caiaphas, the men who had handed Jesus over a few weeks earlier (4:5–6). The question put to them is: “By what power or by what name did you do this?” (4:7).

Jesus had promised that when his disciples were brought before rulers, the Holy Spirit would teach them what to say (Luke 12:11–12). Peter, who had denied Jesus before a servant girl in the high priest’s courtyard, now speaks before the high priest himself.

Structure.

  • v. 8a: Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit.
  • vv. 8b–10: the healing was done by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, crucified and raised by God.
  • v. 11: Psalm 118:22 fulfilled: the rejected stone.
  • v. 12: there is salvation in no other name.

Key words.

  • onoma, name: The word of the trial: the council asks “by what name?” and Peter answers with the name of Jesus. In the Bible a name stands for the person and his power. The name “Jesus” means “God saves” (CCC 430).
  • sesōstai / sōtēria, healed / salvation: In v. 9 the word translated “healed” is the verb “to save”, and in v. 12 its noun, “salvation”. With one Greek word family Peter moves from one man’s healing to the salvation of all.
  • exouthenētheis, rejected: Peter quotes Psalm 118:22 with small changes. Where the Greek psalm says “rejected”, he uses a sharper word, “treated as nothing”, and adds “by you, the builders”. The builders are the leaders in front of him.

Verse by verse.

“Then Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, said to them, “Rulers of the people and elders,” (4:8)

Courage here is a gift, not a matter of temperament. The same Peter was afraid beside a charcoal fire (John 18:17–18).

“if we are questioned today because of a good deed done to someone who was sick” (4:9)

A touch of irony: they are on trial for an act of kindness.

“by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead.” (4:10)

“You” are the members of the council, the rulers who had handed Jesus over. This is not a charge against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). As always in Acts, the weight falls on what God did: “whom God raised”.

“There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved.”” (4:12)

“Given” by God: the name is a gift, not a human achievement. “Must” means that this is the way God has chosen.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse in its teaching on the name of Jesus: the divine name that alone brings salvation is present in the person of the Son, and from now on all may call upon it (CCC 432). The declaration Dominus Iesus (2000) builds on it in teaching that Jesus Christ is the one and universal Saviour. The Church also teaches that his grace reaches, in ways known to God, those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel but seek God with a sincere heart (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847).

Psalm 118:1 + 8–9, 21–23, 26 + 28 + 29 · The stone the builders rejected

Setting.

Psalm 118 is a song of thanksgiving, sung in procession to the Temple by one whom God has rescued from mortal danger. It closes the Hallel (Psalms 113–118), sung at the great feasts and at Passover. Today the Church sings it because Peter has just quoted it before the council: the psalm becomes the answer of the whole assembly to his words.

Structure.

  • v. 1: give thanks, for his steadfast love endures forever.
  • vv. 8–9: better to take refuge in the Lord than to trust in mortals or princes.
  • vv. 21–23: you answered me; the rejected stone has become the cornerstone.
  • vv. 26, 28–29: blessed is the one who comes; you are my God; give thanks.

Key words.

  • ḥāsâ, take refuge: To shelter under someone, as under a rock or under wings. The psalm sets this trust against trust in “princes”, which fits a day when Peter stands before rulers and does not fear them.
  • ’eben mā’ăsû habbônîm, the stone that the builders rejected: A stone thrown aside as unfit, then set at the head of the corner. Jesus applied it to himself (Mark 12:10–11), and both Peter in Acts and 1 Peter 2:7 take it up.
  • bārûk habbā’, blessed is the one who comes: The greeting for pilgrims arriving at the Temple. The crowds greeted Jesus with it as he entered Jerusalem (Mark 11:9), and the Church sings it at every Mass within the Holy, Holy, Holy.

Verse by verse.

“It is better to take refuge in the Lord than to put confidence in princes.” (118:9)

Peter and John had no protector among the powerful. Their refuge was the Lord.

“The stone that the builders rejected has become the chief cornerstone. This is the Lord’s doing; it is marvelous in our eyes.” (118:22–23)

The Christian reading does not take the psalm away from Israel. It sees in Israel’s festival song the pattern of the cross and the Resurrection, and it gives the credit where the psalm gives it: “the Lord’s doing”.

“Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.” (118:26)

On Palm Sunday these words greeted the shepherd going to lay down his life. At Mass they greet him as he comes to us on the altar.

How the Church reads it.

The early Church found in this psalm one of its clearest prophecies of the Resurrection, and the New Testament quotes verse 22 several times (Mark 12:10; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7; compare Ephesians 2:20). In the Roman liturgy it is the psalm of Easter Day and is heard often through the Easter season. Christ is the cornerstone on which the Church is built (CCC 756).

1 John 3:1–2 · We are God’s children now

Setting.

The First Letter of John was written, probably near the end of the first century, to communities formed by the Gospel of John. Tradition attributes it to St John the Apostle; many scholars think it comes from a disciple or a circle close to him, sharing the Gospel’s language of light, love, truth and abiding. Some members had left the community (2:19), and the letter reassures those who remain about who they are before God.

Structure.

  • v. 1a: see what love the Father has given us: we are called children of God, and we are.
  • v. 1b: why the world does not know us.
  • v. 2: we are children now; what we will be is not yet revealed; we will be like him and see him as he is.

Key words.

  • potapēn agapēn, what love: The word means “of what kind”, and it once meant “from what country”: a love so unusual that it seems to come from somewhere else.
  • tekna theou, children of God: Not only a title. The letter adds at once, “and that is what we are”. Those words, kai esmen in Greek, are missing in some late manuscripts and so in the King James Version, but the ancient witnesses have them.
  • phanerōthē, is revealed: Used twice in verse 2: what we will be has not yet been revealed; when he is revealed. Our future is hidden in his coming.

Verse by verse.

“See what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God; and that is what we are.” (3:1)

We are children by adoption, through the only Son. Baptism makes us adopted children of God and members of Christ (CCC 1265).

“The reason the world does not know us is that it did not know him.” (3:1)

“The world” in John’s writings often means human life organized without God. Christians should not be surprised to be misunderstood.

“Beloved, we are God’s children now; what we will be has not yet been revealed.” (3:2)

Now and not yet: the shape of Christian hope in one sentence.

“What we do know is this: when he is revealed, we will be like him, for we will see him as he is.” (3:2)

The Catechism quotes this verse in speaking of heaven, where the blessed see God face to face (CCC 1023), and calls this contemplation the beatific vision (CCC 1028).

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, preaching on this letter, says that the whole life of a good Christian is a holy desire: we do not yet see, but by longing we are stretched so that we may be filled when he comes (in substance). The letter itself joins the gift to the task: all who have this hope in him purify themselves, as he is pure (3:3). Being God’s child is not a reward for good behaviour; good behaviour is the family likeness that grace slowly forms in us.

John 10:11–18 · The good shepherd lays down his life

Setting.

Jesus speaks these words in Jerusalem after healing the man born blind, whom some of the leaders then drove out (John 9). The background is Ezekiel 34, where God rebukes the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves and not the flock, and promises: “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep” (Ezekiel 34:15) and “I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David” (34:23). In Israel God himself is the shepherd (Psalm 23), and David the shepherd-king is the model of the king to come. Jesus claims both promises.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–13: the good shepherd and the hired hand.
  • vv. 14–15: mutual knowledge, as between the Father and the Son; he lays down his life.
  • v. 16: other sheep; one flock, one shepherd.
  • vv. 17–18: he lays down his life of his own accord, to take it up again; the Father’s command.

Key words.

  • ho poimēn ho kalos, the good shepherd: Kalos means good in the sense of noble and beautiful, good as a thing should be. He is not only kind; he is the true shepherd, the model of every shepherd.
  • tithēmi tēn psychēn, I lay down my life: The verb “lay down” comes five times in these verses. It is the same verb John uses when Jesus lays aside his garments to wash the disciples’ feet (13:4): the shepherd gives up his life as one lays down a cloak.
  • misthōtos, hired hand: A man paid by the day. The sheep are not his, so he does not risk himself for them. The contrast is between love and self-interest.

Verse by verse.

““I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” (10:11)

Ordinary shepherds risked their lives against lions and bears (1 Samuel 17:34–35). This shepherd gives his. The death of the shepherd is the life of the flock.

“I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father.” (10:14–15)

In the Bible “to know” is to love and to be bound together. The bond between Jesus and his own is drawn into the bond between the Son and the Father.

“I have other sheep that do not belong to this fold. I must bring them also, and they will listen to my voice. So there will be one flock, one shepherd.” (10:16)

The other sheep are above all the Gentiles who will believe. The Latin Vulgate wrote “one fold”, but the Greek says “one flock”: the unity is in the one shepherd whose voice all hear.

“No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it up again.” (10:18)

The Catechism quotes these words to show the sovereign freedom of the Son of God as he went to his death (CCC 609). The Resurrection is the second half of the same act of love.

How the Church reads it.

The Good Shepherd is one of the oldest images in Christian art: in the Roman catacombs he appears as a young shepherd carrying a sheep on his shoulders. St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, warns that a pastor can flee without moving a step, by keeping silent when he sees the wolf come (in substance). The Catechism describes the Church as a sheepfold whose one gate is Christ, and as a flock which, though governed by human shepherds, is unfailingly led and nourished by Christ himself, the Good Shepherd (CCC 754). The Second Vatican Council asks every bishop to keep before his eyes the example of the Good Shepherd (Lumen Gentium 27).

Questions people ask

Does “no other name” mean that only Christians can be saved?

No one is saved except through Christ, the one mediator between God and humankind (1 Timothy 2:5; CCC 846). The Church also teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ and his Church, yet seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will as their conscience shows it, can attain salvation (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). Their salvation too comes from Christ, by grace. This is why the Church remains missionary (CCC 848).

Who are the “other sheep” of John 10:16?

Most Fathers and commentators understand them as the Gentiles, who were not of the fold of Israel and would be gathered through the preaching of the apostles, as Acts shows. The verse is also read as a call to pray and work for the unity of all Christians, so that there may be one flock under one shepherd.

Why is this Sunday a day of prayer for vocations?

Since 1964, when it was first kept at the call of St Paul VI, the Church has kept the World Day of Prayer for Vocations on Good Shepherd Sunday. The Lord himself told us to ask the Lord of the harvest to send out labourers into his harvest (Matthew 9:38). Every vocation, to the priesthood, to consecrated life or to marriage, is a share in the shepherd’s self-giving.

For the Latin reader

Today is the World Day of Prayer for Vocations, which St Paul VI instituted and which was first kept in 1964; the Pope usually publishes a message for it. The Collect of the day asks that the humble flock may reach where the brave Shepherd has gone before (in substance). In many parishes priests and religious speak of their call, and families are asked to pray for vocations. It is a good Sunday to pray by name for the priests who baptized, taught and absolved you, and for the young people of your parish.

For study and teaching

  • Peter says there is no other name by which we must be saved. How do I speak of Jesus, gently and clearly, to someone of another faith or of none?
  • What “rejected stone” in my parish or family might God be choosing to build upon?
  • John says “we are God’s children now”. Where do I most need to believe this about myself, and about someone I find hard to love?
  • The good shepherd lays down his life; the hired hand runs away. In my own responsibilities at home, at work or in the parish, when am I tempted to act like a hired hand?
