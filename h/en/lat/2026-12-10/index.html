Word for the day

“Truly I tell you, among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist; yet the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.” Matthew 11:11

Reflection

“For I, the LORD your God, hold your right hand; it is I who say to you, ‘Do not fear, I will help you.’” Isaiah calls Israel a “worm”, small and weak, yet God will make it a threshing sledge. For the poor and thirsty he opens rivers on bare heights and turns the desert into pools, “so that all may see and know… that the hand of the LORD has done this.”

Jesus praises John the Baptist: no one born of women is greater. Yet “the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he”. John stands at the threshold; the disciples of Jesus enter the Kingdom through Baptism and become children of God. Greatness in the Kingdom is not our achievement but God's gift, received by the little ones who let God take their hand.

Full homily

Dear friends, God speaks through Isaiah with great tenderness: do not fear, you worm Jacob, you insect Israel! I will help you, says the Lord; your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel (Isaiah 41:14). God calls his people a worm, not to insult them but to show how small and weak they feel, and how strong he is for them.

He promises: I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys; I will make the wilderness a pool of water (Isaiah 41:18). God turns dryness into life.

In the Gospel Jesus praises John the Baptist: among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist. Yet the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he (Matthew 11:11).

How can the least be greater than John? Not because we are holier than John, but because we live after the coming of Christ. We have received what John only announced: the death and resurrection of Jesus, baptism, the Holy Spirit, the Eucharist. We are given more than the greatest prophet.

This should give us both gratitude and responsibility. If we have received so much, how much more should we bear fruit? John lived in the desert on locusts and wild honey and gave his life for the truth. What do we give?

When you feel small, remember: God says, do not fear, I will help you. And when you feel proud, remember: all you have is gift. Let us thank God today for the riches of the Kingdom, and use them generously this Advent. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

“Do not fear”, God says three times in this chapter of Isaiah to a people who feel as small as a worm, and he promises to turn the desert into a garden. The psalm praises the Lord who is good to all. In the Gospel Jesus names John the Baptist as the greatest of those born of women, and as the Elijah who was to come. God’s great work begins from what looks small and dry, and the kingdom now stands at the door.

Isaiah 41:13–20 · I will help you

Setting.

The exiles in Babylon have lost their land, their king and their Temple. In this chapter God is speaking of the conqueror he is raising up from the east (41:2), who would later be named as Cyrus. He turns to tell Israel, his servant, not to be afraid. Our passage has two parts: a promise to the helpless and a picture of a new Exodus.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: God holds Israel’s hand: Do not fear.
  • vv. 15–16: the helpless one becomes a threshing sledge.
  • vv. 17–20: God gives water and trees in the desert, so that all may know his hand.

Key words.

  • gō’ēl, Redeemer: The next of kin, who by law had to buy back a relative from slavery or debt (Leviticus 25; Ruth 4). God makes himself Israel’s closest relative.
  • tôla‘at, worm: A picture of how small and despised the exiles felt. God does not deny their weakness; he speaks tenderly to it.
  • qādôš yiśrā’ēl, the Holy One of Israel: Isaiah’s favourite name for God. It joins two things that seem far apart: the God beyond all things and this one small people.

Verse by verse.

“For I, the Lord your God, hold your right hand; it is I who say to you, “Do not fear, I will help you.”” (41:13)

Parents hold the hand of a small child like this. The same picture is used for Cyrus in 45:1, but there it is for a task; here it is for comfort.

“Do not fear, you worm Jacob, you insect Israel! I will help you, says the Lord; your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel.” (41:14)

A note on the text. The Hebrew as it stands reads “men of Israel”, which the POC follows. “Insect” in the NRSV follows the Syriac version, which keeps the parallel with “worm”.

“I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys; I will make the wilderness a pool of water, and the dry land springs of water.” (41:18)

The journey home is pictured as a new Exodus, with water in the desert as at the rock (Exodus 17). Seven kinds of tree follow in v. 19, a whole forest where there was only sand.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these verses the coming of the true Redeemer, the Son of God who became our next of kin by taking our flesh. The Catechism recalls that he came to give his life as a ransom for many (CCC 622). The water in the desert also points to the Spirit and to Baptism, the living water Christ promises (John 7:37–39).

Matthew 11:11–15 · No one greater than John

Setting.

John is in Herod’s prison (11:2). He has sent his disciples to ask whether Jesus is the one who is to come. After answering them, Jesus turns to the crowd and speaks about John, who stands at the turning point between the prophets and the kingdom.

Structure.

  • v. 11: John is the greatest, yet the least in the kingdom is greater.
  • vv. 12–13: from John until now the kingdom suffers violence; the prophets and the law led up to him.
  • vv. 14–15: John is the Elijah who is to come. Let anyone with ears listen.

Key words.

  • biazetai, suffers violence: A difficult word. It can be passive, “suffers violence”, as the NRSV and the POC take it, recalling what is happening to John and will happen to Jesus. Others take it as middle, the kingdom “forces its way”. Many spiritual writers read the verse of the strong effort needed to enter the kingdom.
  • mikroteros, least: Literally “smaller”. It does not compare holiness. John stands at the threshold, and those who enter the kingdom receive what he could only announce.

Verse by verse.

“Truly I tell you, among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist; yet the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.” (11:11)

The point is the greatness of what has come, not a low view of John. It is a statement about the gift of the kingdom.

“and if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come.” (11:14)

Malachi 4:5–6 promised that Elijah would come before the day of the Lord. “If you are willing” shows that this cannot be proved: it is seen by those who accept it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that John surpasses all the prophets and is the last of them; he inaugurates the Gospel, already from his mother’s womb welcomes the coming of Christ, and rejoices as “the friend of the bridegroom” (CCC 523; see CCC 719). Each Advent the Church lets John prepare her again, so that she may recognise the one he points to.

For study and teaching

  • God speaks tenderly to a “worm”. Where do I feel small and helpless, and can I hear “Do not fear” there?
  • What has the Lord already given me in the kingdom, by Baptism and the Eucharist, that I forget to be thankful for?
