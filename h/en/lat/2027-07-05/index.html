Word for the day

“Then Jacob woke from his sleep and said, “Surely the Lord is in this place—and I did not know it!” And he was afraid, and said, “How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.”” Genesis 28:16–17

Reflection

Jacob is alone. He has cheated his brother Esau of his blessing and has to run for his life, a man with a staff, a bag and a guilty conscience, walking from Beer-sheba toward Haran, a journey of some five hundred miles. When the sun sets, he stops at “a certain place,” and the Bible does not even name it. He takes “one of the stones of the place” and puts it under his head, and falls asleep. Then he dreams: “there was a ladder set up on the earth, the top of it reaching to heaven; and the angels of God were ascending and descending on it.” And above it stands the Lord himself, who does not scold the runaway but renews the promises made to Abraham and Isaac: the land, a great family, a blessing for all the families of the earth, and then the sentence that Jacob most needs: “Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you back to this land; for I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you.” Jacob wakes up and says one of the best-known sentences of the Book of Genesis: “Surely the Lord is in this place—and I did not know it!” The cold roadside is the “house of God” and the “gate of heaven.” He sets up the stone as a pillar, pours oil on it, and names the place Bethel, “house of God.” Then he makes a vow, still a bargain, as if God had to prove himself: “If God will be with me ... then the Lord shall be my God.” But the God who gave the promise is patient, and will teach Jacob over the years. The Church uses this very text when she dedicates a church: “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven” (Catechism 1180–1181). The Psalm answers Jacob's night: “You who live in the shelter of the Most High ... will say to the Lord, ‘My refuge and my fortress; my God, in whom I trust.’”

Matthew compresses two healings into one chapter and weaves them together, and the woman's story is placed inside the ruler's. A leader of the synagogue, whose name Mark and Luke give as Jairus, kneels before Jesus: “My daughter has just died; but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live.” This father has no promise to rely on, only a hope. Jesus gets up and follows him. On the road a woman who has had a flow of blood for twelve years comes up behind and touches the fringe of his cloak, one of the tassels that a Jewish man wore by the commandment of the Law. She does not dare to speak; she is ritually unclean, and she does not want to make Jesus unclean. “If I only touch his cloak, I will be made well.” Jesus turns and sees her, and says a tender word: “Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.” With that word the nameless woman is received into the family. Then they reach the house. The flute players and the crowd making a commotion are the professional mourners of the day. Jesus says: “Go away; for the girl is not dead but sleeping.” And they laughed at him. When the crowd has been put outside, he goes in and takes her by the hand, and the girl gets up. Jacob learned in the night that the Lord is with him wherever he goes. The ruler and the woman learn that the Lord is with them in the street, in their trouble. And we learn the same on St Anthony Zaccaria's day: this physician from Cremona, who became a priest at twenty-six, knew that a person's deepest sickness is not of the body, and he spent his short life, only thirty-six years, healing souls.

Homily outline

Theme: “Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go.”

Jacob's stone at Bethel, the Lord who walks beside the runaway, and the touch of faith that reaches the hem of his cloak.

1. Surely the Lord is in this place

  • A runaway with a stone for a pillow.
  • The ladder and the angels.
  • The gate of heaven.
“Then Jacob woke from his sleep and said, “Surely the Lord is in this place—and I did not know it!”” Genesis 28:16
“Very truly, I tell you, you will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man.” John 1:51

Apply: Three times today, in a place you do not think of as holy, say: Surely the Lord is in this place.

2. I am with you

  • The promise to the wanderer.
  • Not until I have done what I have promised.
  • With you always, to the end of the age.
“Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you back to this land; for I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you.” Genesis 28:15
“And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Matthew 28:20

Apply: If you are far from home or from loved ones, say this promise aloud before you sleep each night this week.

3. Take heart, daughter

  • A father with nothing but a request.
  • A woman who dared only to touch.
  • The Lord who turns and sees.
“he will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge; his faithfulness is a shield and buckler.” Psalm 91:4
“Jesus turned, and seeing her he said, “Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.”” Matthew 9:22

Apply: At each Communion this week, make a conscious act of faith: if I only touch him, I will be made well.

God meets the runaway Jacob on a stone, promises to be with him wherever he goes, and makes the roadside the gate of heaven.

Jesus meets a father and a woman in need, honours the faith of the one who only touches his cloak, and takes the dead girl by the hand.

Take-home resolution: Look for the Lord in the ordinary places you pass through today, and bring one fear to him in prayer.

Full homily

Dear friends, think of Jacob on that night. He is a man on the run. He has cheated his brother, he has left his father's house, he is going to a place he does not know. When the sun sets, he does not even have a bed. He puts a stone under his head. We all know what it is to be at a low point, alone, ashamed, unsure of the future. And that is where God meets him. In a dream he sees a ladder, and angels going up and down, and the Lord standing beside him. And what does God say? Not a scolding. He says: Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go (Genesis 28:15).

When Jacob wakes he says one of the great lines of the Bible: Surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it! (Genesis 28:16). How many times has God been in our place, in the hospital room, in the bus, in the kitchen, in the dark, and we did not know it? Today's reading invites us to open our eyes. The stone that was a pillow becomes a pillar. The roadside becomes the gate of heaven. And centuries later Jesus tells Nathanael: you will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man (John 1:51). Jesus himself is the ladder: God and man in one person. In him heaven has come down.

In the Gospel we meet two people who need that ladder. A ruler of the synagogue: his daughter has just died, and he comes to Jesus with nothing but a request. And a woman, who has suffered for twelve years, who is afraid to speak: she touches the fringe of his cloak. And Jesus turns and sees her. Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well (Matthew 9:22). Perhaps nobody had spoken so tenderly to her in twelve years. Then he comes to the ruler's house, where the flute players are already playing. The girl is not dead but sleeping. And they laughed at him. He takes her by the hand, and she gets up (Matthew 9:24–25).

Dear friends, if you are at a low point today, if you are far from home or from God, remember Jacob's stone. The Lord is in this place. And remember the fringe of the cloak: you do not need great words, only a touch of faith. Today the Church remembers St Anthony Zaccaria, a doctor who became a priest because he saw that souls too need a physician. May he help us to notice the sickness of the heart, in us and around us, and to bring it to the one who takes us by the hand.

For children

Children, Jacob was far from home, and it was night, and he was afraid. He lay down with a stone for a pillow. In his dream he saw a ladder up to heaven, and God said: I am with you wherever you go! When Jacob woke up he said: God is here, and I did not know it! In the Gospel a girl was very sick, and Jesus took her by the hand and made her well. Jesus is with us too, when we are at home and when we are far away. Today we remember St Anthony Zaccaria, a doctor who became a priest to help people love Jesus.

Prayer

God of Jacob, who met a runaway on the road and promised never to leave him, we thank you that you are in this place and in every place where we walk. Give us eyes to see the gate of heaven in the ordinary. Lord Jesus, who took the daughter of the ruler by the hand and called the woman who touched your cloak your daughter, take us by the hand when we are afraid, and let our faith touch you. Through the intercession of St Anthony Zaccaria, physician of body and soul, heal what is sick in us and in your Church, and make us eager to live by the word of St Paul. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A man on the run, a stone for a pillow, and the first thing he learns about God: the Lord is in this place, and Jacob did not know it. In the Gospel a synagogue leader and a nameless woman, one asking for a daughter’s life and the other for her own health, find that the same Jesus meets both. Psalm 91 gives the prayer of both: “My God, in whom I trust.”

Genesis 28:10–22a · The house of God and the gate of heaven

Setting.

Jacob is fleeing. He has taken his brother Esau’s blessing by deceit (chapter 27), and at his mother Rebekah’s urging he sets out for Haran, to her brother Laban, to escape Esau’s anger and find a wife (27:41–45; 28:1–5). It is the first night of his life away from home, and he is alone, with nothing but a staff (32:10).

Background. Bethel, “house of God”, was an ancient holy place in the hill country north of Jerusalem, called Luz before. Later Israel remembered it as a place of worship (Judges 20:18; 1 Kings 12:29). The ladder, in Hebrew sullām, appears only here in the Bible. It is probably a stairway, like those that led up the temple towers of Mesopotamia, linking earth to heaven.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–12: the night, the stone, the dream of the stairway and the angels.
  • vv. 13–15: the Lord stands beside Jacob and renews to him the promise made to Abraham and Isaac.
  • vv. 16–17: Jacob wakes in awe.
  • vv. 18–22a: the pillar, the oil, the name Bethel, and Jacob’s vow.

Key words.

  • sullām, ladder, stairway: The only occurrence in the Bible. The angels first go up and then come down: heaven’s messengers are already at work on earth before Jacob even knows it.
  • Bêt-’ēl, Bethel, house of God: Jacob renames the place. A site that was just “a certain place” has become the house of God, because God has made himself known there.
  • maṣṣēbāh, pillar: A standing stone set up as a memorial of a meeting with God. Jacob pours oil on it, as a sign of consecration.

Verse by verse.

“Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go” (28:15)

God’s promise to a runaway. Jacob has done nothing to deserve it, and the promise comes before any change in him. God is with him on the road, not only at the shrine.

“Surely the Lord is in this place” (28:16)

The next words are “and I did not know it”. The lesson is that God is present even where we are not aware of him, and prayer is often a waking up to a presence already given.

“How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.” (28:17)

Awe, not comfort, is Jacob’s first response. The Church uses these words in the rite of dedicating a church, because every church is meant to be a house of God and a gate of heaven.

“If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go” (28:20)

Jacob’s vow is bargaining and trust together. It is the first prayer of a man who is still learning, and God does not rebuke it.

How the Church reads it.

In the Gospel of John, Jesus tells Nathanael that he will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man (John 1:51). The Fathers saw in this that Christ himself is the ladder, the one place where heaven and earth are joined, and Bethel is where God has met humanity. St Augustine saw in the stone anointed with oil a figure of Christ, the Anointed One, which is what the word “Christ” means (in substance, City of God 16.38). Jacob’s vow of a tenth is an early mention of tithing in the Bible (Abraham had already given a tenth, Genesis 14:20), which Israel later made part of its worship.

Matthew 9:18–26 · The hand, the cloak and the sleeping girl

Setting.

Jesus is still at the table of Matthew in Capernaum, speaking about the new wine of his teaching (9:14–17), when a synagogue leader comes in and kneels. Matthew tells the story briefly: the girl has “just died”. Mark (5:21–43) and Luke (8:40–56) give the man’s name, Jairus, and tell that the girl was still alive when he first came. The healing of the woman is set inside the story of the girl, so that the two miracles meet.

Background. The woman had suffered bleeding for twelve years, which according to Leviticus 15:25–27 kept her from the Temple while it lasted. This was a state of ritual impurity, not a moral fault, but a long illness would have meant a long separation from worship and from many in the community. She touches the “fringe” of Jesus’ cloak: the tassels that every observant Jewish man wore at the corners of his garment (Numbers 15:38–39). Jesus himself keeps this practice.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19: the leader’s request; Jesus goes with him.
  • vv. 20–22: on the way, the woman touches the fringe of his cloak and is healed.
  • vv. 23–25: at the house: flute players and the crowd; Jesus takes the girl by the hand and she gets up.
  • v. 26: the report spreads.

Key words.

  • kraspedon, fringe, tassel: The tassel at the corner of the garment, commanded in Numbers 15:38–39 as a reminder of God’s commandments. The woman reaches for the very place that tells of obedience to God.
  • sesōken, has saved, has made well: The Greek verb sōzein means both to heal and to save. Jesus’ words to the woman are about her body and about her whole person.
  • ēgerthē, she got up, she was raised: The verb used for the girl rising is the same one the New Testament uses for Jesus’ Resurrection. Matthew makes the first rising a sign of the last.

Verse by verse.

“If I only touch his cloak, I will be made well.” (9:21)

Her faith is quiet and secret, and it is real. She does not ask; she reaches. Jesus does not let her go unnoticed: he turns and sees her.

“Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.” (9:22)

He calls her “daughter”, which restores her to the family of Israel in the sight of all, and the same word “take heart” that he said to the paralytic (9:2).

“the girl is not dead but sleeping” (9:24)

Death is called sleep because it will not have the last word. They laugh, because they know she is dead; Jesus speaks as the one who will wake her.

“he went in and took her by the hand, and the girl got up.” (9:25)

The gesture is simple and the result complete. Jesus does not stand at a distance; he takes the dead girl’s hand.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus often asked the sick to believe, and that the sick tried to touch him, for power came out of him and healed them all; in the sacraments Christ continues to “touch” us in order to heal us (CCC 1504). The raising of Jairus’ daughter, the widow’s son and Lazarus are signs that foreshadow his own Resurrection, though these did not yet bring the final victory over death (CCC 994). The Church has long read the two miracles together: the woman, whom no one could help, and the girl, whom no one could save, are both given to Jesus’ power, and it is the woman whom he calls “daughter” (9:22).

For study and teaching

  • Jacob said, “the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it.” Where might God be present in my ordinary day without my noticing?
  • The woman reached out to touch only Jesus’ cloak. What is the smallest act of faith I can make today?
