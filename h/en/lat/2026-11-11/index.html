Word for the day

“Then one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, praising God with a loud voice. He prostrated himself at Jesus' feet and thanked him. And he was a Samaritan.” Luke 17:15–16

Reflection

Ten lepers cry out from a distance, “Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!” Jesus sends them to the priests, and on the way they are cleansed. All ten are healed; only one comes back, praising God aloud and falling at Jesus' feet. He is a Samaritan, a foreigner. Jesus tells him: “Your faith has made you well,” literally “has saved you”. Ten received healing; one received salvation, because he turned back to the Healer.

Paul tells Titus that God “saved us, not because of any works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy, through the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit”. Baptism is our cleansing; gratitude is our turning back. St Martin of Tours, a soldier who shared his cloak with a beggar and later became a monk and bishop, lived out that gratitude in mercy toward the poor.

Full homily

Dear friends, ten lepers stood at a distance and cried out: Jesus, Master, have mercy on us. Jesus sent them to the priests, and on the way they were cleansed (Luke 17:12–14). All ten were healed. Only one came back, praising God with a loud voice and falling at Jesus' feet, and he was a Samaritan, a foreigner.

Jesus asks: were not ten made clean? Where are the other nine? And to the one who returned he says: your faith has made you well (Luke 17:17–19), or, as the word also means, has saved you. Ten received healing; one received salvation, because he came back to the Healer.

Paul reminds Titus that God saved us not because of any works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy, through the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit (Titus 3:5). Everything begins with mercy, and mercy received calls for thanks.

Today we remember St Martin of Tours, the soldier who shared his cloak with a beggar and saw Christ wearing it that night. Gratitude turned into generosity. Let us keep a list this week: three thanks each night, and one act of mercy each day. Amen.

For children

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you have cleansed me in the waters of Baptism. Let me not be like the nine who forgot, but turn back each day to thank you, and like St Martin share what I have with the poor. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul reminds Titus what Christians once were and what God has done: “he saved us … through the water of rebirth”. In the Gospel ten lepers are healed, but only one, a Samaritan, comes back to give thanks. Baptism and the Eucharist meet here: we are washed by mercy, and the right answer to mercy is thanksgiving.

Titus 3:1–7 · The water of rebirth

Setting.

Paul ends his instructions to Titus with how Christians should live in society: obedient to lawful authority, ready for good work, gentle to all. The reason is not social respectability but memory: we too were once lost, and God saved us by mercy.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: conduct towards rulers and neighbours.
  • v. 3: what we once were.
  • vv. 4–7: what God did: a short hymn of salvation, naming the Father, Christ and the Holy Spirit.

Key words.

  • philanthrōpia, loving kindness: God’s love for humankind. Greek kings boasted of their philanthropy; Paul gives the title to God.
  • loutron palingenesias, washing of rebirth: A clear reference to Baptism: a washing that gives new birth (compare John 3:5).
  • klēronomoi, heirs: Those who receive an inheritance as children of the house. Baptism makes us heirs of eternal life.

Verse by verse.

“But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared,” (3:4)
“he saved us, not because of any works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy, through the water of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit.” (3:5)

Salvation is pure mercy. Our works follow it; they do not buy it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses these words as one of the names of Baptism: “the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit” (CCC 1215). The whole Trinity is at work in vv. 4–7: the Father’s kindness, the Spirit poured out, through Jesus Christ our Savior.

Luke 17:11–19 · The one who turned back

Setting.

Jesus is travelling along the border between Samaria and Galilee. Lepers had to live apart and call out from a distance (Leviticus 13:45–46). Only a priest could declare them clean and allow them back into the community (Leviticus 14).

Structure.

  • vv. 11–13: ten lepers cry for mercy from a distance.
  • v. 14: “Go and show yourselves to the priests”; on the way, they are made clean.
  • vv. 15–16: one turns back, praises God and falls at Jesus’ feet: a Samaritan.
  • vv. 17–19: Jesus’ questions and his word: “your faith has made you well.”

Key words.

  • allogenēs, foreigner: A rare word. It is the word used on the stone notices in the Temple that forbade foreigners to go beyond the outer court. The one counted an outsider is the one who comes back to praise God.
  • eucharistōn, giving thanks: The root of our word “Eucharist”. The Samaritan’s thanksgiving at Jesus’ feet is a picture of the Church’s worship.

Verse by verse.

“Then one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, praising God with a loud voice.” (17:15)

All ten were cleansed; one saw. Seeing what God has done is the start of thanksgiving.

“Then he said to him, “Get up and go on your way; your faith has made you well.”” (17:19)

The Greek verb also means “has saved you”. The nine were healed in body; the Samaritan received more.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that every event and need can become an offering of thanksgiving (CCC 2638), and that the Eucharist is above all thanksgiving to the Father (CCC 1360). At Mass we are invited to be the one who turns back.

For the Latin reader

St Martin of Tours (died 397) was a soldier and still a catechumen when, at Amiens, he cut his cloak in half for a freezing beggar. His biographer Sulpicius Severus tells that Christ appeared to him that night wearing the half cloak, and that Martin then asked for Baptism (in substance). He became a monk and then Bishop of Tours. Today’s two readings meet in his story: the water of rebirth, and a life of thankful love.

For study and teaching

  • Titus 3:3 recalls what we were before grace. How does remembering my own past make me gentler with others?
  • For what healing in my life have I not yet turned back to give thanks?
