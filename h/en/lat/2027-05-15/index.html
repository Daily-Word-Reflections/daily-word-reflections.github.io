Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? Follow me!”” John 21:22

Reflection

For fifty days the Church has read the Acts of the Apostles, from the Ascension at Jerusalem to the shipwreck at Malta. Today we reach the last page, and it ends in Rome. Paul has arrived as a prisoner, but a strange kind of prisoner: “Paul was allowed to live by himself, with the soldier who was guarding him.” Three days later he calls together the leaders of the Jewish community of Rome. He speaks to them as “brothers,” and he insists that he has “done nothing against our people or the customs of our ancestors,” and that he has “no charge to bring against my nation.” Then he says why he wears a chain: “it is for the sake of the hope of Israel that I am bound with this chain.” Paul never stopped being a son of Israel; the risen Messiah is, for him, the fulfilment of Israel's own hope, not its rejection. And the book ends with a picture rather than a conclusion: “He lived there two whole years at his own expense and welcomed all who came to him, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance.” Luke does not tell us how Paul's trial ended, or how he died. The last word of Acts in Greek is akōlytōs, “without hindrance.” The apostle is in chains, but the word of God is not. The story of the Church is left open, for us to continue. The Psalm gives the reason for that boldness: “The Lord is in his holy temple; the Lord's throne is in heaven,” and “the upright shall behold his face.”

The Gospel of John ends in the same open way. On the shore of the lake the risen Jesus has just asked Peter three times, “Do you love me?” and told him how he would one day glorify God by his death. Peter turns, sees the beloved disciple following them, and asks a very human question: “Lord, what about him?” Jesus answers gently but firmly: “If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? Follow me!” Each disciple has his own road. Peter will die a martyr; John, tradition tells us, will live long and die in peace. Comparison is a waste of time; what matters is following. Then the evangelist steps forward: “This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them, and we know that his testimony is true.” The Gospel rests on the witness of one who reclined next to Jesus at the supper and stood at the foot of the cross with Mary. And it ends with a smile: “if every one of them were written down, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written.” Tonight, at the Vigil of Pentecost, the Church will hear Jesus cry out: “Let anyone who is thirsty come to me.” The last weekday of Easter ends where the novena began, in the upper room, waiting with Mary for the gift that will write the rest of the story in us.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we come to the last page of two books. Since Easter we have been reading the Acts of the Apostles and the Gospel of John. Both end today, and both end in a surprising way: not with a full stop, but with an open door.

Acts ends in Rome. Paul is a prisoner, chained to a soldier, but he is not silent. He calls the Jewish leaders of the city his brothers, and he tells them: it is for the sake of the hope of Israel that I am bound with this chain (Acts 28:20). Then for two whole years he welcomed all who came to him, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance (Acts 28:31). We do not hear how his trial ended. Luke leaves the story open, because it is not finished. We are the next chapter.

John's Gospel ends on the shore of the lake. Peter has just been told how he would die, and he looks at John and asks: Lord, what about him? How often we do the same! Why does he have it easier? Why did she get that gift and not me? Jesus answers: what is that to you? Follow me! (John 21:22). Each of us has a road. Our task is not to measure the roads of others, but to walk our own behind Jesus.

Tonight, at the Vigil of Pentecost, the Church will pray with the apostles and with Mary for the gift of the Holy Spirit. Let us bring to that prayer our chains and our comparisons. The Spirit who made Paul bold in chains and made John a true witness will make us bold and true as well. The world could not contain all the books about Jesus; let the Holy Spirit write a few more lines in our lives. Come, Holy Spirit. Amen.

For children

Children, today two books in the Bible come to their last page. In the first one, St Paul is a prisoner in Rome, but he still tells everyone about Jesus. In the second, Peter asks Jesus: what about John? And Jesus says: don't worry about him, you follow me! Jesus has a special path just for you. Tomorrow is Pentecost, the birthday of the Church! Let us say: Come, Holy Spirit!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you told Peter: What is that to you? Follow me! Deliver us from comparing ourselves with others, and give us the grace to follow you on the road you have chosen for each of us. Like Paul in Rome, let us proclaim your kingdom with boldness, even when we are bound; like the beloved disciple, let us bear true witness to what we have seen of your love. As Easter draws to its close, we wait with Mary and the apostles: send us your Holy Spirit, the Advocate, the giver of life. Come, Holy Spirit. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two books come to their end today. Acts closes with Paul in Rome, under guard, welcoming everyone and proclaiming the kingdom of God “with all boldness and without hindrance”. John’s Gospel closes with Peter told to follow, the beloved disciple who bears witness, and the confession that the world could not hold the books that might be written. Both endings are open: the word goes on. Psalm 11 promises that the upright shall behold God’s face. This evening the Church begins the Solemnity of Pentecost with the Vigil Mass.

Acts 28:16–20, 30–31 · Without hindrance

Setting.

After the storm and shipwreck on Malta (27:1–28:10), Paul and his companions reach Italy and travel to Rome, where the believers come out to meet them (28:13–15). The “we” of v. 16 shows that the narrator travelled with Paul; the Church’s tradition sees in him Luke, Paul’s companion (Colossians 4:14; 2 Timothy 4:11).

Background. Paul lives under a kind of house arrest, chained to a soldier (28:20), while he waits for his case to be heard by the emperor. Acts does not tell the end of his trial or his death. Early Christian tradition, from St Clement of Rome onwards, holds that Paul died a martyr in Rome under Nero. He is honoured with Peter on 29 June, and his tomb is venerated on the Ostian Way.

Structure.

  • v. 16: arrival in Rome; Paul lives by himself with a soldier guarding him.
  • vv. 17–20: Paul calls the Jewish leaders of Rome: I have done nothing against our people; I am bound for the hope of Israel.
  • (vv. 21–29, left out: the discussion and the mixed response.)
  • vv. 30–31: two whole years, welcoming all, proclaiming the kingdom with boldness and without hindrance.

Key words.

  • hē elpis tou Israēl, the hope of Israel: The hope of the Messiah and of the resurrection. Paul does not preach another religion; he preaches the fulfilment of Israel’s hope in Jesus.
  • parrēsia, boldness: Open, confident speech, the gift for which the apostles prayed in 4:29. It is a fruit of the Spirit.
  • akōlytōs, without hindrance: The very last word of Acts in Greek. Paul is chained, but the word of God is not (2 Timothy 2:9).

Verse by verse.

““Brothers, though I had done nothing against our people or the customs of our ancestors,” (28:17)

Paul calls the Jewish leaders “brothers” and speaks of “our people”. To the end he is a son of Israel. When v. 19 says that “the Jews objected”, it means his accusers in Jerusalem, and Paul adds that he has no charge to bring against his nation.

“it is for the sake of the hope of Israel that I am bound with this chain.”” (28:20)

His chain is not a shame but a witness. He wears it for the hope he shares with his people.

“He lived there two whole years at his own expense and welcomed all who came to him,” (28:30)

Even as a prisoner Paul keeps an open house. His lodging becomes a church.

“proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance.” (28:31)

Acts began with Jesus speaking about the kingdom of God (1:3) and ends with Paul doing the same. The story is unfinished on purpose: it continues in every generation of the Church.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen Rome as the city where Peter and Paul bore their final witness. St Irenaeus, near the end of the second century, spoke of the Church in Rome as founded and established by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul (in substance). The ending of Acts invites each reader to carry on the story: the Spirit who led Paul to Rome now sends the Church to the ends of the earth.

John 21:20–25 · What is that to you? Follow me!

Setting.

These are the last verses of John’s Gospel. Chapter 21 is an epilogue after the first ending of the Gospel (20:30–31). Jesus has just asked Peter three times, “Do you love me?”, entrusted his sheep to him, and foretold the death by which he would glorify God (21:15–19). Now Peter turns and sees the beloved disciple.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: Peter sees the beloved disciple and asks, “Lord, what about him?”
  • vv. 22–23: Jesus: what is that to you? Follow me! The rumour that the disciple would not die is corrected.
  • v. 24: this disciple is the witness who wrote these things.
  • v. 25: the world could not contain the books.

Key words.

  • akolouthei moi, follow me: The first call of the disciples (1:43) and the last word of Jesus to Peter (21:19, 22). The whole Gospel lies between two calls to follow.
  • menein, remain: The same verb as “abide”. “If it is my will that he remain until I come” (v. 22). The beloved disciple’s way is to remain, Peter’s way is to follow to death.
  • martyria, testimony: The Gospel ends with witness: “we know that his testimony is true” (v. 24).

Verse by verse.

“When Peter saw him, he said to Jesus, “Lord, what about him?”” (21:21)

A very human question. Peter has just been told of his own death and wants to know about his friend’s path.

““If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? Follow me!”” (21:22)

Each disciple has his own call. Comparison is a temptation; the answer to it is to follow. Jesus does not tell Peter everything, only what is his to do.

“This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them, and we know that his testimony is true.” (21:24)

The “we” is the community that received his witness and vouches for it. The Gospel rests on the testimony of an eyewitness.

“if every one of them were written down, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written.” (21:25)

A loving exaggeration. No book can hold all of Jesus. What is written is enough for faith (20:31), but the mystery is always greater.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church firmly holds the historical character of the four Gospels, and that the sacred authors wrote them by selecting some things from the many that had been handed on (Dei Verbum 19). St Augustine saw in Peter and John two lives of the Church: Peter the active life of labour and suffering in this world, John the contemplative life that will be complete in the world to come (in substance). Both belong to the one Church, and both follow the one Lord.

Questions people ask

Who wrote the Gospel of John?

The Gospel says that it comes from “the disciple whom Jesus loved” (21:20, 24). From St Irenaeus onwards, the Church’s tradition identifies him with John the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve. Many scholars think that the Gospel grew from his testimony and was put in its final form by his disciples, who say “we know that his testimony is true”. Either way, the Church receives it as apostolic, inspired witness.

For the Latin reader

This evening, in the Roman Rite, the Vigil Mass of Pentecost may be celebrated. The Roman Missal provides an extended form of this Vigil, with several Old Testament readings (Genesis 11 on Babel, Exodus 19 on Sinai, Ezekiel 37 on the dry bones, Joel 3 on the Spirit poured out), so that the Church may wait in prayer like the apostles with Mary in the upper room.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus says to Peter, “what is that to you? Follow me!” In what area of my life do I compare myself with others instead of following?
  • Paul welcomed “all who came to him” even in chains. Who comes to my door, and how do I welcome them?
