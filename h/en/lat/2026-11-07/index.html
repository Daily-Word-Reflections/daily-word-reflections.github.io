Word for the day

“No slave can serve two masters; for a slave will either hate the one and love the other, or be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and wealth.” Luke 16:13

Reflection

Jesus draws the lesson of yesterday's parable: “Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much.” Money is “a very little” compared with the true riches of the Kingdom, but how we handle it reveals our heart. Then the sharp line: “You cannot serve God and wealth.” Wealth is not evil, but it wants to be a master. The Pharisees, “lovers of money”, ridicule him; Jesus answers that God knows the heart.

Paul, thanking the Philippians for their gift, shows the freedom of one who serves God alone: “I have learned to be content with whatever I have… I can do all things through him who strengthens me.” He has known plenty and hunger, and he is free in both. Their generosity to him is “a fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God”, and God will supply their every need.

Full homily

Dear friends, Jesus draws the lesson from yesterday's parable: whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much (Luke 16:10). Money is a very little compared with the true riches of the Kingdom, but the way we handle it shows what is in our heart.

Then he draws a sharp line: you cannot serve God and wealth (Luke 16:13). Wealth is not evil in itself, but it always wants to become a master. The Pharisees, lovers of money, laughed at him; Jesus answered that God knows their hearts.

Paul thanks the Philippians for their gifts and tells them his secret: he has learned to be content with little or with plenty, because he can do all things through him who strengthens him (Philippians 4:12–13). A free heart is not owned by its possessions.

Here is a practical step: decide on a regular share of your income for God and for the poor, and give it first, not from what is left over. It is a small thing, but faithfulness in small things is the training ground for great ones. Amen.

For children

Prayer

Lord, you alone are my Master. Free my heart from the love of money, make me faithful in small things, and teach me, like Paul, to be content in every situation, for I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul thanks the Philippians for their gift and says he has learned to be content in every circumstance, because he can do all things in Christ. Jesus teaches that we cannot serve both God and wealth. Money is a test of where the heart rests.

Philippians 4:10–19 · I can do all things through him who strengthens me

Setting.

At the end of the letter Paul thanks the Philippians for a gift they sent him in prison through Epaphroditus (2:25; 4:18). They had helped him from the beginning, even when he was in Thessalonica. Paul wants to thank them without suggesting that his peace depended on their money.

Structure.

  • v. 10: joy at their renewed care.
  • vv. 11–13: the secret of contentment.
  • vv. 14–17: their past sharing and its true profit.
  • vv. 18–19: the gift as a sacrifice; God will supply every need.

Key words.

  • autarkēs, content: A word of the Stoic philosophers for self-sufficiency. Paul changes its meaning: his sufficiency is not in himself but in Christ (v. 13).
  • memyēmai, I have learned the secret: Literally “I have been initiated”, a word used of the mystery religions. Paul’s secret is learned through experience with Christ.
  • osmē euōdias, fragrant offering: The language of Old Testament sacrifice (Genesis 8:21; Exodus 29:18). A gift to a poor apostle is worship offered to God.

Verse by verse.

“Not that I am referring to being in need; for I have learned to be content with whatever I have.” (4:11)
“I can do all things through him who strengthens me.” (4:13)

In its context, this verse is about contentment in hunger and plenty. “All things” means facing every situation, rich or poor, with Christ’s strength.

“And my God will fully satisfy every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus.” (4:19)

They supplied Paul’s need; God will supply theirs. Generosity does not empty us.

How the Church reads it.

Paul calls a gift of money “a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God”. The Catechism notes that from the very beginning Christians have brought, with the bread and wine for the Eucharist, gifts to share with those in need (CCC 1351). The collection at Mass belongs to this tradition.

Luke 16:9–15 · You cannot serve God and wealth

Setting.

These sayings follow the parable of the shrewd manager and draw lessons from it about the use of money. Luke notes that some Pharisees who heard this “were lovers of money” and laughed at Jesus. Luke speaks of these particular listeners, not of the Pharisees as a whole.

Structure.

  • v. 9: make friends by means of dishonest wealth.
  • vv. 10–12: faithfulness in little and in much.
  • v. 13: no one can serve two masters.
  • vv. 14–15: the response of the listeners and Jesus’ answer.

Key words.

  • mamōnas, wealth: An Aramaic word for property or money, perhaps from a root meaning that in which one trusts. Jesus treats it as a rival master.
  • aiōnioi skēnai, eternal homes: Literally “eternal tents”, the dwellings of the age to come.
  • bdelygma, abomination: A strong word, used in the Old Testament for idolatry. What people prize above God becomes an idol.

Verse by verse.

“make friends for yourselves by means of dishonest wealth so that when it is gone, they may welcome you into the eternal homes.” (16:9)

Money will fail us at death. Used for the poor, it makes friends who will welcome us. “Dishonest wealth” means worldly money, which so easily leads to injustice.

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much;” (16:10)
“No slave can serve two masters;” (16:13)

Jesus does not say “should not” but “cannot”. Money is a servant, never a master.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism counts the service of money among the forms of idolatry, and cites the Lord’s word, “You cannot serve God and mammon” (CCC 2113). It also quotes St John Chrysostom: not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life (CCC 2446).

For study and teaching

  • Paul learned contentment “in any and all circumstances”. What do I find it hardest to be content without?
  • Who are the “friends” my money is making for eternity?
