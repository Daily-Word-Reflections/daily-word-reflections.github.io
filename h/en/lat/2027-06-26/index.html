Word for the day

“The centurion answered, “Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof; but only speak the word, and my servant will be healed.”” Matthew 8:8

Reflection

It is the hottest hour of the day at the oaks of Mamre, near Hebron. Old Abraham is sitting at the entrance of his tent, the time when no sensible traveller is on the road. “He looked up and saw three men standing near him.” What follows is the most famous picture of hospitality in the Bible, and it is full of movement: Abraham “ran from the tent entrance to meet them,” bowed to the ground, “hastened into the tent to Sarah,” “ran to the herd.” A man of ninety-nine is running. He offers “a little water” and “a little bread” and then lays out a feast: cakes from three measures of choice flour, a calf “tender and good,” curds and milk, and he himself stands by them under the tree while they eat, like a servant. The narrator has already told us the secret that Abraham does not yet know: “The LORD appeared to Abraham.” In welcoming three strangers, he is welcoming God. The Letter to the Hebrews remembers this scene: “Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for by doing that some have entertained angels without knowing it” (Hebrews 13:2). Many Fathers of the Church saw in the three visitors, addressed now as “my lord” and now as “they,” a first shadow of the mystery of the Holy Trinity, and the famous Russian icon of Andrei Rublev paints them at Abraham's table. Then the guests ask, “Where is your wife Sarah?” and the promise is repeated, this time in her hearing: “your wife Sarah shall have a son.” Sarah, listening behind the tent flap, laughs to herself, as Abraham had laughed. And the Lord asks the question that runs through the whole Bible: “Is anything too wonderful for the LORD?” Sarah, afraid, denies it: “I did not laugh.” God answers gently and firmly: “Oh yes, you did laugh.” God is not offended by her laugh; he simply will not let her pretend.

Today's psalm is the song of another woman who received a promise that seemed impossible. To Mary in Nazareth the angel Gabriel used almost the same words that were spoken at Mamre: “For nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37). Sarah laughed and hid; Mary asked her question and then said yes. And her Magnificat ends by reaching back to this very tent: God has helped his servant Israel, “according to the promise he made to our ancestors, to Abraham and to his descendants forever.” On Saturdays the Church likes to honour Mary, and the readings give us good reason today. In the Gospel Jesus enters Capernaum and meets a Roman centurion, an officer of the occupying army, a Gentile. He asks for help for his paralysed servant. Jesus offers to come, and the centurion answers with words that the Church has placed on our lips before every Communion: “Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof; but only speak the word, and my servant will be healed.” He knows that Jesus' word is enough, as a soldier's command is enough. Jesus is amazed and promises that “many will come from east and west and will eat with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven.” Abraham's table at Mamre has become a table for all nations. The warning that follows, that “the heirs of the kingdom” may be left outside, is addressed to anyone who presumes on God's gifts; it is not a judgment on the Jewish people (Catechism 597–598), and it speaks first of all to us who are already at the table. Then Jesus enters Peter's house and touches the hand of his mother-in-law; the fever leaves her, “and she got up and began to serve him.” By evening he heals all who are brought to him, and Matthew sees the Servant of Isaiah at work: “He took our infirmities and bore our diseases.”

Homily outline

Theme: “Is anything too wonderful for the LORD?”

Hospitality at Mamre, a promise that makes Sarah laugh and Mary sing, and a soldier whose faith amazes Jesus.

1. Abraham runs to serve

  • The heat of the day.
  • Water, bread, a feast.
  • Welcoming strangers, welcoming God.
“He said, “My lord, if I find favor with you, do not pass by your servant. Let a little water be brought, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree.” Genesis 18:3–4
“Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for by doing that some have entertained angels without knowing it.” Hebrews 13:2

Apply: This week offer simple hospitality to someone who is not family: a meal, a cup of tea, a place to rest.

  • Sarah laughs behind the tent.

  • Mary believes and sings.

Apply: Write down the situation you find most impossible, and pray over it each evening this week with the Magnificat.

3. Only speak the word

  • A Gentile officer.
  • Many will come from east and west.
  • Healed in order to serve.
“He sent out his word and healed them, and delivered them from destruction.” Psalm 107:20
“The centurion answered, “Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof; but only speak the word, and my servant will be healed.” Matthew 8:8

Apply: Before each Communion this week, name silently one person you bring to the Lord with the centurion's words.

At Mamre God comes as a guest and asks Sarah, and us, whether anything is too wonderful for him.

Take-home resolution: Welcome one guest this week as if Christ himself had come to your door.

Full homily

Dear friends, picture the scene. It is the hottest part of the day. Old Abraham is sitting at the door of his tent. He looks up and sees three strangers. And this man of ninety-nine years runs to meet them, bows down, brings water for their feet, has bread baked and a calf prepared, and stands beside them like a servant while they eat (Genesis 18:1–8). He does not know who they are. But the Bible tells us: the Lord appeared to Abraham. When he welcomed strangers, he welcomed God.

Then the visitor says: your wife Sarah shall have a son. Sarah is listening behind the tent, and she laughs to herself. I am old, my husband is old. And the Lord asks: why did Sarah laugh? Is anything too wonderful for the Lord? (Genesis 18:14). That is the question of today. Is anything too wonderful for him? Is your family situation too difficult? Is your sin too great? Is your sorrow too deep? Nothing is too wonderful for the Lord.

Centuries later an angel said the same thing to a young girl in Nazareth: nothing will be impossible with God. Sarah laughed and hid. Mary believed, and sang the song we prayed today as our psalm: the Mighty One has done great things for me. On this Saturday let us ask Mary to teach us her faith.

And in the Gospel a Roman officer shows us that faith can be found where we least expect it. Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof; but only speak the word (Matthew 8:8). We say his words at every Mass. Let us say them today with his faith, carrying to Jesus the people we love. His word is enough. Amen.

For children

Children, one very hot day Abraham saw three visitors coming. He ran to meet them, gave them water and bread and a big meal. Guess who was visiting? God himself! Then God said: Sarah, you will have a baby. Sarah laughed, because she was very old. But God said: is anything too hard for the Lord? No! Nothing is too hard for God. And when visitors come to your house, be kind and share, like Abraham!

Prayer

Lord God, you came to Abraham at the door of his tent in the heat of the day, and he ran to welcome you. Teach us to see you in the stranger and the guest. When we laugh at your promises or hide our doubts, ask us again: Is anything too wonderful for the Lord? Give us the faith of Mary, who believed that nothing is impossible with you, and the faith of the centurion, who trusted your word alone. Lord Jesus, you took our infirmities and bore our diseases; heal the sick we bring to you today, and raise us up to serve you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

At the oaks of Mamre Abraham runs to welcome three strangers in the heat of the day, and receives from them the promise that Sarah will have a son. Sarah laughs, and the Lord asks, “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” In the Gospel a centurion, a Gentile, trusts that Jesus can heal his servant with a word, and Jesus heals Peter’s mother-in-law and many others. Instead of a psalm the Church sings Mary’s Magnificat, for Mary is the woman in whom God did the most wonderful thing of all, and who sang that God remembered his mercy, “according to the promise he made to our ancestors, to Abraham and to his descendants forever”.

Genesis 18:1–15 · Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?

Setting.

Abraham is camped by the oaks of Mamre near Hebron. In the desert world, hospitality to travellers was a sacred duty; a stranger might die without it. Abraham does more than his duty. He runs, bows, has water brought for their feet, and has a feast prepared.

The narrator tells us at once that “the Lord appeared to Abraham”, but Abraham sees three men. Through the story the text moves between “they” and “the Lord”, and the reader is left to wonder at the mystery.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the Lord appears; Abraham sees three men and runs to meet them.
  • vv. 3–8: the welcome: water, rest, bread, a calf, curds and milk.
  • vv. 9–10: “Where is your wife Sarah?” The promise of a son.
  • vv. 11–15: Sarah laughs; “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?”; Sarah’s fear and denial.

Key words.

  • mahar, hasten: Verbs of haste appear again and again: Abraham runs (rûṣ), hastens (mahar) to Sarah, runs to the herd, and the servant hastens. An old man in the midday heat rushes to serve.
  • ṣāḥaq, to laugh: Sarah laughs “to herself”, out of long disappointment. The same root gives the name Isaac. God does not punish her laughter; he answers it with a question and a promise.
  • hăyippālēʾ, is it too wonderful: From peleʾ, wonder, a deed beyond human power. The angel Gabriel will echo this to Mary: “nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37).

Verse by verse.

“He looked up and saw three men standing near him.” (18:2)

Hebrews 13:2 recalls this scene: “by doing that some have entertained angels without knowing it.”

“Let a little water be brought, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree.” (18:4)

Abraham promises “a little” and gives a great deal. True hospitality is generous and humble at once.

“Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” (18:14)

The central verse. The question is addressed to Abraham and Sarah, and to every reader who has given up hope.

“Oh yes, you did laugh.” (18:15)

God is gentle with Sarah. He does not reject her; he simply tells her the truth. The promise stands.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the remarkable hospitality at Mamre is the prelude to the annunciation of the true Son of the promise (CCC 2571). Many Fathers, among them St Ambrose and St Augustine, saw in the three visitors a hint of the mystery of the Holy Trinity (in substance), and the famous icon by Andrei Rublev shows the three seated at Abraham’s table. The Church does not say that Abraham knew the Trinity; she says that God was already preparing his people to receive the full revelation of who he is.

Matthew 8:5–17 · Only speak the word

Setting.

Jesus enters Capernaum, the lakeside town where he made his home. A centurion, an officer in charge of about a hundred soldiers, comes to ask for help. Many scholars think he served Herod Antipas, the ruler of Galilee, rather than a Roman legion; either way he was a Gentile. Matthew then adds two more healings, Peter’s mother-in-law and the sick brought at evening, and a fulfilment text from Isaiah.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–9: the centurion’s request and his word of humble faith.
  • vv. 10–13: Jesus marvels at his faith; many will come from east and west; the servant is healed.
  • vv. 14–15: Peter’s mother-in-law is healed and serves him.
  • vv. 16–17: many are healed at evening; Isaiah 53:4 is fulfilled.

Key words.

  • hekatontarchos, centurion: A commander of a hundred men. In the Gospels and Acts centurions often appear in a good light: at the Cross (27:54) and Cornelius (Acts 10).
  • hikanos, worthy: Literally “fit” or “sufficient”. The centurion knows that a Jew might not enter a Gentile’s house without becoming unclean, and he does not want to burden Jesus. His humility opens him to grace.
  • diēkonei, she served: From the same root as diakonos, servant. The healed woman at once uses her restored strength to serve Jesus. Healing leads to service.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof; but only speak the word, and my servant will be healed.” (8:8)

The Church has made these words her own. At every Mass, before Communion, the faithful say: “Lord, I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof, but only say the word and my soul shall be healed.”

“in no one in Israel have I found such faith.” (8:10)

Jesus marvels at a Gentile’s faith. This is a sign of the mission to the nations that the Gospel will open at its end (28:19).

“many will come from east and west and will eat with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven,” (8:11)

The image of the great banquet of the nations, foretold in Isaiah 25:6. The patriarchs of Israel sit at that table with them.

“He took our infirmities and bore our diseases.” (8:17)

Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4, the song of the suffering servant. Jesus does not heal from a distance; he takes our weakness on himself, and in the end he will carry it to the Cross.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes the centurion’s words in its teaching on preparing for Holy Communion (CCC 1386). St Augustine praised the centurion: by calling himself unworthy he showed himself worthy, so that Christ entered not his walls but his heart (in substance, Sermon 62). The warning about “the heirs of the kingdom” (v. 12) is said in the manner of the prophets, to shake anyone who presumes on privilege; the Church hears it as addressed to Christians too (7:21–23). It is not a judgment on the Jewish people, whom God has not rejected (CCC 839; Nostra Aetate 4).

For the Latin reader

On Saturdays in Ordinary Time, when no obligatory memorial falls, the Latin Church may keep the memorial of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Today’s response, the Magnificat, fits it well: the Lord who asked Abraham “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” fulfilled his promise to Abraham in Mary’s Son.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham ran to serve strangers in the heat of the day. Whom can I welcome this week, at home or in the parish, as if I were welcoming the Lord?
  • I say the centurion’s words before every Communion. What does it mean for me to say “only say the word” and truly mean it?
