Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.”” John 14:6

Reflection

Paul continues his sermon in the synagogue of Antioch in Pisidia. He speaks with warmth to his own people and to the God-fearers: “My brothers, you descendants of Abraham's family, and others who fear God, to us the message of this salvation has been sent.” He tells, briefly and honestly, what happened in Jerusalem: those in authority there did not recognize Jesus or understand the prophets read every sabbath, and “even though they found no cause for a sentence of death, they asked Pilate to have him killed.” They took him down from the tree and laid him in a tomb. Then comes the heart of every apostolic sermon: “But God raised him from the dead.” The Resurrection is God's own answer to the injustice of the Cross. It is not a private feeling of the disciples; it is a public fact with witnesses: “for many days he appeared to those who came up with him from Galilee to Jerusalem, and they are now his witnesses to the people.”

Paul tells his hearers that this is good news for them: “what God promised to our ancestors he has fulfilled for us, their children, by raising Jesus.” And he quotes the second Psalm, which today is also our responsorial psalm: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you.” In the Psalm these words were spoken over the king of Israel on the day of his anointing. Paul hears in them the Father's voice on Easter morning, raising his Son and enthroning him as the true King. The Church reads this passage with care. When Luke speaks of “the residents of Jerusalem and their leaders,” he is not blaming a whole people. Paul himself is a Jew speaking to Jews, and he calls them “brothers” and “children” of the promise. The Church teaches that all sinners were the authors of Christ's Passion, and that the Jews are not to be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4; Catechism 597–598).

The Gospel takes us again to the Upper Room. Jesus knows his friends are frightened: he has spoken of betrayal, of Peter's denial, of going away. So he says: “Do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father's house there are many dwelling places.” He is going ahead to prepare a place, and he will come back to take them with him. Thomas, honest as ever, objects: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” And Jesus answers with one of the greatest sentences in the Gospel: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” Not a map, but a person. Today the Church honours two martyrs who walked that way to the end. St George, a soldier according to ancient tradition, died for Christ at Lydda in Palestine, probably in the persecution of Diocletian, and has been loved in East and West ever since. St Adalbert, bishop of Prague, gave his life as a missionary among the pagan Prussians in 997. For both, Jesus was the way, and death was only the last step home to the Father's house.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul preaches to his own people in the synagogue. He calls them brothers, children of Abraham. He tells them what happened to Jesus: though no cause for death was found, he was condemned, taken down from the tree, laid in a tomb. And then the words that are the heart of our faith: but God raised him from the dead (Acts 13:30).

The Resurrection is God's answer to every injustice. When the world says the last word is death, God says: life. And there are witnesses. The apostles saw him, ate with him, and gave their lives for this truth.

In the Gospel Jesus sees his friends afraid. He says: do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me (John 14:1). He promises a place in his Father's house, with many dwelling places. There is room for you there.

Thomas asks: Lord, how can we know the way? And Jesus answers: I am the way, and the truth, and the life (John 14:6). The way to the Father is not a set of directions; it is a person to follow.

Today we remember two martyrs. St George, a soldier, refused to deny Christ and was killed in Palestine. Here in Kerala we love him as Geevarghese Sahada. St Adalbert, a bishop, went to preach Christ to a pagan people and was killed in 997. For them, Jesus was the way, even when the way led through death.

We may not be asked to die for Christ. But we are asked every day to choose his way, his truth, his life. Let us ask St George and St Adalbert for courage. Do not let your hearts be troubled. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: in my Father's house there are many rooms, and I am getting one ready for you!

St George was a brave soldier who loved Jesus more than anything. Let us say: Jesus, make me brave like St George!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, way, truth and life, no one comes to the Father except through you. When our hearts are troubled, steady them with faith in you. You have gone ahead to prepare a place for us in the Father's house; keep us on the way that leads there. Through the prayers of St George, who gave his life for you, and of St Adalbert, who preached your Gospel to the end, make us brave witnesses of your Resurrection, and strengthen all who suffer for your name today. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul’s sermon reaches its heart: “God raised him from the dead”, and so fulfilled what he had promised to the ancestors, as the second psalm says: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you.” The same psalm is today’s responsorial psalm. In the Gospel, on the night before he dies, Jesus calms troubled hearts: he goes to prepare a place, and he himself is the way there. Today is the optional memorial of St George, martyr, and of St Adalbert, bishop and martyr.

Acts 13:26–33 · God raised him from the dead

Setting.

Paul turns from history to the present: “to us the message of this salvation has been sent.” Like the apostles throughout Acts, he tells the Passion briefly and lays the weight on the Resurrection and its witnesses. He speaks to fellow Jews and God-fearers as brothers, and he describes the condemnation of Jesus in Jerusalem as an act done without understanding, which God turned into the fulfilment of the prophets (compare 3:17).

Structure.

  • vv. 26–29: the word sent to us; the Passion according to the Scriptures.
  • vv. 30–31: God raised him; the witnesses from Galilee.
  • vv. 32–33: the good news: the promise fulfilled; Psalm 2.

Key words.

  • agnoēsantes, not recognizing: Literally “not knowing”. Peter said the same in Jerusalem (3:17). The New Testament treats this ignorance as a real fault in those responsible, yet one that leaves room for repentance, and it never extends the guilt to all Jews of that time or of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).
  • xylon, tree: Paul says Jesus was taken down from the “tree”, the word of Deuteronomy 21:22–23, where one hanged on a tree is under a curse. Peter uses it too (5:30; 10:39), and Paul explains it in Galatians 3:13: Christ became a curse for us.
  • gegennēka, I have begotten: In Psalm 2 the king of David’s line is declared God’s son on the day he is enthroned. The apostles see that “today” in the Resurrection: Jesus, eternally the Son, is now enthroned in power as Son in his risen humanity (Romans 1:4).

Verse by verse.

“Because the residents of Jerusalem and their leaders did not recognize him or understand the words of the prophets that are read every sabbath, they fulfilled those words by condemning him.” (13:27)

The irony is deliberate: the very prophets read aloud every sabbath were fulfilled by those who did not grasp them. The Church adds that all sinners are the authors of Christ’s Passion (CCC 598).

“But God raised him from the dead;” (13:30)

One short verse carries the whole sermon. The verses before it tell what people did; this one tells what God did.

“and they are now his witnesses to the people.” (13:31)

Paul does not count himself among those who came up from Galilee. He rests his preaching on the witnesses of the Easter appearances, as he does in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Resurrection fulfils the promises of the Old Testament and of Jesus himself, and it joins Psalm 2 to the Resurrection as Paul does here (CCC 652–653). Each Sunday the Church gathers as Paul’s hearers did, to hear the Scriptures read and to be told that they are fulfilled in the risen Christ.

John 14:1–6 · I am the way, and the truth, and the life

Setting.

The supper is over and Jesus has foretold Peter’s denial (13:38). Into the disciples’ fear he speaks the first words of his long farewell. These words are often read at funerals, and rightly; but they were first spoken to men who were about to see their Master arrested and killed.

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Do not let your hearts be troubled”: faith in God and in Jesus.
  • vv. 2–3: the Father’s house, a place prepared, a promise to come again.
  • vv. 4–6: Thomas’ question; “I am the way”.

Key words.

  • tarassesthō, be troubled: The same verb was used of Jesus himself, troubled in spirit before the betrayal (13:21). He does not ask from them a calm he has never known; he has passed through trouble and speaks from beyond it.
  • monai, dwelling places: From the verb menō, to remain, a favorite word of John. The Father and the Son will make their “home” (monē) with whoever loves Jesus (14:23). Heaven begins as an indwelling now.
  • hodos, way: Not only a road to walk but a Person to walk with. The first Christians were called people “of the Way” (Acts 9:2).

Verse by verse.

“In my Father’s house there are many dwelling places.” (14:2)

Many rooms: room enough for all, and each person known. Earlier in John “my Father’s house” was the Temple (2:16); now it is the Father’s own presence, to which the risen body of Jesus, the true Temple, leads.

“Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” (14:5)

Thomas asks what the others only think. His honest question draws out one of the greatest sayings in the Gospel: a doubt spoken to Jesus becomes a door.

“I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” (14:6)

St Augustine puts it simply: walk by Christ the man and you come to Christ who is God; by him you go to him (in substance).

How the Church reads it.

The Church confesses with this verse that Christ is the one mediator between God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5). For troubled hearts the verse is first a comfort: the way home is not a set of directions but a Person who walks with us. The Easter season lets us hear it in the light of the empty tomb: he has gone ahead, and the place is ready.

Questions people ask

Does “No one comes to the Father except through me” mean that those who are not Christians cannot be saved?

All who are saved are saved through Christ. The Second Vatican Council teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church, yet seek God with a sincere heart and, moved by grace, try to do his will as they know it through conscience, can attain eternal salvation (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). There is one way, and the grace of Christ reaches further than our knowledge of it. This is no reason to slacken the mission; it is the reason for it (CCC 848).

For the Latin reader

St George, a soldier martyred probably at Lydda in Palestine early in the fourth century, is honored in East and West alike; the stories of the dragon came much later and teach in picture that faith overcomes evil. Many churches in Kerala bear his name. St Adalbert (Vojtěch), Bishop of Prague, was martyred in 997 while preaching to the Prussians near the Baltic coast. Both did what Paul describes today: they were witnesses of the risen Lord to the people, at the cost of their lives.
