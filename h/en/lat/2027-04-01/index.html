Word for the day

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” Luke 24:39

Reflection

The lame man who was healed at the Beautiful Gate will not let go of Peter and John. He clings to them, and the whole crowd comes running to Solomon's Portico, “utterly astonished.” Peter sees the faces turned towards him and refuses the glory at once: “why do you stare at us, as though by our own power or piety we had made him walk?” The power is not theirs. “The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, the God of our ancestors has glorified his servant Jesus.” Then Peter tells the story of Good Friday in a few sharp words: the Holy and Righteous One was rejected, a murderer was preferred, “and you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. To this we are witnesses.” What a title for the crucified Lord: the Author of life, the one from whom all life begins, put to death, and death could not hold him.

Peter does not speak as an accuser who wants to crush his hearers. He is himself a man who denied the Lord three times only a few weeks before. So his next words are full of tenderness: “And now, friends, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” They echo the prayer of Jesus on the cross: Father, forgive them. The Church has always refused to lay the death of Christ on the Jewish people as a whole; the Second Vatican Council taught this plainly (Nostra Aetate 4), and the Catechism adds that all sinners were the authors of his Passion, we Christians most of all (CCC 597–598). Peter's call is to everyone, then and now: “Repent therefore, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out, so that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord.” He reminds them that they are heirs of the promise made to Abraham: “in your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” The Risen One was sent “first to you, to bless you.” The Psalm marvels at the dignity God gives to frail human beings: “what are human beings that you are mindful of them, mortals that you care for them?” The Letter to the Hebrews reads this psalm of Jesus, crowned with glory after the suffering of death.

In the Gospel the two disciples from Emmaus are still telling their story when Jesus himself stands among them and says, “Peace be with you.” Instead of joy there is terror: “They were startled and terrified, and thought that they were seeing a ghost.” The Lord answers their fear with his body: “Why are you frightened, and why do doubts arise in your hearts? Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” Luke adds one of the most human lines in the Gospels: “in their joy they were disbelieving and still wondering.” The news is too good to believe. So Jesus asks, “Have you anything here to eat?” They give him a piece of broiled fish, and he eats it in their presence. The Risen Lord is not a memory, not a vision, not a spirit floating above the earth. He is the same Jesus, with the same body that was nailed to the cross, now raised to a glory that will never die.

Then he does what he did on the road to Emmaus: “he opened their minds to understand the scriptures.” Everything written about him “in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms” had to be fulfilled: the Messiah was to suffer and to rise on the third day, “and that repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things.” Notice how today's two readings answer each other. In the Gospel Jesus says, “You are witnesses”; in Acts Peter stands in the Temple and says, “To this we are witnesses.” The Church is born as a community of witnesses to a real, bodily Resurrection. The Collect of the day prays for the newly baptized, that the many nations united in confessing his name may be one in faith and in deeds. That is the Easter mission: to carry repentance and forgiveness to every nation, beginning from our own Jerusalem, our home and our parish.

Full homily

Dear friends, a man who could not walk for forty years now clings to Peter and John, and a whole crowd runs together to see. Peter could have enjoyed the attention. Instead he says: why do you stare at us, as though by our own power or piety we had made him walk? (Acts 3:12). The healing belongs to Jesus.

This is a good test for all of us. When God does something good through us, do we keep the credit, or do we pass it on to him? A humble witness points away from himself, to the Risen Lord.

Then Peter says hard words: you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. But at once he adds: I know that you acted in ignorance (Acts 3:17). He does not condemn; he invites. Repent, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out. Peter, who had denied the Lord, knows how good it is to be forgiven.

In the Gospel Jesus stands among his disciples and they think he is a ghost. So he says: look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see (Luke 24:39). And he eats a piece of broiled fish in front of them. Our faith is not in a ghost or an idea. Jesus is truly risen in his body, the same body that was wounded for us.

Luke tells us that in their joy they were disbelieving. Sometimes the good news is so big we are afraid to believe it. Our sins forgiven? Death defeated? Heaven open? Yes. The Lord shows us his wounds so that we may dare to believe.

Then he opens the Scriptures and says: you are witnesses of these things (Luke 24:48). That word is for us too. At this Mass the Risen Lord will stand among us and give us his Body. Let us go home and be his witnesses, beginning in our own Jerusalem, our own family. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, the friends of Jesus thought they were seeing a ghost. Jesus said: touch me! And he ate a piece of fish. Jesus is really alive!

Jesus is alive with a real body, and he loves us. When you are afraid, say: Jesus, give me your peace.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The lame man healed at the Beautiful Gate is now clinging to Peter and John, and a crowd gathers. Peter turns the astonishment into a sermon: the power is not ours, it is the name of the Jesus whom God has raised. In the Gospel the two disciples from Emmaus are still telling their story when Jesus himself stands among the eleven, shows his hands and feet, eats before them, and opens their minds to the Scriptures. Psalm 8 marvels that God crowns a mortal with glory and honour, and Easter shows how far that crowning goes.

Both readings turn on the same word: witnesses. Peter says, “To this we are witnesses”, and Jesus says, “You are witnesses of these things.” The key words today are name, Scripture and witness.

Acts 3:11–26 · The Author of life, whom God raised

Setting.

This is Peter's second sermon in Acts. A man lame from birth has just been healed at the gate of the Temple (3:1–10). The crowd runs to Solomon's Portico, the covered colonnade along the east side of the Temple court, where Jesus himself once walked and taught (John 10:23). Peter speaks to fellow Israelites as one of them: “the God of our ancestors”.

The sermon is full of old titles for Jesus: servant, Holy and Righteous One, Author of life, Messiah, the prophet like Moses. Luke gathers them to show that the Resurrection does not replace Israel's Scriptures. It opens them.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: the crowd stares; not by our own power or piety.
  • vv. 13–16: God has glorified his servant; you rejected him, God raised him; his name has healed this man.
  • vv. 17–18: you acted in ignorance; God fulfilled what the prophets foretold.
  • vv. 19–21: repent and turn; times of refreshing; the Messiah kept in heaven until the restoration.
  • vv. 22–26: Moses, Samuel and the prophets; the blessing promised to Abraham, sent first to you.

Key words.

  • pais, servant (or child): The word used for the Servant of the Lord in the Greek of Isaiah 52:13: “my servant shall prosper; he shall be exalted and lifted up”. Peter points to the suffering servant who is raised.
  • archēgos tēs zōēs, Author of life: Archēgos is the one who leads the way and begins something: a pioneer, a founder. The contrast is sharp: they asked for a murderer, a taker of life, and rejected the one from whom life comes.
  • apokatastasis, restoration: “The time of universal restoration” is the final renewal of all things that the prophets promised, when Christ comes again. It does not mean that everyone is automatically saved, an idea later linked with Origen that the Church did not accept.

Verse by verse.

“as though by our own power or piety we had made him walk?” (3:12)

The first thing Peter does is to step out of the way. A minister of Christ points beyond himself.

“and you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead. To this we are witnesses.” (3:15)

The whole Easter faith in a single verse: what people did, what God did, and the apostles' task. The life that was killed could not stay dead.

““And now, friends, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” (3:17)

Peter does not condemn his hearers. He calls them friends and offers them a way back, as Jesus prayed from the Cross, “Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing” (Luke 23:34).

“When God raised up his servant, he sent him first to you, to bless you by turning each of you from your wicked ways.”” (3:26)

The sermon ends with blessing, not threat. The promise to Abraham has not been withdrawn from his children. It is offered to them first.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites this very passage when it teaches that neither all Jews of Jesus' time nor Jews today may be held responsible for his death, and it takes up Peter's words about ignorance (CCC 597). It adds that all sinners were the authors of Christ's Passion (CCC 598). The Second Vatican Council says the same in Nostra Aetate 4. Peter's sermon is a model for preaching: it tells the truth about sin, puts God's action in the centre, and ends with an open door.

Luke 24:35–48 · Touch me and see

Setting.

The two disciples have run back from Emmaus to Jerusalem in the dark. Before they can finish, the eleven tell them that the Lord has appeared to Simon (24:34). Then Jesus himself comes. This is Luke's last great scene before the Ascension, and it gathers all that Luke wants the Church to know about the risen Lord: he is real, he is the same, and he is the key to the Scriptures.

Structure.

  • v. 35: the Emmaus report: known in the breaking of the bread.
  • vv. 36–37: “Peace be with you”; they think they see a ghost.
  • vv. 38–43: hands and feet; flesh and bones; he eats broiled fish.
  • vv. 44–46: everything written in the law, the prophets and the psalms; he opens their minds.
  • vv. 47–48: repentance and forgiveness to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem; you are witnesses.

Key words.

  • pneuma, ghost, spirit: Here it means a disembodied spirit, a phantom. Jesus rules this out at once. The risen body is not a vision.
  • sarx kai ostea, flesh and bones: Jesus shows that he is the same one who was crucified, with his wounds. His body is transformed and glorious, but it is still his body.
  • dianoigō, to open: The same verb is used of the eyes of the Emmaus disciples (24:31) and of the Scriptures opened to them (24:32). Now he opens their minds. The risen Christ is the one who opens.

Verse by verse.

“Then they told what had happened on the road, and how he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.” (24:35)

Two meetings with the Lord stand side by side: in the word on the road and in the breaking of the bread. The Mass has the same two tables.

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.”” (24:39)

The wounds are his signature. He does not come back with a new identity. The Lord of glory is the one who was nailed.

“and he took it and ate in their presence.” (24:43)

He does not need food. He eats to give them proof, and to restore the table fellowship that was broken on Thursday night.

“Then he opened their minds to understand the scriptures,” (24:45)

The law, the prophets and the psalms name the three parts of the Hebrew Bible, the psalms standing first among the Writings. Christ is not one topic in Scripture among others. He is the key to the whole.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the risen Jesus established direct contact with his disciples through touch and the sharing of a meal, to show that he is not a ghost (CCC 645), and that his Resurrection fulfils the promises of the Old Testament and of Jesus himself (CCC 652). It cites this verse when it teaches that Christ must open our minds if Scripture is not to remain a dead letter (CCC 108). The mission in verse 47 is the Church's to this day: to proclaim repentance and forgiveness in his name.

Questions people ask

Peter says “you killed the Author of life”. Does this blame the Jewish people?

No. Peter is a Jew speaking to fellow Jews in Jerusalem, and in the same breath he says they acted in ignorance and offers them the blessing first. The Church teaches that the death of Christ cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597). Each of us, by sin, has a share in why he died (CCC 598).

Why does the risen Jesus eat fish?

To show the disciples that he is not a ghost and that his risen body is real (CCC 645). Luke is answering the doubt that a resurrection is only a spiritual way of speaking. The Church confesses that Christ rose in the same body, now glorified.

For the Latin reader

During the Octave of Easter every day is celebrated as a solemnity in the Roman Rite: the Gloria is sung, the Sequence Victimae paschali laudes may be used, and the double Alleluia is added to the dismissal. Thursday has long been linked in devotion with the Last Supper, and today's Gospel shows the risen Lord at table with his friends. Families might read the whole of Luke 24 together this evening, Easter as Luke tells it.

For study and teaching

  • Peter begins by saying, “not by our own power”. Where do I need to step aside so that Christ is seen?
  • Peter calls those who rejected Jesus “friends” and offers them blessing. How do I speak to those who have hurt the Church or me?
  • Jesus shows his wounds to prove who he is. What does that say about my own wounds?
  • Which part of Scripture do I find hardest to understand, and how might I ask Christ to open it?
