Word for the day

“And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” John 17:3

Reflection

Paul is on his way to Jerusalem, hurrying to arrive before Pentecost (Acts 20:16). His ship stops at Miletus, a port about fifty kilometres south of Ephesus, and he sends for the elders of the Ephesian church. When they arrive he speaks to them as a father who knows he will not see his children again. This is the only speech in Acts that Paul gives to Christian leaders, and it is his farewell. He looks back first: “You yourselves know how I lived among you the entire time from the first day that I set foot in Asia, serving the Lord with all humility and with tears.” He reminds them of the trials he endured, including plots by those who opposed him, and of how he held nothing back: “I did not shrink from doing anything helpful, proclaiming the message to you and teaching you publicly and from house to house.” His message was simple and complete: “repentance toward God and faith toward our Lord Jesus,” to Jews and Greeks alike.

Then he looks ahead: “as a captive to the Spirit, I am on my way to Jerusalem, not knowing what will happen to me there, except that the Holy Spirit testifies to me in every city that imprisonment and persecutions are waiting for me.” Paul does not know the details, only the direction. And he is at peace: “I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the good news of God's grace.” The word “course” is the runner's track; Paul sees his life as a race to be run to the end. He can say, with a clear conscience, “I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole purpose of God.” Not a selection of pleasant truths, but the whole of it. The Psalm gives thanks to the God “who daily bears us up,” and to whom “belongs escape from death”: that is the confidence with which Paul walks towards chains.

In the Gospel we hear the beginning of the great prayer of Jesus. “He looked up to heaven and said, ‘Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you.’” The hour is the cross, and Jesus calls it glory: the moment when the love of the Father and the Son is most fully shown. Then he defines eternal life, not as endless time but as a relationship: “this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” To know, in the Bible, is to love and be united. Jesus can say what Paul will echo at Miletus: “I glorified you on earth by finishing the work that you gave me to do.” And he prays for his disciples: “I am asking on their behalf … because they are yours … And now I am no longer in the world, but they are in the world, and I am coming to you.” These are words of the Ascension. Jesus goes to the Father, and he leaves his friends in the world, held in his prayer. Paul at Miletus, handing his flock to God, is praying the same prayer in his own way.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we stand on the beach at Miletus and listen to a farewell. Paul has called the elders of Ephesus, and he speaks to them for the last time. What does he remember? Not his success, not his miracles, but this: I served the Lord with all humility and with tears (Acts 20:19).

He tells them: I did not shrink from doing anything helpful; I taught you publicly and from house to house. I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole purpose of God (Acts 20:20, 27). Paul did not give his people only the easy parts of the Gospel. He gave them all of it, because he loved them.

Then he looks ahead. He is going to Jerusalem, and he does not know exactly what will happen, only that chains are waiting. Yet he says: I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course (Acts 20:24). Paul sees his life as a race. What matters is not how long it is, but that it is finished well.

Each of us has a course to run. A mother, a farmer, a student, a priest, a sick person in bed: each has a ministry received from the Lord Jesus. The question is not whether it is easy, but whether we will finish it with faith.

In the Gospel Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven and prays: Father, the hour has come. And he tells us what eternal life is: this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent (John 17:3). Eternal life is not only after death. It begins now, when we know God, not just about him, but as a friend knows a friend.

Jesus says: I am no longer in the world, but they are in the world, and I am coming to you. These are Ascension words. He has gone to the Father, but he has not stopped praying for us. In these days before Pentecost, let us let him pray for us, and let us run our course to the end, with the Holy Spirit as our strength. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, St Paul had to say goodbye to his friends in Ephesus. He told them: I loved you, I taught you everything about Jesus, and I even cried for you. Now I must go where God sends me.

Jesus prayed to his Father for his friends, and he prays for you too. He wants you to know God as your best friend. Say: Jesus, I want to know you more!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, when your hour came, you lifted up your eyes to heaven and prayed for your friends. Pray for us now, as you go to the Father. Teach us that eternal life is to know the only true God and you whom he has sent; let us know you, love you and be one with you. Give us the heart of Paul, who served you with humility and tears, held nothing back from your people, and ran his course to the end. Bless all pastors of your Church, that they may shepherd your flock faithfully and declare the whole purpose of God. Keep us in your name while we remain in the world, and send us your Holy Spirit, for whom we wait with Mary. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul, on his way to Jerusalem, calls the elders of Ephesus to Miletus and speaks to them as a father who knows he will not see his children again. He wants only to finish his course and the ministry received from the Lord Jesus. In the Gospel Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven and begins his great prayer: “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son.” Both readings are farewells, and both look to a work completed. Psalm 68 blesses the Lord who daily bears us up.

Acts 20:17–27 · To finish my course

Setting.

Paul is hurrying to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost (20:16). He does not go up to Ephesus but sends for its elders to come to him at Miletus, a port some distance to the south. This is the only speech in Acts that Paul gives to Christians, to the leaders of a church he founded. It is a farewell speech, like the farewells of Jacob, Moses, Joshua and Samuel, and like Jesus’ own farewell at the Last Supper.

Background. Paul had spent about three years at Ephesus (20:31). It was there, many scholars think, that he wrote 1 Corinthians (1 Corinthians 16:8).

Structure.

  • vv. 17–18a: the elders of Ephesus come to Miletus.
  • vv. 18b–21: looking back: humility, tears, trials, teaching in public and from house to house.
  • vv. 22–24: looking ahead: bound by the Spirit for Jerusalem, prison and persecution awaiting.
  • vv. 25–27: they will not see him again; he has declared the whole purpose of God.

Key words.

  • presbyteroi, elders: The leaders of the local church. In v. 28 (tomorrow’s reading) Paul calls the same men episkopoi, overseers. In this early period the two names were used for the same ministry; later the bishop (episkopos) came to be distinguished from the presbyters.
  • dromos, course, race: Paul pictures his life as a race to be finished. 2 Timothy 4:7 uses the same word: “I have finished the race”.
  • boulē tou theou, the purpose of God: “The whole purpose of God” (v. 27): God’s saving plan, all of it. Paul did not choose only the easy or popular parts of the Gospel.

Verse by verse.

“serving the Lord with all humility and with tears,” (20:19)

The word for “serving” is the work of a slave. Paul’s ministry was not a career but service, and his tears show how much the people mattered to him.

“teaching you publicly and from house to house,” (20:20)

The early Church gathered in homes. Teaching the faith in the family and the home is as old as the Church.

“And now, as a captive to the Spirit, I am on my way to Jerusalem, not knowing what will happen to me there,” (20:22)

Literally “bound in the Spirit”. Before he is bound with chains in Jerusalem (21:33), he is already bound by the Spirit. He goes as Jesus went up to Jerusalem, knowing that suffering waits.

“But I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus,” (20:24)

Paul does not despise his life; he holds it as less than the mission. The ministry is not his own: he “received” it.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this speech as a mirror for every bishop and priest, and for all who serve in the Church. Commentators often call the Miletus speech Paul’s pastoral testament. Paul’s words “the whole purpose of God” remind teachers of the faith to hand on the whole Gospel, the hard parts with the consoling ones. The Second Vatican Council asks priests to be fathers and shepherds to their people, giving their lives as Christ did (Presbyterorum Ordinis 6 and 13, in substance).

John 17:1–11a · Father, the hour has come

Setting.

John 17 is the longest prayer of Jesus in the Gospels, spoken at the end of the Last Supper before he goes out to the garden. It is often called the “priestly prayer”: Jesus, about to offer himself, prays for himself (vv. 1–5), for his disciples (vv. 6–19), and for all who will believe through their word (vv. 20–26). Today and tomorrow we hear the first two parts.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: glorify your Son; eternal life is to know you.
  • vv. 4–5: the work finished; the glory before the world existed.
  • vv. 6–8: the disciples received the word and believed.
  • vv. 9–11a: I pray for them; they are in the world, I am coming to you.

Key words.

  • doxazō, to glorify: In John, the Son is glorified on the Cross, where God’s love is shown in full. The “hour” of glory is the hour of the Passion.
  • zōē aiōnios, eternal life: Not only life after death but the life of God begun now. Verse 3 defines it: to know the Father and Jesus Christ whom he sent.
  • ginōskō, to know: To know in the biblical sense is not only to have information but to be in a living relationship. Eternal life is friendship with God.

Verse by verse.

“he looked up to heaven and said, “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you,” (17:1)

Jesus looks up to heaven as he did at the tomb of Lazarus (11:41). The priest at Mass, in the First Eucharistic Prayer, recalls that at the Last Supper Jesus raised his eyes to heaven.

“And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” (17:3)

The faith of Israel in the one God, “the only true God”, is kept whole. Jesus Christ is named with him because the Father is known in the Son whom he sent.

“I glorified you on earth by finishing the work that you gave me to do.” (17:4)

He speaks as if the Cross were already done. On the Cross he will say, “It is finished” (19:30), with a verb from the same root.

“I am asking on their behalf; I am not asking on behalf of the world,” (17:9)

This does not mean Jesus does not care for the world, which the Father so loved (3:16). Here he prays for the disciples whom he is sending into the world, so that through them the world may believe (17:21).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism devotes a section to this prayer and says that Christian tradition rightly calls it the “priestly” prayer of Jesus, the prayer of the high priest at the hour of his Passover (CCC 2746–2747). St Cyril of Alexandria, commenting on this chapter, saw Christ here as our high priest and intercessor (in substance). Jesus prays now as he will pray for ever at the Father’s right hand: “he always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25).

Questions people ask

What does Jesus mean by “the glory that I had in your presence before the world existed”?

These words (17:5) show that the Son existed with the Father before creation, as the Prologue says: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God” (1:1). The eternal Son became man; in his Passion and Resurrection his humanity enters the glory that he always had as God.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says he wants only to finish his course. What is the course God has given me, and what is distracting me from it?
  • Jesus says eternal life is to know the Father and the Son. What helps me to know God, not only to know about him?
