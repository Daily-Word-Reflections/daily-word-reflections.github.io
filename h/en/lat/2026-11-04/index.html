Word for the day

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” Luke 14:27

Reflection

Large crowds are following Jesus, and he turns and speaks to them plainly: discipleship costs everything. To “hate” father and mother is a Semitic way of saying “to prefer less”; no love, however good, may come before him. Then two short parables: the man building a tower and the king going to war. Both sit down first and count the cost. Jesus wants disciples who know what they are choosing.

Paul tells the Philippians to “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who is at work in you”. Grace and effort are not rivals: God gives both the will and the work. Then we shine “like stars in the world”. St Charles Borromeo, Archbishop of Milan after the Council of Trent, counted the cost: he reformed his diocese, founded seminaries, and stayed with his people through the plague of 1576, when many fled.

Full homily

Dear friends, large crowds were following Jesus, and he turned and spoke plainly: whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple (Luke 14:27). He does not want admirers; he wants disciples who know what it costs.

Two short pictures make the point. A man about to build a tower first sits down and counts the cost. A king about to go to war first sits down and considers whether he can win (Luke 14:28–31). Faith is not a sudden feeling; it is a decision taken with open eyes.

Paul tells the Philippians to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, because it is God who is at work in them, and to shine like stars in a crooked world (Philippians 2:12–15). We count the cost, but the strength to pay it comes from God.

Today we remember St Charles Borromeo, a rich young cardinal who counted the cost and gave everything for the reform of the Church and the care of the sick in time of plague. Tonight, let each of us ask: what comes before Christ in my life? Amen.

For children

Children, following Jesus is like building a tower: we plan, we pray and we never give up. Shine like little stars by being kind and honest!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you call me to carry my cross and follow you. Give me courage to count the cost and to choose you above all, and let me shine in the world like St Charles, faithful to the end. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul tells the Philippians to work out their salvation, knowing that God is at work in them, and to shine like stars. Jesus tells the crowds that following him means carrying the cross and counting the cost. Today the Church remembers St Charles Borromeo, a bishop who did both.

Philippians 2:12–18 · Shine like stars in the world

Setting.

Right after the hymn of Christ’s self-emptying, Paul draws the practical lesson with “Therefore”. The Philippians must live out their salvation in a hostile city, without grumbling, while Paul himself may soon die in prison.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: work out your salvation, for God is at work in you.
  • vv. 14–16: no murmuring; shine like stars, holding fast to the word of life.
  • vv. 17–18: even if Paul is poured out as a libation, he rejoices, and they must rejoice with him.

Key words.

  • katergazesthe, work out: To bring to completion what has been given. Salvation is God’s gift, but it must be lived.
  • phōstēres, stars, lights: The word for the heavenly lights in Genesis 1:14 (Greek). Compare Daniel 12:3: the wise “shall shine like the brightness of the sky”.
  • spendomai, I am poured out as a libation: A drink offering poured over a sacrifice. Paul sees his possible death as an offering added to the Philippians’ sacrifice of faith.

Verse by verse.

“work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who is at work in you, enabling you both to will and to work for his good pleasure.” (2:12–13)

The two halves belong together. We work because God works; our effort is the fruit of his grace, not a rival to it. “Fear and trembling” is reverence before God at work, not anxiety.

“children of God without blemish in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation, in which you shine like stars in the world.” (2:15)

Paul takes the words of Deuteronomy 32:5, where a “crooked and perverse generation” described unfaithful Israel, and gives the believers the opposite calling: to be blameless children of God.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine often quoted Philippians 2:13 to teach that God works in us both the willing and the doing, so that our good works are at the same time his gifts and truly ours (in substance). The Church teaches that grace does not take away our freedom but heals and raises it: the grace of Christ is not a rival of our freedom (CCC 1742).

Luke 14:25–33 · Count the cost

Setting.

Large crowds are now walking with Jesus toward Jerusalem, where the cross awaits him (9:51). He turns to them and tells them plainly what being his disciple will cost.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–27: two conditions: to “hate” family and life, and to carry the cross.
  • vv. 28–32: two parables: the tower builder and the king going to war.
  • v. 33: the conclusion: renounce all possessions.

Key words.

  • misei, hates: A Semitic way of saying “love less, put second” (compare Genesis 29:30–31, where Leah is “unloved”, that is, loved less than Rachel). Matthew’s parallel says: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me” (Matthew 10:37).
  • stauros, cross: For Luke’s readers, carrying the cross meant the walk of a condemned man to execution. Luke adds “daily” in 9:23.
  • apotassetai, gives up: To say farewell to, to take leave of. The disciple loosens his grip on everything he owns.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” (14:27)
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, does not first sit down and estimate the cost, to see whether he has enough to complete it?” (14:28)

Jesus does not want enthusiasm that dies halfway. Discipleship is a decision made with open eyes.

“So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” (14:33)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse: Jesus enjoins his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone and to renounce all that they have for his sake (CCC 2544). This detachment takes different forms in religious, priests and lay people, but it is asked of all: nothing may come before Christ.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus really want us to hate our parents?

No. He commands us to honour them (Luke 18:20). “Hate” here is a Semitic idiom for choosing one thing over another. When love for family and love for Christ conflict, Christ must come first.

For the Latin reader

St Charles Borromeo (1538–1584), Archbishop of Milan, was a nephew of Pope Pius IV and helped bring the Council of Trent to its conclusion. He worked on the Roman Catechism, founded seminaries for the training of priests, visited every parish of his huge diocese, and stayed with his people during the plague of 1576, caring for the sick and selling his goods for the poor. His motto was Humilitas, humility. He shows what today’s readings ask: a life poured out, and the cost counted and paid.

For study and teaching

  • Philippians 2:13 says God is at work in me. Where do I see his work in my will and actions this week?
  • Which “tower” in my life have I begun without counting the cost, and what would it take to finish it with Christ?
