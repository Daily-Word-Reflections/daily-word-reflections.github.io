Word for the day

“And he said to them, “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.”” Mark 16:15

Reflection

The council looks at Peter and John and is puzzled. These are not scholars trained in the law; they are “uneducated and ordinary men,” fishermen from Galilee. Yet they speak with a boldness that cannot be explained by learning. Then the leaders understand where it comes from: they “recognized them as companions of Jesus.” Time spent with Jesus had left its mark. And there is something they cannot argue against: “When they saw the man who had been cured standing beside them, they had nothing to say in opposition.” A man who had never walked now stands in the middle of the court. So they send the Apostles out and talk among themselves: “What will we do with them? For it is obvious to all who live in Jerusalem that a notable sign has been done through them; we cannot deny it.” Their only plan is to stop the news from spreading: “let us warn them to speak no more to anyone in this name.”

Peter and John give an answer that has inspired Christians in every age of persecution: “Whether it is right in God's sight to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge; for we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard.” They are respectful; they do not insult the council or stir up the crowd. But they will not be silent about the truth. They have seen the Risen Lord; they have heard his voice; to keep quiet would be to deny what is real. The leaders threaten them again and let them go, because “all of them praised God for what had happened.” The Psalm gives the Apostles' song of victory: “The LORD is my strength and my might; he has become my salvation.” And, from the lips of the risen Christ himself: “I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the LORD.” The Church is bound to obey lawful authority in all that is just; but when any power commands what God forbids, or forbids what God commands, the Christian must answer as Peter did.

The Gospel is taken from the ending of St Mark, a short summary of the Easter appearances. Jesus appears first to Mary Magdalene, “from whom he had cast out seven demons.” She goes and tells the disciples, who are “mourning and weeping,” but “they would not believe it.” He appears “in another form” to two of them walking into the country, the Emmaus disciples, and again “they did not believe them.” At last he appears to the Eleven at table, “and he upbraided them for their lack of faith and stubbornness, because they had not believed those who saw him after he had risen.” It is a surprisingly honest page. The first witnesses of the Resurrection were not credulous people looking for a miracle; they were slow, sad and hard to convince. That is why their faith, once it came, is so trustworthy.

And then, to these very men who had doubted, Jesus entrusts the whole world: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” He does not wait for perfect disciples. He sends the forgiven, the slow of heart, the ones who were rebuked a moment earlier. The two readings meet here: the Apostles who once would not believe are now the ones who “cannot keep from speaking.” The Octave of Easter ends tomorrow, but its message is just beginning. The Collect prays that God, who gives increase to the peoples who believe, may clothe the newly baptized with blessed immortality. In ancient Rome the neophytes wore their white baptismal garments all through this week and laid them aside at the end of it; the white garments were put away, but the life of Christ they signified was meant to be carried into every street and home.

Full homily

Dear friends, the leaders look at Peter and John and see ordinary men, fishermen without formal training. Yet they speak with a boldness that astonishes everyone. And the leaders understand: they had been with Jesus (Acts 4:13).

That is the secret of every true witness. Not cleverness, not money, not position, but time spent with Jesus. If we pray, read the Gospel and come to Mass, people will see it in our faces and hear it in our words.

The council orders them never again to speak in the name of Jesus. Peter and John answer: whether it is right in God's sight to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge; for we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard (Acts 4:19–20). They are respectful, but they will not deny the truth.

The Gospel tells us something surprising. The Eleven did not believe Mary Magdalene. They did not believe the two disciples from Emmaus. When Jesus came, he upbraided them for their lack of faith and stubbornness (Mark 16:14). The Apostles were not easy believers. Their faith was won by the Risen Lord himself.

And yet, to these same slow disciples, Jesus says: go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation (Mark 16:15). He does not wait until we are perfect. He sends us as we are, forgiven and loved.

Tomorrow is Divine Mercy Sunday. Today is a good day to make a good confession and finish the novena. Let us ask for the grace to be with Jesus, so that we too cannot keep from speaking of him. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The trial of Peter and John ends without a sentence. The council cannot deny the sign, because the healed man is standing right there, so it forbids them to speak in the name of Jesus. Their answer has been repeated by Christians in every age: we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard. Psalm 118 sings for them: “I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the Lord.”

The Gospel is the summary of Easter in the longer ending of Mark. Twice the news is brought and twice it is not believed, until the Lord himself rebukes the eleven and then, without waiting for them to become perfect, sends them to the whole creation. The weekdays of the Octave end as they began, with witnesses. The key words today are boldness, unbelief and go.

Acts 4:13–21 · We cannot keep from speaking

Setting.

This passage continues yesterday's scene before the council. Peter has just declared that there is no other name by which we must be saved. Now Luke shows us the council's reaction, and lets us overhear its private discussion once the apostles have been sent out.

The healed man, more than forty years old (4:22), is the silent centre of the scene. A man lame from birth for forty years was well known at the Temple gate, and nobody can pretend that nothing has happened.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the council sees boldness in uneducated men, and recognises them as companions of Jesus.
  • v. 14: the healed man stands beside them; nothing to say.
  • vv. 15–17: the private debate: we cannot deny it; let us stop it spreading.
  • v. 18: the order: not to speak or teach in the name of Jesus.
  • vv. 19–20: the answer: judge whether we should obey you rather than God; we cannot keep silent.
  • v. 21: more threats, release; the people praise God.

Key words.

  • parrēsia, boldness: Literally “saying everything”: frank, open speech that does not hide. It becomes a key word of Acts (4:29, 31) and is one of the gifts the Church asks of the Spirit.
  • agrammatoi kai idiōtai, uneducated and ordinary: Not necessarily unable to read, but without the training of the scribal schools: laymen, not experts in the law. The council is surprised that such men argue from Scripture so well.
  • sēmeion, sign: Luke calls the healing a “notable sign”. A sign is a miracle seen for its meaning: it points to the power of the risen Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John and realized that they were uneducated and ordinary men, they were amazed and recognized them as companions of Jesus.” (4:13)

The best compliment a disciple can receive: that people can tell he has been with Jesus. Their education was the three years at his side.

“What will we do with them? For it is obvious to all who live in Jerusalem that a notable sign has been done through them; we cannot deny it.” (4:16)

The council does not dispute the fact. Its question is how to manage it. Truth that cannot be denied can still be avoided.

“But Peter and John answered them, “Whether it is right in God’s sight to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge;” (4:19)

They speak with respect to the lawful authorities of their people and ask them to judge. Yet they are clear that God's command comes first. Peter will say it more briefly in the next chapter: “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (5:29).

“for we cannot keep from speaking about what we have seen and heard.”” (4:20)

This is not stubbornness but witness. They speak because of what they have seen. The faith of the Church rests on that seeing.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that handing on the faith means above all proclaiming Jesus Christ, and that those who have met him cannot keep from speaking (CCC 425). On conscience and authority it teaches that citizens are not bound in conscience to obey civil commands contrary to the moral order or the Gospel, and it cites the apostles' answer “We must obey God rather than men” (CCC 2242). Their response is never violence; it is witness, prayer and patience, as the next scene of Acts shows (4:23–31).

Mark 16:9–15 · Go into all the world

Setting.

The oldest Greek manuscripts we have of Mark, the great fourth-century codices Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, end the Gospel at 16:8, with the women running from the tomb in fear. Other manuscripts add this longer ending (16:9–20), which many scholars think was written in the second century by another hand, drawing on the Easter stories of Luke and John. St Irenaeus already quotes it before AD 200 as part of Mark. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture and part of the canonical Gospel, and it is read in the liturgy.

The passage reads like a brief catechism of Easter. Each appearance is told in a sentence or two, and unbelief runs through them all.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–11: first to Mary Magdalene; she tells the mourners; they do not believe.
  • vv. 12–13: to two walking into the country; they tell the rest; they do not believe.
  • v. 14: to the eleven at table; he rebukes their unbelief and hardness of heart.
  • v. 15: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.”

Key words.

  • apisteō, not to believe: The word runs through the passage like a refrain (vv. 11, 13, 14). The first witnesses of the Resurrection were not credulous people. They had to be overcome by the evidence.
  • sklērokardia, hardness of heart: A biblical word for the refusal to let God's word in. Mark has spoken of the disciples' hardened hearts before (6:52; 8:17).
  • pasē tē ktisei, to the whole creation: Not only to every nation but to all creation. The good news concerns the whole world that God made and that waits for its deliverance (Romans 8:19–21).

Verse by verse.

“[[Now after he rose early on the first day of the week, he appeared first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons.” (16:9)

The NRSV prints the double brackets to show that this ending is missing from the oldest manuscripts. The first witness is a woman whom Jesus had delivered. The one who had been most bound is the first to see him risen from death.

“But when they heard that he was alive and had been seen by her, they would not believe it.” (16:11)

The Gospel is honest about the apostles. Their faith did not come easily, and that makes their witness more credible, not less.

“Later he appeared to the eleven themselves as they were sitting at the table; and he upbraided them for their lack of faith and stubbornness,” (16:14)

Jesus rebukes them, and in the very next breath he sends them. He does not wait for perfect apostles. He makes apostles out of forgiven unbelievers.

“And he said to them, “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” (16:15)

The Church's mission in one sentence. The verb “go” turns the small upper room into a starting point for the whole world.

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent received the books of Scripture “with all their parts” as they are read in the Church, and this longer ending of Mark has always been read as Scripture. The Catechism cites the next verse, “The one who believes and is baptized will be saved” (16:16), to show that the Lord tied the forgiveness of sins to faith and baptism (CCC 977, 1257). It also notes the apostles' doubt, and draws the lesson that their faith in the Resurrection was born of the risen Lord's action, not of their own imagination (CCC 643–644).

Questions people ask

If the longer ending of Mark is not in the oldest manuscripts, is it really Scripture?

Yes. The Church received it in her canon, and the Council of Trent confirmed the books of Scripture with all their parts as they are read in the Church. Whether the evangelist himself wrote these verses is a question of history; that they are inspired and belong to the Gospel is a matter of the Church's faith. Many other parts of the Bible also took shape through more than one hand.

Did Peter and John disobey lawful authority?

They were respectful and asked the council itself to judge. But no human authority can order someone to deny what God has done. The Church teaches obedience to legitimate authority, and also that we must refuse a command that is against God's law (Acts 5:29; CCC 2242). Their way of refusing was peaceful: they kept speaking and they prayed.

For the Latin reader

Saturday of the Easter Octave closes the white week of the newly baptized. In the ancient Roman practice they wore their white baptismal garments through the Octave and laid them aside at its end, which is why the next Sunday was known in Latin as Dominica in albis. Tonight's First Vespers already belongs to the Second Sunday of Easter, the Sunday of Divine Mercy. A good family custom for today: each person names one thing they have “seen and heard” of God this past week.

For study and teaching

  • People recognised that Peter and John “had been with Jesus”. What would show others that I have been with him?
  • The council could not deny the sign, yet it avoided it. Is there a truth I know but keep avoiding?
  • The apostles believed slowly. How does their honesty help my own doubts?
  • Christ sent forgiven unbelievers into the world. To whom is he sending me this week?
