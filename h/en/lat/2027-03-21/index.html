Palm Sunday of the Passion of the Lord

21 March 2027 · Latin Rite

Open in the Daily Word app

Readings

  • Gospel at the Procession Mark 11:1–10 (or John 12:12–16) · Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord!
  • First Reading Isaiah 50:4–7 · I did not hide my face from insult and spitting; I know that I shall not be put to shame
  • Responsorial Psalm Psalm 22:7–8, 16–17, 18–19, 22–23 · My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?
  • Second Reading Philippians 2:6–11 · He humbled himself; therefore God also highly exalted him
  • The Passion Mark 14:1–15:47 (shorter form: 15:1–39) · The Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ according to Mark

Word for the day

“he humbled himself and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross.” Philippians 2:8

Reflection

Palm Sunday has two faces, and the liturgy shows both. It begins with a joyful procession and ends in the silence of the tomb. Here is how the day unfolds, step by step, so that a family can follow it. 1. Gathering outside. Where possible, the people assemble outside the church, in another church, a hall or the parish grounds, holding branches; in Kerala these are usually tender coconut fronds, kuruthola, often woven into crosses and flowers. The priest and ministers wear red. 2. The opening acclamation. All sing “Hosanna to the Son of David; blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, the King of Israel. Hosanna in the highest.” 3. Greeting and blessing. The priest greets the people, briefly invites them to accompany the Lord as he enters his city to accomplish his Passion and Resurrection, blesses the branches with a prayer and sprinkles them with holy water in silence. 4. The Gospel of the entrance. A deacon or the priest proclaims Mark 11:1–10 (or John 12:12–16): the two disciples, the colt never ridden, the cloaks and leafy branches, and the cry “Hosanna! Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord!” 5. The procession. After the invitation “let us go forth in peace,” the procession moves to the church: the thurible, the cross adorned with branches, candles, the Book of the Gospels, the clergy and then all the people with their palms, singing Psalm 24 and Psalm 47 and the ancient hymn “All glory, laud and honour to you, Redeemer King.” 6. Entering the church. As the procession enters, a responsory recalls the children of the Hebrews who went out to meet the Lord. The priest venerates the altar, goes to the chair and, omitting the usual introductory rites, says the Collect. Where a procession outside is not possible, the Missal provides a solemn entrance, with the blessing of branches near the church door or inside, or a simple entrance with the entrance antiphon.

Then the mood changes. The first reading is the third song of the Servant of the Lord: “I gave my back to those who struck me, and my cheeks to those who pulled out the beard; I did not hide my face from insult and spitting.” Yet the Servant is not crushed: “The Lord GOD helps me… therefore I have set my face like flint.” The Psalm is the one Jesus himself prayed on the cross: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” It describes with uncanny precision the mocking, the pierced hands and feet, the garments divided by lot, and then turns at the end into praise: “I will tell of your name to my brothers and sisters.” St Paul's hymn in Philippians gives the whole mystery in a few lines: Christ, “though he was in the form of God,” emptied himself, took the form of a slave, “humbled himself and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God also highly exalted him,” so that every knee should bend and every tongue confess “that Jesus Christ is Lord.” The procession of palms and the road to the cross are one movement: the King enters his city in order to give his life.

This year the Passion is read according to St Mark, the oldest and most stark of the four. It begins in Bethany, where an unnamed woman breaks an alabaster jar and pours costly nard on Jesus' head; he says she has anointed his body for burial and “what she has done will be told in remembrance of her.” At the Last Supper he takes bread, blesses, breaks and says, “Take; this is my body,” and over the cup, “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.” In Gethsemane he prays, “Abba, Father, for you all things are possible; remove this cup from me; yet, not what I want, but what you want,” while his friends sleep. He is betrayed with a kiss; “All of them deserted him and fled,” even a young man who runs away leaving his linen cloth behind. Before the high priest he is silent until asked whether he is the Messiah: then he answers plainly, “I am.” Peter denies him three times and weeps. Pilate, knowing Jesus is innocent, hands him over to satisfy the crowd; soldiers mock him with purple and thorns; Simon of Cyrene carries his cross. On the cross he is mocked by all, and at three in the afternoon he cries out the first words of Psalm 22. He breathes his last; the curtain of the Temple is torn in two; and a Roman centurion, a pagan, makes the confession that Mark's whole Gospel has been waiting for: “Truly this man was God's Son!” The women watch from a distance, and Joseph of Arimathea lays him in a tomb cut from the rock.

How the Passion is proclaimed. It is read without candles and incense, without the usual greeting, “The Lord be with you,” and without signing the book, because the Lord is going to his death. Often three readers share it: a narrator, a reader for the other voices, and the priest for the words of Christ, while the whole assembly speaks the words of the crowd. When we say “Crucify him!” we confess that it is our sins that crucified him; the Church teaches that the Passion cannot be blamed on all the Jews of that time or on the Jewish people today, and that all sinners were the authors of his suffering (Catechism 597–598; Nostra Aetate 4). At the words “he breathed his last,” everyone kneels in silence for a moment. After a brief homily the Mass continues as usual. Take your blessed palm home and place it behind the crucifix or the family's holy pictures; next year it will be returned to be burned for the ashes of Ash Wednesday. Then keep this week holy: the Chrism Mass, when the bishop blesses the holy oils with his priests (often on Holy Thursday morning, in some dioceses earlier in the week); the Mass of the Lord's Supper on Thursday evening; Good Friday, a day of fasting and abstinence; and the great Easter Vigil on Saturday night. Because 25 March falls on Holy Thursday this year, the Solemnity of the Annunciation is transferred to Monday 5 April 2027, after the Octave of Easter.

Homily outline

Theme: “Truly this man was God's Son!”

The humble King, the Servant who did not turn back, the cry of the forsaken Son, and the name above every name.

1. Hosanna to the King

  • The colt never ridden.
  • Cloaks and branches.
  • Save us, we pray.
“Rejoice greatly, O daughter Zion! Shout aloud, O daughter Jerusalem! Lo, your king comes to you; triumphant and victorious is he, humble and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey.” Zechariah 9:9
“Then those who went ahead and those who followed were shouting, “Hosanna! Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the coming kingdom of our ancestor David! Hosanna in the highest heaven!”” Mark 11:9–10

Apply: Carry your palm with faith today, and welcome Jesus as King of your home.

2. The Servant who did not turn back

  • A face set like flint.
  • Gethsemane: Abba, Father.
  • Not what I want.
“I gave my back to those who struck me, and my cheeks to those who pulled out the beard; I did not hide my face from insult and spitting.” Isaiah 50:6
“He said, “Abba, Father, for you all things are possible; remove this cup from me; yet, not what I want, but what you want.”” Mark 14:36

Apply: Pray the prayer of Gethsemane each night of Holy Week over the hardest thing in your life.

3. Forsaken, yet the Son

  • The cry of Psalm 22.
  • The torn curtain.
  • The centurion's confession.
“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from helping me, from the words of my groaning?” Psalm 22:1
“Now when the centurion, who stood facing him, saw that in this way he breathed his last, he said, “Truly this man was God's Son!”” Mark 15:39

Apply: Kneel before the crucifix on Good Friday and make the centurion's confession your own.

4. Emptied and exalted

  • In the form of God.
  • The form of a slave.
  • Every knee shall bend.
“By myself I have sworn, from my mouth has gone forth in righteousness a word that shall not return: “To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear.”” Isaiah 45:23
“he humbled himself and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God also highly exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name,” Philippians 2:8–9

Apply: Bow your head at the name of Jesus each time you hear it this week.

The King hailed with palms enters Jerusalem to give his life.

Through the cross, the forsaken Son is revealed and exalted, and every knee shall bend.

Take-home resolution: Keep the whole of Holy Week: the Lord's Supper, the Passion and the Easter Vigil.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today we waved palms and sang Hosanna. And a few minutes later we heard ourselves say: Crucify him! Palm Sunday holds both, the joy of the welcome and the sorrow of the cross, because the King who enters Jerusalem is going there to die.

Jesus comes on a colt, as Zechariah foretold: humble and riding on a donkey. Not a war horse, not an army. The crowd cries: Hosanna! Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord! (Mark 11:9). Hosanna means save us. He will save us, but not in the way they expected.

Isaiah shows us the Servant: I gave my back to those who struck me… I did not hide my face from insult and spitting (Isaiah 50:6). And in Gethsemane Jesus prays: Abba, Father… remove this cup from me; yet, not what I want, but what you want (Mark 14:36). He does not turn back. He freely gives himself for us.

On the cross he cries out: My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? (Mark 15:34). He enters into every human loneliness and abandonment. Psalm 22, which begins in that cry, ends in praise. And at that moment a Roman soldier, a foreigner, says what the disciples had not been able to say: truly this man was God's Son! (Mark 15:39).

St Paul sums it up: he humbled himself and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God also highly exalted him (Philippians 2:8–9). The way down is the way up. The cross is the throne of the King.

Take your palm home and put it behind the crucifix. Let it ask you every day: which crowd am I in? Come back on Thursday evening, on Friday afternoon, and on Saturday night for the Easter Vigil. Walk with Jesus to the end, and you will see the morning of the Resurrection. Amen.

For children

Children, today we carried palms like the people in Jerusalem, and shouted Hosanna for Jesus our King! But Jesus was a gentle King, riding on a little donkey.

This week Jesus will die on the cross for us and rise again at Easter. Keep your palm near a cross at home, and say every night: Jesus, I love you, thank you for dying for me.

Prayer

Almighty ever-living God, who as an example of humility for the human race to follow caused our Saviour to take flesh and submit to the Cross, graciously grant that we may heed his lesson of patient suffering and so merit a share in his Resurrection. Who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, King of humble peace, we greet you with palms and we follow you to the cross. Do not let our Hosanna turn into Crucify; keep us awake with you in Gethsemane, faithful with the women at the cross, and full of hope beside your tomb, until Easter morning. Amen.
