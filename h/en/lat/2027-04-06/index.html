Word for the day

“And just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.” John 3:14–15

Reflection

St Luke paints a second portrait of the first community in Jerusalem: “the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common.” This is not a political programme; it is what the Resurrection does to people. In the middle of the passage Luke places its source: “With great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all.” Because Christ is risen, nobody needs to cling to land or money as if this life were everything. The result echoes the promise of Deuteronomy, “there will be no one in need among you”: “There was not a needy person among them.” Luke then introduces a man we will meet again and again, a Levite from Cyprus called Joseph, whom the apostles nicknamed Barnabas, “son of encouragement.” He sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet. Before Barnabas ever preaches a sermon, he gives. The Psalm crowns the risen Christ as king: “The LORD is king, he is robed in majesty.”

The Gospel continues the night conversation with Nicodemus, a teacher of Israel who came to Jesus in the dark. Jesus has told him that he must be born from above, of water and the Spirit, and Nicodemus cannot grasp it: “How can these things be?” Jesus answers with an image from the wind, which in Greek is the same word as Spirit: “The wind blows where it chooses, and you hear the sound of it, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.” The newly baptized of Easter are exactly this: people born again, whose new life comes from a source the world cannot see. Then Jesus points to the cross. In the desert, when serpents were biting the people, Moses lifted a bronze serpent on a pole, and whoever looked at it lived. “So must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.” In John's Gospel “lifted up” means both the cross and the glory: the Son is raised on the wood and raised to the Father. Nicodemus does not understand tonight; but at the end of the Gospel he will come back in daylight, carrying a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes, to bury the One who was lifted up. The Spirit had been blowing in him all along.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Luke tells us that the first Christians were of one heart and soul, and no one said that anything he owned was his own (Acts 4:32). Why were they like this? Luke gives the reason in the very next sentence: with great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus. When we really believe that Christ is risen, our grip on things loosens.

Then we meet Barnabas, son of encouragement. He sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet. Later he will be the one who welcomes the converted Saul when everyone else is afraid of him. A generous hand and an encouraging heart go together. Every parish needs its Barnabas.

In the Gospel Nicodemus is puzzled about being born again. Jesus says: the wind blows where it chooses; so it is with everyone born of the Spirit (John 3:8). We cannot see the Spirit, but we can see what he does: a selfish man begins to share, a frightened woman begins to speak, a family begins to pray again.

And Jesus points to the cross: as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up (John 3:14). Look at him and live. Let us look at him today, at the altar where his sacrifice is made present, and ask him to make us one heart and soul, and to make each of us a Barnabas for someone this week. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, after Easter the friends of Jesus shared everything they had, so no one was hungry. A kind man called Barnabas gave away his field to help the poor, and everyone called him son of encouragement, because he always made people feel brave and happy. Can you be a Barnabas today? Say something kind to someone who is sad!

Prayer

Lord God, King robed in majesty, you raised your Son and lifted him up to your right hand. Pour out on us the Spirit who blows where he wills, and make us one heart and one soul, generous with what we have, encouraging to one another, and steady in our witness to the Resurrection. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The first reading repeats Sunday's picture of the Jerusalem community, one heart and soul, and adds a name: Joseph, called Barnabas, the son of encouragement, who sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet. The Gospel continues the night conversation with Nicodemus. Being born from above is the work of the Spirit, who blows like the wind, and the Son of Man must be lifted up like the serpent in the wilderness. Psalm 93 sings that the Lord is king. The key words today are encouragement, Spirit and lifted up.

Acts 4:32–37 · Barnabas, son of encouragement

Setting.

Luke follows his summary of the community's sharing (vv. 32–35, read on Sunday) with a concrete example. Barnabas will become one of the great figures of Acts: he vouches for Saul when the disciples fear him (9:27), is sent to the new Church at Antioch (11:22–24), and travels with Paul on the first mission (13:2).

Structure.

  • vv. 32–35: one heart and soul; great power in the witness to the Resurrection; no one in need.
  • vv. 36–37: Joseph Barnabas, a Levite from Cyprus, sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet.

Key words.

  • hyios paraklēseōs, son of encouragement: Paraklēsis means encouragement, comfort, exhortation. It is related to Paraklētos, the name Jesus gives the Holy Spirit (John 14:16). Barnabas's new name describes his gift: he lifts others up.
  • Leuitēs, Levite: A member of the tribe of Levi, which served in the Temple. The law gave the Levites no tribal land (Numbers 18:20), though by this time some, like Barnabas, owned property.

Verse by verse.

“Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul,” (4:32)

Unity of heart comes before any sharing of goods, and makes it possible.

“There was a Levite, a native of Cyprus, Joseph, to whom the apostles gave the name Barnabas (which means “son of encouragement”).” (4:36)

A new name marks a new life, as with Simon who became Peter. The apostles name him for what grace has made of him.

“He sold a field that belonged to him, then brought the money, and laid it at the apostles’ feet.” (4:37)

Luke names him because the gift was real and whole. The next chapter shows the opposite in Ananias and Sapphira (5:1–11).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, on the communion of saints, quotes the words about holding everything in common, and urges Christians to be ready to help the needy (CCC 952). The Church keeps the memorial of St Barnabas, apostle, on 11 June. A parish needs people with his gift: those who welcome newcomers, vouch for the doubtful, and give without being asked.

John 3:7b–15 · So must the Son of Man be lifted up

Setting.

Nicodemus, a Pharisee and a leader of his people, has come to Jesus by night (3:1–2). Jesus has told him that no one can see the kingdom of God without being born anōthen, a word that means both “again” and “from above”. Nicodemus took the first meaning; Jesus means the second. Nicodemus appears again later, defending Jesus (7:50–51) and bringing spices for his burial (19:39).

Structure.

  • vv. 7b–8: you must be born from above; the wind blows where it chooses.
  • vv. 9–12: “How can these things be?”; earthly and heavenly things.
  • v. 13: no one has ascended except the one who descended.
  • vv. 14–15: as Moses lifted up the serpent, so the Son of Man; eternal life for whoever believes.

Key words.

  • pneuma, wind, Spirit: One Greek word for both, as Hebrew rûaḥ. Jesus plays on it: you cannot see the wind, only its effects. So it is with those born of the Spirit.
  • hypsōthēnai, to be lifted up: In John this verb means both the lifting up on the Cross and the exaltation to glory (8:28; 12:32–33). The Cross is already the throne.

Verse by verse.

“The wind blows where it chooses, and you hear the sound of it, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes.” (3:8)

New birth is not something we produce. It is received, like the wind, from God.

“No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended from heaven, the Son of Man.” (3:13)

Only the one who came from the Father can speak of heavenly things. In him heaven and earth are joined.

“And just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up,” (3:14)

In Numbers 21:8–9 those bitten by serpents looked at the bronze serpent and lived. The Book of Wisdom says they were saved not by what they saw but by God, the Saviour of all (Wisdom 16:7). So we look to the crucified and live.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites verse 13 when it teaches that Christ's Ascension is bound up with his first descent from heaven in the Incarnation (CCC 661), and it reads the new birth of water and Spirit as baptism (CCC 1215). St Augustine, preaching on this Gospel, says that as the Israelites looked on the serpent and were healed of its bite, so we look on Christ crucified and are healed of sin (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was the bronze serpent an idol?

No. God commanded it, and it was a sign pointing to his healing power (Wisdom 16:6–7). Later, when people began to burn incense to it, King Hezekiah destroyed it (2 Kings 18:4). The Catechism mentions it as an example of an image that God himself ordained, pointing ahead to salvation through the Word made flesh (CCC 2130).

For study and teaching

  • Barnabas was named for the way he encouraged others. Whom can I encourage this week?
  • Jesus says the Spirit blows where it chooses. Where have I seen the Spirit at work in a way I did not expect?
