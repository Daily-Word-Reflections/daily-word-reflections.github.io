Word for the day

“And looking at those who sat around him, he said, “Here are my mother and my brothers! Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.”” Mark 3:34–35

Reflection

After weeks of great feasts the Church returns to the green Sundays of Ordinary Time, the season of growth, and today's readings go back to the very beginning, to the garden. Adam and the woman have eaten from the forbidden tree. They have hidden among the trees. And God comes looking: “But the Lord God called to the man, and said to him, ‘Where are you?’” It is the first question God asks in the Bible, and it is not the question of a policeman but of a father looking for a lost child. God knows where Adam is; Adam needs to know where he is. Adam answers with the first confession of human fear: “I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.” Sin has made the friend of God afraid of God. Then comes the chain of excuses: “The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate.” The man blames the woman and, quietly, God who gave her; the woman blames the serpent: “The serpent tricked me, and I ate.” Nobody says: I did it. Sin divides; it turns people against each other and against God. But God does not abandon them. To the serpent he speaks a curse and, hidden inside it, the first promise of salvation: “I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.” The Church calls this verse the Protoevangelium, the first Gospel (Catechism 410–411). The war will not last for ever; a descendant of the woman will crush the serpent's head, though he himself will be wounded. Christians have seen in that offspring Christ, wounded on the Cross and victorious, and in the woman, Mary, the new Eve. The Psalm is the cry of everyone who has hidden from God and now calls to him: “Out of the depths I cry to you, O Lord … If you, O Lord, should mark iniquities, Lord, who could stand? But there is forgiveness with you.”

St Paul, in the second reading, speaks from the depths of his own sufferings. He has been afflicted, perplexed, persecuted, struck down. Yet he keeps preaching, because he believes: “just as we have the same spirit of faith that is in accordance with scripture, ‘I believed, and so I spoke,’ we also believe, and so we speak, because we know that the one who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also with Jesus.” Faith in the Resurrection gives him courage: “So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” Every ache of growing older, every illness, every loss is the outer tent wearing out, while within, God is building something new. “For this slight momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure, because we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen.” And then the great hope: “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” Adam lost the garden; Christ prepares for us a house that will never be lost.

The Gospel shows Jesus surrounded by opposition from two sides. Such crowds gather at the house that “they could not even eat.” His relatives hear what people are saying, “He has gone out of his mind,” and come to take charge of him. The scribes from Jerusalem go much further: “He has Beelzebul, and by the ruler of the demons he casts out demons.” Jesus answers with clear logic: “How can Satan cast out Satan? If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand.” Then a short parable: “no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his property without first tying up the strong man.” Satan is the strong man who has held humanity captive since the garden; Jesus is the stronger one who has come to bind him and set his prisoners loose. That is the promise of Genesis 3:15 coming true before their eyes. Then a solemn warning: all sins and blasphemies can be forgiven, “but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit can never have forgiveness.” This is not a single careless word but a hardened refusal to recognise God's saving work, calling the Holy Spirit's work the devil's; whoever refuses mercy to the end shuts the door from the inside (Catechism 1864). Finally his mother and his brothers arrive and stand outside. In the language of the time “brothers” meant close relatives; the Church has always taught that Mary remained ever a virgin and that these were cousins or kinsmen (Catechism 500). Jesus looks at those seated around him and says: “Here are my mother and my brothers! Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.” He does not reject his mother; he reveals what makes her truly great. No one ever did the will of God more perfectly than Mary, who said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord” (Luke 1:38). She is his mother twice over, by flesh and by faith. And the door is opened to us: whoever does the will of God belongs to the family of Jesus. Adam hid; the new family of Jesus sits around him, listening.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, after the great feasts of Pentecost, the Holy Trinity and the Body and Blood of Christ, we come back today to the green Sundays of Ordinary Time. And the first reading takes us back to the very beginning, to a garden, and to a question. Adam and Eve have disobeyed. They hear God walking in the garden, and they hide. And God calls: where are you? (Genesis 3:9).

It is the first question God asks in the whole Bible, and he is still asking it. Not as a policeman, but as a father whose child has run away. God knows where Adam is. But Adam needs to say it. And notice what Adam says: I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid. Sin always does this. It makes us afraid of the God who loves us. And then comes the blame: the woman you gave me, she gave me the fruit. The woman says: the serpent tricked me. Nobody says, I did it. How often in our families do we do the same: it is his fault, it is her fault, it is the government's fault.

But God does not give up on them. Speaking to the serpent, he makes a promise: I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head (Genesis 3:15). This is the first Good News in the Bible. One day a child of the woman will crush the serpent. That child is Jesus, and the woman, in the fullest sense, is Mary, the new Eve, who said yes where Eve said no.

In the Gospel we see that promise coming true. The scribes say Jesus is working with the devil. Jesus answers: no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his property without first tying up the strong man (Mark 3:27). Satan is the strong man who has held us captive since the garden. Jesus is the stronger one who has come to tie him up and set us loose. Every exorcism, every confession, every Baptism is a sign that the strong man is bound.

Jesus also warns about the sin against the Holy Spirit, which cannot be forgiven. Do not be frightened by this. It does not mean that God's mercy has a limit. It means that if someone refuses God's mercy to the very end, saying that God's goodness is evil, he shuts the door from the inside. Anyone who is worried that he has committed this sin has not committed it, because he still wants God's mercy.

Finally, his mother and his relatives stand outside, and Jesus says: whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother (Mark 3:35). He is not pushing his mother away. He is praising her! No one did the will of God like Mary: here am I, the servant of the Lord. And he opens the door to us. You and I can be the family of Jesus. So let us answer God's question today: where are you? Here I am, Lord. I come out of hiding. I want to do your will. And as St Paul says, let us not lose heart, for God is preparing for us a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens. Amen.

For children

Children, have you ever broken something and then hidden so that nobody would find you? That is what Adam and Eve did after they disobeyed God. They hid in the garden. But God came looking for them and called: where are you? God was not coming to shout at them. He came because he loved them and wanted them back.

Jesus is God's answer to Adam and Eve. He came to beat the devil and bring us back home. And he said that everyone who does what God wants is part of his family. So when you do something wrong, do not hide. Say sorry to God and to your parents, and you will feel his love.

Prayer

O God, from whom all good things come, grant that we, who call on you in our need, may at your prompting discern what is right, and by your guidance do it. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord God, you came looking for Adam when he hid; you still ask each of us, Where are you? Give us the courage to come out of hiding and to answer without excuses. Lord Jesus, stronger than the strong man, set us loose from every bondage of sin. Make us your true family, doing the will of the Father as your Mother did, and keep us from losing heart until we reach the house not made with hands. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

After the great feasts of Easter, Pentecost and the Body and Blood of Christ, the Sundays in Ordinary Time resume. The Tenth Sunday of Year B sets side by side the first sin in the garden and the conflict around Jesus in Galilee. In Genesis God seeks the man who hides, “Where are you?”, and promises that the offspring of the woman will strike the serpent’s head. In the Gospel Jesus is accused of working by the power of Satan; he answers that he is the stronger one who binds the strong man. His family comes to take him home, and he declares that whoever does the will of God is his brother and sister and mother.

St Paul, in the second reading, speaks from his own sufferings: “So we do not lose heart … our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” Psalm 130, the De profundis, joins the readings together: out of the depths we cry to the Lord, for with him there is steadfast love and great power to redeem.

Genesis 3:9–15 · Where are you?

Setting.

The first chapters of Genesis teach, in the language of story and image, truths about God, the world and humanity. The Church teaches that the account of the fall uses figurative language but affirms a real event, a fault committed at the beginning of human history (CCC 390). In chapter 2 God placed the man and the woman in the garden and gave them one command. In chapter 3 the serpent persuades them to doubt God’s goodness, and they eat.

Our passage begins just after the sin. The man and the woman, who were naked and not ashamed (2:25), now hide among the trees when they hear God walking in the garden in the cool of the day.

Structure.

  • v. 9: God calls: “Where are you?”
  • v. 10: the man: “I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.”
  • vv. 11–13: God questions; the man blames the woman, and God who gave her; the woman blames the serpent.
  • v. 14: the curse on the serpent.
  • v. 15: enmity between the serpent and the woman, between his offspring and hers; “he will strike your head”.
  • ¶ God does not curse the man or the woman here; he curses the serpent and the ground (3:17). The promise of victory comes before the words of punishment to the man and the woman.

Key words.

  • ʾayyekkâ, Where are you?: God knows where the man is. The question is an invitation to come out of hiding and to tell the truth. It is the first question God asks in the Bible.
  • ʾêbâ, enmity: A lasting hostility. God himself sets it between the woman and the serpent, so that the human race will not remain on the side of evil.
  • zeraʿ, offspring, seed: A word that can mean one descendant or many. The promise speaks of a long struggle between the two lines, and of one from the woman’s line who will strike the serpent’s head.

Verse by verse.

“But the Lord God called to the man, and said to him, “Where are you?”” (3:9)

Sin makes man hide; God comes looking. The whole story of salvation begins here, with God seeking the one who is lost.

“I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.” (3:10)

Before the sin there was trust and no shame. Now there is fear. The first fruit of sin is broken relationship with God.

“The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate.” (3:12)

The man blames the woman and, indirectly, God. Sin divides husband and wife and makes us shift the blame.

“I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.” (3:15)

The serpent’s blow to the heel is painful; the blow to the head is fatal. Textual note: the Hebrew says “he” or “it”, meaning the offspring; the Greek translation has “he”. The Latin Vulgate of St Jerome read “she”, which shaped much Christian art showing Mary crushing the serpent. Both readings point to the same victory: Christ wins it, and Mary, his mother, shares in it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls this verse the “Protoevangelium”, the first announcement of the Gospel: the Messiah and Redeemer, the battle between the serpent and the woman, and the final victory of a descendant of hers (CCC 410). It adds that many Fathers and Doctors of the Church have seen the woman as Mary, the mother of Christ, the “new Eve” (CCC 411). St Irenaeus wrote that the knot of Eve’s disobedience was loosed by Mary’s obedience (in substance, Against Heresies 3.22.4).

The Church also reads the passage as the revelation of original sin: the first parents lost the original holiness and justice they had received from God, not only for themselves but for all human beings (CCC 416–417). But the first word after the sin is God’s search and God’s promise. Pope St Leo the Great said that Christ’s grace gave us blessings better than those the demon’s envy had taken away (in substance; quoted in CCC 412).

2 Corinthians 4:13–5:1 · So we do not lose heart

Setting.

Paul wrote Second Corinthians after painful conflicts with the community in Corinth, some of whom despised him as weak and unimpressive. In chapters 4 and 5 he answers by speaking of the treasure of the Gospel carried “in clay jars” (4:7): the frailty of the apostle shows that the power belongs to God.

Our passage follows his list of sufferings: afflicted, perplexed, persecuted, struck down, but not crushed or destroyed (4:8–9). He explains why he keeps going: faith in the resurrection.

Structure.

  • v. 13: “I believed, and so I spoke”: faith that speaks.
  • v. 14: God who raised Jesus will raise us too.
  • v. 15: everything is for your sake, so that thanksgiving may increase.
  • vv. 16–18: the outer nature wastes away, the inner is renewed; slight affliction, eternal glory; seen and unseen.
  • 5:1: the earthly tent and the eternal house from God.

Key words.

  • episteusa, dio elalēsa, I believed, and so I spoke: Paul quotes Psalm 116:10 in its Greek form (in the Greek numbering, Psalm 115:1). The psalm is a song of thanksgiving by one rescued from death.
  • ho esō anthrōpos, the inner nature: Literally “the inner man”. Not a soul trapped in a body, but the person as renewed by the Spirit, growing day by day toward the resurrection.
  • baros doxēs, weight of glory: In Hebrew the word for glory, kābôd, also means weight. Paul, who knew Hebrew, sets the “weight” of glory against the lightness of present affliction.

Verse by verse.

“we also believe, and so we speak,” (4:13)

Faith cannot keep silent. The apostle preaches because he believes.

“because we know that the one who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also with Jesus,” (4:14)

The resurrection of Jesus is the pledge of ours. Paul’s courage rests not on his strength but on this certainty.

“So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” (4:16)

Age, illness and labour wear the body down. The life of grace grows at the same time. Every sick and elderly Christian can make these words their own.

“For this slight momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure,” (4:17)

Paul’s afflictions were not slight (11:23–28). He calls them slight only when weighed against the glory to come.

“For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” (5:1)

A tent is temporary, a house lasting. Paul, a tentmaker by trade (Acts 18:3), knew both well.

How the Church reads it.

The Church prays with this hope in the Preface for the Dead: for your faithful, Lord, life is changed, not ended, and when this earthly dwelling turns to dust, an eternal dwelling is made ready in heaven (in substance). The Catechism teaches that Christ will raise us up on the last day, and that by Baptism we already share in his heavenly life (CCC 1002–1003). Christian suffering is not meaningless: united with Christ it becomes a way to glory (compare CCC 1521).

Mark 3:20–35 · Whoever does the will of God

Setting.

Jesus has begun his ministry in Galilee. He has healed many, cast out demons, and just chosen the Twelve (3:13–19). Crowds press around him, so that he and his disciples cannot even eat. Two groups react: his own family, who think he has gone out of his mind, and scribes come down from Jerusalem, who say he works by the power of Beelzebul.

Background. Mark often places one story inside another, so that each explains the other. Here the family sets out (vv. 20–21), the scribes accuse (vv. 22–30), and the family arrives (vv. 31–35). Both groups misjudge Jesus, but in different ways: the family out of worry, the scribes out of hostility.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: the crowd; his family set out to restrain him: “He has gone out of his mind.”
  • v. 22: the scribes from Jerusalem: “He has Beelzebul.”
  • vv. 23–27: the parables: a kingdom divided; a house divided; the strong man bound.
  • vv. 28–30: every sin can be forgiven, but not blasphemy against the Holy Spirit.
  • vv. 31–35: his mother and brothers outside; “Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.”

Key words.

  • hoi par’ autou, his family: Literally “those from his side”, his relatives. The phrase is deliberately general. Mark does not say that Mary shared their opinion.
  • Beelzeboul, Beelzebul: An old name of a Canaanite god, “Baal the prince”, used in Jesus’ day as a name for the chief of the demons. The scribes cannot deny the exorcisms, so they explain them as the work of evil.
  • ischyros, the strong man: Satan is the strong man; Jesus is the stronger one whom John the Baptist announced (1:7). His exorcisms show that the strong man is already being bound.

Verse by verse.

“When his family heard it, they went out to restrain him, for people were saying, “He has gone out of his mind.”” (3:21)

Jesus’ zeal seemed excessive even to those who loved him. Matthew and Luke leave this verse out; Mark keeps the hard memory.

“How can Satan cast out Satan?” (3:23)

Jesus answers with simple logic. If his work destroys the power of evil, it cannot come from evil.

“But no one can enter a strong man’s house and plunder his property without first tying up the strong man;” (3:27)

The parable of Jesus’ whole mission. He has come to set captives loose from the power of evil.

“Truly I tell you, people will be forgiven for their sins and whatever blasphemies they utter;” (3:28)

A word of immense mercy comes first: there is no sin God cannot forgive.

“Whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.” (3:35)

Jesus does not reject his family; he opens it wide. The bond of faith and obedience to God creates a new family around him.

How the Church reads it.

On the sin against the Holy Spirit the Catechism teaches that there are no limits to the mercy of God, but anyone who deliberately refuses to accept his mercy by repenting rejects the forgiveness of sins and the salvation offered by the Holy Spirit (CCC 1864). The sin is unforgivable not because God refuses, but because the sinner refuses to be forgiven.

On the “brothers” of Jesus the Catechism explains that the Church has always understood these as close relations, according to a common way of speaking in the Old Testament; James and Joseph, called brothers of Jesus, are the sons of another Mary (CCC 500; compare Mark 6:3; 15:40). Mary remained ever virgin (CCC 499). The Second Vatican Council says that Mary received these words of her Son in faith, for she heard and kept the word of God faithfully (Lumen Gentium 58). St Augustine taught that Mary was more blessed in receiving the faith of Christ than in conceiving the flesh of Christ (in substance, On Holy Virginity 3). She is the first of those who do the will of God.

Questions people ask

Does this Gospel mean that Jesus rejected his mother?

No. Jesus is not pushing Mary away; he is showing where true closeness to him lies. No one did the will of God more fully than Mary, who said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). She is doubly his mother: by giving him flesh and by her faith. The Church honours her as the model disciple.

What is the unforgivable sin? Could I have committed it without knowing?

It is not a single word said in anger or a sin of weakness. It is the deliberate, lasting refusal to accept God’s mercy, calling good evil and refusing to repent (CCC 1864). Anyone who is worried about having committed it shows by that very worry that they have not hardened their heart. The door of confession is open.

Did God really make a serpent speak? How should we read Genesis 3?

The Church teaches that the account uses figurative language but affirms a real event at the beginning of human history (CCC 390). The serpent stands for the tempter, whom Scripture later calls “that ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan” (Revelation 12:9; CCC 391). What matters is the truth taught: human beings, created good, misused the liberty God gave them by distrusting him, and God at once promised salvation.

For the Latin reader

With this Sunday the Sundays of the Latin Church return to the green of Ordinary Time, already worn on weekdays since Pentecost, until the end of the liturgical year. In Year B the Sunday Gospels follow Mark; today’s passage is one of the earliest in his account of the Galilean ministry. The Church in India, which celebrated the Body and Blood of Christ last Sunday, kept the Sacred Heart of Jesus on Friday and the Immaculate Heart of Mary on Saturday. Genesis 3:15 and Mark 3:35 together give a fitting close to that week: the woman whose offspring defeats the serpent is also the first disciple who does the will of God. The opening prayer of today’s Mass asks God, from whom all good things come, that by his inspiration we may discern what is right and by his guidance do it.

For study and teaching

  • God asks Adam, “Where are you?” Where am I hiding from God, and what would it mean to answer him honestly?
  • Adam blamed Eve and Eve blamed the serpent. In what situations do I find it hard to take responsibility for my own wrongs?
  • Paul says, “we do not lose heart”. Who among the sick or elderly in our parish could be encouraged by these words, and how?
  • Jesus calls those who do God’s will his family. How can our parish feel more like a family to those who have no family?
