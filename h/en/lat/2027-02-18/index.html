Word for the day

““Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” Matthew 7:7

Reflection

Queen Esther stands at the edge of a terrible choice. A royal decree has ordered the destruction of her whole people, the Jews of Persia, and only she can plead with the king. But to enter his presence uninvited is to risk death. Before she goes, she fasts, lays aside her royal robes, covers her head with ashes, and lies on the ground in prayer: “O my Lord, you only are our king; help me, who am alone and have no helper but you, for my danger is in my hand.” She remembers how God chose Israel from among all the nations and kept every promise. Then she asks for courage and the right words: “Remember, O Lord; make yourself known in this time of our affliction, and give me courage… Put eloquent speech in my mouth before the lion.” This is how the saints pray when everything depends on God: humbly, remembering his faithfulness, and asking boldly. Her prayer is answered; the king's heart is turned, and her people are saved.

In the Gospel Jesus encourages exactly this kind of prayer. “Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” Three verbs, each a little more persistent than the last. Then he appeals to every parent's heart: “Is there anyone among you who, if your child asks for bread, will give a stone?… If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him!” God is not a reluctant official who must be pestered; he is a Father who loves to give. And the passage ends with the Golden Rule: “In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” The one who receives generously from the Father learns to give generously to others. Lenten prayer and Lenten charity are one movement of the heart.

Full homily

Dear friends, Queen Esther knew her people were about to be destroyed. Before going to the king, she lay on the ground in prayer: O my Lord, you only are our king; help me, who am alone and have no helper but you (Esther C:14).

She did not pretend to be strong. She remembered God's faithfulness and asked for courage and the right words. And God turned the king's heart. Prayer from the depths is never wasted.

Jesus says: ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you (Matthew 7:7). If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him! (Matthew 7:11).

And Jesus adds: in everything do to others as you would have them do to you (Matthew 7:12). Let us ask the Father with trust today, and then give to others what we hope to receive. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: ask God for what you need, and he will give you good things, because he is your Father. Tonight, ask God for something for someone else, and trust him.

Prayer

Lord our King, you heard Queen Esther when she was alone and had no helper but you. Hear us when we call on you in our need. Teach us to ask, to search and to knock with the trust of children, and to give to others the goodness we long to receive from you. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A queen with no one to turn to and a Teacher who says “Ask” stand side by side today. Esther prays alone, with her life in danger and nothing to rely on but God. Jesus tells us why such a prayer is not foolish: we are speaking to a Father. Both readings teach the boldness of those who know their poverty and know whom they are asking. The key words are alone, ask and how much more.

Esther C:12, 14–16, 23–25 (NRSV 14:1, 3–5, 12–14) · No helper but you

Setting.

The Book of Esther comes to us in two forms. The Hebrew tells of a Jewish queen in the Persian court who saves her people from a decree of destruction planned by the king’s minister Haman; it never names God. The Greek form adds six longer passages, including the prayers of Mordecai and Esther, so that what happens is seen to be God’s work. The Catholic Church receives the book with these additions as Scripture.

The numbering is confusing. Lectionaries call this passage C:12–25. The NRSV, following the older numbering from St Jerome’s Latin Bible, prints it as chapter 14. The wording also differs a little from one Bible to another, because the Greek and Latin texts differ.

The situation: the decree has gone out. Mordecai has asked Esther to go to the king, and no one may enter his presence uninvited on pain of death. Esther asks all the Jews of Susa to fast for three days with her. Then, before going in, she prays.

Structure.

  • C:12 (14:1): Esther, in mortal fear, flees to the Lord.
  • C:14–16 (14:3–5): “no helper but you”, and the memory of what God has done for Israel.
  • C:23–25 (14:12–14): “Remember”: a request for courage and for words, and a cry to be saved.

Key words.

  • monē, alone: In the Greek, Esther is “the one who is alone”. She is a queen in a palace with servants around her, yet at this hour she is utterly alone.
  • boēthos, helper: The word the Greek Psalms often use of God. “No helper but you” is not despair. It is the most exact prayer she can say.
  • leōn, lion: Her name for the king, who holds the power of life and death over her. She does not pretend that he is not dangerous.

Verse by verse.

“O my Lord, you only are our king; help me, who am alone and have no helper but you,” (14:3)

She is the wife of the most powerful king of her world, and she says there is only one King. That sentence will give her the courage to walk into the throne room.

“for my danger is in my hand.” (14:4)

This is an idiom: her life is on the line, in her own hands.

“Remember, O Lord; make yourself known in this time of our affliction, and give me courage,” (14:12)

She does not ask to be spared the task. She asks for courage to do it. This is a good model of prayer in fear.

“Put eloquent speech in my mouth before the lion,” (14:13)

Prayer and practical action are not opposites. She has fasted and prayed, and she will still dress, walk in, and speak carefully (Esther 5).

“But save us by your hand, and help me, who am alone and have no helper but you, O Lord.” (14:14)

She moves from “me” to “us” and back: her own fear and her people’s danger are one prayer.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer (CCC 2559), and Esther prays from that place. She is a model of intercession: one person who stands before power for the sake of many. Christian writers and artists, above all in the Middle Ages, often saw in Esther a figure of Mary, the queen who intercedes for her people. Today’s reading also reminds us of Israel’s long experience of threatened destruction. We read it with reverence for the Jewish people, who still remember Esther each year at the feast of Purim.

Matthew 7:7–12 · How much more will your Father give

Setting.

We are near the end of the Sermon on the Mount. The sermon has asked a great deal: love of enemies, purity of heart, trust instead of worry. Here Jesus gives the means to live it: ask. Then he sums up the whole with one rule of life. The words “this is the law and the prophets” (v. 12) reach back to the start of his teaching: “I have come not to abolish but to fulfill” (5:17).

Structure.

  • v. 7: three commands: ask, search, knock.
  • v. 8: three promises, for “everyone”.
  • vv. 9–10: two questions about a father and a child.
  • v. 11: the argument: how much more.
  • v. 12: the Golden Rule.

Key words.

  • aiteite, zēteite, krouete, ask, search, knock: All three are present imperatives in Greek, which suggest something continuing: keep asking, keep searching, keep knocking. Each is a stronger action than the last.
  • ponēroi ontes, you who are evil: Said to the disciples in passing, and it is said without any drama. Even sinful parents know how to give good things to their children. The argument depends on it.
  • agatha, good things: Luke’s parallel reads: “how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit” (Luke 11:13). Good things in the end means God’s own gift of himself.

Verse by verse.

“Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” (7:7)

“It will be given” and “it will be opened” do not say who does it, but the passive points to God, as often in the Gospels.

“Is there anyone among you who, if your child asks for bread, will give a stone?” (7:9)

Jesus asks his hearers to look at what they know from home. Bread and fish were everyday food by the lake. A father who gave a stone or a snake would not be a father.

“how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him!” (7:11)

This argument from the lesser to the greater is the centre of the passage. The best parent we have known is only the beginning of what God is.

“In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” (7:12)

Earlier Jewish teaching already said this in negative form: “what you hate, do not do to anyone” (Tobit 4:15), and Rabbi Hillel said much the same (in substance). Jesus states it as a positive duty: do not wait, begin.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus teaches filial boldness in prayer (CCC 2610), and that the Gospel’s moral law is summed up in the Golden Rule (CCC 1970; compare 1789). St Augustine wrote to the widow Proba that God wants our desire to be exercised in prayer, so that we may grow large enough to receive what he is preparing to give (in substance; Letter 130). Those words give an answer to anyone who feels that prayer takes too long.

Questions people ask

I asked and knocked, and nothing happened. Was the promise false?

This is an honest question, and the Catechism takes it up: we often ask wrongly, or for what is not good for us, or we want the gift more than the Giver (CCC 2735–2737). Jesus himself asked for the cup to pass, and he received not an escape but the Resurrection. The promise is that the Father always gives good things, not that he always gives the thing I named. Asking again, even in the dark, is itself part of what he is giving.

For study and teaching

  • Esther says that she has “no helper but you”. Is that my true prayer, or do I keep God as a last resort behind other plans?
  • Is there something I stopped asking God for because I grew tired? What would it mean to keep knocking?
