Word for the day

“Indeed, God did not send the Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him.” John 3:17

Reflection

The apostles have been healing and teaching in Solomon's Portico, and the crowds are growing. The high priest and the Sadducees, “filled with jealousy,” arrest them and lock them in the public prison. But at night “an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, brought them out, and said, ‘Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life.’” The angel does not send them to safety; he sends them straight back to the place where they were arrested. At daybreak they are teaching again. Luke tells the rest almost with a smile: the council meets, the guards are sent, and they report a prison “securely locked” with the guards at the doors and nobody inside. The authorities are perplexed; the captain fetches the apostles “without violence,” afraid of the people. The same Lord who left the tomb sealed and empty opens the prison of his witnesses. Luke is describing a struggle with particular leaders of the Temple in that hour, not with the Jewish people, who in this very scene hold the apostles in honour. The Psalm sings what the apostles had just lived: “The angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them.”

The Gospel gives us perhaps the best known verse of the Bible: “For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, so that everyone who believes in him may not perish but may have eternal life.” And the next verse tells us what kind of love it is: “God did not send the Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him.” Judgment is not God's wish; it is what happens when people prefer darkness to light, “because their deeds were evil.” Today the Church remembers a man who spent his life bringing light to children who lived in darkness: St John Baptist de la Salle (1651–1719). Born into a wealthy family in Reims, a canon of the cathedral at sixteen and a priest at twenty-seven, he was drawn almost against his will into helping a few poor schoolmasters. Step by step he took them into his own house, gave away his fortune to the hungry in the famine of 1684, resigned his canonry and founded the Brothers of the Christian Schools, lay religious devoted entirely to teaching the poor without fees. He taught in French instead of Latin, grouped pupils in classes, opened schools for working youths on Sundays and trained teachers in what were among the first teacher-training colleges. He met lawsuits, slander and opposition even from churchmen, and he died on Good Friday, 7 April 1719. He was canonized in 1900, and in 1950 Pius XII named him patron of all teachers. He used to tell his Brothers, in substance, that they could work miracles by touching the hearts of the children entrusted to them: that is John 3:17 in a classroom.

Full homily

Dear friends, the apostles are locked in prison, and in the night an angel opens the doors. Does he tell them to run away? No. He says: go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life (Acts 5:20). God frees us not for our comfort but for our mission.

In the morning the guards find the prison locked, the doors guarded, and nobody inside. It sounds like Easter morning: a sealed tomb, and nobody inside. The risen Lord cannot be locked away, and neither can his word.

Then Jesus says to Nicodemus: God so loved the world that he gave his only Son; God did not send the Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him (John 3:16–17). God is not waiting to catch us. He is waiting to save us. The only thing that keeps us from the light is our own preference for darkness.

Today we remember St John Baptist de la Salle. He was rich and comfortable, but he saw poor children growing up in darkness, without school and without faith. He gave away his fortune, gathered teachers, and opened schools without fees. He said to his Brothers, in substance: you can work miracles by touching the hearts of children. Let us pray today for every teacher, and let us be people who bring others into the light. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, today is the day of St John Baptist de la Salle, the friend of all teachers. He opened schools for poor children so that they could learn to read and learn about Jesus. Let us say a prayer for our teachers today, and say thank you to them tomorrow!

O God, who chose Saint John Baptist de la Salle to educate young people in the Christian life, raise up teachers in your Church ready to devote themselves with all zeal to the human and Christian formation of the young. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the memorial of St John Baptist de la Salle, patron of teachers, both readings speak of a light that cannot be locked away. In Acts the high priest’s party arrests the apostles a second time, and in the night an angel opens the prison and sends them back to teach in the temple. In John, the best-loved verse of the Gospel says where that light comes from: God so loved the world that he gave his only Son. Psalm 34 is the song of people who were rescued: “The Lord hears the cry of the poor”.

Acts 5:17–26 · The prison that could not hold the word

Setting.

Luke tells of two arrests of the apostles. In chapter 4 Peter and John were held overnight and warned not to speak in the name of Jesus. They went on speaking, signs multiplied, and crowds gathered in Solomon’s Portico (5:12–16). Now the high priest and his party arrest the apostles as a group.

The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy who ran the temple. Acts 23:8 notes that they said there is no resurrection, no angel and no spirit. Luke lets the irony speak for itself: an angel releases men who preach the Resurrection.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–18: jealousy and arrest.
  • vv. 19–21a: the angel opens the doors; at daybreak the apostles are teaching in the temple.
  • vv. 21b–24: the council gathers; the prison is found locked and empty.
  • vv. 25–26: the apostles are found teaching and brought back, without violence.

Key words.

  • zēlos, jealousy: The word can mean zeal or jealousy. Here it is the resentment of those whose authority feels threatened. Luke uses it again when the leaders in Pisidian Antioch see the crowds around Paul (13:45).
  • stratēgos tou hierou, captain of the temple: The priest who commanded the temple police, a guard of Levites. In the running of the temple he stood close in rank to the high priest.
  • ta rhēmata tēs zōēs tautēs, the message about this life: Literally “the words of this life”. The angel does not say “this teaching” but “this life”. The Gospel is the life of the risen Christ offered to people; Peter has already called him “the Author of life” (3:15).

Verse by verse.

“But during the night an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, brought them out,” (5:19)

Acts tells of three such releases: here, Peter in 12:6–11, and Paul and Silas in 16:25–26. God does not always rescue his witnesses this way: Stephen and James will die (7:60; 12:2). But he shows that no human power can lock up his word.

“Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life.” (5:20)

The angel does not send them into hiding but back to the most public place in Jerusalem. The release is given for witness, not for safety.

“but without violence, for they were afraid of being stoned by the people.” (5:26)

The people held the apostles in high regard (5:13). Luke takes care to show that the opposition came from a group of leaders, not from the people of Jerusalem as a whole.

How the Church reads it.

Paul, writing from his own chains, puts the lesson of this scene in one line: “the word of God is not chained” (2 Timothy 2:9). The Church has read such stories with sober hope. Her witnesses are not always freed from prison, and many have died there, yet the word they carried has gone on. The courage of the apostles is not a gift of temperament. Luke shows it as the work of the Holy Spirit, whom they received at Pentecost.

John 3:16–21 · God so loved the world

Setting.

These verses close the night conversation with Nicodemus, the Pharisee who came to Jesus by night (3:1–2). Jesus has just said that the Son of Man must be lifted up as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness (3:14–15).

Ancient Greek had no quotation marks, and scholars differ on whether vv. 16–21 are still the words of Jesus or the evangelist’s own reflection on them. The NRSV keeps them within the words of Jesus. Either way the Church receives them as the word of God.

Structure.

  • v. 16: the gift: God’s love gives the only Son.
  • vv. 17–18: the purpose: to save, not to condemn.
  • vv. 19–21: the judgment: the light has come, and people show what they love by whether they come to it.

Key words.

  • monogenēs, only, only-begotten: Used of an only child, as of the widow’s son at Nain (Luke 7:12). In John it marks the unique Son, who is not one son among many. The Creed says “the Only Begotten Son of God”.
  • kosmos, world: In John the world can be the creation God loves (3:16), and also humanity closed against God (15:18). God loves even the world that does not love him back.
  • krisis, judgment: Both a verdict and the separation it brings about. Jesus does not come to pass sentence; his coming is itself the light in which people decide.

Verse by verse.

“For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son,” (3:16)

“Gave” looks toward the cross, since Jesus has just spoken of being lifted up (3:14). Paul says the same in other words: “He who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us” (Romans 8:32).

“God did not send the Son into the world to condemn the world,” (3:17)

The Catechism teaches that the Son did not come to judge but to save, and that whoever rejects grace in this life already judges himself (CCC 679).

“But those who do what is true come to the light,” (3:21)

Not “those who know the truth” but those who do it. In John truth is something lived. Nicodemus, who came by night, will come into the open at the burial of Jesus (19:39).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism places John 3:16 among the texts that say why the Word became flesh: so that we might know the love of God (CCC 458). Verse 18 speaks of refusing the light, not of never having seen it. The Church teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel but seek God with a sincere heart can also attain salvation by his grace (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847).

Questions people ask

Does John 3:16 mean that only believing matters, and not how we live?

No. The same passage ends with deeds: those who do evil hate the light, and those who do what is true come to it (3:20–21). Faith in John means coming into the light and living in it. The Catechism teaches that faith deprived of hope and love does not fully unite the believer to Christ, and quotes James: faith apart from works is dead (CCC 1815).

For the Latin reader

St John Baptist de la Salle (1651–1719) was a priest and canon of Reims who gave up his canonry and his family wealth to found the Brothers of the Christian Schools, religious brothers who are teachers and not priests. He opened schools for poor boys, taught reading in French before Latin, and trained teachers with great care. He died on Good Friday, 7 April 1719, near Rouen, and Pope Pius XII named him patron of teachers in 1950. Like the apostles sent back to “tell the people”, those who teach the young share in the Church’s work of light.

For study and teaching

  • Where does my faith stay locked away, and where is the Lord sending me back to speak, gently and in the open?
  • John 3:16 is often quoted. What changes if I read it slowly, with my own name in place of “the world”?
