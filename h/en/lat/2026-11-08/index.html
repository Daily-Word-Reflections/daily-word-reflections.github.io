Word for the day

“Keep awake therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” Matthew 25:13

Reflection

As the liturgical year draws to its close, the readings turn our eyes to the end: the return of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, and our readiness. The tone is not fear but longing. The psalm sets it: “O God, you are my God, I seek you, my soul thirsts for you.” Wisdom, says the first reading, is not hard to find for those who love her: “she will be found sitting at the gate”.

The parable of the ten bridesmaids is about a wedding, the great biblical image of God's covenant love. All ten are invited; all ten have lamps; all ten fall asleep. The difference is the oil. The wise brought flasks with their lamps; the foolish did not. When the cry comes at midnight, “Look! Here is the bridegroom!”, the oil cannot be borrowed. Some things no one can do for us: our own faith, our own prayer, our own charity.

The Fathers asked what the oil means. St Augustine saw it as charity; others saw good works, or the grace of the Holy Spirit kept alive by prayer. All agree that it is something gathered slowly, day by day, not bought in a hurry at the last moment. The Catechism reminds us that each person receives his eternal retribution at death, in a particular judgement (CCC 1022). Readiness is not a single act but a habit.

Paul gives the hope behind the vigilance. The Thessalonians grieved for members who had died before Christ's return. Paul says: do not grieve “as others do who have no hope”. Since Jesus died and rose, God will bring with him those who have died, “and so we will be with the Lord forever”. The bridegroom is coming not to catch us out but to take us in.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, every Malayali knows what it is to wait for a wedding procession. Today Jesus tells a story about ten young women waiting for the bridegroom.

All ten were invited. All ten had lamps. And all ten fell asleep, because the bridegroom was late. Jesus does not blame them for sleeping. The difference was simple: five had brought extra oil, and five had not.

At midnight the cry went up: “Look! Here is the bridegroom!” The foolish ones said, “Give us some of your oil.” But the wise could not. Why? Because some things cannot be shared at the last moment. My faith cannot replace yours. My prayer cannot be your prayer. The Fathers said the oil is charity, good works, the life of grace. These are gathered slowly, drop by drop, day by day.

The first reading says Wisdom is easy to find: “she will be found sitting at the gate.” God is not hiding. He is waiting at the door of our day. Those who rise early to seek him find him. So the question is not whether God is available, but whether we go to him.

And St Paul reminds us why we wait with hope and not fear. Some in Thessalonica had lost loved ones. Paul says: do not grieve as those who have no hope. Jesus died and rose, and those who died in him will rise with him, “and so we will be with the Lord forever.” This November, as we pray for our dead, these words are our comfort.

So let us fill our flasks this week. A few minutes of prayer each morning. A Sunday Mass that is never missed. A kindness to someone in need. Drop by drop, the lamp is filled. And when the Bridegroom comes, may he find our lamps burning.

For children

Children, have you ever waited for a guest who was late?

Jesus told a story about ten girls waiting for a bridegroom with lamps. Five brought extra oil, and five forgot.

The oil is like our love for Jesus: we fill it up a little every day when we pray and are kind.

Tonight, before sleeping, say: “Jesus, I love you. Keep my lamp burning!”

Prayer

Almighty and merciful God, graciously keep from us all adversity, so that, unhindered in mind and body alike, we may pursue in freedom of heart the things that are yours. Keep our lamps burning until the Bridegroom comes. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Church is near the end of her year. In November she prays for the dead, and the Sunday readings turn to the last things: death, the coming of the Lord, and how to be ready. Today the Lectionary pairs a poem about Wisdom with the parable of the wise and foolish bridesmaids, and the link is in the word itself. Wisdom “is found by those who seek her” and waits “at the gate”; the wise bridesmaids are those who are ready when the door opens.

The psalm gives the voice of the seeker: “my soul thirsts for you”, a prayer of the night and of the dawn. St Paul, writing to a young Church that was grieving its first dead, tells them that those who have died in Christ are not lost: the Lord will come, and “so we will be with the Lord forever”. Seeking, thirsting, hoping and keeping awake: these are the four movements of the day.

Wisdom 6:12–16 · Wisdom waits at the door

Setting.

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, most likely at Alexandria in Egypt, in the century before Christ. Its author is a devout Jewish teacher who speaks in the person of Solomon, the wise king, to encourage faithful Jews living among Greek culture. It belongs to the deuterocanonical books, received by the Catholic Church as inspired Scripture.

Chapter 6 is a hinge. Chapters 1–5 speak of the destiny of the just and the wicked; from chapter 6 the book turns to praise Wisdom herself. The chapter opens with an address to kings and judges (6:1): those who rule others must first seek Wisdom. Our passage is the invitation that follows.

Structure.

  • v. 12: Wisdom is radiant and does not fade; those who love and seek her find her.
  • v. 13: she hurries to make herself known.
  • v. 14: whoever rises early to seek her finds her already sitting at the gate.
  • v. 15: to think of her is understanding; to keep watch for her brings relief from anxiety.
  • v. 16: she goes about seeking those worthy of her.
  • ¶ The passage moves in a circle. It begins with us seeking Wisdom (v. 12) and ends with Wisdom seeking us (v. 16). The search is real, but the one we seek is already looking for us.

Key words.

  • sophia, wisdom: In this book Wisdom is spoken of as a person, a radiant woman who comes from God (see 7:25–26). She is not a goddess; she is God’s own wisdom reaching out to human beings.
  • orthrizein, to rise early: The verb used in v. 14 means to get up at dawn for a purpose. The Greek of Psalm 63, today’s psalm, uses the same verb: “to you I rise at dawn”. Seeking God is a morning work.
  • agrypnein, to keep watch, to stay awake: Used in v. 15 for the one who is vigilant on Wisdom’s account. It is the attitude the Gospel asks of the bridesmaids: “Keep awake”.

Verse by verse.

“Wisdom is radiant and unfading, and she is easily discerned by those who love her, and is found by those who seek her.” (6:12)

Love comes before seeing. Wisdom is not first a matter of cleverness but of desire: those who love her recognise her.

“One who rises early to seek her will have no difficulty, for she will be found sitting at the gate.” (6:14)

The gate of an ancient town was the place of business, judgment and meeting. The Greek speaks of the seeker’s own gates: the one who gets up early to look for Wisdom finds her already waiting at his door.

“because she goes about seeking those worthy of her, and she graciously appears to them in their paths, and meets them in every thought.” (6:16)

Here the search is reversed. Wisdom walks the roads looking for people. This prepares the Christian reader for the Gospel, where the Son goes out to seek the lost.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul calls Christ “the power of God and the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24), and the Fathers read the great Wisdom poems as speaking of the eternal Son, the Word through whom all things were made (in substance). The Latin Church sings to him on 17 December, in the first of the O Antiphons: “O Wisdom, who came from the mouth of the Most High”. To seek Wisdom, for a Christian, is to seek Christ, and to discover that he has been seeking us first.

Psalm 63:2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8 · A thirst that keeps watch

Setting.

The heading of the psalm places it in the wilderness of Judah, the dry hill country that falls away towards the Dead Sea, where David hid from his enemies. It is the prayer of someone far from the sanctuary who remembers having seen God there, and who longs for him as dry ground longs for rain.

Structure.

  • vv. 2–4: longing, and the memory of God’s glory in the sanctuary.
  • vv. 5–6: praise and a soul satisfied as with a feast.
  • vv. 7–8: remembering God through the night, under the shadow of his wings.

Key words.

  • nepeš, soul: The word first means the throat, then the breath and the whole living self. When the psalmist says “my soul thirsts”, the thirst is felt in the whole person.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love: God’s faithful, covenant love. The psalm says this love is “better than life”: to be loved by God matters more than to be alive.
  • ṣēl kenāpêkā, the shadow of your wings: An image of shelter, like a bird covering its young, and perhaps also of the wings of the cherubim over the ark in the sanctuary.

Verse by verse.

“O God, you are my God, I seek you, my soul thirsts for you” (63:2)

The Greek and Latin versions read “to you I keep watch at dawn”, which is why this psalm has long been a morning prayer.

“Because your steadfast love is better than life, my lips will praise you.” (63:4)
“when I think of you on my bed, and meditate on you in the watches of the night” (63:7)

The night watches recall the midnight of the Gospel. The one who thinks of God in the night is the one who is awake when the cry comes.

How the Church reads it.

From early centuries the Church has sung this psalm at dawn, and the Liturgy of the Hours still gives it to Morning Prayer of the first Sunday of the four week cycle. On this Sunday it becomes the prayer of the wise bridesmaids: a soul that thirsts for God and keeps watch in the night.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 · We will be with the Lord forever

Setting.

This is probably the oldest writing in the New Testament, from about AD 50–51. Paul had founded the Church at Thessalonica in northern Greece but had to leave in a hurry (Acts 17:1–10). The new Christians were waiting eagerly for the Lord’s return. When some of them died, the others were troubled: would the dead miss the coming of Christ?

Paul answers with the heart of the faith: Jesus died and rose, and those who belong to him share his destiny.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the purpose: that you may not grieve like those who have no hope.
  • v. 14: the creed: Jesus died and rose again; so God will bring the dead with him.
  • vv. 15–17: a word of the Lord: the dead in Christ will rise first, and the living will join them to meet the Lord.
  • v. 18: therefore, encourage one another.

Key words.

  • koimasthai, to fall asleep: The Greek word for those who have died, which the NRSV translates “those who have died” and the POC “those who have fallen asleep”. From the same root comes koimētērion, a sleeping place, our word “cemetery”. For Christians the grave is a bed, not an end.
  • parousia, coming: The word used for the visit of an emperor or a governor to a city. Christians used it for the glorious coming of Christ.
  • apantēsis, meeting: When an important visitor came, the citizens went out of the city to meet him and escort him back in. Paul pictures the Church going out to meet her Lord. Matthew uses the same word in today’s Gospel: “Come out to meet him” (25:6).

Verse by verse.

“But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers and sisters, about those who have died, so that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope.” (4:13)

Paul does not forbid grief. He forbids hopeless grief. Christians weep at a funeral, but they weep as people who expect to meet again.

“For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have died.” (4:14)

The Greek can be read in two ways: “God will bring through Jesus those who have died”, or “God will bring with him those who have died through (that is, in) Jesus”. The POC takes the second way and speaks of those who have fallen asleep in Jesus. Both say the same thing: the dead are held by Christ.

“Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up in the clouds together with them to meet the Lord in the air; and so we will be with the Lord forever.” (4:17)

The images are drawn from Old Testament pictures of God’s coming, clouds and trumpet (Exodus 19:16; Daniel 7:13). Paul does not give a timetable. His last words are the point: “we will be with the Lord forever”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that we will rise “on the last day”, citing this passage (CCC 1001), and that to live in heaven is “to be with Christ” (CCC 1025). The Roman Missal puts the same hope into the Preface for the Dead: for the faithful, life is changed, not ended (in substance). In November, when the Church prays for the faithful departed, Paul’s last line is her instruction to every family in mourning: “encourage one another with these words”.

Matthew 25:1–13 · The cry at midnight

Setting.

Jesus speaks these words in Jerusalem, a few days before his Passion, on the Mount of Olives (24:3). His long teaching about the end (chapters 24–25) closes with three parables. This first one asks one question: who will be ready when the Lord comes, even if he comes late?

In village weddings of the time, the bridegroom came at night to bring his bride to his own house, and young women of the family went out with lights to welcome the procession and enter the feast with it. A delay was nothing unusual at such weddings.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: ten young women, all with lamps; five also take oil.
  • vv. 5–7: the delay, the sleep, the cry at midnight, the lamps trimmed.
  • vv. 8–9: the request for oil and the refusal.
  • vv. 10–12: the door shut, the late knocking, “I do not know you”.
  • v. 13: the lesson for the listener.

Key words.

  • lampas, lamp, torch: Probably a torch with a head of cloth soaked in oil, which burned for a short time and had to be fed. The foolish had lit their torches; what they lacked was the oil to keep them burning.
  • elaion, oil: Oil in Scripture is a sign of joy, healing and anointing. In the parable it is the one thing the foolish cannot borrow.
  • grēgoreite, keep awake: A present imperative: keep on watching. It is the word Jesus will say to Peter, James and John in Gethsemane a few days later (26:38–41).

Verse by verse.

“But at midnight there was a shout, ‘Look! Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him.’” (25:6)

The arrival comes at the darkest hour, when no one expects it. The cry is good news: the one they were waiting for is here.

“The foolish said to the wise, ‘Give us some of your oil, for our lamps are going out.’” (25:8)

The Greek is present tense: the lamps are going out. They had started well. What fails is not the beginning but the perseverance.

“Keep awake therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” (25:13)

All ten slept, so “keep awake” cannot mean never sleeping. It means living each day so that, whenever the call comes, the oil is there.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, preaching on this parable, saw the oil as charity, the love that keeps faith alive (in substance); St John Chrysostom saw it as mercy to the poor (in substance). Both point to a life of love that cannot be produced at the last moment. The Catechism reminds us that the Church is the bride who waits for the bridegroom (CCC 796), and that we must watch, since we know neither the day nor the hour (CCC 1036).

Questions people ask

Is 1 Thessalonians 4:17 about a secret “rapture” before the end?

The word “caught up” (Latin rapiemur) gave English the word “rapture”, but Paul is not describing a secret removal of believers. He is describing the one glorious coming of Christ, when the dead rise and the living join them to meet the Lord, as citizens go out to meet a king. The Catholic Church teaches one coming of Christ in glory at the end of time, followed by the resurrection and the judgment (CCC 1001, 1038–1040).

Were the wise bridesmaids selfish?

The parable is not a lesson in sharing goods; elsewhere Jesus commands generosity. It teaches that readiness for God is personal. Faith and love can be supported by others, but at the decisive moment no one can stand in for us.

Is the Book of Wisdom really part of the Bible?

Yes. Wisdom is one of the deuterocanonical books, part of the Greek Old Testament used by the early Church and received as Scripture by the Catholic Church (Council of Trent; CCC 120). The New Testament echoes it in several places.

For the Latin reader

In the Roman calendar these are the last Sundays of the year. Next Sunday the readings speak of the talents, and on 22 November the year ends with Christ the King; Advent begins on 29 November. November is the month of the faithful departed, and many parishes read out the names of those who died in the past year. Today’s second reading is one of the readings the Lectionary offers for funerals. At every baptism a lighted candle is given with the prayer that the flame of faith be kept alive, so that when the Lord comes the newly baptised may go out to meet him with all the saints (in substance). Every baptised Catholic is one of the ten, holding a lamp.

For study and teaching

  • Wisdom 6 begins with us seeking and ends with Wisdom seeking us. Where have I noticed God looking for me first?
  • Psalm 63 is a prayer of the night and the dawn. When in my day do I “thirst” for God?
  • How does 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 change the way I grieve and pray for my own dead this November?
  • What is the “oil” that keeps my lamp burning, and what makes it run low?
