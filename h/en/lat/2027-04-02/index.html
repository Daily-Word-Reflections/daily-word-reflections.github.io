Word for the day

“That disciple whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It is the Lord!”” John 21:7

Reflection

Peter and John are still speaking to the people in the Temple when the priests, the captain of the temple guard and the Sadducees arrive, “much annoyed because they were teaching the people and proclaiming that in Jesus there is the resurrection of the dead.” The Sadducees did not believe in any resurrection at all, and here were two Galileans announcing that it had already begun in Jesus. The Apostles spend the night in custody. Yet Luke slips in a quiet line of victory: “many of those who heard the word believed; and they numbered about five thousand.” The word of God is not locked up with its preachers. The next morning the Temple leaders gather, Annas and Caiaphas among them, the same men before whom Jesus stood a few weeks earlier. Their question is the right one, though they ask it in the wrong spirit: “By what power or by what name did you do this?”

Then Peter, “filled with the Holy Spirit,” answers. This is the man who, on the night of the Passion, could not admit to a servant girl that he knew Jesus. Now he stands before the high priest and says: “this man is standing before you in good health by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead. This Jesus is ‘the stone that was rejected by you, the builders; it has become the cornerstone.’ There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved.” The Psalm of the day sings the same verse that Peter quotes: “The stone that the builders rejected has become the chief cornerstone. This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous in our eyes.” The Church has always held this truth firmly: Jesus Christ is the one Saviour of the whole world. The Second Vatican Council adds that God's grace, which always comes through Christ, can reach in ways known to God those who through no fault of their own do not yet know him (Lumen Gentium 16). That is not a reason to be silent; it is a reason to speak his name with love, as Peter did.

The Gospel takes us back to Galilee, to the Sea of Tiberias. Seven disciples are together, and Simon Peter says simply, “I am going fishing.” They go with him, “but that night they caught nothing.” A long, empty night of work: many fishermen, many parents, many young people looking for a job know that night. “Just after daybreak, Jesus stood on the beach; but the disciples did not know that it was Jesus.” He calls to them as a father calls his children: “Children, you have no fish, have you?” They answer, “No.” He tells them to cast the net to the right side of the boat, and now they cannot haul it in for the number of fish. The beloved disciple sees with the eyes of love before anyone else: “It is the Lord!” Peter, impulsive as ever, puts on his clothes and jumps into the sea to reach him first.

On the shore a charcoal fire is already burning, with fish on it, and bread. The only other charcoal fire in John's Gospel is the one in the high priest's courtyard where Peter warmed himself and denied his Master (John 18:18). Now the Lord lights a new fire, of welcome. He asks for some of the fish they have caught, and Peter hauls the net ashore, “full of large fish, a hundred fifty-three of them; and though there were so many, the net was not torn.” Then come the gentlest words: “Come and have breakfast.” None of them dares ask, “Who are you?” They know. “Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish.” The Fathers saw here a picture of the Church: the boat of Peter, the net that gathers every kind of people and does not tear, and the Lord who feeds his tired disciples with bread from his own hands. Without him the night is empty; with him the net is full, and breakfast is ready on the shore.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the leaders of the Temple ask Peter and John a question: by what power or by what name did you do this? (Acts 4:7). It is a good question for every Christian. When people see us forgive, serve or hope, they should be able to ask: in whose name do you do this?

Peter answers with the courage of the Holy Spirit: by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead. A few weeks before, he was afraid of a servant girl. Now he is not afraid of the high priest. The Resurrection and the Spirit have changed him.

And Peter says: there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved (Acts 4:12). Jesus is not one teacher among many. He is the Saviour. That is why we pray in his name, are baptized in his name and live in his name.

In the Gospel the disciples have fished all night and caught nothing. Many of us know nights like that: work without fruit, prayers that seem unanswered, efforts that fail. At daybreak Jesus stands on the shore and calls: children, you have no fish, have you? He knows our empty nets.

He tells them to cast the net again, on the right side. They obey, and the net is full. The beloved disciple cries out: it is the Lord! (John 21:7). Love recognizes him first. When we obey his word, even in small things, we begin to see that he was with us all along.

On the shore the Lord has already prepared a fire and breakfast. He says: come and have breakfast. And he takes the bread and gives it to them. This is what he does at every Mass. Today, on this First Friday, let us come to him tired as we are, and let him feed us. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, the disciples fished all night and caught nothing. Jesus told them to try again, and the net was full of fish! John shouted: it is the Lord!

Then Jesus made breakfast for his friends. Jesus takes care of us too. Let us say: thank you, Jesus, for loving us!

Prayer

Almighty ever-living God, who gave us the Paschal Mystery in the covenant you established for reconciling the human race, so dispose our minds, we pray, that what we celebrate by professing the faith we may express in deeds. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen. Let us also pray: Lord Jesus, you stood on the shore at daybreak while your friends toiled in vain. Call to us in our empty nights, teach us to cast our nets at your word, and feed us at your fire with the bread of your own hands, so that with the beloved disciple we may say: It is the Lord! Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Yesterday Peter preached in the Temple. Today he is arrested for it, and stands before the very council that condemned Jesus. Filled with the Holy Spirit, the fisherman who once denied his Lord now declares that there is salvation in no other name. Psalm 118 gives him his text: the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone.

In the Gospel the same Peter has gone back to his boats in Galilee. After a night of empty nets, a stranger on the shore tells them where to cast, and the beloved disciple cries, “It is the Lord!” Jesus has a charcoal fire ready, with bread and fish. The key words today are name, stone and breakfast: the risen Lord is both the foundation of the Church and the host at her table.

Acts 4:1–12 · No other name under heaven

Setting.

The healing of the lame man and Peter's sermon have drawn a crowd, and the Temple authorities step in. Luke names the groups: the priests, the captain of the Temple (the officer in charge of the Temple police), and the Sadducees. The Sadducees were a priestly party who did not accept the resurrection of the dead (compare Luke 20:27; Acts 23:8), so the apostles' preaching troubled them twice over.

The next morning the council meets: rulers, elders and scribes, with Annas and Caiaphas. Annas had been high priest earlier and remained the senior figure of the family; his son-in-law Caiaphas held the office from about AD 18 to 36. Nothing certain is known of the John and Alexander named here. This is the first time the apostles stand where Jesus stood.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: arrest at evening; yet about five thousand believe.
  • vv. 5–7: the council assembles: “By what power or by what name?”
  • vv. 8–10: Peter, filled with the Spirit: by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth.
  • v. 11: the rejected stone has become the cornerstone.
  • v. 12: no other name by which we must be saved.

Key words.

  • onoma, name: In the Bible a name stands for the person and his power. The question “by what name?” is the theme of these chapters, and the word appears again and again (3:6, 16; 4:7, 10, 12, 17, 18).
  • plēstheis pneumatos hagiou, filled with the Holy Spirit: Jesus had promised that when his disciples were brought before rulers, the Holy Spirit would teach them what to say (Luke 12:11–12). Here the promise is kept.
  • kephalē gōnias, cornerstone: Literally “head of the corner”: the stone that holds two walls together, or the top stone that completes an arch. Psalm 118:22 is quoted of Jesus by Jesus himself (Luke 20:17) and by Peter again in his letter (1 Peter 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“much annoyed because they were teaching the people and proclaiming that in Jesus there is the resurrection of the dead.” (4:2)

The apostles do not only say that Jesus rose. They say that in him the resurrection of the dead has begun. Easter is the first fruits of a harvest.

“Then Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, said to them, “Rulers of the people and elders,” (4:8)

A few weeks before, in a courtyard of the high priest's house, a servant girl was enough to make Peter deny Jesus. Now he speaks to the high priest's council itself. The difference is the Spirit.

“This Jesus is ‘the stone that was rejected by you, the builders; it has become the cornerstone.’” (4:11)

Peter adds “by you” to the psalm. He applies the verse to the leaders before him, as Jesus did in the parable of the vineyard. The same rejection is the place where God builds.

“There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among mortals by which we must be saved.”” (4:12)

The healing of one man's feet becomes a sign of salvation for all. The Greek verb for “healed” in 4:9 is sesōstai, from the same root as “saved” here: bodily healing points to the whole salvation that only Christ gives.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it explains the name of Jesus: the name of the Son of God made man is the only one through which we are saved (CCC 432, 452). The Church holds both truths together. Christ is the one Saviour of all, and those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel, yet seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will, can also attain salvation, always through Christ's grace (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). The declaration Dominus Iesus (2000) restated this teaching for our time.

John 21:1–14 · It is the Lord!

Setting.

John 20 closed with words that sound like the end of the book (20:30–31). Chapter 21 is an epilogue, and many scholars think it was added by the evangelist's circle, though it is found in every manuscript of the Gospel and belongs to it as Scripture. The scene moves to Galilee, to the lake John calls the Sea of Tiberias, after the city Herod Antipas built on its western shore.

Seven disciples are there. Peter goes back to the work he knew, and the others go with him. Readers have long noticed how much this recalls the first call by the lake in Luke 5:1–11: a night of nothing, a word from Jesus, a great catch.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: seven disciples; “I am going fishing”; a night with nothing.
  • vv. 4–6: a stranger at daybreak; cast to the right; a net too full to haul.
  • vv. 7–8: the beloved disciple sees; Peter leaps into the sea.
  • vv. 9–11: a charcoal fire, fish and bread; one hundred fifty-three large fish; the net is not torn.
  • vv. 12–14: “Come and have breakfast”; he gives the bread and the fish; the third appearance.

Key words.

  • paidia, children: A warm, familiar word, like “lads”. The risen Lord speaks to tired fishermen as a father would.
  • anthrakia, charcoal fire: The word occurs only twice in the New Testament: here, and at the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter denied Jesus (18:18). The scene is set for Peter's threefold profession of love that follows (21:15–17).
  • ouk eschisthē, was not torn: From the verb that gives schisma, a split. The same root is used of the seamless tunic that was not torn (19:24). Many Fathers saw here the unity of the Church that holds all the nations.

Verse by verse.

“Simon Peter said to them, “I am going fishing.” They said to him, “We will go with you.” They went out and got into the boat, but that night they caught nothing.” (21:3)

Night in John is often more than a time of day. Without him the disciples can do nothing (15:5), and their own skill brings up an empty net.

“That disciple whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It is the Lord!”” (21:7)

Love sees first; Peter acts first. The beloved disciple recognises, and Peter jumps into the sea. The Church needs both.

“So Simon Peter went aboard and hauled the net ashore, full of large fish, a hundred fifty-three of them; and though there were so many, the net was not torn.” (21:11)

St Jerome says that Greek writers on nature counted one hundred fifty-three kinds of fish, so the catch stands for all the nations (in substance). St Augustine notes that 153 is the sum of the numbers from 1 to 17 (in substance). Whatever the number holds, the point is fullness, and Peter is the one who draws the net to Jesus.

“Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish.” (21:13)

The words echo the feeding of the five thousand by this same lake (6:11). The Lord who was their guest at Emmaus is here the host.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read this scene as a picture of her mission: the disciples fish, but the catch comes at the Lord's word, and Peter draws the full net ashore. St Augustine, in his homilies on John, contrasts this catch with the first one in Luke 5, where the nets were breaking: there the Church as she now is, with good and bad; here the Church at the end, gathered whole (in substance). The meal of bread and fish, given by the Lord's own hands, has always been heard with the Eucharist in mind.

Questions people ask

Does “no other name” mean that all who are not baptized are lost?

No. The Church teaches that all salvation comes through Christ, and also that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel or the Church but seek God sincerely and try to do his will as their conscience shows it can be saved, by grace that comes from Christ (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). This does not lessen the duty of mission; it shows how far Christ's grace reaches (CCC 848).

Why do the disciples not recognise Jesus at first?

In several Easter accounts the risen Lord is not known at once: Mary Magdalene, the Emmaus disciples, the fishermen. His risen body is the same body, yet it is in a new state (CCC 645). He is known when he speaks, when he gives, when he breaks bread. Faith learns to recognise him in the same ways today.

Who were the Sadducees?

A party in Judaism of the time, closely linked with the high-priestly families and the Temple. They accepted the books of Moses as authority and denied the resurrection of the dead, against the Pharisees (Acts 23:8). Many in Israel did not share their views. After the Temple was destroyed in AD 70 they disappear from history.

For the Latin reader

The Friday of the Easter Octave keeps no fast: it is a solemnity, and the Friday abstinence does not apply on solemnities. The Lectionary for these days moves between Acts and the appearance stories of all four Gospels. In the Roman Rite the Easter candle stands near the ambo or the altar through the whole Easter season, and today's Gospel by the lake at daybreak suits it well: the risen Lord is the light that comes after a night of empty nets.

For study and teaching

  • Peter once gave way before a servant girl and now speaks before the council. Where do I need the Spirit's courage?
  • How do I hold together that Christ is the only Saviour and that God's grace reaches people I do not know?
  • “That night they caught nothing.” Where have I worked hard without asking the Lord?
  • Jesus cooks breakfast for tired fishermen. How can I serve those around me in small, practical ways this Easter?
