Word for the day

“Jesus said to her, “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?”” John 11:25–26

Reflection

Moses finishes the work that he began at the foot of Sinai, and the reading says again and again: he did it “as the Lord had commanded Moses.” The tabernacle is set up on the first day of the first month of the second year, a year, almost to the day, after the Exodus. The ark is brought in, the tablets of the covenant are laid inside it, the mercy seat is set above it, and a curtain screens the ark from sight. Then the Lord answers: “the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle. Moses was not able to enter.” God has come to live in the middle of his people, and he is too great to be entered or used. When the cloud lifts, Israel moves; when it stays, Israel stays; and by night “fire was in the cloud.” The Psalm is the song of one who longs for such a house: “My soul longs, indeed it faints for the courts of the Lord... Even the sparrow finds a home, and the swallow a nest for herself... at your altars, O Lord of hosts.” The Church gives us this reading on the day of three friends of Jesus because Bethany was, for him, such a dwelling place: a house where he was welcome.

The proper Gospel of the memorial is from St John. Bethany is “near Jerusalem, some two miles away,” and Lazarus has been dead four days. “Many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them.” When she hears that Jesus is coming, Martha goes out to meet him, while Mary stays at home. Martha is practical and honest, and the Gospels show her often at the centre of things. She says what many of us have said at a graveside: “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” But she goes on: “even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him.” Jesus tells her, “Your brother will rise again.” She answers with the faith of Israel: “I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.” And Jesus lifts her from a future hope to a present Person: “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?” Her answer is one of the great confessions of the Gospels, like the one Peter gives at Caesarea Philippi: “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” St Luke gives the other well-known scene, the alternative Gospel today: Martha “welcomed him into her home,” and was “distracted by her many tasks,” while Mary “sat at the Lord's feet and listened to what he was saying.” Jesus does not scold Martha for serving, but for being “worried and distracted by many things”: “there is need of only one thing. Mary has chosen the better part, which will not be taken away from her.” In St John's account of the supper at Bethany a week before the Passion, all three are together: “Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those at the table with him. Mary took a pound of costly perfume... The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume.” Martha serves, Lazarus sits at table as a witness, and Mary anoints. The Church honours them together as a household where Jesus was a friend, since 2021 on one day. Eastern tradition says Lazarus became bishop of Kition in Cyprus, and Western legends place the family in Provence; these are traditions, not Scripture. The Gospels give us the three of them in their home, and that is enough.

Full homily

Dear friends, the first reading ends with a great scene: the tent is finished, and the glory of the Lord fills it, so that even Moses cannot go in. God has come to live among his people. And the Psalm sings what every heart wants: How lovely is your dwelling place, O Lord of hosts (Psalm 84:1). Where is God's dwelling place today? In church, yes. But also in a home like the one in Bethany, where Jesus was welcomed.

Today the Church remembers three friends of Jesus: Martha, Mary and Lazarus. We know them as a household. In Luke, Martha welcomed him into her home, and was busy with serving, while Mary sat at his feet to listen. Jesus said: Martha, Martha, you are worried and distracted by many things; there is need of only one thing. Mary has chosen the better part (Luke 10:41–42). He was not scolding the service; he was warning against the worry. We can serve him in a hundred ways, but he also wants us to sit.

In John's Gospel Martha is the one who goes out to meet Jesus when Lazarus has died. She says what we all say: Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died (John 11:21). It is an honest prayer, with a little reproach in it. And Jesus does not rebuke her. He lifts her faith: I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live. Do you believe this? And she answers with one of the greatest confessions in the Gospels: Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world (John 11:27).

What a woman! She serves, she argues, she believes. And her brother Lazarus, who never speaks a word in the Gospels, is called out of the tomb. Dear friends, in our homes there are Marthas, Marys and Lazaruses: those who serve, those who pray, and those who say little but are living signs of what Jesus can do. Let us welcome Jesus in. Let us serve with love, listen with attention, and believe in the one who is the resurrection and the life. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus had friends in a village called Bethany: Martha, Mary and their brother Lazarus. When Jesus visited, Martha cooked and cleaned. Mary sat down next to Jesus and listened to him. Jesus said that listening is very special. And when Lazarus died, Jesus cried, because he loved him. Then he called: Lazarus, come out! And Lazarus came alive again! Jesus is stronger than death. Today let us help at home, and let us also sit quietly and listen to Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you were a guest in the home of Martha, Mary and Lazarus, and you wept at the grave of your friend. Be a guest in our homes, and make them places of hospitality, prayer and peace. Teach us Martha's honest faith, Mary's attentive listening and Lazarus's silent witness. You are the resurrection and the life: strengthen our faith and bring our dead into your light. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The book of Exodus ends with a house for God. Moses sets up the tabernacle exactly as the Lord commanded, and the cloud covers it and the glory of the Lord fills it, so that even Moses cannot enter. God has come to live among his people and to travel with them. In the Gospel, on the memorial of Martha, Mary and Lazarus, Martha goes out to meet Jesus on the road to Bethany, speaks honestly of her grief, and is brought to the great confession: “I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” Jesus’ own word to her, “I am the resurrection and the life,” is at the heart of Christian hope.

The Gospel is the proper one for today. The Church also allows Luke 10:38–42, the visit to Martha and Mary, in its place: Martha welcomes Jesus, and Mary sits at his feet.

Exodus 40:16–21, 34–38 · The glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle

Setting.

The last chapter of Exodus closes the story that began with slavery in Egypt. After the instructions for the tabernacle (chapters 25–31) and the golden calf (chapter 32), the craftsmen have made everything (chapters 35–39), and now Moses sets it up. The date given, the first day of the first month of the second year, is almost one year after the Exodus, which began in the same first month (12:2).

The chapter repeats the phrase “as the Lord had commanded Moses” again and again (40:16, 19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29, 32). The point is obedience: the house of God is built according to his word and not according to human invention.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–19: Moses sets up the tabernacle, its frames and its covering, as the Lord commanded.
  • vv. 20–21: he puts the tablets in the ark, places the mercy seat above it and screens the ark with the curtain.
  • vv. 34–35: the cloud covers the tent of meeting; the glory of the Lord fills it; Moses cannot enter.
  • vv. 36–38: when the cloud lifts, the people set out; by day it is a cloud, by night a fire, before the eyes of all Israel.

Key words.

  • miškān, tabernacle, dwelling: From the verb šākan, “to dwell”. The tabernacle is God’s dwelling among his people, a movable tent for a people on the move.
  • kābôd, glory: From a root meaning “heavy”. God’s glory is his weighty, real presence, the sign that he is here. It fills the tabernacle (40:34–35), and later Solomon’s temple (1 Kings 8:10–11).
  • šākan, to settle, to dwell: The verb used for the cloud “settling” on the tent (v. 35). Later Jewish writers used the related word Shekinah for God’s dwelling presence, though the noun does not appear in the Bible itself.

Verse by verse.

“Moses did everything just as the Lord had commanded him.” (40:16)

The opening line of the chapter sets its tone. Moses is faithful, and what he builds is what God has asked.

“Then the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle.” (40:34)

God answers. The house is not complete when it is built; it is complete when God fills it. A building becomes a dwelling by his presence.

“Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting because the cloud settled upon it,” (40:35)

Even Moses, who spoke to God face to face, cannot enter when the glory fills the tent. Nearness to God does not remove reverence.

“For the cloud of the Lord was on the tabernacle by day, and fire was in the cloud by night,” (40:38)

The book ends with the guiding cloud and fire, the same presence that led Israel out of Egypt. God is with his people at every stage of their journey.

How the Church reads it.

The New Testament takes up the language of this passage. St John says that the Word became flesh and “lived among us” (John 1:14); the Greek verb means “pitched his tent”. St Luke says that the power of the Most High “overshadowed” Mary (Luke 1:35), using the verb the Greek Old Testament uses for the cloud over the tent (Exodus 40:35). The Catechism points to the cloud that covered the tent of meeting as an image of the Holy Spirit who comes down on the Virgin Mary and overshadows her (CCC 697). In the same way the Church sees in her churches, and above all in the tabernacle where the Blessed Sacrament is kept, a dwelling of God among his people.

John 11:19–27 · I am the resurrection and the life

Setting.

Lazarus of Bethany, the brother of Martha and Mary, has died, and Jesus has delayed two days before going to him (11:6). When he arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb four days (11:17). Bethany is a village about two miles from Jerusalem (11:18), so many friends and neighbours have come to console the sisters.

John says that “many of the Jews” had come to Martha and Mary. Here the phrase simply means Judeans, the people of the area, and they come with kindness; there is no blame in it. The Gospel is the proper one for the memorial of the three friends of Jesus, and it is also read at the funerals of many Christians.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: mourners at the house; Martha goes out to meet Jesus while Mary stays at home.
  • vv. 21–22: Martha’s words: “if you had been here,” and her trust: “even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask”.
  • vv. 23–24: Jesus says Lazarus will rise; Martha answers with the faith of her people in the last day.
  • vv. 25–26: “I am the resurrection and the life”; “Do you believe this?”
  • v. 27: Martha’s confession of faith.

Key words.

  • egō eimi, I am: The solemn “I am” of John’s Gospel, echoing the divine name revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14). Jesus uses it with a predicate seven times in the Gospel (the bread of life, the light of the world, the door, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, the way, the truth and the life, the true vine).
  • anastasis, resurrection: Literally “standing up again”. Martha believes in a general resurrection on the last day, a hope held by many Jews of her time (Daniel 12:2). Jesus gives that hope a face: the resurrection is a person.
  • pisteuō, I believe: The verb of John’s Gospel, often followed by “into” or “in” someone. Faith is trust in a person, here in Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” (11:21)

A cry of grief, and also of faith: she knows that his presence would have made a difference. Her sister says the same words in verse 32. Honest grief is not lack of faith.

“I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.” (11:24)

Martha answers with the faith she was taught. It is true, but it is far away and cold. Jesus takes her from “the last day” to a person standing in front of her.

“I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live,” (11:25)

Resurrection is not only an event at the end of time; it is Jesus himself. Whoever is joined to him by faith lives with the life that death cannot end.

“Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” (11:27)

Martha’s confession is among the fullest in the Gospels, with that of Peter (Matthew 16:16). It names Jesus as Messiah, Son of God and the one who was to come, and she says it with a dead brother in the tomb.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus links faith in the resurrection to his own person: “I am the Resurrection and the life” (CCC 994). The raising of Lazarus is a sign that announces his own Resurrection, and the Church uses these words at the funeral of her children. Martha’s confession is the faith of the Church. The Church honours Martha, Mary and Lazarus together as friends of Jesus and as a family that shows faith, service and listening to his word, as the other Gospel suggests (Luke 10:38–42). Contemplation and service belong together; neither is complete without love.

Questions people ask

Is Mary of Bethany the same person as Mary Magdalene?

No. Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus, is a different person from Mary Magdalene, who is named separately in the Gospels (Luke 8:2; John 19:25; 20:1). Old traditions in the West combined several women, but the Church has not taught that they are one, and the liturgy now keeps them apart: Mary Magdalene was celebrated on 22 July, and today the family of Bethany is honoured.

For the Latin reader

Until 2021 the Roman calendar kept only St Martha on 29 July. By decree of the Congregation for Divine Worship, issued at the wish of Pope Francis, her sister Mary and her brother Lazarus were added, so that today it is the memorial of all three. The Gospel of Luke is also sometimes read on this day. The Church honours Martha as the patron of those who serve, in homes, hospitals and kitchens, and the sisters together as a sign that service and prayer are not rivals.

For study and teaching

  • Martha spoke her grief to Jesus without hiding it. What do I need to say honestly to him today, even if it sounds like a complaint?
  • Jesus did not argue with Martha; he brought her from “the last day” to himself. What do I believe about the resurrection, and how does it change the way I face death?
