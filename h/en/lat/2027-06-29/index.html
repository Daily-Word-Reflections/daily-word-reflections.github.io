Word for the day

“He said to them, “But who do you say that I am?” Simon Peter answered, “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.”” Matthew 16:15–16

Reflection

Today the Church honours together the two apostles on whom, under Christ, she was built: Peter the fisherman of Galilee, to whom Jesus gave the keys, and Paul the scholar of Tarsus, who carried the Gospel to the nations. They were very different men, and they once disagreed openly at Antioch (Galatians 2:11). But they confessed the same Lord, served the same Church, and according to the ancient tradition of the Church of Rome both gave their lives for Christ in that city under the emperor Nero, in the mid-60s of the first century: Peter crucified, tradition says head downwards, near the Vatican Hill, and Paul beheaded outside the walls on the road to Ostia. Their tombs have been venerated there ever since, and over them stand St Peter's Basilica and the Basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls. That is why they share one feast. The first reading shows Peter in chains. King Herod Agrippa I, wanting to win favour, has already “had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword,” the first of the Twelve to die for Christ, and now he arrests Peter during the Passover festival, guarded by “four squads of soldiers.” The future looks clear: Peter will be next. But Luke tells us what the Church was doing: “While Peter was kept in prison, the church prayed fervently to God for him.” And on the very night before his trial, Peter is “sleeping between two soldiers,” bound with two chains. He sleeps, because he has put his life in the Lord's hands. Then the cell fills with light; an angel taps him: “Get up quickly.” The chains fall off, the iron gate opens “of its own accord,” and Peter, who thought he was dreaming, finds himself in the street and says: “Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me.” Peter's deliverance is also a picture of Easter: the night, the command to rise, the gate that opens. The Psalm is Peter's song: “I sought the LORD, and he answered me, and delivered me from all my fears,” and “The angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them.”

The second reading is Paul's farewell. Writing from prison in Rome to his beloved Timothy, he knows that the end has come: “As for me, I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come.” A libation was wine poured out on the altar over a sacrifice; Paul sees his coming death as an offering poured out for Christ. Then he looks back over his life with three images, from the army, the stadium and the treasury: “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” He does not boast; he gives thanks, and he waits for “the crown of righteousness” that the Lord will give “not only to me but also to all who have longed for his appearing.” He knows what it is to be abandoned by friends, but adds: “the Lord stood by me and gave me strength, so that through me the message might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear it.” Peter was rescued from prison; Paul says, “I was rescued from the lion's mouth,” and is sure that the Lord will rescue him “from every evil attack and save me for his heavenly kingdom.” For both apostles the final rescue was not escape from death but passage through death into the kingdom. Paul's last word is not about himself: “To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen.”

The Gospel takes us north to Caesarea Philippi, a city built by Herod's son Philip near a great rock cliff with a shrine to the pagan god Pan. There, far from Jerusalem, among the gods of the nations, Jesus asks two questions. The first is easy: “Who do people say that the Son of Man is?” The disciples report the opinions: John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah, a prophet. The second cannot be answered by reporting what others say: “But who do you say that I am?” Simon speaks for all: “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” Jesus calls him blessed, and tells him that this confession is not human cleverness: “flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven.” Then Jesus gives him a new name and a mission. In Aramaic the word play is exact: “you are Kepha, and on this kepha I will build my church”: you are Rock, and on this rock. A rock is what does not move when the storm comes. Jesus promises that “the gates of Hades,” the power of death, “will not prevail against” his Church. And he gives Peter “the keys of the kingdom of heaven.” Every Jew knew the words of Isaiah about the chief steward of the king's house: “I will place on his shoulder the key of the house of David; he shall open, and no one shall shut” (Isaiah 22:22). The steward governs the household in the king's name. To “bind” and “loose” means to forbid and permit, to exclude and to reconcile, with authority that heaven confirms. The Catholic Church believes that this ministry of Peter did not end with Peter, because the Church still needs a rock and a steward until the Lord returns. The Bishop of Rome, the successor of Peter, is “the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity both of the bishops and of the whole company of the faithful” (Lumen Gentium 23; Catechism 882). And Peter's weakness did not cancel his mission: the same man would deny Jesus three times, and be asked three times by the risen Lord, “Do you love me?” and told, “Feed my sheep” (John 21:15–17). The Church is founded not on Peter's strength, but on Christ's prayer for him: “I have prayed for you that your own faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). Today we thank God for Peter and Paul, for the faith we received through them, and for Pope Leo XIV, who sits today in Peter's chair.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today the Church celebrates two great men together, Peter and Paul. They were very different. Peter was a fisherman from Galilee, with rough hands and a quick tongue. Paul was a learned man from Tarsus, a Pharisee who once persecuted the Church. They even argued with each other. But Christ made them the two pillars of his Church, and both gave their lives for him in Rome. Over their tombs stand today two great basilicas, and from every nation pilgrims come to pray there.

In the Gospel Jesus asks his disciples: who do people say that the Son of Man is? That is an easy question. We can all report what others say about Jesus: a good teacher, a prophet, a great man. Then comes the hard question: but who do you say that I am? (Matthew 16:15). Nobody can answer that for us, not our parents, not the priest. Peter answers: you are the Messiah, the Son of the living God. And Jesus says: flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven. Faith is a gift. Let us ask for it today.

Then Jesus says to Peter: you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 16:18–19). A rock that holds firm, keys that open and close. Jesus knew that his Church would need a visible shepherd to keep it together. That ministry did not end with Peter. Today it is carried by Pope Leo XIV. We do not follow the Pope because he is clever or because we like him; we follow him because Christ built his Church on Peter and his successors. And we pray for him, because his burden is heavy.

Do not forget: this rock was a weak man. Peter sank in the water. He denied Jesus three times. But the Lord prayed for him that his faith might not fail, and after the Resurrection asked him three times: do you love me? Feed my sheep. The Church is not built on Peter's strength but on Christ's mercy to Peter. That gives hope to all of us who are weak.

In the first reading Peter is in prison, chained between two soldiers, waiting for death. And what does the Church do? While Peter was kept in prison, the church prayed fervently to God for him (Acts 12:5). And the chains fell off. Dear friends, there are Christians in prison today, in many countries, also in our own. There are priests and bishops under pressure. Do we pray for them fervently? Peter was rescued by the prayer of the Church.

And Paul, at the end of his life, writes from prison: I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith (2 Timothy 4:7). Could we say that at the end of our lives? Let us ask Peter to give us his faith, and Paul to give us his courage. And let us leave this church today with Peter's answer in our hearts and on our lips: you are the Messiah, the Son of the living God. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul the Church honours the two apostles who gave their lives in Rome and whom she calls her pillars. The first reading tells how the Lord rescued Peter from Herod’s prison while the church prayed for him. In the second, Paul, near the end, says, “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith,” and trusts that the Lord will rescue him for his heavenly kingdom. In the Gospel Peter confesses Jesus as the Messiah, the Son of the living God, and Jesus answers, “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church.”

Psalm 34 joins the readings: “The angel of the Lord encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them.” Both apostles were rescued many times, and in the end both were delivered through martyrdom into the kingdom. The Vigil Mass has its own readings (Acts 3:1–10; Galatians 1:11–20; John 21:15–19).

Acts 12:1–11 · The Lord has sent his angel and rescued me

Setting.

King Herod here is Herod Agrippa I, grandson of Herod the Great, who ruled Judea from about 41 to 44 AD. He killed James, the brother of John, the first of the Twelve to be martyred, and then arrested Peter at the time of the Passover. Luke tells the story so that it recalls the first Passover: a night of deliverance, an angel of the Lord, a command to rise quickly with belt and sandals.

Luke writes that Herod saw that the killing of James “pleased the Jews”. In Acts this refers to the leaders and those in Jerusalem who at that moment opposed the apostles, not to the Jewish people as a whole. Peter, James and the whole Jerusalem church were themselves Jews, and Luke records that many thousands of Jews believed (Acts 21:20).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: Herod kills James and imprisons Peter during the feast of Unleavened Bread.
  • v. 5: Peter is kept in prison; the church prays fervently for him.
  • vv. 6–7: the night before his trial, an angel wakes Peter; the chains fall off.
  • vv. 8–10: belt, sandals, cloak; through the guards and the iron gate.
  • v. 11: Peter comes to himself: “Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me.”

Key words.

  • ektenōs, fervently: Stretched out, earnestly, without stopping. A related word describes Jesus’ prayer in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44). The whole church is at prayer while Peter sleeps.
  • angelos kyriou, angel of the Lord: The heavenly messenger who acts for God. The angel of the Lord also opened the prison doors for the apostles earlier (5:19).
  • exeilato, rescued: Delivered, snatched out. The same verb is used by Stephen of God rescuing Joseph and Israel in Egypt (7:10, 34). Peter’s night is a small exodus.

Verse by verse.

“He had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword.” (12:2)

The first apostle to die for Christ. Jesus had told James and John that they would drink his cup (Mark 10:39).

“While Peter was kept in prison, the church prayed fervently to God for him.” (12:5)

Two powers face each other: the prison of the king and the prayer of the church. Luke shows which one is stronger.

“Peter, bound with two chains, was sleeping between two soldiers,” (12:6)

He sleeps on the night before his trial. Peter, who once slept in Gethsemane from weakness, now sleeps in peace, trusting God.

“Fasten your belt and put on your sandals.” (12:8)

The words recall the Passover command: “your loins girded, your sandals on your feet” (Exodus 12:11). It happens during the same feast.

“Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me” (12:11)

Only when he stands in the street does Peter understand. Faith often recognises God’s action afterwards.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this reading the power of prayer for the Pope and the bishops. The Catechism teaches that the Roman Pontiff, as Peter’s successor, is the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity both of the bishops and of the whole company of the faithful (CCC 882, quoting Lumen Gentium 23). In every Mass the Church names the Pope and the bishop of the diocese in the Eucharistic Prayer, praying for them as the Jerusalem church prayed for Peter. The angel’s rescue does not mean that Peter will never suffer. He will be rescued many times, and in the end will give his life, as Jesus foretold (John 21:18–19).

2 Timothy 4:6–8, 17–18 · I have kept the faith

Setting.

The Second Letter to Timothy presents Paul as a prisoner in Rome, near the end of his life, writing to his beloved disciple. It is one of the three Pastoral Letters. Many scholars think these letters were written by a disciple of Paul in his name after his death; others hold that Paul wrote them himself. The Church receives them as inspired Scripture, the voice of Paul to the Church.

Today’s verses are Paul’s farewell. He looks back on his life, and forward to the judgment, and remembers how the Lord stood by him when everyone else deserted him at his first defence (4:16).

Structure.

  • v. 6: I am already being poured out as a libation; the time of my departure has come.
  • v. 7: the good fight, the race, the faith kept.
  • v. 8: the crown of righteousness, for me and for all who have longed for his appearing.
  • vv. 17–18: the Lord stood by me and gave me strength; he will rescue me for his heavenly kingdom. Glory to him.

Key words.

  • spendomai, to be poured out as a libation: In ancient sacrifices wine or oil was poured out on the altar. Paul sees his coming death as an offering poured out to God (compare Philippians 2:17).
  • analysis, departure: A word used for loosing a ship from its moorings or striking a tent. Death is a setting out, not an end.
  • stephanos, crown: The wreath given to the winner in the games. Paul has finished the race; the Lord, the just judge, will give the crown.

Verse by verse.

“As for me, I am already being poured out as a libation,” (4:6)

Paul’s life has been given to God day by day. His martyrdom will be the last drop of the offering.

“I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” (4:7)

Three pictures: the athlete in the contest, the runner at the finishing line, and the steward who has kept safe what was entrusted to him. It is not pride, but thanksgiving for grace.

“and not only to me but also to all who have longed for his appearing.” (4:8)

The crown is not only for apostles. It is promised to every believer who loves the Lord’s coming.

“But the Lord stood by me and gave me strength,” (4:17)

When others left him, the Lord did not. Paul’s whole mission depended on that strength, so that “all the Gentiles might hear”.

“To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen.” (4:18)

Paul ends with praise. Even in chains, the last word belongs to God.

How the Church reads it.

From the earliest times the Church has honoured Peter and Paul together. St Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth about the year 96, recalled the two great apostles who suffered in his own day; St Irenaeus spoke of the Church of Rome as founded and organised by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul (Against Heresies 3.3.2). The Catechism calls martyrdom the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473). In today’s Preface the Church praises God that Peter was foremost in confessing the faith and Paul its outstanding preacher, and that both, by different ways, gathered together the one family of Christ.

Matthew 16:13–19 · You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church

Setting.

Caesarea Philippi lay at the foot of Mount Hermon, near the sources of the Jordan, in the territory of Herod’s son Philip. It was a place of pagan shrines, built against a great rock face. There, far from Jerusalem, Jesus asks his disciples who he is.

Mark and Luke tell Peter’s confession too, but only Matthew gives Jesus’ answer to Peter with the blessing, the new name, the promise of the Church and the keys.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: “Who do people say that the Son of Man is?” John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah, a prophet.
  • vv. 15–16: “But who do you say that I am?” Peter: “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.”
  • v. 17: Blessed are you; my Father revealed this to you.
  • v. 18: you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church; the gates of Hades will not prevail.
  • v. 19: the keys of the kingdom; binding and loosing.

Key words.

  • Petros / petra, Peter / rock: In Greek the name Peter (Petros) and the word rock (petra) are nearly the same. In Aramaic, which Jesus spoke, the word is the same, Kēpāʾ; that is why Paul often calls him Cephas (1 Corinthians 15:5; Galatians 2:9).
  • ekklēsia, church: The assembly called together. In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew word for the assembly of Israel. Jesus speaks of “my church”, the people he gathers.
  • kleis, keys: Isaiah 22:22 tells how the key of the house of David was given to Eliakim, the steward of the king’s palace: “he shall open, and no one shall shut.” The keys mean authority over the house in the name of the king.

Verse by verse.

“But who do you say that I am?” (16:15)

The question is addressed to every generation. Opinions about Jesus are not enough; he asks for a personal answer.

“You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” (16:16)

Peter speaks for all the Twelve. The confession joins the hope of Israel, the Messiah, with the deepest truth of Jesus, the Son of God.

“For flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven.” (16:17)

Faith is a gift from the Father, not a human discovery. The Catechism uses this verse to teach that faith is a grace (CCC 153).

“you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it.” (16:18)

Simon receives a new name that is a mission. The Church is Christ’s (“my church”), built by him on Peter. The powers of death will not overcome it.

“whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven.” (16:19)

In Jewish teaching, to bind and loose meant to forbid and permit, and also to exclude from the community and to receive back. What Peter does in Christ’s name, heaven confirms. The same power is later given to the apostles together (18:18).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Peter’s confession of Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God, was the Father’s gift, and that on the rock of this faith confessed by St Peter Christ built his Church (CCC 424, 442). It teaches that Jesus entrusted a specific authority to Peter: the power of the keys designates authority to govern the house of God, which is the Church (CCC 553), and that the Lord made Simon alone the rock of his Church, while the office of binding and loosing was also given to the college of apostles united to its head (CCC 881). The Second Vatican Council teaches the same in Lumen Gentium 18 and 22. Peter’s authority comes from Christ, and it is a service of unity.

Questions people ask

Is the rock Peter himself or Peter’s faith?

Both, and the two are not opposed. Some Fathers emphasised Peter’s faith, others Peter himself. The Catechism says Christ built his Church “on the rock of this faith confessed by St Peter” (CCC 424), and also that the Lord “made Simon alone… the rock of his Church” (CCC 881). The rock is Peter the believer, confessing the faith given by the Father, and his successors who keep that faith for the whole Church.

Why are Peter and Paul celebrated on the same day?

Because tradition holds that both gave their lives in Rome under Nero, Peter by crucifixion and Paul by the sword, and the Church of Rome has kept their memory together since ancient times. A Roman calendar of the fourth century already lists 29 June. The two were very different, the fisherman from Galilee and the learned Pharisee from Tarsus, and they even disagreed openly once (Galatians 2:11–14), but they were united in Christ and in martyrdom.

What does this feast mean for Christians who do not accept the Pope’s authority?

Orthodox and many other Christians also honour Peter and Paul and keep this feast. The Catholic Church holds that Christ gave Peter and his successors a ministry of unity. St John Paul II, aware that the papacy has also been a difficulty for other Christians, invited them to help find a way of exercising this ministry that all could recognise (Ut Unum Sint 95). On this feast Catholics pray for the Pope and for the unity of all who believe in Christ.

For the Latin reader

In the Latin Church today is a solemnity. In Rome the Holy Father blesses the pallia, the woollen bands worn by metropolitan archbishops as a sign of their communion with the Successor of Peter, for the archbishops appointed in the past year. Peter’s Pence, the offering of the faithful for the Pope’s works of charity, is traditionally linked with this day. Tomorrow the Church remembers the first martyrs of Rome, who died under Nero in the same persecution. The bones honoured as St Peter’s lie beneath the high altar of St Peter’s Basilica, and St Paul’s tomb is venerated in the basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls.

For study and teaching

  • The church prayed fervently for Peter in prison. Do I pray regularly for the Pope and my bishop? How could my family make this a habit?
  • Paul says, “I have kept the faith.” What have I received in faith that I must hand on to my children or to others?
  • Peter and Paul were very different men. What can our parish learn from them about unity among people who are not alike?
