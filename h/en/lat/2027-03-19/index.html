Word for the day

“When Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took her as his wife” Matthew 1:24

Reflection

In the middle of the last fortnight of Lent the Church puts on white for the man to whom God entrusted his greatest treasures. St Joseph never speaks a single word in the Gospels, yet everything depends on his listening. The first reading takes us back a thousand years before him. King David wants to build a house for God, and God answers through the prophet Nathan that he will build a house for David instead: “I will raise up your offspring after you… and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be a father to him, and he shall be a son to me… Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me.” By the time of Jesus the royal line of David had fallen into obscurity; its heir was a carpenter in Nazareth. Matthew's genealogy ends with “Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah.” Notice the care: Joseph is not called the father who begot Jesus, but the husband of Mary. Yet through Joseph's legal fatherhood, through his giving the child a name, Jesus is grafted into the house of David. The angel greets him as “Joseph, son of David.” God's promise to David passes through the hands of this silent, faithful man.

Matthew tells us what Joseph faced. Mary, already betrothed to him, “was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit.” Joseph, “being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, planned to dismiss her quietly.” His righteousness is not cold law; it is mercy and reverence before a mystery he does not yet understand. Then the angel speaks in a dream: “do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit. She will bear a son, and you are to name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.” And Joseph's whole answer is action: “When Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took her as his wife.” St Paul's words about Abraham in the second reading fit Joseph perfectly: “Hoping against hope, he believed,” and his faith “was reckoned to him as righteousness.” Where the alternative Gospel is read, Luke shows Joseph and Mary searching three days for the twelve-year-old Jesus and finding him in the Temple. Mary says, “your father and I have been searching for you in great anxiety,” and Jesus answers, “Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?” Joseph accepts that the boy he raises belongs first to another Father, and still Jesus “went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them.” The Son of God learned to work, to pray the psalms and to keep the law at Joseph's side.

The Church has slowly come to see how great this hidden man is. Blessed Pius IX declared St Joseph Patron of the Universal Church in 1870; Leo XIII wrote about him in Quamquam Pluries (1889); St John Paul II called him the Guardian of the Redeemer (Redemptoris Custos, 1989); and Pope Francis, in Patris Corde (2020), described him as a beloved, tender, obedient, accepting, creatively courageous and working father, a father in the shadow. Joseph is patron of families, of workers, of fathers, of those who must make hard decisions in the night, and of a happy death, since he died, as tradition holds, in the arms of Jesus and Mary. In many parishes in Kerala this day brings a festive meal for the poor and for all who come, remembering the man who fed the Holy Family with the work of his hands. Even in Lent, today is a day of joy. We ask St Joseph to guard the Church as he guarded the child and his mother, and to teach us his way: to listen, to rise, and to do what God asks without many words.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, in the middle of Lent we put on white to honour St Joseph. In the whole Gospel he does not say a single word. Yet God entrusted to him his two greatest treasures: his only Son and the Virgin Mary.

God promised David: I will raise up your offspring after you… and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever (2 Samuel 7:12–13). A thousand years later the heir of David is a carpenter in Nazareth. Through Joseph, who gives the child his name, Jesus becomes son of David. God keeps his promises through humble people.

Joseph's world is turned upside down: Mary is with child. Being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, he planned to dismiss her quietly (Matthew 1:19). His goodness is full of mercy. But God has a bigger plan, and an angel tells him in a dream: Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife (Matthew 1:20).

Then come the most beautiful words about him: when Joseph awoke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him (Matthew 1:24). No argument, no delay. Like Abraham, hoping against hope, he believed (Romans 4:18). Joseph teaches us that faith is not many words, but quiet obedience.

At Nazareth Jesus went down with Mary and Joseph and was obedient to them. The Son of God learned to work with wood, to pray and to keep the law at Joseph's side. Every father, every worker, every person who cares for others in silence can look to Joseph and find his own vocation honoured.

Let us entrust ourselves today to St Joseph: our families, our work, our Church and our last hour. May he teach us to listen to God in the night, to rise, and to do what God asks. St Joseph, pray for us. Amen.

Take-home resolution: This week I will entrust my family, my work and my last hour to St Joseph.

Dear brothers and sisters, long ago in Egypt there was a great famine. When the people cried to Pharaoh for bread, he said: go to Joseph; what he says to you, do (Genesis 41:55). That Joseph had stored grain and saved a whole nation. The Church has always seen in him a figure of our St Joseph, who guarded the true Bread from heaven, Jesus.

Joseph of Nazareth was not only obedient; he was a protector. When Herod sought to kill the child, an angel said to him: get up, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt, and remain there until I tell you (Matthew 2:13). Then Joseph got up, took the child and his mother by night, and went to Egypt (Matthew 2:14). He became a refugee to keep Jesus alive. Pope Francis spoke of Joseph's creative courage: in a foreign land, with no work and no home, he found a way.

In 1870 Blessed Pius IX declared St Joseph Patron of the Universal Church. The reason is simple. Christ is the head of the body, the church (Colossians 1:18). The man who guarded the body of Jesus at Nazareth now guards his body, the Church, throughout the world. Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain (Psalm 127:1). Joseph built a house where God was at home.

Tradition also calls him the patron of a holy death, because he died, we believe, in the arms of Jesus and Mary. What better way could anyone die? So Christians have always prayed to him for their last hour, that they too may die with Jesus and Mary beside them.

So let us go to Joseph. Let us bring him our families, our work, our worries about money and housing, and our last hour. He who protected Jesus in the night will protect us. And he will lead us, as he led the Child, to the altar of God at every Mass. St Joseph, pray for us. Amen.

Dear friends, the alternative Gospel for today takes us to Jerusalem. Every year Joseph and Mary went to Jerusalem for the festival of the Passover (Luke 2:41). The law asked men to go up to the Lord three times a year (Deuteronomy 16:16). Joseph did not leave religion to his wife. He led his household in worship. A father who prays and goes to church teaches more than a hundred sermons.

Then the terrible moment. On the way home they discover that Jesus is not with them. Assuming that he was in the group of travelers, they went a day's journey (Luke 2:44). Then three days of searching through crowded streets. Every parent here knows that fear. When they find him in the temple, Mary says: Child, why have you treated us like this? Look, your father and I have been searching for you in great anxiety (Luke 2:48).

Notice: your father and I. Mary speaks of Joseph as the father of Jesus. He was not his father by blood, but he was truly his father by love, by law and by care. And he suffered as a father suffers. The bride in the Song of Songs says: I will seek him whom my soul loves (Song of Songs 3:2). Joseph and Mary sought Jesus with that love.

Jesus answers: did you not know that I must be in my Father's house? (Luke 2:49). They did not understand. Joseph had to accept that this child belonged first to God. And yet: then he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them (Luke 2:51). The Son of God obeyed Joseph. Joseph kept his place with humility, neither possessive nor absent.

Today let us pray for all fathers, and for parents who are searching for children who have left home or left the faith. Do not give up. Joseph searched three days and found Jesus in the house of God. Let us bring our children to the Mass, and let us ask St Joseph to help every father to lead, to protect and to love. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church sets aside the purple of Lent for white and keeps the solemnity of St Joseph, the husband of Mary. All the readings follow one promise on its way. God tells David through Nathan that an heir of his will have a throne forever. St Paul recalls Abraham, who believed a promise when all appearances were against it. Matthew shows both promises reaching their goal through a man of David’s line who says nothing and does what he is told. Today’s psalm sings of the same promise to David.

The Gospel may be either Matthew or Luke 2:41–51a; a note on Luke is among the questions below. The key words are house, hoping against hope and righteous.

2 Samuel 7:4–5a, 12–14a, 16 · I will establish his kingdom

Setting.

David is king in Jerusalem and has brought the ark there (2 Samuel 6). Settled in his house of cedar, he wants to build a house for God, since “the ark of God stays in a tent” (7:2). The prophet Nathan approves at first, but that night the word of the Lord comes to him.

This oracle is the foundation of Israel’s hope for a son of David. The word “covenant” does not appear in this chapter, but Psalm 89, today’s psalm, uses it: “I have made a covenant with my chosen one” (89:3).

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5a: God’s word comes to Nathan: are you the one to build me a house?
  • vv. 12–13: David’s heir: his kingdom and his throne.
  • v. 14a: father and son.
  • v. 16: David’s house, kingdom and throne, forever.
  • ¶ The Lectionary omits vv. 5b–11, where God recalls what he has done for David, and vv. 14b–15, where he promises that even if the heir sins, his steadfast love will not depart.

Key words.

  • bayit, house: The whole chapter plays on this one word. It can mean a palace (David’s), a temple (the one he plans) and a dynasty (what God promises). David offers God a house; God says “the Lord will make you a house” (7:11).
  • zera‘, offspring: Literally “seed”. It first points to Solomon, who will build the Temple (1 Kings 6), and it opens out toward a son beyond him.
  • ne’man, made sure: From the same root as our word Amen: firm, reliable. The future of David’s house rests on God’s fidelity, not on the conduct of its kings.

Verse by verse.

“Are you the one to build me a house to live in?” (7:5)

David’s idea is generous, but God will not be housed by a king’s plan. The first move in this story, as in every story of grace, is God’s.

“I will raise up your offspring after you, who shall come forth from your body, and I will establish his kingdom.” (7:12)

The promise looks beyond David’s death. He will not live to see what is promised, a thing he shares with Abraham.

“I will be a father to him, and he shall be a son to me.” (7:14)

A king of Israel was called God’s son by adoption. Hebrews 1:5 applies this verse to Christ, in whom it is true in a way no king of Judah could have dreamed.

“Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me; your throne shall be established forever.” (7:16)

Solomon’s kingdom split, and the last king of Judah went into exile. Israel went on hoping because this word still stood. Psalm 89 itself shows the pain of that waiting.

How the Church reads it.

The angel says to Mary: “the Lord God will give to him the throne of his ancestor David” (Luke 1:32). The Catechism says that God called Joseph to take Mary as his wife so that Jesus should be born into the messianic lineage of David (CCC 437), and that many recognized in Jesus the promised son of David (CCC 439). Joseph holds no throne. He is a workman in a small town, and through him the line is handed on. Jesus also speaks of the true house of God, “the temple of his body” (John 2:21).

Romans 4:13, 16–18, 22 · Hoping against hope

Setting.

St Paul writes to the Christians of Rome, probably around AD 57, before going to Jerusalem and hoping to reach them. In chapters 1–4 he argues that Jews and Gentiles alike are made right with God by grace through faith in Christ. Abraham is his great witness, since Genesis says he believed and God “reckoned it to him as righteousness” (Genesis 15:6), before circumcision and long before the law given at Sinai.

Paul does not despise the law: “we uphold the law” (3:31). His point is about where the promise comes from.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the promise came through the righteousness of faith.
  • vv. 16–17: it rests on grace, for all Abraham’s descendants, in the presence of the God who gives life.
  • v. 18: hoping against hope.
  • v. 22: reckoned as righteousness.

Key words.

  • epangelia, promise: What God binds himself to do. A promise can only be received and trusted. This is Paul’s key word in the chapter.
  • par’ elpida ep’ elpidi, hoping against hope: Literally “beyond hope, in hope”. All human reasons for hoping have gone, and Abraham hopes because of God.
  • logizomai, to reckon: A word from accounting: to enter to someone’s account. Paul is quoting Genesis 15:6. For the Catholic understanding, God’s reckoning is not merely a legal entry: justification truly makes us just and renews us within (CCC 1987–1989).

Verse by verse.

“For the promise that he would inherit the world did not come to Abraham or to his descendants through the law but through the righteousness of faith.” (4:13)

Genesis promised Abraham a land and many nations. Paul widens it to “the world”: all peoples are to share in the blessing.

“in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his descendants,” (4:16)

If it rested on our record, it would never be secure. Because it rests on grace, it holds.

“who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist.” (4:17)

The God of Abraham’s faith is the Creator and the God of resurrection. This is what makes hope possible when things look finished.

“Hoping against hope, he believed that he would become “the father of many nations,”” (4:18)

The verse fits Joseph as well. In the dark, with no explanation that anyone could check, he trusted what God said.

“Therefore his faith “was reckoned to him as righteousness.”” (4:22)

The Lectionary ends here, and Matthew’s Gospel then calls Joseph righteous (Matthew 1:19). The word links the two.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Abraham shows what faith is (CCC 146), and that Christian hope has its origin and model in his (CCC 1819). St John Paul II wrote in Redemptoris Custos that Joseph was the first to share in the faith of the Mother of God (5). Faith, as Paul says elsewhere, works “through love” (Galatians 5:6), and by grace our deeds truly bear fruit (CCC 2008–2010). Joseph’s faith, like Abraham’s, lives in what he does.

Matthew 1:16, 18–21, 24a (or Luke 2:41–51a) · He did as the angel of the Lord commanded him

Setting.

Matthew’s Gospel opens with the genealogy of Jesus, “the son of David, the son of Abraham” (1:1), and each line runs “the father of”. At v. 16 the pattern breaks: Joseph is “the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born”. In Greek “of whom” is feminine. So the genealogy leaves a question: how does Jesus belong to David’s line if Joseph is not his natural father? Vv. 18–25 give the answer.

Background. Betrothal in that time was a binding bond. The couple were already called husband and wife, and it could be ended only by a formal divorce, but the woman often stayed in her family home for a time before moving to her husband’s house. It is in this interval that Mary is found to be with child.

Structure.

  • v. 16: the end of the genealogy.
  • v. 18: Mary is with child “from the Holy Spirit”.
  • v. 19: Joseph’s decision.
  • vv. 20–21: the angel in a dream: do not be afraid; name him.
  • v. 24a: he wakes and obeys.
  • ¶ This is the first of four dreams in which God directs Joseph (1:20; 2:13; 2:19; 2:22). The Gospels do not record a single word he said.

Key words.

  • dikaios, righteous: In Matthew, one who lives by God’s will as the law teaches it. His righteousness shows here in his care both for God’s law and for Mary’s name.
  • apolyō, to dismiss: The usual word for ending a marriage or a betrothal by divorce. “Quietly” probably means with the fewest possible witnesses, without a public charge.
  • Iēsous, Jesus: The Greek form of the Hebrew Yeshua, from the name Yehoshua, “the Lord saves” (CCC 430). The father gave the name. By naming the child, Joseph takes him legally as his son, and so Jesus becomes, in law, a son of David.

Verse by verse.

“and Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah.” (1:16)

Matthew chooses his words with care: Jesus is born of Mary, and Joseph’s place is that of her husband.

“Her husband Joseph, being a righteous man and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, planned to dismiss her quietly.” (1:19)

The Fathers read this in more than one way. St Jerome says that Joseph, knowing Mary’s purity, kept silent about a mystery he did not understand (in substance). St Bernard says that in reverence he felt unworthy to stay near so great a mystery (in substance). Others think he believed that she had sinned and yet would not shame her. On every reading he is merciful.

“Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to take Mary as your wife, for the child conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.” (1:20)

Elsewhere in Matthew “son of David” is said of Jesus alone. The title reminds Joseph who he is and why he is needed. The Catechism says that the angel’s words teach the virginal conception, a divine work beyond all human understanding (CCC 497).

“She will bear a son, and you are to name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.” (1:21)

Joseph is given a task, not an explanation of everything. The name says what the child will do.

“he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him;” (1:24)

This is Joseph’s whole answer: he gets up and does it. St John Paul II calls this the “obedience of faith” in Joseph (Redemptoris Custos 4, in substance).

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine defended the marriage of Mary and Joseph as a true marriage, and Joseph as truly a father to Jesus, though not by the flesh (in substance). Pius IX declared him patron of the universal Church in 1870, and Pope Francis marked the 150th anniversary with the letter Patris Corde (2020), which speaks of him as a father in the shadows, working, accepting and creatively courageous. The Catechism commends us to St Joseph as patron of a happy death (CCC 1014).

Questions people ask

Did Mary and Joseph have other children? Verse 25 says “until”.

The Greek word for “until” says what happened up to a point, not what happened afterward. The Church confesses Mary’s perpetual virginity (CCC 499). The “brothers” of Jesus in the Gospels are understood as close relatives; the word could have that wider sense in Semitic usage (CCC 500).

What does Joseph teach us if he never speaks?

That holiness does not depend on being heard. His whole witness is doing what God asks, at once and in the dark, for the sake of those in his care. Countless fathers and workers, whose lives no one will ever write about, can see themselves in him.

What of the other Gospel, Luke 2:41–51a?

There Jesus, aged twelve, stays behind in the Temple. Mary says, “your father and I have been searching for you in great anxiety” (2:48), and Jesus speaks of his Father’s house. Then “he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them” (2:51). So the Son of God learned as a boy to obey this man, in the home he provided (CCC 532, 534).

For the Latin reader

In the Roman calendar this is a solemnity: the Gloria and the Creed are said at Mass even in Lent. This year it falls on a Friday, and the Church’s law does not bind us to Friday abstinence when a solemnity falls on that day (Code of Canon Law, canon 1251). St Joseph’s name has stood in the Roman Canon since St John XXIII added it in 1962, and it was added to the other Eucharistic Prayers in 2013. Many Catholics keep March as St Joseph’s month. Two days from now Holy Week begins, and we can ask him, who protected the child from those who sought his life, to stay with us as we walk beside the Son at the Passion.

For study and teaching

  • David wanted to do something great for God, and God said he would do it himself. What am I trying to build by myself that I should let God build?
  • Joseph acted without having the full picture. What is the next plain thing God is asking of me, even if I do not see where it leads?
  • Paul says the promise rests on grace. Where do I still act as if it rests on my record?
  • Who are the people in my care whom I protect, as Joseph did, without being noticed? How can I honor those who do this for me?
