Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, whoever receives one whom I send receives me; and whoever receives me receives him who sent me.” John 13:20

Reflection

Paul and his companions sail from Cyprus to the mainland of Asia Minor, and Luke slips in a small, painful detail: “John, however, left them and returned to Jerusalem.” Young John Mark turned back. We are not told why: fear of the hard road over the Taurus mountains, homesickness, or disagreement. Later this desertion would cause a sharp quarrel between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39). But the story does not end there. Years afterwards Paul would write from prison: “Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful in my ministry” (2 Timothy 4:11). And the Church remembers this same Mark as the writer of the Gospel that bears his name. The one who turned back was given a second chance, and he became a pillar. God does not throw away those who fail.

At Antioch in Pisidia, high on the plateau, Paul and Barnabas go to the synagogue on the sabbath. After the reading of the Law and the Prophets the officials invite them to speak: “Brothers, if you have any word of exhortation for the people, give it.” Paul stands up and begins with deep respect: “You Israelites, and others who fear God, listen.” His sermon is a loving retelling of Israel's history: God “chose our ancestors,” brought them out of Egypt “with uplifted arm,” “put up with them in the wilderness” for forty years, gave them the land, the judges, and the kings. Then David, “a man after my heart, who will carry out all my wishes.” And from David's line, as God promised, comes the Savior, Jesus. Paul's first preaching is not a rejection of Israel's story but a proclamation of its fulfilment. The Psalm sings the same faithfulness: “I will sing of your steadfast love, O Lord, forever,” and recalls God's oath to David: “I have found my servant David; with my holy oil I have anointed him.”

Paul ends this part of his speech with John the Baptist, who said: “I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.” The greatest of the prophets knew he was only a servant. The Gospel takes us back to the Last Supper, just after Jesus has knelt and washed the feet of his disciples. He says: “Very truly, I tell you, servants are not greater than their master, nor are messengers greater than the one who sent them.” If the Lord washed feet, his apostles must do the same. “If you know these things, you are blessed if you do them.” Even the shadow of betrayal is present: Jesus quotes the Psalm, “The one who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me,” so that when it happens the disciples may not lose faith but believe that “I am he.” And then a promise that gives the humblest messenger great dignity: “whoever receives one whom I send receives me; and whoever receives me receives him who sent me.” The servant is small, but the One who sends him is God.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's first reading there is one small, sad sentence: John, however, left them and returned to Jerusalem (Acts 13:13). John Mark turned back from the mission. Perhaps he was afraid. Perhaps he was homesick. We do not know.

But we know the end of the story. Years later, Paul, who had been angry with him, wrote: get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful in my ministry (2 Timothy 4:11). And the Church remembers him as St Mark, the writer of a Gospel. God does not throw away those who fail. If you have turned back from something, he is calling you again.

Then Paul preaches in the synagogue. He begins with respect: you Israelites, and others who fear God, listen. He tells the story of God's faithfulness: Egypt, the desert, the land, the judges, King David. And from David's family, God has brought a Savior, Jesus, as he promised. Our God keeps his promises.

Paul remembers John the Baptist, who said: I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet (Acts 13:25). The greatest prophet knew he was a servant.

In the Gospel Jesus has just washed the feet of his disciples. He says: servants are not greater than their master (John 13:16). If the Lord kneels, we must kneel too. And he gives his messengers a great dignity: whoever receives one whom I send receives me.

Let us ask today for humble hearts, ready to serve, and for the grace never to give up on anyone, not even on ourselves. Alleluia. Amen.

For children

Children, John Mark got scared and went home in the middle of a journey with Paul. But later he came back, and he even wrote one of the four Gospels!

If we make a mistake, Jesus gives us another chance. Let us say: Jesus, help me to try again!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Servant and Master, you knelt to wash the feet of your disciples and you sent them out as the Father sent you. Make us humble servants, glad to do the lowly tasks of love. Thank you for your faithfulness through all generations, from Abraham and David to this very day. When we turn back like John Mark, call us again and make us useful in your service. Teach us to receive in your messengers your own presence, and in you the Father who sent you. You live and reign for ever and ever. Alleluia. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul’s first recorded sermon is preached in a synagogue, after the reading of the Law and the Prophets. He tells Israel’s story from Egypt to David and then names its fulfilment: “Of this man’s posterity God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised.” In the Gospel, just after the washing of the feet, Jesus tells the Twelve that whoever receives the one he sends receives him. Psalm 89 sings of God’s covenant with David, the thread that ties both readings together.

Acts 13:13–25 · A word of exhortation

Setting.

From Cyprus the missionaries sail to the south coast of Asia Minor and go inland to Antioch in Pisidia, a Roman colony, not to be confused with Antioch in Syria. Luke now calls Saul by his Roman name, Paul (13:9), and Paul takes the lead. John Mark goes back to Jerusalem; Luke gives no reason, but the departure will later divide Paul and Barnabas (15:37–39). The order of the synagogue service matches what we know from other sources: readings from the Law and the Prophets, then a word of teaching (compare Luke 4:16–21).

Structure.

  • vv. 13–15: the journey to Antioch in Pisidia; the invitation after the readings.
  • vv. 16–22: God’s deeds from the exodus to David.
  • vv. 23–25: from David to Jesus, with John the Baptist as witness.

Key words.

  • logos paraklēseōs, word of exhortation: The same phrase describes the Letter to the Hebrews (Hebrews 13:22). It seems to have been a name for a sermon after the readings, as the homily follows the readings at Mass.
  • phoboumenoi ton theon, those who fear God: Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel and attended the synagogue without becoming full converts, like Cornelius (10:2). Paul addresses them together with the Israelites (v. 16): the bridge by which the Gospel would reach the nations was already standing.
  • sōtēr, Savior: God has brought Israel “a Savior”, the title the angel gave at Bethlehem (Luke 2:11). Paul presents Jesus not as a break with Israel’s story but as its promised goal.

Verse by verse.

“I have found David, son of Jesse, to be a man after my heart, who will carry out all my wishes.” (13:22)

A weaving together of Scripture: Psalm 89:20 (“I have found my servant David”), 1 Samuel 13:14 (“a man after his own heart”) and Isaiah 44:28 (“he shall carry out all my purpose”). Paul preaches as a Jew formed by the Scriptures.

“Of this man’s posterity God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised;” (13:23)

The center of the sermon. God keeps a promise across a thousand years.

“I am not worthy to untie the thong of the sandals on his feet.” (13:25)

Untying sandals was a slave’s task. The last and greatest of the prophets places himself below even that service.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Paul’s sermon a model of how the Old Testament is read in the light of Christ: Israel’s history is not set aside but fulfilled. The Second Vatican Council, following St Augustine, says that the New Testament is hidden in the Old and the Old is made manifest in the New (Dei Verbum 16). At Mass the homily stands where Paul’s “word of exhortation” stood: after the readings, opening them for today.

John 13:16–20 · Whoever receives one whom I send

Setting.

Jesus has just washed the feet of his disciples (13:1–15) and told them to do the same for one another. These verses draw out the meaning: the servant is not above the master, and blessing lies not in knowing but in doing. In the same breath Jesus speaks of the betrayer, and yet he ends with the dignity of those he sends.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–17: servant and master; the blessing of doing.
  • v. 18: “The one who ate my bread”: Psalm 41.
  • v. 19: foretold so that they may believe “I am he”.
  • v. 20: receiving the one sent.

Key words.

  • apostolos, messenger, one who is sent: The word in v. 16 translated “messengers” is apostolos, the only place it occurs in John’s Gospel. The Twelve hear their name and their place at the same moment: sent ones, no greater than the one who sends them.
  • egō eimi, I am he: Literally “I am”. In Isaiah God says “I am he” to show that he alone foretells and fulfils (Isaiah 43:10). Jesus speaks so that his Passion, when it comes, will reveal who he is rather than disprove it.
  • makarioi, blessed: As in the Beatitudes. John has only two such beatitudes (13:17; 20:29). Here the blessing belongs not to knowing the example but to doing it.

Verse by verse.

“you are blessed if you do them.” (13:17)

Knowing about service is easy; the blessing is promised to those who wash feet.

“The one who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.” (13:18)

From Psalm 41:9, the prayer of a man betrayed by a trusted friend. To eat someone’s bread was to be bound in friendship; lifting the heel pictures a kick, or turning one’s back. The betrayal comes from within the circle of the table.

“whoever receives one whom I send receives me; and whoever receives me receives him who sent me.” (13:20)

A chain runs from the Father to the humblest messenger of the Gospel. Jewish teaching knew a similar principle about envoys, later written in the Mishnah: a man’s agent is like the man himself (Berakhot 5:5).

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in v. 20 a foundation of apostolic ministry: Christ sends the apostles as the Father sent him (20:21), and whoever receives them receives him (CCC 858). The dignity of the minister is not his own; it is Christ’s, and so it calls first for humility, since the servant is not greater than the master. The betrayer at the table reminds the Church that even those closest to Christ must keep watch over their hearts.

For study and teaching

  • Paul read Israel’s history as leading to Jesus. Can I tell the story of my own life in the same way, seeing where God kept his promises?
  • Whom has Christ sent into my life this week? How did I receive them?
