Word for the day

“Therefore it says, “Sleeper, awake! Rise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you.”” Ephesians 5:14

Reflection

In the middle of a page of practical advice, Paul suddenly quotes a few lines that sound like a song: “Sleeper, awake! Rise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you.” Many scholars think this was a hymn sung at Baptism in the first Christian communities. One can imagine the newly baptised coming up from the water while the community sang it over them. The words gather the whole of Easter into three short lines: we were asleep, we were dead, and Christ, the Sun risen from the tomb, has shone on us. Paul has just said that “everything exposed by the light becomes visible”. The light of Christ does not only warm us; it shows us the truth about ourselves, and what is brought into his light is healed. That is why the Church has always loved to call Baptism illumination. In the Easter season the paschal candle stands near the altar in some of our churches, and the newly baptised receive a lit candle: they are sleepers who have been woken and dead who have risen.

Then Paul draws the consequences for daily life. “Be careful then how you live, not as unwise people but as wise, making the most of the time, because the days are evil.” The Christian is awake, and an awake person does not waste the day. He warns against drunkenness, which dulls the mind and wrecks families, and offers another kind of fullness instead: “be filled with the Spirit, as you sing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs among yourselves, singing and making melody to the Lord in your hearts, giving thanks to God the Father at all times and for everything in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.” The Spirit-filled Church is a singing and thankful Church. Our Syro-Malabar tradition knows this well: the Qurbana is sung, the hours of prayer are sung, and the homes of our grandparents rang each evening with the family prayer and its songs. Paul ends with a word that opens the next section on family life: “Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.” Mutual service is the fruit of the Spirit; a home where each gives way to the other for Christ's sake is already full of his light.

The Gospel is Mark's account of the feeding of the five thousand. It is late, the place is deserted, and the disciples want to send the crowd away to buy food. Jesus answers: “You give them something to eat.” They protest that two hundred denarii would hardly be enough. “How many loaves have you? Go and see.” Five, and two fish. Then Mark describes what Jesus does with words every Christian recognises: “Taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven, and blessed and broke the loaves, and gave them to his disciples to set before the people.” Took, blessed, broke, gave: these are the very actions of the Last Supper and of every Qurbana. All ate and were filled, and twelve baskets of fragments were left over, one for each apostle, as if to say that the Church will never run out of this bread. Two lessons stand out for this Easter Friday. First, Jesus works through the disciples: he does not drop bread from the sky but asks them to bring what little they have and to hand it out. Second, he feeds the people with abundance: the Risen Lord still gives himself in the Eucharist to all who come, and he still says to his Church in the face of every hunger, of body and of soul, “You give them something to eat.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today Paul gives us a little hymn, and Mark gives us the feeding of the five thousand.

The hymn says: “Sleeper, awake! Rise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you.” The first Christians probably sang it at Baptism. We were asleep, we were dead, and the Risen Christ has shone on us. That is why Baptism is called illumination, and why we receive a lit candle.

Then Paul says: be careful how you live; make the most of the time; do not get drunk, but be filled with the Spirit, singing psalms and hymns and “giving thanks to God the Father at all times and for everything.” A Spirit-filled home is a singing and thankful home.

In the Gospel, the crowd is hungry and the place is deserted. Jesus says to the disciples: “You give them something to eat.” They have only five loaves and two fish. He takes them, blesses, breaks and gives them through the disciples, and all are filled, with twelve baskets left over.

These are the actions of every Holy Qurbana. The Risen Lord still feeds his people with his own Body, and he still asks us to bring our small loaves and to share them with the hungry.

So today, let us wake up to Christ's light, give thanks for everything, and bring our little gifts to him. He will make them enough. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus fed five thousand people with five loaves and two fish! He took the bread, said thank you to God, broke it and shared it.

At the Holy Qurbana Jesus gives us the best bread of all: himself. Today, share something with a friend, like Jesus did!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Sun of the Resurrection, you woke us from sleep and raised us from death in Baptism; shine on us today. Fill us with your Holy Spirit, so that we may live wisely, sing your praises and give thanks to the Father at all times and for everything. As you fed the hungry crowd in the deserted place, feed us with the Bread of your Body, and make us generous to all who hunger. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Ephesians calls the sleeper to rise from the dead so that Christ may shine on him, and then describes a community filled with the Spirit, singing and giving thanks to the Father at all times. Mark shows a crowd in a deserted place at evening, and Jesus who takes five loaves, looks up to heaven, blesses, breaks and gives. Both readings end at a table of thanksgiving, the table the Church sets in every Holy Qurbana. The key words are light, thanksgiving and broken bread.

Ephesians 5:13–21 · Christ will shine on you

Setting.

The second half of Ephesians turns from God's plan in Christ to the life that follows from it. Just before this passage the letter contrasts the works of darkness with life as “children of light” (5:8). Ephesians presents itself as Paul's; many scholars think a disciple wrote it in his name, while others hold that it is Paul's own.

Verse 14 introduces a quotation with “Therefore it says”, but the words are not found in the Old Testament. Many scholars think they come from an early Christian hymn sung at baptism, when the newly baptized came up from the water into the light.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: what the light exposes becomes light; the baptismal call to rise.
  • vv. 15–17: live wisely; make the most of the time.
  • v. 18: not drunk with wine, but filled with the Spirit.
  • vv. 19–21: psalms and hymns, thanksgiving at all times, mutual submission.

Key words.

  • epiphausei, will shine on: The verb is used of the dawn. Christ comes on the sleeper as the sun rises on the world. The early Church called baptism “enlightenment” (compare Hebrews 6:4).
  • exagorazomenoi ton kairon, making the most of the time: Literally “buying up the opportune moment”, as a merchant buys up goods while they are available. Each day holds chances for good that will not come again.
  • plērousthe en pneumati, be filled with the Spirit: A command in the present tense: keep on being filled. Being filled with the Spirit is not a single event but a way of life.

Verse by verse.

“Sleeper, awake! Rise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you.” (5:14)

Clement of Alexandria knew a longer form of this hymn, which goes on to speak of Christ as the Sun of the Resurrection (in substance). In the season of the Resurrection we hear it as our own baptismal call.

“Do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery; but be filled with the Spirit,” (5:18)

At Pentecost some onlookers thought the apostles were drunk (Acts 2:13). The Spirit gives a joy that does not come from a cup.

“giving thanks to God the Father at all times and for everything in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.” (5:20)

The Greek verb is eucharisteō, from which the word Eucharist comes.

How the Church reads it.

The Church's sung liturgy takes up this passage: psalms, hymns and spiritual songs addressed to the Father through Christ in the Spirit. The Catechism teaches that singing and music serve the liturgy when they express prayer, unite the assembly and give solemnity to the celebration (CCC 1157). Thanksgiving “at all times and for everything” is the spirit of the Holy Qurbana, the great thanksgiving of the Church. Verse 21 opens the passage on Christian households that follows; all its instructions stand under mutual reverence for Christ.

Mark 6:35–44 · Five loaves and two fish

Setting.

The apostles have returned from their first mission, and Jesus has taken them to a deserted place to rest. A great crowd follows, and he has compassion on them, “because they were like sheep without a shepherd” (6:34), recalling Numbers 27:17 and Ezekiel 34. He teaches them, and now it is late.

Apart from the Resurrection, this is the only miracle of Jesus told in all four Gospels. John adds that the Passover was near (John 6:4), which fits Mark's “green grass” of spring.

Structure.

  • vv. 35–36: the disciples' practical advice: send them away.
  • vv. 37–38: “You give them something to eat”; five loaves and two fish.
  • vv. 39–40: the crowd seated in groups on the green grass.
  • v. 41: the blessing and the breaking.
  • vv. 42–44: all are filled; twelve baskets; five thousand men.

Key words.

  • erēmos topos, deserted place: The wilderness recalls Israel fed with manna (Exodus 16). God feeds his people where nothing grows.
  • eulogēsen, blessed: The Jewish blessing over bread praises God who brings forth bread from the earth. Jesus takes, blesses, breaks and gives, the same four actions as at the Last Supper (Mark 14:22).
  • klasmata, broken pieces: The word for the fragments. The Didache, a very early Christian writing, uses the same word for the bread broken at the Eucharist (Didache 9).

Verse by verse.

“You give them something to eat.” (6:37)

The disciples see a problem to send away; Jesus sees people to feed, and he makes his disciples part of the answer.

“Then he ordered them to get all the people to sit down in groups on the green grass.” (6:39)

Mark says literally “table-groups, table-groups”. Many hear Psalm 23 here: the shepherd makes his flock lie down in green pastures and spreads a table.

“Taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven, and blessed and broke the loaves, and gave them to his disciples to set before the people;” (6:41)

He gives through the disciples. The Lord still feeds his people through the hands of those he sends.

“and they took up twelve baskets full of broken pieces and of the fish.” (6:43)

Twelve, the number of the tribes of Israel and of the apostles. There is more than enough.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the multiplication of the loaves, when the Lord says the blessing, breaks and distributes the loaves through his disciples to feed the multitude, prefigures the superabundance of the one bread of his Eucharist (CCC 1335). The Holy Qurbana is this table: the Lord's compassion for the hungry, his blessing to the Father, his body broken and given through the hands of his ministers. And the command “You give them something to eat” sends the Church to the hungry of the world.

Questions people ask

Was the multiplication a real miracle, or only the crowd sharing what they had brought?

All four Gospels present it as a miracle worked by Jesus, and the Church reads it so. The idea that the crowd simply shared their food is a modern explanation that the texts do not support. The sign is real, and it points beyond itself to the Bread of Life.

For study and teaching

  • Where is the Lord saying to me, “You give them something to eat”?
  • Ephesians asks for thanks “at all times and for everything”. For what do I find it hardest to give thanks?
