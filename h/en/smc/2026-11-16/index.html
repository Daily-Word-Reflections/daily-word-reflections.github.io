Word for the day

“You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength… You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Mark 12:30–31

Reflection

A scribe asks which commandment is first. Jesus joins two texts of the Law, Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18: love God with everything, and love your neighbour as yourself. The scribe agrees that this is “much more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices,” and Jesus tells him, “You are not far from the kingdom of God.”

Paul's hymn shows what this love looks like: patient, kind, not envious or boastful, not insisting on its own way. Without it, tongues, prophecy and even martyrdom are nothing. Love is not one gift among others; it is the life of the Church, the Bride's wedding garment.

Full homily

Dear friends, a scribe asks which commandment is the first of all. Jesus joins two texts of the Law: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind and strength; and love your neighbour as yourself (Mark 12:29–31). The scribe agrees that this is much more than all burnt offerings, and Jesus says: you are not far from the kingdom of God.

Paul describes what this love looks like: love is patient, love is kind; it is not envious or boastful or arrogant; it bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Love never ends (1 Corinthians 13:4–8).

Today, read these verses putting your own name in place of the word love: am I patient? am I kind? Wherever something is lacking, let us take one step forward this week. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: love God with all your heart and love others as yourself. Be patient with a friend today!

Prayer

God of love, teach me to love you with all my heart, soul, mind and strength, and my neighbour as myself. Make me patient and kind, for without love I am nothing. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today both readings ask what matters most. Paul tells the Corinthians, proud of their spectacular gifts, that without love even the greatest gifts are nothing. In the Gospel a scribe asks Jesus which commandment is first, and Jesus joins love of God and love of neighbour into one answer. The two readings explain each other: the “greatest gift” is the fulfilment of the “greatest commandment”.

1 Corinthians 13:1–10 · Without love I am nothing

Setting.

Corinth was a rich, mixed port city, and its young Church was divided. In chapters 12–14 Paul deals with spiritual gifts. Some Corinthians prized speaking in tongues above everything. Chapter 13 stands between the teaching on the body of Christ (ch. 12) and practical rules for worship (ch. 14). It is not a wedding poem first of all, but a correction of a gifted and quarrelsome community.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: gifts without love: tongues, prophecy, knowledge, faith, even giving everything away, are worth nothing.
  • vv. 4–7: what love does and does not do: fifteen verbs describing love in action.
  • vv. 8–10: love never ends; the gifts are partial and will pass when the complete comes.

Key words.

  • agapē, love: The self-giving love that seeks the good of the other. Paul describes it not with adjectives but with verbs: love is something one does.
  • makrothymei, is patient (v. 4): Literally “is long-tempered”: slow to anger, able to wait. The Greek Old Testament uses the same idea of God himself, “slow to anger” (Exodus 34:6).
  • to teleion, the complete (v. 10): What is whole and finished: the vision of God at the end. Then the partial gifts will no longer be needed, but love will remain.

Verse by verse.

“If I give away all my possessions, and if I hand over my body so that I may boast, but do not have love, I gain nothing.” (13:3)

A note on the text. The Greek manuscripts differ by one letter. Some of the oldest read “so that I may boast” (kauchēsōmai), which the NRSV follows. Others read “to be burned” (kauthēsomai), which the Latin Vulgate and the Malayalam POC follow (ദഹിപ്പിക്കാൻ). Either way the point is the same: even martyrdom without love would gain nothing.

“Love is patient; love is kind; love is not envious or boastful or arrogant” (13:4)

St Paul could almost be describing Christ. Put the name of Jesus in place of “love” and the sentences remain true.

“Love never ends.” (13:8)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this chapter to describe charity, the greatest of the theological virtues, and calls it the “form” of all the virtues: it gives them life and order (CCC 1822–1827, quoting 1 Corinthians 13:1–7). St Thérèse of Lisieux, reading chapters 12 and 13, understood that her vocation in the heart of the Church was love (Story of a Soul, ms. B, in substance).

Mark 12:28–34 · Not far from the kingdom of God

Setting.

Jesus is teaching in the Temple in Jerusalem in the last days before his Passion. One group after another has questioned him to trap him. This scribe is different: he heard that Jesus “answered them well” and asks a sincere question. Later rabbinic tradition counted 613 commandments in the Law, and teachers of the Law did discuss which ones were weightier.

Structure.

  • v. 28: the scribe's question.
  • vv. 29–31: Jesus' answer: the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–5) and love of neighbour (Leviticus 19:18).
  • vv. 32–33: the scribe repeats and approves, adding that love is more than sacrifices.
  • v. 34: “You are not far from the kingdom of God.”

Key words.

  • Shema, Hear (v. 29): The first word of Deuteronomy 6:4, the prayer devout Jews say morning and evening. Jesus begins with listening before loving.
  • holos, all (v. 30): Repeated four times: all your heart, soul, mind and strength. Love of God takes the whole person, not a part.

Verse by verse.

“you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.” (12:30)
“You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” (12:31)

Jesus was asked for one commandment and gave two. They cannot be separated: love of neighbour is the proof of love of God (compare 1 John 4:20).

“You are not far from the kingdom of God.” (12:34)

The scribe understands; he is near. The one step left is to follow the one who is speaking to him. The Kingdom is not only a teaching but a person.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the Ten Commandments with this answer of Jesus: the two great commandments sum up the whole Law (CCC 2055, 2196). This Gospel shows a moment of agreement between Jesus and a Jewish teacher on the heart of the Law, and the Church sees in it the common root she shares with the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4).

Questions people ask

Is 1 Corinthians 13 about marriage?

Paul wrote it for a whole community troubled by pride and division. It fits a wedding well, because marriage needs exactly this patient love, but it applies to every Christian relationship.

For study and teaching

  • Read 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 and put your own name in place of “love”. Which line is hardest to say honestly?
  • The scribe was “not far” from the Kingdom. What one step separates me from following Christ more fully?
