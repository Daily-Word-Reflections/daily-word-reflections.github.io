Word for the day

“Then I heard the voice of the Lord saying, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” And I said, “Here am I; send me!”” Isaiah 6:8

Reflection

This is the Sunday after the Ascension. Three days ago we watched the Lord taken up; next Sunday we will celebrate the coming of the Spirit. Today the Church stands in between, and the four readings tell one story: the Lord who reigns in glory sends people out. The Gospel of Mark ends with that double movement. The Risen Lord appears to the eleven “as they were sitting at the table”; he reproaches them for their unbelief, and then, astonishingly, he entrusts the whole world to these same slow-hearted men: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” Then “the Lord Jesus, after he had spoken to them, was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” And the last verse of the Gospel says: “they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them and confirmed the message by the signs that accompanied it.” The Lord is seated in heaven and working on earth at the same time. That is the meaning of the Ascension for the Church: he has not gone away from his work; he does it now through us. One of those eleven was Thomas, and the good news he carried reached our shores. Every Syro-Malabar Christian who hears this Gospel is the fruit of that sending.

The first reading shows us the throne from which the sending comes. “In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and lofty; and the hem of his robe filled the temple.” The seraphs cry to one another: “Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory.” We sing that hymn in every Holy Qurbana, joining the angels before the throne of the One who has ascended. Isaiah's first response is fear: “Woe is me! I am lost, for I am a man of unclean lips.” Then a seraph touches his lips with a live coal from the altar, and says, “your guilt has departed and your sin is blotted out.” Syriac Fathers such as St Ephrem saw in that coal, taken from the altar with tongs and placed on the lips, a figure of the Holy Qurbana, the fire of the Lord's Body given to us. Only after he is purified does Isaiah hear the question: “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” And he answers, “Here am I; send me!” The mission that follows is hard: the people will not listen, the land will be laid waste. Yet the chapter ends with hope: “The holy seed is its stump.” God never abandons his people; from the stump of Jesse the Messiah will come.

The Acts of the Apostles shows the Church obeying that sending in the days between the Ascension and Pentecost. Just before today's reading we hear that the apostles “were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). Then Peter stands up among about a hundred and twenty believers. The circle of the Twelve has been broken by the betrayal of Judas, and it must be restored before the Spirit comes. The one chosen must be a witness: “one of the men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us, one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” They propose two, Joseph called Barsabbas and Matthias. They do not campaign; they pray: “Lord, you know everyone's heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen.” The lot falls on Matthias, “and he was added to the eleven apostles.” Here, in the presence of Mary, we see the apostolic Church taking care for its own continuity: the ministry of the apostles is handed on. Our bishops stand in that same line of witnesses, and the Synod of our Church still begins its elections with prayer to the Lord who knows every heart.

Paul's letter to the Philippians sings the whole mystery we are celebrating. “Let the same mind be in you that was in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as something to be exploited, but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave, being born in human likeness.” He went down, all the way to the Cross. “Therefore God also highly exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bend, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.” The Ascension is that exaltation made visible: the One who emptied himself now sits at the right hand of God. But Paul did not write this hymn only to tell us about Christ; he wrote it to change the way the Philippians lived together. “Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility regard others as better than yourselves.” The way up for the Church is the way Christ went: the way down, in humility and service. A parish quarrelling about positions, a family where everyone wants the first place, will not proclaim the Gospel well. A community that has the mind of Christ will be sent, like Isaiah and like the eleven, and the Lord will work with it.

Full homily

Dear friends, on Thursday we celebrated the Ascension, and next Sunday we will celebrate Pentecost. Today we stand in between, and the readings tell us one thing: the Lord who reigns in glory sends his people out.

Isaiah sees the Lord on a high throne, and the angels singing, “Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts.” We sing that same hymn at every Holy Qurbana. Isaiah is afraid: “Woe is me! I am lost, for I am a man of unclean lips.” An angel touches his lips with a burning coal from the altar, and his sin is taken away. Our Syriac Fathers saw in that coal a picture of the Holy Qurbana, placed on our lips. Then God asks, “Whom shall I send?” And Isaiah answers, “Here am I; send me!”

In Acts, the apostles are praying with Mary, the mother of Jesus. Judas is gone, and the circle of the Twelve must be complete before the Spirit comes. They choose a witness to the Resurrection. They do not make speeches; they pray: “Lord, you know everyone's heart.” Matthias is chosen. The work of the apostles is handed on. Our bishops stand in that line today. Let us pray for them.

Paul sings of Jesus, who “emptied himself, taking the form of a slave”, and went down to the Cross. “Therefore God also highly exalted him.” The Ascension is that exaltation. But Paul says: let the same mind be in you. Do nothing from selfish ambition. Regard others as better than yourselves. The Church goes up the way Christ went: by going down in humility and service.

In the Gospel the Risen Lord scolds his disciples for their unbelief, and then he sends these same weak men: “Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” He is taken up into heaven, and “they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them.” He is in heaven and at work on earth at the same time.

Thomas was one of those eleven. Because he went, we are here. Now the Lord asks each of us: Whom shall I send? Maybe across the sea, maybe across the road to a neighbour. Let us answer with Isaiah: Here am I; send me. And let us pray with Mary this week for the Holy Spirit, who will give us the strength to go. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Isaiah heard God ask, “Whom shall I send?” and he said, “Here am I; send me!”

Before Jesus went up to heaven, he sent his friends to tell everyone about him. Jesus wants you to be his helper too. This week, can you tell one friend something good about Jesus? Say to him: ‘Here I am, Jesus!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you emptied yourself and became obedient unto death, and the Father has exalted you and seated you at his right hand. With the seraphs we cry, Holy, holy, holy. Touch our lips with the live coal of your altar, take away our guilt, and make us answer, Here am I; send me. Guide the successors of your apostles as you guided the choice of Matthias. Give us your mind, humble and loving, so that our communities may proclaim you. Work with us, Lord, wherever you send us, until every tongue confesses that you are Lord, to the glory of the Father. With Mary your Mother we pray: send us your Holy Spirit. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Lord ascended on Thursday, and next Sunday the Spirit will come at Pentecost. This Sunday stands in between, and its four readings are about being sent. In the Temple Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned, is purified by a burning coal and answers, “Here am I; send me!” In the upper room, while the disciples wait in prayer with Mary, Peter leads the community in choosing Matthias, so that the Twelve are complete before the Spirit comes. Paul sings of Christ who emptied himself and was highly exalted, so that every tongue should confess him Lord. In Mark the risen Lord sends the eleven into all the world, is taken up into heaven, and goes on working with them. The words to watch are send, witness and Lord. Today is the third day of the novena to the Holy Spirit.

Isaiah 6:1–13 · Isaiah is purified and sent

Setting.

King Uzziah of Judah (also called Azariah) reigned for many years, and his reign was strong and prosperous. He died about 740 before Christ, as the power of Assyria was rising in the north. Chronicles remembers that late in life he presumed to burn incense in the Temple and was struck with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). In the year of an earthly king’s death, Isaiah sees the true King.

Many scholars think this vision is Isaiah’s call, placed here at the head of a section that opens in the first person (chapters 6–8). The scene is the Temple in Jerusalem, but the throne is God’s heavenly throne: the Temple can hold only the hem of his robe.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the vision: the Lord enthroned, the seraphs, the cry “Holy”, the shaking and the smoke.
  • v. 5: “Woe is me!”: the prophet sees himself before the King.
  • vv. 6–7: the burning coal from the altar touches his lips; guilt removed.
  • v. 8: “Whom shall I send?” “Here am I; send me!”
  • vv. 9–10: a hard commission: the people will hear and not understand.
  • vv. 11–13: “How long?” Until the land is emptied; yet a stump remains, a holy seed.

Key words.

  • śĕrāpîm, seraphs: Probably from śārap, to burn: “burning ones”. They cover their faces before God’s glory and their feet in humility. Even the purest creatures cannot look on God directly.
  • qādôš, holy: Hebrew has no simple word for “most”, so it repeats: “holy, holy, holy” means holiness beyond all measure. Holiness here is God’s utter otherness and purity, which fills the whole earth with glory.
  • hinnēnî, here am I: The answer of Abraham (Genesis 22:1), of Moses at the bush (Exodus 3:4) and of the boy Samuel (1 Samuel 3:4). It is not an offer of skills but of oneself: “I am here, available.”

Verse by verse.

“I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and lofty; and the hem of his robe filled the temple.” (6:1)

John’s Gospel says of this vision: “Isaiah said this because he saw his glory and spoke about him” (John 12:41). The Church reads Isaiah’s sight of the King as a glimpse of the glory of the Son.

“Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory.” (6:3)

The song of heaven, which the Book of Revelation hears again around the throne (Revelation 4:8). The Church has made it the song of every Eucharist.

“Woe is me! I am lost, for I am a man of unclean lips,” (6:5)

Close to God, Isaiah does not feel proud but unclean, as Peter fell at Jesus’ knees and called himself a sinful man (Luke 5:8). The nearer we come to the light, the more we see our own shadows.

“your guilt has departed and your sin is blotted out.” (6:7)

The coal comes “from the altar”. Purification is God’s work, not Isaiah’s; the prophet is cleansed before he is sent.

““Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” And I said, “Here am I; send me!”” (6:8)

The “us” may be God speaking with the heavenly court around him. Many Fathers heard in it a hint of the Trinity. God asks; he does not force. Isaiah volunteers before he knows how hard the mission will be.

“The holy seed is its stump.” (6:13)

Textual note: this last clause is not found in the ancient Greek translation, the Septuagint, but it is in the Hebrew text and in the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran. The tree is cut down, but life remains in the stump. Isaiah will later say, “A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse” (11:1).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Isaiah’s cry, “Woe is me! I am lost”, to show that before the glory of the thrice-holy God we discover our own smallness and sin (CCC 208). The hard words of vv. 9–10 are taken up by Jesus to explain why many do not understand his parables (Matthew 13:14–15) and by Paul at the end of Acts (28:26–27). They describe a mystery of hardening that Paul says is partial and not final (Romans 11:25–26); they are never a curse on Israel.

St Ephrem, the harp of the Spirit, loved this coal. The seraph, he says, did not touch the fire with his fingers but took it with tongs, while we take the Fire into our mouths in the Bread (in substance, Hymns on Faith 10). The Syriac tradition speaks of the Holy Mysteries as the living coal, and every Holy Qurbana joins the song of the seraphs: “Holy, holy, holy”.

Acts 1:15–26 · Matthias is added to the eleven apostles

Setting.

The Lord has been taken up (1:9–11), and the disciples have gone back to the upper room in Jerusalem. Luke names the eleven and says that they “were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus, as well as his brothers” (1:13–14). The Spirit has not yet come. In this waiting time Peter, whom Jesus had told to strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:32), stands up to lead.

Matthew tells the death of Judas differently: he hanged himself, and the chief priests bought a potter’s field with the money (Matthew 27:3–10). Both accounts agree that the money bought a field that came to be called the Field of Blood. Luke’s point is not to describe Judas in detail but to say that his place among the Twelve must be filled.

Structure.

  • v. 15: Peter stands among about a hundred and twenty.
  • vv. 16–17: the Scripture about Judas had to be fulfilled; he had a share in this ministry.
  • vv. 18–19: an aside on Judas’s end and the Field of Blood.
  • v. 20: two verses from the Psalms.
  • vv. 21–22: the condition: a witness from the baptism of John to the Ascension.
  • vv. 23–26: two names, a prayer, the lot; Matthias is added to the eleven.

Key words.

  • martys, witness: The apostle is first of all a witness to the Resurrection (v. 22), one who has been with Jesus and can say what he saw. In later Christian use the word came to mean a martyr, one whose witness is sealed with his blood.
  • episkopē, position of overseer: The word in v. 20, from Psalm 109:8 in its Greek form. In the later Church it became the word for the office of a bishop (episkopos, overseer). Here it names the apostolic charge that Judas abandoned.
  • klēros, lot, share: The word frames the story: Judas “was allotted his share” in this ministry (v. 17), and the disciples “cast lots” (v. 26). Through Latin, this word gave the Church the word “clergy”: those who have received a share in the ministry.

Verse by verse.

“In those days Peter stood up among the believers (together the crowd numbered about one hundred twenty persons) and said,” (1:15)

Before Pentecost, before any sermon, Peter already acts as the one who strengthens his brothers. He does not decide alone; he puts the question before the community.

“one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” (1:22)

The Twelve recall the twelve tribes of Israel; Jesus had promised that they would sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel (Luke 22:30). Their number had to be whole when the Spirit came upon the renewed people of God.

“Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen” (1:24)

“Lord” here most likely means Jesus, who chose the apostles in the beginning (1:2). The community proposes; the Lord chooses.

“and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” (1:26)

Casting lots was an ancient way of leaving the choice to God (Proverbs 16:33). This is the last time the New Testament mentions it. After Pentecost the Church decides with the Spirit’s guidance: “it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” (Acts 15:28).

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this choice the beginning of apostolic succession. The apostles took care to appoint others to continue their mission, and the bishops have succeeded them (CCC 861–862). St Clement of Rome, writing at the end of the first century, already says that the apostles appointed men and gave instructions that others should succeed them (in substance, 1 Clement 42–44). St John Chrysostom admires that Peter, the first of the apostles, did nothing on his own authority but acted with the whole company (in substance, Homilies on Acts 3). Authority in the Church is service, exercised in communion.

Philippians 2:1–11 · Every tongue shall confess that Jesus Christ is Lord

Setting.

Philippi was a Roman colony in Macedonia, and its church was the first Paul founded in Europe (Acts 16:11–40). The community was dear to him, but it was not without rivalries: later he begs two women, Euodia and Syntyche, to be of the same mind in the Lord (4:2). So Paul asks for unity and gives the deepest reason for it: the mind of Christ.

Verses 6–11 have the rhythm of a hymn. Many scholars think it was sung by Christians before Paul quoted it; others think Paul composed it himself. Either way it is one of the oldest witnesses to the faith that Jesus, who was in the form of God, became man and is now Lord.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: if there is any encouragement in Christ, be of one mind; humility; look to the interests of others.
  • v. 5: let the same mind be in you that was in Christ Jesus.
  • vv. 6–8: the way down: form of God, emptied, form of a slave, obedient to death on a cross.
  • vv. 9–11: the way up: highly exalted, the name above every name, every knee, every tongue: Jesus Christ is Lord. ¶ The hymn moves like a great V: down to the Cross, then up to the throne. The Ascension we have just celebrated is the top of the second stroke.

Key words.

  • morphē, form: Not a mere appearance but the real way of being. Christ was “in the form of God”, truly God, and took “the form of a slave”, truly a man. The same word is used for both, so that both natures are equally real.
  • harpagmos, something to be exploited: A rare word, and its exact sense is debated: something to be grasped, or something to be clung to for one’s own advantage. Either way the sense is clear: Christ did not use his equality with God for himself.
  • ekenōsen, emptied himself: From this verb comes the word kenosis. Christ did not cease to be God. As the ancient liturgy sings, he remained what he was and took up what he was not. He emptied himself by taking on our poverty, not by losing his divinity.
  • Kyrios, Lord: The Greek Bible used this word for the divine name of the God of Israel. Paul takes the words of Isaiah, where God says, “To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear” (Isaiah 45:23), and applies them to Jesus. To confess Jesus as Lord is to confess him as God.

Verse by verse.

“Let the same mind be in you that was in Christ Jesus,” (2:5)

The great hymn is introduced for a very practical reason: a quarrelling parish. Doctrine and daily life are not separate; the mystery of Christ is the pattern for how we treat one another.

“who, though he was in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as something to be exploited,” (2:6)

Adam grasped at being like God (Genesis 3:5) and fell. Christ, who truly was equal with God, did not grasp, and he raised us up.

“but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave, being born in human likeness.” (2:7)

The Son did not merely look human; “being born” is literally “becoming”. He became what we are.

“even death on a cross.” (2:8)

Crucifixion was the death of slaves and rebels. In a Roman colony like Philippi, where citizens were proud that the law protected them from it, these words were shocking. The lowest point of the hymn is the place of our salvation.

“Therefore God also highly exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name,” (2:9)

The verb is a strong one, “super-exalted”. The Ascension is the Father’s answer to the Son’s obedience. The humanity that was humbled is the humanity that is now enthroned.

“every knee should bend, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.” (2:10–11)

The confession of Christ does not take glory away from the Father; it gives glory to him. To kneel at the name of Jesus is to worship the one God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes vv. 5–8 when it teaches the Incarnation: the Son of God assumed a human nature in order to accomplish our salvation in it (CCC 461). The Councils of the Church, from Nicaea onward, read this hymn as a witness that the Son is truly God and truly man. St Augustine gave a rule for reading the Gospels: some things are said of Christ according to the form of God, in which he is equal to the Father, and some according to the form of a slave, in which the Father is greater (in substance, On the Trinity 1). Both are true of the one Lord.

Mark 16:14–20 · Go into all the world and proclaim the good news

Setting.

Today’s Gospel comes from what is called the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20). The two oldest complete Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus, end the Gospel at 16:8, and many scholars think these verses were added early by another hand, gathering up appearances told in the other Gospels and Acts. They are found in the great majority of manuscripts, and St Irenaeus already quotes them late in the second century. The Council of Trent received the sacred books “with all their parts” as they were read in the Church, and the Church accepts these verses as inspired Scripture.

Earlier in this ending the disciples refused to believe Mary Magdalene (16:11) and the two who had met him on the road (16:13). Now he appears to the eleven themselves at table.

Structure.

  • v. 14: he appears to the eleven at table and rebukes their unbelief.
  • vv. 15–16: the mission: all the world, the whole creation; faith and Baptism.
  • vv. 17–18: the signs that will accompany those who believe.
  • v. 19: he is taken up into heaven and sits at the right hand of God.
  • v. 20: they go out everywhere; the Lord works with them.

Key words.

  • ktisis, creation: “The whole creation” (v. 15). The good news is not for one people only, nor even for human beings alone: Paul says that the creation itself waits for its release from decay, to share the glory of the children of God (Romans 8:19–21).
  • anelēmphthē, was taken up: The same verb Luke uses for the Ascension (Acts 1:2, 11). The Lord is not simply gone; he has been received into the glory of the Father.
  • synergountos, worked with: “The Lord worked with them” (v. 20), literally “working together with”. The Ascension does not leave the Church alone; it begins a new kind of presence in which the Lord acts through his disciples.

Verse by verse.

“he upbraided them for their lack of faith and stubbornness,” (16:14)

The founders of the Church were not heroes of faith from the first. The Lord sends men he has just had to rebuke. The mission rests on his choice, not on their merit.

“Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation.” (16:15)

Our Church remembers that one of the eleven at that table, the apostle Thomas, went as far as India.

“The one who believes and is baptized will be saved;” (16:16)

Faith and Baptism belong together. The Catechism cites this verse in teaching that Baptism is necessary for salvation for those to whom the Gospel has been proclaimed and who have had the possibility of asking for it (CCC 1257). The verse does not add “and is not baptized” to the second half: what condemns is the refusal to believe.

“they will pick up snakes in their hands, and if they drink any deadly thing, it will not hurt them;” (16:18)

Acts tells how Paul, bitten by a viper on Malta, shook it off and suffered no harm (Acts 28:3–6). These are promises of protection in the mission, not tests to perform. Jesus himself refused to put God to the test (Matthew 4:7; Deuteronomy 6:16).

“was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.” (16:19)

The words of Psalm 110:1, “Sit at my right hand”. To sit at God’s right hand is to share his rule. The man Jesus, with his wounded and risen body, now reigns.

“And they went out and proclaimed the good news everywhere, while the Lord worked with them and confirmed the message by the signs that accompanied it.” (16:20)

The Gospel of Mark ends not with the disciples standing and looking up, but with them going out. The Lord in heaven and the disciples on earth work together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the Ascension with v. 19 (CCC 659). It teaches that the Ascension marks the definitive entrance of Jesus’ humanity into God’s heavenly domain, from where he will come again, and that he precedes us there so that we, his members, may live in the hope of being with him forever (CCC 665–666). St Leo the Great, preaching on this feast, said that what was visible in our Redeemer has passed over into the sacraments (in substance, Sermon 74). The Lord who has gone up still touches us, in the Word and the Holy Mysteries.

Questions people ask

If the oldest manuscripts end Mark at 16:8, is today’s Gospel really Scripture?

Yes. Whether or not Mark himself wrote these verses, the Church has received them from very early times as part of the inspired Gospel, and they are read in her liturgy. Inspiration does not depend on our knowing which hand held the pen. The Church distinguishes the scholarly question of authorship from the question of canonicity, and on the second she is clear.

Why did the apostles replace Judas, but not James when he was killed (Acts 12:2)?

Judas left his place by betrayal; James kept his to the end and died a faithful witness. The Twelve were complete when the Spirit came at Pentecost, as the foundation of the renewed people of God, and the Book of Revelation sees the twelve names of the apostles on the foundations of the holy city (Revelation 21:14). After that the apostles’ mission continued through those they appointed, not by refilling the number of the Twelve.

When Christ “emptied himself”, did he stop being God?

No. The Church teaches that the Son remained fully God when he became fully man, without confusion and without separation (Council of Chalcedon, 451). He “emptied himself” by taking the form of a slave, hiding his glory and accepting our weakness and death. The God who cannot suffer chose to suffer in the flesh he made his own.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syro-Malabar calendar the Ascension of our Lord was kept on Thursday, 6 May, the fortieth day of the Resurrection season. This Sunday is the last of that season. Next Sunday, with Pentecost, the Church begins the Season of the Apostles, which remembers how the Spirit sent the apostles to the ends of the earth. Our Church carries in its very name the memory of that sending: the faith brought to India by Mar Thoma, one of the eleven at the table in today’s Gospel.

These days between Ascension and Pentecost are the Church’s first novena: the disciples in prayer with Mary, waiting. Many of our families and parishes pray the novena to the Holy Spirit through this week. In every Holy Qurbana we sing with Isaiah’s seraphs, “Holy, holy, holy”, and receive from the altar the living coal that cleanses lips and hearts. Each Qurbana ends with a sending; this Sunday reminds us that we, too, are sent.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah says “Here am I; send me!” before he knows the cost. Where is God asking me to say yes today?
  • The disciples prayed together with Mary while they waited for the Spirit. How will my family keep this week of waiting before Pentecost?
  • Paul gives the hymn of Christ’s humility to settle quarrels. Where in my parish or home is the mind of Christ most needed?
  • The Lord “worked with them”. In what ordinary work of mine can I ask him to work with me?
