Word for the day

“They answered, “The emperor’s.” Then he said to them, “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.”” Matthew 22:21

Reflection

In the last week of the Season of the Apostles the readings turn to a question that every disciple faces in the street, in the office and at the ballot box: how does a Christian live under earthly government? The Gospel begins with a trap. “Then the Pharisees went and plotted to entrap him in what he said.” They send their disciples together with the Herodians, the party that supported the house of Herod and therefore Roman rule. The two groups did not normally agree, but they agree now against Jesus. They begin with flattery: “Teacher, we know that you are sincere, and teach the way of God in accordance with truth, and show deference to no one.” Then the question: “Is it lawful to pay taxes to the emperor, or not?” If Jesus says yes, he loses the crowds who hate the Roman tax; if he says no, the Herodians can report him to the governor as a rebel. Jesus, “aware of their malice”, asks for the coin used for the tax. They produce a denarius. The detail matters: they carry Caesar's coin, with Caesar's image and an inscription calling him a son of a god, even into the precincts of the Temple. “Whose head is this, and whose title?” “The emperor’s.” “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.”

The answer is not a clever escape. It draws a line through every human life. The coin bears the emperor's image, so give it back to him: pay what is due, take part in the life of the city, respect the law. But the human person bears another image. “So God created humankind in his image, in the image of God he created them” (Genesis 1:27). If the coin belongs to the one whose image it carries, then you belong wholly to God, whose image you carry. Caesar may ask for taxes; he may not ask for your soul. The early Christians lived exactly this way. They paid taxes and prayed for the emperors, but they refused to offer incense before the emperor's statue, and many died for that refusal. Peter and the apostles, who will be honoured on Tuesday, had already said it before the council in Jerusalem: “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (Acts 5:29).

Paul's letter to the Romans, written to Christians living in the capital of the empire, gives the other side of the teaching. “Let every person be subject to the governing authorities; for there is no authority except from God, and those authorities that exist have been instituted by God.” Paul is not praising Nero, nor saying that every ruler is good. He is saying that order in society, law, courts and public service are part of God's providence for human life; without them the weak are devoured by the strong. The ruler is “God’s servant for your good”. That phrase is both an honour and a judgment: authority exists for the good of the people, and it will answer to God for how it serves. Then comes a very ordinary instruction: “For the same reason you also pay taxes, for the authorities are God’s servants, busy with this very thing.” Honest taxes, honest bills, no bribe to the officer and no bribe taken from the citizen: these are part of Christian holiness. Paul adds that we obey “not only because of wrath but also because of conscience”. A Christian is honest not only when a camera is watching but because God sees.

For Christians in India this teaching is very practical. We live in a great democracy, among neighbours of many faiths. We are called to be good citizens: to vote with a formed conscience, to pay our taxes, to keep the traffic rules, to refuse the small corruption that has become normal, and to serve in public office with clean hands. At the same time we pray for those in authority, as Paul asked Timothy to pray “for kings and all who are in high positions, so that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and dignity” (1 Timothy 2:2). And when a law or an order commands what God forbids, the Christian answers with Peter and with the martyrs: God first. On this Friday of the Season of the Apostles let us give back to Caesar the coin, and give back to God ourselves.

Full homily

Dear friends, today some clever men try to trap Jesus. They ask him: should we pay taxes to the emperor or not? Whatever he answers, they think, he will be in trouble.

Jesus asks for a coin. Whose picture is on it? The emperor's. “Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s” (Matthew 22:21).

The coin carries the emperor's image, so it goes back to him. But what carries God's image? You do. Every person is made in the image of God. So the coin may go to the government, but you yourself belong to God.

Saint Paul says that all authority comes from God, and that rulers are God's servants for our good (Romans 13:1, 4). So we pay our taxes, we keep the law, we vote with a good conscience, and we pray for those who govern. A Christian does not take or give bribes, even small ones.

But if any power commands what God forbids, we say with Peter and the apostles, “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (Acts 5:29). This week let us be honest citizens, and let us give back to God the only thing he asks for: our whole heart. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus looked at a coin with the king's picture on it and said: give the king what has his picture. Then he meant something wonderful: God's picture is on you! You belong to God. So be honest, obey good rules, and give your whole heart to God!

Prayer

Lord God, you are the source of all authority, and every human being bears your image. Bless our country and all who govern it, from the village panchayat to the Parliament; give them wisdom, honesty and a care for the poor. Make us good citizens, honest in our taxes and duties and faithful to our conscience. Keep us from every form of corruption, and give us courage, like your apostles and martyrs, to obey you first when the world asks what you forbid. We give back to you ourselves, made in your image, through Jesus Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what a Christian owes to the state, and both answer by placing the state under God. Paul tells the Christians in Rome that every lawful authority has its place from God and serves the common good, so taxes and obedience are a matter of conscience. In Matthew, Jesus is asked a trap question about the Roman tax; he asks whose image is on the coin, and gives an answer that has guided the Church ever since: give the emperor what is his, and give God what is God’s. The coin bears Caesar’s image; the human person bears God’s.

Romans 13:1–6 · There is no authority except from God

Setting.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth, probably around AD 57, to a Church he had not yet visited. Nero was emperor, in the calm early years of his reign. The Christians of Rome were a small group with no power, some of them Jews who had been expelled under Claudius and had returned (Acts 18:2). The Roman historian Tacitus reports loud complaints in the city about indirect taxes around AD 58. Paul has just told his readers not to repay evil for evil and to leave vengeance to God (12:17–21). Now he speaks of the ruler who punishes wrongdoing as God’s servant for the common good.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: every person should be subject; all authority comes from God; to resist it is to resist God’s order.
  • vv. 3–4: rulers exist to restrain evil and encourage good; the authority is “God’s servant for your good”.
  • v. 5: obey not only from fear of punishment but for the sake of conscience.
  • v. 6: this is also why you pay taxes.

Key words.

  • hypotassesthai, to be subject: Literally “to place oneself under”, to accept one’s place in an order. It is not blind obedience to anything a ruler commands, but respect for the order that God wills for life in society.
  • diakonos theou, God’s servant: Paul calls the civil authority by the same word he uses for ministers of the Gospel. The ruler is a servant, not a master, and he is accountable to God for the good of the people.
  • syneidēsis, conscience: The inner judgment by which a person knows right and wrong before God. Paul grounds civic duty in conscience; the same conscience can also tell a Christian when a command must be refused (Acts 5:29).

Verse by verse.

“Let every person be subject to the governing authorities; for there is no authority except from God” (13:1)

Paul does not say that every ruler is good, but that authority as such belongs to God’s plan for human life together. Rulers receive their power in trust.

“for it is God’s servant for your good.” (13:4)

Here is the measure of all authority: the good of those it serves. An authority that works against the good of its people departs from its own purpose.

“Therefore one must be subject, not only because of wrath but also because of conscience.” (13:5)

A Christian is an honest citizen even when no one is watching, because he obeys God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it teaches that every human community needs an authority, and that authority has its source in God (CCC 1897–1899). It adds that authority is exercised legitimately only when it seeks the common good by morally lawful means; if rulers enact unjust laws, these do not bind in conscience (CCC 1903). Citizens owe rulers respect, taxes and a share in the common good (CCC 2238–2240), and they are obliged in conscience not to follow civil commands that are contrary to the moral order, for “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (CCC 2242; Acts 5:29). Paul himself, who wrote these lines, was later put to death under the same emperor, faithful to Christ to the end.

Matthew 22:15–22 · Whose head is this?

Setting.

Jesus is in the Temple in Jerusalem during his last days before the Passion. After three parables against the leaders (21:28–22:14), they try to trap him. Two groups who were usually opposed join together: some disciples of the Pharisees, who resented Roman rule, and the Herodians, supporters of the house of Herod, who depended on Rome. The tax in question was the poll tax paid directly to Rome, a sore sign of foreign rule. If Jesus said “Pay it”, he could lose the people; if he said “Do not pay”, he could be reported to the governor as a rebel.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: the plot and the flattering question: is it lawful to pay the tax to the emperor?
  • vv. 18–19: Jesus sees their malice and asks for the coin.
  • vv. 20–21: “Whose head is this?” “The emperor’s.” Give to the emperor and give to God.
  • v. 22: they are amazed and go away.

Key words.

  • kēnsos, tax: From the Latin census: the tax counted per head. It had to be paid in Roman coin.
  • eikōn, head, image: The silver denarius of Tiberius carried his portrait and an inscription calling him son of the divine Augustus. The word is the one used in the Greek Old Testament for man made “in the image” of God (Genesis 1:27).
  • apodote, give back, render: Not simply “give” but “give back”, pay what is owed. The coin already belongs to Caesar; the person already belongs to God.

Verse by verse.

“Teacher, we know that you are sincere, and teach the way of God in accordance with truth” (22:16)

The flattery is meant to push him into a dangerous answer. Yet what they say is true, more true than they know.

“Show me the coin used for the tax.” (22:19)

Jesus does not carry the coin himself; his questioners produce it, in the Temple. Their own pockets give the answer.

“Give therefore to the emperor the things that are the emperor’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” (22:21)

The two halves are not equal. Caesar may claim coins; God claims the whole person. The state has a real place, but it is not God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this saying when it teaches the duty of citizens and its limit: when the commands of civil authority are contrary to the moral order, the citizen must refuse, for one must give to Caesar what is Caesar’s and to God what is God’s (CCC 2242). Tertullian drew out the meaning of the image: give the coin with Caesar’s image to Caesar, and give to God yourself, who bear God’s image (in substance, On Idolatry 15). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the political community and the Church are independent in their own fields, yet both serve the same human persons (Gaudium et Spes 76).

Questions people ask

Does Romans 13 mean Christians must obey any government, even a cruel one?

No. Paul describes what authority is for: to reward good and restrain evil. The Church teaches that laws contrary to the moral order do not bind in conscience, and that citizens must refuse commands that go against God’s law (CCC 1903, 2242). The apostles themselves told the council in Jerusalem, “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (Acts 5:29). Christians obey lawful authority out of conscience, and for the same reason they may not obey it in what is evil.

For study and teaching

  • Paul calls the ruler “God’s servant for your good”. How can I pray for and support those who govern, and also help them serve the common good?
  • If the coin belongs to Caesar because it bears his image, what does it mean that I bear God’s image? What in my life have I not yet given back to God?
