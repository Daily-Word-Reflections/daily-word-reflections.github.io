Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.”” John 14:6

Reflection

Today the Church honours two of the Twelve together, Philip and James. The Gospels tell us more about Philip than about many of the apostles, and almost all of it comes from John. Philip was “from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter” (John 1:44), a fishing town on the Sea of Galilee. Jesus found him and said simply, “Follow me”, and Philip at once became a messenger: he found Nathanael and told him, “We have found him about whom Moses in the law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus son of Joseph from Nazareth.” When Nathanael scoffed, “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?”, Philip did not argue; he said, “Come and see” (John 1:45–46). Before the feeding of the five thousand, Jesus tested him: “Where are we to buy bread for these people to eat?”, and Philip, practical to the end, did the arithmetic: “Six months’ wages would not buy enough bread for each of them to get a little” (John 6:5–7). When some Greeks came to the feast and said, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus”, it was to Philip that they came (John 12:21). An early tradition, recorded by the historian Eusebius from a letter of Bishop Polycrates of Ephesus at the end of the second century, says that Philip was buried at Hierapolis in Asia Minor. The second apostle is James, the son of Alphaeus (Matthew 10:3). The Latin tradition has long identified him with James ‘the less’ and with James ‘the brother of the Lord’, who led the Church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Galatians 1:19) and to whom the risen Lord appeared (1 Corinthians 15:7); the Eastern tradition has generally distinguished these figures. We need not settle the question to honour him: an apostle chosen by Christ, a witness of the Resurrection, and a pillar of the first Church. The two have been honoured together in the West since the dedication of the Basilica of the Holy Apostles in Rome, where their relics are kept, in the sixth century.

The Gospel of the feast is the conversation of the Last Supper in which Philip spoke. Jesus begins with words that Christians have read at countless deathbeds: “Do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father’s house there are many dwelling places.” He is going to prepare a place, and he will come again and take us to himself, “so that where I am, there you may be also.” Thomas, honest as always, objects: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” And Jesus gives the answer that sums up the whole Gospel: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” In the longer reading, Philip asks the question that lies under every human search for God: “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied.” Jesus answers with gentle sorrow: “Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” Three days before the Ascension these words take on a special weight. Jesus is going to the Father; but he is not leaving a map, he is leaving himself. The way to the Father is not a technique or a set of rules but a Person, the Son made man. The Church in India lives among many sincere seekers who speak of many paths to God. We honour every true search, for God is at work in it; but we cannot keep silent about the One who said, ‘I am the way.’

The two Old Testament readings sing of the victory God gives his faithful ones. Exodus gives the song of Moses on the shore of the sea, which the Church sings in the night of Easter: “I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea. The Lord is my strength and my might, and he has become my salvation.” The apostles saw a greater exodus: the Lord passing through death and bringing his people out of the slavery of sin. Wisdom then describes the day when the despised righteous will be vindicated: “Then the righteous will stand with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed them and those who make light of their labors.” Their persecutors will say in amazement, “Why have they been numbered among the children of God? And why is their lot among the saints?” Tradition remembers that both Philip and James gave their lives for Christ. James, the brother of the Lord, was put to death in Jerusalem in the early sixties of the first century, as the Jewish historian Josephus records; of Philip's death the accounts are later and differ. The world thought their lives were wasted. The Church sings with Moses that the Lord has triumphed in them.

In the Epistle Paul explains why an apostle cannot be silent: “If I proclaim the gospel, this gives me no ground for boasting, for an obligation is laid on me, and woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” He makes himself “a slave to all” so that he might win more of them; to Jews he becomes as a Jew, to those outside the law as one outside the law, to the weak weak: “I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” This is not compromise of the truth but the humility of love, which goes to people where they are. Philip lived it when he said, “Come and see”, and when he brought the Greeks to Jesus. The weekday readings of this Sixth Monday add a word for us all. Peter calls the baptised “living stones” built into “a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ”, and in Matthew Jesus warns against a religion that is only outward, cleaning “the outside of the cup” while the inside stays unclean. The apostles' faith was not a show. It was a life given. On their feast we ask for that same apostolic faith, the faith of the Church that is built on the foundation of the apostles, and for the courage to say to our friends, as Philip did, ‘Come and see.’

Full homily

Dear friends, today we honour two apostles, Philip and James. Philip was a practical man from Bethsaida. When Jesus called him, he ran to his friend Nathanael and said, “Come and see” (John 1:46). James led the Church in Jerusalem, and the risen Lord appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7).

At the Last Supper Philip asked, “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied.” Jesus answered, “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (John 14:8–9). We do not need to search far for God. He has shown his face in Jesus.

Jesus also said, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). On Thursday he will go up to the Father. He does not leave us a map; he leaves us himself. To follow him is to be on the way to the Father's house.

Paul says, “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” (1 Corinthians 9:16). The apostles could not keep silent. They gave their lives for Christ. Like the righteous in the Book of Wisdom, they now stand with great confidence before God.

Each of us can be a little apostle. We can say to a friend, ‘Come and see.’ Let us ask Sts Philip and James for their courage and their faith. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Philip found Jesus and ran to his friend: ‘Come and see!’

Who is a friend you can bring to Jesus? Invite them to church, or tell them a story about Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the way, the truth and the life, and whoever sees you sees the Father. On the feast of your apostles Philip and James, keep us in the faith they proclaimed and sealed with their lives. Calm our troubled hearts, show us the Father, and give us the courage to say to our friends, Come and see. Make us living stones in your Church, clean within and without, until we reach the dwelling place you have gone to prepare for us. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church keeps the feast of two apostles, Philip and James, and the readings trace the road of an apostle from deliverance to mission. Israel on the far shore of the sea sings, “I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously.” Wisdom pictures the righteous, once mocked, standing with great confidence before those who despised them. Paul says, “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel,” and tells how he became all things to all people. In John, Jesus comforts his disciples on the night before his death and answers Thomas: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.” The words to watch are song, confidence and way. The apostles sang of a victory that was not theirs, endured the scorn of the world, and spent their lives showing others the way that is a person.

Exodus 15:1–6 · I will sing to the Lord

Setting.

Exodus 14 tells how Israel, pursued by Pharaoh’s army, passed through the sea on dry ground while the waters returned upon the Egyptians. Chapter 15 answers that deliverance with song. Many scholars consider this “Song of the Sea” one of the oldest poems in the Bible. A short refrain sung by Miriam and the women with tambourines closes the chapter (15:20–21).

Israel did nothing to win this victory: they were told to “stand firm” and “keep still” (14:13–14). The song is therefore pure praise. It is a song about God, not about the singers.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the opening cry: I will sing; horse and rider thrown into the sea.
  • vv. 2–3: who the Lord is: my strength, my salvation, my God, my father’s God; a warrior, whose name is the Lord.
  • vv. 4–5: what he has done: chariots and army sunk like a stone.
  • v. 6: the right hand of the Lord, glorious in power.

Key words.

  • gā’ōh gā’â, he has triumphed gloriously: Literally “he has risen up high”, a Hebrew doubling for emphasis. God is exalted above every power that set itself against him.
  • yĕšû‘â, salvation: “He has become my salvation” (v. 2). The noun comes from the same root as the name Yehoshua, Joshua, Jesus: “the Lord saves”.
  • ’îš milḥāmâ, warrior: Literally “a man of war”. The image is of God fighting for those who cannot fight for themselves. The Lord’s war ends in a song, not in boasting.

Verse by verse.

““I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea.” (15:1)

The first song of the redeemed in the Bible. The Book of Revelation shows those who have conquered singing “the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb” (Revelation 15:3).

“The Lord is my strength and my might, and he has become my salvation; this is my God, and I will praise him, my father’s God,” (15:2)

Personal and ancestral at once: “my God” and “my father’s God”. The faith received from the fathers becomes the singer’s own.

“The Lord is a warrior; the Lord is his name.” (15:3)

The verse joins power and name. The name revealed to Moses at the bush (3:14–15) is now known by what God has done.

“your right hand, O Lord, shattered the enemy.” (15:6)

The right hand is the hand of strength and action. Christians confess that the Risen Christ is seated at the right hand of the Father.

How the Church reads it.

In the Easter Vigil of the Roman rite this song follows the reading of the crossing of the sea, because the exodus is the great figure of Christ’s passage from death to life and of our passage through the waters of Baptism. The Catechism teaches that the crossing of the Red Sea, a true liberation of Israel, announces the liberation wrought by Baptism (CCC 1221). The apostles were the first to sing the new song: the enemy overthrown was not an army but death itself.

Wisdom 5:1–7 · The righteous will stand with great confidence

Setting.

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, most likely in Alexandria, in the century or so before Christ. Chapter 2 gave the speech of the ungodly, who plot against the righteous man because his life rebukes theirs: “let us test what will happen at the end of his life; for if the righteous man is God’s child, he will help him” (2:17–18). The Church has always heard in those words the mockery at the foot of the Cross (Matthew 27:43).

Chapter 5 shows the reversal at the judgment. The same scoffers now see the righteous standing with confidence, and they confess their mistake. This is one of the clearest Old Testament witnesses to the hope that God will vindicate the just beyond death.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the righteous stands with confidence; the unrighteous are shaken at his unexpected salvation.
  • vv. 3–5: their confession: we mocked him; why is he counted among the children of God?
  • vv. 6–7: we strayed from the way of truth; we never knew the way of the Lord.

Key words.

  • parrēsia, confidence: Boldness, openness of speech, the frankness of one who has nothing to hide. The righteous, once silenced, now stands before his accusers without fear. In Acts the apostles preach “with all boldness” (Acts 4:29, 31).
  • huioi theou, children of God: The scoffers had denied that the righteous man was God’s child (2:18); now they see him numbered among the children of God. Jesus calls the peacemakers “children of God” (Matthew 5:9).
  • hodos, way: The scoffers confess that they did not know “the way of the Lord” (v. 7). Today’s Gospel answers them: Jesus is the way.

Verse by verse.

“Then the righteous will stand with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed them” (5:1)

To “stand” is the posture of one acquitted at the judgment. The apostles, once flogged and scorned, stand now in glory.

“they will be amazed at the unexpected salvation of the righteous.” (5:2)

Unexpected to the scoffers, not to God. They had thought that the end of the righteous was dishonour.

“We thought that their lives were madness and that their end was without honor.” (5:4)

The world often judges the saints this way. St Paul writes, “We are fools for the sake of Christ” (1 Corinthians 4:10).

“So it was we who strayed from the way of truth,” (5:6)

The confession comes too late for those who refused, but it is written for us while there is time.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this passage on the feasts of martyrs and apostles because it describes their story: rejected in this world, vindicated by God. The apostles, says the Catechism, were chosen as witnesses of the Resurrection and became the foundation stones of the Church (CCC 642). Ancient tradition holds that both Philip and James gave their lives for Christ. Their confidence was not in their own strength but in the God who raised Jesus, the Righteous One.

1 Corinthians 9:16–23 · Woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel

Setting.

Paul founded the Church at Corinth around the year 50 and wrote this letter a few years later from Ephesus. In chapters 8–10 he deals with food offered to idols, and urges the “strong” to give up their rights for the sake of the weak. Chapter 9 is his own example: as an apostle he had the right to be supported by the community, but he gave it up so as not to hinder the Gospel.

Today’s verses explain why. Preaching is not a career Paul chose; it is a charge laid on him. His only “reward” is to offer the Gospel without charge, and to become all things to all people for its sake.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–17: an obligation is laid on me; woe to me if I do not preach.
  • v. 18: my reward: to preach without charge.
  • v. 19: a slave to all, to win more.
  • vv. 20–22: to the Jews as a Jew; to those outside the law as one outside it; to the weak, weak.
  • v. 23: all for the sake of the gospel, to share in its blessings.

Key words.

  • anankē, obligation, necessity: A compulsion laid on him from outside. Paul did not volunteer; the Risen Lord seized him (Philippians 3:12).
  • oikonomia, commission, stewardship: The charge of a steward who manages his master’s house. Paul is a steward of the mysteries of God (4:1), not the owner of the Gospel.
  • kerdainō, to win, to gain: The word of the merchant who makes a profit, used five times in vv. 19–22. The profit Paul seeks is people won for Christ.

Verse by verse.

“woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” (9:16)

The apostle cannot keep silent. The prophets felt the same: Jeremiah says the word was like a fire shut up in his bones (Jeremiah 20:9).

“but if not of my own will, I am entrusted with a commission.” (9:17)

A steward does not own what he hands out. Paul’s only reward (v. 18) is to give the Gospel without payment, as it was given to him.

“I have made myself a slave to all, so that I might win more of them.” (9:19)

He describes his mission in the words of the Lord, who came “not to be served but to serve” (Mark 10:45).

“am under Christ’s law” (9:21)

Paul’s flexibility is not lawlessness. He is bound by the law of Christ, which is love (Galatians 6:2).

“I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” (9:22)

Not changing the Gospel to please people, but changing himself to reach them.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, writing to St Jerome, explains that Paul became all things to all not with the cunning of a deceiver but with the love of one who feels with others (in substance). The Second Vatican Council quotes Paul’s “woe to me” when it teaches that the Church is missionary by her very nature (Ad Gentes 1–2), and St Paul VI in Evangelii Nuntiandi (1975) says that the Church exists in order to evangelize (no. 14). Philip, who brought Nathanael to Jesus (John 1:45–46) and brought the Greeks who wished to see him (12:20–22), is an apostle of this kind: one who leads others to the Lord.

John 14:1–6 · I am the way, and the truth, and the life

Setting.

The scene is the Last Supper. Jesus has washed his disciples’ feet, Judas has gone out into the night, and Jesus has said that he is going where they cannot follow now, and that Peter will deny him (13:36–38). The disciples are afraid. Today’s words begin the long farewell discourse of John 14–17.

Two apostles speak in this chapter. Thomas asks about the way (14:5), and just after our reading Philip says, “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied” (14:8). Today’s feast of St Philip and the Gospel’s question from Thomas, our father in faith, meet in the same conversation.

Structure.

  • v. 1: do not let your hearts be troubled; believe in God and in me.
  • vv. 2–3: many dwelling places in the Father’s house; I go to prepare a place and will come again.
  • v. 4: you know the way.
  • v. 5: Thomas: we do not know where you are going; how can we know the way?
  • v. 6: I am the way, and the truth, and the life; no one comes to the Father except through me.

Key words.

  • tarassō, to trouble, to disturb: The same verb is used of Jesus himself, troubled in spirit before Lazarus’s tomb (11:33) and at the betrayal (13:21). He who was troubled for us tells us not to be troubled.
  • monai, dwelling places: From the verb menō, “to remain, abide”, one of John’s key words. The Father’s house is not a place of temporary lodging but of abiding.
  • hodos, way: Thomas asks for a road; Jesus gives himself. The first Christians called their faith “the Way” (Acts 9:2).

Verse by verse.

““Do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me.” (14:1)

Faith in Jesus is placed beside faith in God. The comfort Jesus gives is not a promise that nothing hard will happen, but trust in a person.

“In my Father’s house there are many dwelling places.” (14:2)

There is room. No one who comes to the Father through Christ is turned away for lack of space.

“I will come again and will take you to myself, so that where I am, there you may be also.” (14:3)

The goal of the Christian life is not a place but being with Christ.

“Thomas said to him, “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?”” (14:5)

Thomas speaks for every honest disciple. His question draws out one of the greatest sayings in the Gospel. The same Thomas will later confess, “My Lord and my God!” (20:28).

“Jesus said to him, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” (14:6)

He is the way because he is the truth, God’s faithful revelation, and the life, God’s own life shared with us.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine summarizes this verse: as God, Christ is the homeland we are going to; as man, he is the way by which we go (in substance). The Catechism opens its section on life in Christ by saying that the first and last point of reference of catechesis will always be Jesus Christ himself, “the way, and the truth, and the life” (CCC 1698). The Church teaches that Christ is the one mediator and way of salvation, and also that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know him may be saved by his grace working in ways known to God (CCC 846–848).

Questions people ask

Which James is this?

The Roman tradition identifies today’s apostle as James the son of Alphaeus (Matthew 10:3), often called James the Less, and has also identified him with James “the brother of the Lord”, leader of the Church in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19). Many Eastern traditions and many modern scholars distinguish the two. The Church does not require one view. He is not James the son of Zebedee, the brother of John.

Why are Philip and James celebrated together?

In the Roman tradition their relics were placed together in the Basilica of the Twelve Apostles in Rome in the sixth century, and so they came to share a feast. Many calendars, ours among them, keep them together.

Does “No one comes to the Father except through me” mean that non-Christians cannot be saved?

Jesus is the one way to the Father, and all who are saved are saved through him. But the Church teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ or his Church, yet seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will as their conscience shows it, may attain salvation by his grace (Lumen Gentium 16; CCC 847). This does not lessen the Church’s duty to proclaim Christ, as Paul says today: “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!”

For the Syro-Malabar reader

For the Syro-Malabar Church, today’s Gospel is a family conversation. Thomas, whom we call our father in faith, asks the question, and the Lord answers with the words that sum up the Gospel. The ancient name of our tradition, Mar Thoma Margam, the Way or Law of Thomas, recalls that the faith we received is not a set of ideas but a road walked behind Christ.

The feast of apostles reminds us that we are an apostolic Church. Philip and James, like Thomas, left everything, carried the Gospel far from home, and, as ancient tradition holds, gave their lives for it. In this season of Resurrection we pray that the Church in India may proclaim Christ with the same confidence and love.

For study and teaching

  • Israel sang after a victory they did nothing to win. When did I last thank God for a deliverance that was entirely his gift?
  • Wisdom describes the righteous mocked as fools. Am I ready to be thought foolish for Christ?
  • Paul says, “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” How do I share my faith with those around me?
  • Thomas asked, “How can we know the way?” What is my honest question to the Lord today, and do I trust him to answer?
