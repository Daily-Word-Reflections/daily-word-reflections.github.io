Word for the day

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much; and whoever is dishonest in a very little is dishonest also in much.” Luke 16:10

Reflection

Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians received his preaching “not as a human word but as what it really is, God's word, which is also at work in you believers.” The word of God, received in faith, works in us.

Jesus teaches faithfulness in small things: honest dealings, trustworthy use of money, an undivided heart. “No slave can serve two masters… You cannot serve God and wealth.” As Christmas approaches, with its spending and celebrations, the Gospel asks whom we really serve.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians received the word of God, which you heard from us, not as a human word but as what it really is, God's word, which is also at work in you believers (1 Thessalonians 2:13). The word of God is not only information; it is power at work in us.

How do we receive Scripture? As an old book? As a duty? Or as God speaking today? When we hear the readings in the Holy Qurbana with faith, the word works in us: consoling, correcting, calling.

The Thessalonians paid a price for receiving the word. They suffered from their own compatriots, as the churches in Judea had. Faithfulness to God's word sometimes costs us friends, opportunities or comfort.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks of faithfulness in small things: whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much; and whoever is dishonest in a very little is dishonest also in much. No slave can serve two masters; you cannot serve God and wealth (Luke 16:10–13).

Faithfulness is tested in small daily matters: an honest bill, a promise kept, a prayer said even when tired, a tithe given, overtime not falsely claimed. These small things reveal whom we really serve.

This Nombu, let us read a small passage of the Gospel each day, receive it as God's own word, and ask: what does this mean for me today? And let us examine our faithfulness in little things. The God who comes at Christmas is faithful; let us be faithful to him. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's two readings are about being faithful with what has been entrusted to us. Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians kept the word of God even under pressure. Jesus says that faithfulness is learned in very small things, and that no one can have two masters. The word of God and the things of daily life make the same demand: a single heart.

1 Thessalonians 2:13–17 · Not a human word

Setting.

This is very probably the earliest of Paul's letters, written about AD 50 or 51, from Corinth, to a young Church he had to leave in a hurry (Acts 17:1–10). The whole of chapter 2 is a look back at his time with them. He is anxious for them, and he is writing with relief and affection.

Structure.

  • v. 13: thanksgiving: they received the word as God's word.
  • vv. 14–16: they have suffered as the churches in Judea suffered; Paul's sharp words against those who opposed the mission.
  • v. 17: Paul's longing to see them.

Key words.

  • paralabontes / edexasthe, received / accepted: Two different verbs in v. 13. The first is the usual word for receiving something handed on, a tradition. The second means to welcome, as one welcomes a guest. The Gospel is both handed on and welcomed from the heart.
  • energeitai, is at work: The English word "energy" comes from the same root. The word of God does not lie still in those who believe. It keeps on working.
  • aporphanisthentes, made orphans: A tender and unusual word (v. 17). Paul has already called himself a nurse and a father to them (2:7, 11). Now he says that being apart from them has left him an orphan.

Verse by verse.

“you accepted it not as a human word but as what it really is, God’s word, which is also at work in you believers.” (2:13)

Paul knows that he is the human voice, yet he insists that it is God who speaks. This is one reason we stand when the Gospel is proclaimed: another is speaking.

“you suffered the same things from your own compatriots” (2:14)

The Thessalonians, mostly Gentiles, were being troubled by their own neighbours. Sharing in suffering makes them one with the mother churches far away in Judea. On the hard words that follow, see the question below.

“we longed with great eagerness to see you face to face.” (2:17)

The teacher of the Gospel is not a lecturer who has moved on. Paul's theology and his affection cannot be separated.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism applies 2:13 to the whole of Scripture: in it the Church finds her nourishment and strength, because she welcomes it not as a human word but as what it really is, the word of God (CCC 104; Dei Verbum 24). The Second Vatican Council also uses this verse to describe the sense of faith of the whole people of God (Lumen Gentium 12).

Luke 16:10–13 · Faithful in a very little

Setting.

These sayings follow the parable of the dishonest manager (16:1–9), which ended with the puzzling advice to "make friends for yourselves by means of dishonest wealth". Luke now gathers sayings about money that explain how a disciple should use it. Later in the same chapter comes the rich man and Lazarus (16:19–31).

Structure.

  • v. 10: the principle: little and much.
  • vv. 11–12: two applications: dishonest wealth and true riches; what belongs to another and what is your own.
  • v. 13: the conclusion: two masters are impossible.

Key words.

  • pistos, faithful, trustworthy: In v. 11 Luke plays on this word: if you are not pistoi with money, who will pisteusei, entrust, the true riches to you? Trust is earned through faithfulness.
  • mamōnas, wealth: An Aramaic word for wealth or property, which the Gospels keep untranslated in Greek, so that older English Bibles say "mammon". Jesus speaks of it almost as of a person, a rival lord.
  • douleuein, to serve as a slave: This is more than having a job. A slave belongs to a master completely. So the saying is not about owning things, but about who owns us.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much” (16:10)

Character is not built on great occasions. It is built in the small honesty of ordinary days, and great occasions only show what is there.

“who will entrust to you the true riches?” (16:11)

Money, Jesus says, is a small thing, a test. The true riches are the goods of the kingdom. How we handle the first shows whether we can be given the second.

“You cannot serve God and wealth.” (16:13)

Jesus does not say that it is difficult; he says that it cannot be done. There is room for only one Lord. The same saying stands in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 6:24).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists money among the things that people can honour in the place of God, and quotes this saying under the sin of idolatry (CCC 2113). It also quotes St John Chrysostom, who says that not to let the poor share in our goods is to steal from them (CCC 2446). Chrysostom says this in his sermons on Lazarus, the story that follows today's Gospel in Luke.

Questions people ask

Does 1 Thessalonians 2:14–16 blame the Jewish people?

No, and it must not be read that way. Paul is himself a Jew (Romans 11:1). He is writing in the heat of a particular conflict about those in Judea who persecuted the first Christians and those who were blocking his mission. The meaning of "God’s wrath has overtaken them at last" is debated by scholars, but it is not a judgment on a whole people. Paul's last word is in Romans: "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29). The Church teaches that the Passion cannot be charged against all Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597), and that sinners were the authors of Christ's sufferings (CCC 598).
