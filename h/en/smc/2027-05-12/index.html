Word for the day

“And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” John 17:3

Reflection

On the night before he died, at the end of the Last Supper, Jesus lifted his eyes to heaven and prayed aloud before his disciples. The Church calls this prayer, which fills the seventeenth chapter of John, the prayer of the Hour or the high-priestly prayer. “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you.” The hour is the hour of the Cross, and Jesus calls it glory. On the Cross the love of the Father and the Son is shown to the world, and through the Cross the Son returns to the glory he had “before the world existed”. We read this prayer in the days after the Ascension because the prayer has been answered: the Son has been glorified and has gone back to the Father. But the prayer has not ended. The Letter to the Hebrews says that the Risen Lord “always lives to make intercession” for us (Hebrews 7:25). What Jesus prayed in the upper room he goes on praying at the Father's right hand, and in these nine days the Church in the upper room joins her prayer to his.

At the centre of the prayer is a definition: “And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” Eternal life is not first of all a long life after death; it is a relationship that begins now. In the Bible to know someone is more than to know facts about him; it is to love him, to share his life, as husband and wife know each other. So eternal life is to know the Father and the Son in the love of the Holy Spirit. A child preparing for First Holy Communion who learns to talk to Jesus has begun eternal life. An old grandmother saying her rosary in the evening is living it. Jesus adds, “I glorified you on earth by finishing the work that you gave me to do.” His whole life was obedience, and that obedience is his glory. We too glorify God not by great deeds but by doing faithfully the work he gives us, in the kitchen, the classroom, the hospital ward, the field.

The First Letter of John shows what that knowledge looks like in practice. “No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God lives in us, and his love is perfected in us.” We cannot see God, but we can see our brother, and in loving him the invisible God becomes present. Then comes the link with Pentecost: “By this we know that we abide in him and he in us, because he has given us of his Spirit.” The Spirit is the gift that makes this love possible. John puts it in words that sum up the whole Gospel: “God is love, and those who abide in love abide in God, and God abides in them.” And he draws a bold consequence: “There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear.” Many Christians live with a fearful religion, afraid of God as of a harsh judge. John says that the closer we come to God's love, the less room fear has. But he will not let us escape into sweet feelings: “Those who say, ‘I love God,’ and hate their brothers or sisters, are liars.” On this day of the novena we ask the Spirit for a love that is real: love that casts out fear before God and love that reaches the brother we can see.

Full homily

Dear friends, on the night before he died, Jesus lifted his eyes to heaven and prayed: “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you.” The hour was the Cross, and Jesus called it glory, because on the Cross God's love was shown to the world.

We read this prayer after the Ascension because it has been answered: Jesus has returned to the glory of the Father. But he has not stopped praying. He “always lives to make intercession” for us. In this novena, we join our prayer to his.

Jesus says: “This is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” Eternal life begins now. To know God is not only to know facts about him; it is to love him and live with him, as a friend. A child who talks to Jesus has begun eternal life.

John tells us: “God is love, and those who abide in love abide in God.” God has given us his Spirit so that we can love. And “perfect love casts out fear.” We do not need to live afraid of God. But John adds: if you say you love God and hate your brother, you are a liar. Love for God must reach the brother we can see.

Let us ask the Holy Spirit today for this love: love that takes away fear, and love that reaches the people around us. Come, Holy Spirit! Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, John says: God is love! God loves you more than anyone can.

When we love one another, God lives in us. And when we know how much God loves us, we are not afraid. Today, say: ‘Jesus, I love you, and I am not afraid!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on the night of your Passion you lifted your eyes to heaven and prayed for us, and now at the Father's right hand you live for ever to intercede for us. Grant us eternal life: to know the Father, the only true God, and you whom he has sent. Pour into our hearts the Spirit of love, so that perfect love may cast out all fear, and we may love the brothers and sisters we can see. Let us glorify the Father by finishing the work he gives us each day. With Mary, who abode in your love, we pray: come, Holy Spirit. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

No one has ever seen God, says John, but when we love one another God lives in us, and he has given us of his Spirit. In the Gospel Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven at the hour of his glory and says what eternal life is: to know the Father and the One he has sent. In this week between the Ascension and Pentecost the two readings belong together. The Son has returned to the glory he had before the world began, and the Spirit he sends teaches us to know God by loving.

1 John 4:12–21 · God is love; he has given us of his Spirit

Setting.

The First Letter of John comes from the same circle as the Fourth Gospel. Tradition links it with the apostle John; many scholars think it was written by an elder of the community that treasured his teaching. It is written to churches shaken by a division: some had left (2:19), denying that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:2).

Against them the letter holds together three things: right faith in Jesus, love for one another, and the gift of the Spirit. Today’s passage weaves all three.

Structure.

  • v. 12: no one has seen God; if we love, God lives in us.
  • vv. 13–16: how we know we abide in him: the Spirit, the confession of Jesus, love. “God is love.”
  • vv. 17–18: love made perfect gives boldness; perfect love casts out fear.
  • vv. 19–21: we love because he first loved us; no love for God without love for the brother or sister.

Key words.

  • menō, abide: The great word of John’s writings, used again and again in this passage. To abide is to stay, to make one’s home. God makes his home in us, and we in him.
  • agapē, love: “God is love” (vv. 8, 16). John does not say only that God loves, but that love is what God is. Love here is not first our feeling but God’s self-giving, seen in the sending of the Son (4:9–10).
  • phobos, fear: The fear John speaks of “has to do with punishment”, the fear of a slave before a master. It is not the holy fear of God that Scripture praises as the beginning of wisdom. The more we love, the less we cower.

Verse by verse.

“No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God lives in us, and his love is perfected in us.” (4:12)

The same words open the Gospel’s statement that the only Son has made the Father known (John 1:18). The God no one has seen becomes visible, in a way, in a community that loves.

“God is love, and those who abide in love abide in God, and God abides in them.” (4:16)

Perhaps the most famous sentence in the letter. It does not make love into God; it says that the God revealed in Jesus is love, and that whoever truly loves is living in him.

“We love because he first loved us.” (4:19)

Textual note: some later manuscripts add an object, “we love him” or “we love God”. The shorter reading, followed by the NRSV, is wider: every true love in us, for God and for our neighbour, is an answer to his love.

How the Church reads it.

Pope Benedict XVI began his first encyclical with v. 16: “God is love, and those who abide in love abide in God, and God abides in them”; these words, he wrote, express with remarkable clarity the heart of the Christian faith (Deus Caritas Est 1). The Catechism teaches that God’s very being is love (CCC 221). St Augustine, preaching on this letter, gave his short rule, “Love, and do what you will”: when the root within is love, nothing but good can come from it (in substance, Homilies on the First Letter of John 7).

John 17:1–5 · This is eternal life: that they may know you

Setting.

The long discourse at the Last Supper (chapters 13–16) ends, and Jesus turns from his disciples to his Father. He prays aloud, first for himself (17:1–5), then for his disciples (17:6–19), then for all who will believe through their word (17:20–26). Christian tradition calls this the “priestly” prayer of Jesus (CCC 2747).

Throughout John’s Gospel the “hour” has been awaited. At Cana Jesus said, “My hour has not yet come” (2:4). Now he says, “the hour has come”. It is the hour of the Cross, which John sees as the hour of glory.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Jesus looks up: “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son.”
  • v. 2: authority over all people, to give eternal life.
  • v. 3: what eternal life is.
  • v. 4: the work on earth finished.
  • v. 5: the glory he had before the world existed.

Key words.

  • hōra, hour: Not a clock hour, but the appointed time of the Father’s plan: the Passion, death, Resurrection and return to the Father, seen as one movement.
  • doxazō, glorify: In the Old Testament God’s glory (kābôd) is his weighty, shining presence. In John the Cross is the place where that glory is most fully seen, because there love goes to the end (13:1).
  • ginōskō, know: Biblical knowing is not only information but personal communion. Eternal life is not a reward added after we know God; knowing him is eternal life, already begun.

Verse by verse.

“he looked up to heaven and said, “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you,” (17:1)

The Son does not seek his own glory; he asks to be glorified so that he may glorify the Father. Their glory is one.

“And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” (17:3)

Some in the fourth century used this verse to deny that the Son is truly God. St Augustine answered that we should read it as one confession: that they may know you and Jesus Christ whom you have sent, as the only true God (in substance, Tractates on John 105). The Son is named with the Father as the one whom to know is life.

“So now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had in your presence before the world existed.” (17:5)

The Son existed before the world, as the Prologue says (1:1). He asks to return to that glory, now as the Word made flesh. In the Ascension our human nature enters the glory of God with him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that when “his hour” came, Jesus prayed to the Father, and that this prayer of the hour fills the end times and carries them toward their consummation (CCC 2746–2749). Last Thursday we kept the Ascension; on Sunday the Spirit will come. Between them the Church prays with the Son’s own prayer: that we may know the Father, and so live.

For study and teaching

  • John says that the God no one has seen becomes visible in our love for one another. Where can others see God in my family this week?
  • Jesus says eternal life is to know the Father. Am I growing in knowing God, or only in knowing about him?
