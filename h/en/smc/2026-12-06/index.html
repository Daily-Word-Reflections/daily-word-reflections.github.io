Word for the day

“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Then the angel departed from her.” Luke 1:38

Reflection

The second Sunday of the season brings us to the heart of the Annunciation: Gabriel is sent to Nazareth, to a virgin betrothed to Joseph, of the house of David. “Greetings, favoured one! The Lord is with you.” Mary is troubled, asks how this can be, and receives the answer: “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” Then she says the word on which our salvation turns: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”

The Old Testament reading offers a surprising contrast. Balaam, a foreign seer hired to curse Israel, cannot see the angel of the Lord standing in his path; even his donkey sees more than he does. Only when the Lord opens his eyes does he bow down, and then he says: “The word God puts in my mouth, that is what I must say.” Balaam obeys God's word reluctantly; Mary welcomes it freely. Balaam later prophesies, “A star shall come out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17); Mary gives birth to that Star.

Isaiah promises that God will blot out transgressions and “pour my spirit upon your descendants.” At Nazareth that promise begins: the Spirit comes upon Mary, and the Word becomes flesh in her womb. Paul asks the Colossians to pray “that God will open to us a door for the word, that we may declare the mystery of Christ.” Mary is the first open door for the Word.

Our Church loves Mary because she shows what every Christian is called to be: a listener who becomes a bearer of Christ. In this season, her “yes” is the model for ours.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, on this second Sunday of Annunciation we enter the house of Nazareth. The angel Gabriel greets a young woman: “Greetings, favoured one! The Lord is with you.” He tells her she will bear a son, the Son of the Most High, whose kingdom will have no end.

Mary is not a passive listener. She asks, “How can this be?” The angel explains: the Holy Spirit will come upon you. And then Mary says the most important word ever spoken by a human being: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” With that yes, God became man.

The first reading tells a strange story. Balaam, a famous prophet, rides along a road and cannot see the angel blocking his path. His donkey sees the angel; the prophet does not. Only when God opens his eyes does Balaam understand. How often we are like Balaam: busy, blind, going our own way, while God stands in front of us.

Mary is the opposite of Balaam. She sees, she listens, she says yes. Isaiah promised: “I will pour my spirit upon your descendants.” At Nazareth that promise came true. And the same Spirit comes upon the bread and wine at every Qurbana and upon us.

This week, let us ask God to open our eyes, and let us learn Mary's word: “Let it be with me according to your word.” Let us pray the Angelus each day and say yes to God in one concrete thing. Then Christ will be born again in us. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the second Sunday of Suvara the Church hears the announcement itself. The readings prepare for it by asking who is able to receive a word from God. In Numbers, an angel stands in the road, and a famous seer cannot see him though his donkey can. In Isaiah, God promises to pour out his Spirit on a guilty and thirsty people. In Colossians, Paul asks for a door for the word. In Luke, the angel comes to a young woman in Nazareth, she listens, and the Word becomes flesh within her. The key words are seeing, pouring out and consenting.

Numbers 22:20–35 · The Lord opened the eyes of Balaam

Setting.

Israel has reached the plains of Moab, near the end of the desert journey (22:1). Balak, king of Moab, is afraid of them, and sends for Balaam son of Beor, a famous seer from far away, to curse them (22:2–6). Balaam is not an Israelite. An inscription found in 1967 at Deir Alla in Jordan speaks of a seer called Balaam son of Beor, so his name was known outside the Bible.

God has first told Balaam not to go (22:12). In today’s reading he allows it (v. 20), then is angry that he goes (v. 22). Scholars note the tension: the story may join older traditions, and it also suggests that God sees the heart of a man who keeps asking until he hears the answer he wants. Later Scripture remembers him as one “who loved the wages of doing wrong” (2 Peter 2:15; compare Jude 11).

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: God’s permission, with one condition.
  • vv. 22–27: the angel blocks the road three times, in places that grow narrower: the field, the lane between walls, the place with no room to turn.
  • vv. 28–30: the Lord opens the donkey’s mouth.
  • vv. 31–35: the Lord opens Balaam’s eyes, and the condition is repeated.

Key words.

  • lĕśāṭān, as an adversary (vv. 22, 32): Śāṭān here is an ordinary noun: someone who blocks the way or opposes. It is used of the angel of the Lord himself. An opposition can be a mercy, because this one saves Balaam’s life.
  • mal’ak YHWH, the angel of the Lord: God’s messenger, whose words are God’s own (compare v. 35 with v. 20). The same kind of visit, with a word that must be received, will come to Mary, though not with a sword.
  • wayĕgal, he opened (literally, uncovered): The verb used for the eyes in v. 31. Balaam is a professional seer. Later he will call himself “the man whose eye is clear” (24:3). Yet sight is God’s gift, as the opening of the donkey’s mouth has already shown.

Verse by verse.

“If the men have come to summon you, get up and go with them; but do only what I tell you to do.” (22:20)

This is the condition of the whole story. Balaam may go, but the word will not be his to sell.

“The donkey saw the angel of the Lord standing in the road, with a drawn sword in his hand” (22:23)

The story is full of humour. The man paid to see the unseen is the only one who cannot see. Three times the donkey saves him, and three times he strikes it.

“Then the Lord opened the mouth of the donkey, and it said to Balaam, “What have I done to you, that you have struck me these three times?”” (22:28)

The animal’s question is more reasonable than its master’s reply. God can speak by the lowliest means when those who should hear have stopped listening.

“Then the Lord opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the angel of the Lord standing in the road” (22:31)

Balaam’s first words now are “I have sinned” (v. 34). He has seen what he had missed, and he must carry only God’s word: in chapters 23–24 he blesses the people he was paid to curse.

How the Church reads it.

2 Peter 2:15–16 uses this story as a warning against those who preach for gain. Balaam’s later oracle, “a star shall come out of Jacob” (24:17), was read by Jews and Christians alike as pointing to the Messiah, and Origen connects it with the star of the Magi (in substance). The Church sees here the lesson Jesus gives thanks for: God reveals himself to the lowly (Luke 10:21). On this Sunday the contrast is clear: the seer who could not see the angel, and the unknown girl who receives him.

Isaiah 43:25–44:5 · I will pour my spirit upon your descendants

Setting.

These verses speak to the exiles in Babylon (Isaiah 40–55). They knew why Jerusalem had fallen, and they wondered if there could be any future for a people so guilty. The passage has two movements. First comes a courtroom scene in which God proves the case against Israel and then forgives the debt. Then comes a promise, marked by “But now hear” (44:1), of water, Spirit and new growth.

Structure.

  • 43:25: God blots out sin “for my own sake”.
  • 43:26–28: the trial: from the first ancestor on, Israel has sinned, and the exile has followed.
  • 44:1–2: “Do not fear”: chosen and formed from the womb.
  • 44:3–4: water on thirsty land, and the Spirit on the children.
  • 44:5: people will gladly claim the name of the Lord.

Key words.

  • mōḥeh, who blots out: The verb for wiping writing from a scroll or wiping a dish clean. The record is not filed away but erased.
  • Yĕšurûn, Jeshurun: A poetic name for Israel, probably from a root meaning “upright” (Deuteronomy 32:15; 33:5, 26). It is a gentle name to use for a guilty people: God calls them what he intends them to be.
  • ’eṣṣōq rûḥî, I will pour my spirit: The verb is used for pouring out water or oil. The same promise, in a different wording, is in Joel 2:28 and is quoted at Pentecost (Acts 2:17).

Verse by verse.

“I, I am He who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins.” (43:25)

“For my own sake”: forgiveness is grounded in who God is, not in what Israel can offer. A note on translation: In v. 28 the Hebrew has “I profaned the princes of the sanctuary”, which the NRSV follows, while the ancient Greek has the princes profaning the holy things, close to the Malayalam POC. Both describe the same catastrophe.

“For I will pour water on the thirsty land, and streams on the dry ground; I will pour my spirit upon your descendants, and my blessing on your offspring.” (44:3)

In dry country, water means life. Water and the Spirit stand side by side in this verse, as they will in John 3:5 and in the font of baptism.

“yet another will write on the hand, “The Lord’s,”” (44:5)

A mark on the hand showed whose a person was. The exile’s shame becomes something people will be glad to claim. These may be foreigners joining Israel or Israelites returning to their name; either way, belonging is now a joy.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only God forgives sins (CCC 1441) and that the prophets’ promises of the Spirit look forward to the Messiah and to the whole messianic people (CCC 715, 1287). It explains the seal of Confirmation with the same picture as 44:5: a seal is a sign of ownership, as soldiers were marked with their leader’s seal (CCC 1295). On a Sunday of Suvara, the promise of Spirit on dry ground prepares for the Spirit who comes upon Mary (Luke 1:35).

Colossians 4:2–6 · A door for the word

Setting.

These are the last instructions of the letter, before the greetings (4:7–18). After teaching the household about daily life (3:18–4:1), Paul turns to the two things that link the Christian to God and to those outside: prayer and speech. He writes in chains: “for which I am in prison”. The letter is sent in his name (4:18); on the question of authorship, Catholic scholars differ.

Structure.

  • v. 2: keep praying, alert and thankful.
  • vv. 3–4: pray for us, that God may open a door.
  • v. 5: walk wisely toward outsiders.
  • v. 6: let your speech be gracious.

Key words.

  • proskartereite, devote yourselves: The word means to hold on firmly, to stay at something. Acts uses the same verb of the first community, devoted to prayer (Acts 1:14; 2:42).
  • thyra tou logou, a door for the word: A prisoner asks for a door, but not for his own release. The door he wants is for the message.
  • exagorazomenoi ton kairon, making the most of the time: Literally “buying up the time”, as a trader buys up what is scarce. The word for time is again kairos, the moment of opportunity.

Verse by verse.

“Devote yourselves to prayer, keeping alert in it with thanksgiving.” (4:2)

Alert prayer recalls the Lord’s word in the garden: “Keep awake and pray” (Mark 14:38). Thanksgiving keeps prayer from becoming only a list of needs.

“At the same time pray for us as well that God will open to us a door for the word, that we may declare the mystery of Christ, for which I am in prison,” (4:3)

“The mystery of Christ” is God’s plan, once hidden and now revealed: “Christ in you, the hope of glory” (1:27). Today’s Gospel shows that mystery beginning in the womb of Mary.

“Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how you ought to answer everyone.” (4:6)

“Gracious” is literally “in grace”. Salt brings out flavour and preserves, and it recalls Jesus’ word in Mark 9:50. The Malayalam POC gives the sense without the picture of salt. The Christian’s speech should have some taste to it, and should be kind without being empty.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism’s section on persevering prayer, “Pray constantly”, is built on texts like this one (CCC 2742–2745). The Second Vatican Council takes up Paul’s phrase in its decree on mission: wherever God opens a door for the word, the mystery of Christ is to be proclaimed to all (Ad Gentes 13). 1 Peter 3:15 gives the same advice: always be ready to answer, “with gentleness and reverence” (3:16).

Luke 1:26–38 · Here am I, the servant of the Lord

Setting.

Last Sunday’s Gospel told of Gabriel’s visit to Zechariah in the Temple. Luke now sets the second scene against it. Then, an old priest in Jerusalem; now, a young woman in Nazareth, a town the Old Testament never mentions. Then, a couple past child-bearing; now, a virgin. Then, doubt and silence; now, a question and consent.

Mary is “engaged”. A Jewish betrothal was a binding legal bond, and the woman normally stayed in her family’s home for a time before moving to her husband’s. Joseph is “of the house of David”, and this is how Jesus will belong legally to David’s line.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: place, people, and the sixth month.
  • vv. 28–29: the greeting, and Mary’s pondering.
  • vv. 30–33: the promise: Jesus, Son of the Most High, heir of David.
  • vv. 34–35: Mary’s question and the answer: the Holy Spirit.
  • vv. 36–37: the sign: Elizabeth; nothing is impossible with God.
  • v. 38: Mary’s consent.

Key words.

  • kecharitōmenē, favored one: A perfect passive participle of the verb from charis, grace: “you who have been graced, and remain so”. The verb is rare (elsewhere in the New Testament only at Ephesians 1:6). The angel uses it in place of her name, as if it were her name. The Latin has gratia plena and the Malayalam POC “full of God’s grace”.
  • episkiasei, will overshadow: This verb is used in the Greek of Exodus 40:35, where the cloud settles on the tent of meeting and God’s glory fills it. Luke uses it again at the Transfiguration (9:34). Mary becomes the new dwelling of God.
  • genoito, let it be: The Greek form is a wish, not mere resignation. Mary does not simply accept what will happen. She wants it. The Latin gives the word that has passed into every language: fiat.

Verse by verse.

“And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”” (1:28)

Chaire, “greetings”, was an ordinary greeting, but it means “rejoice”. Many scholars hear behind it the prophets’ call to daughter Zion (Zephaniah 3:14). “The Lord is with you” are words said in the Old Testament to those given a task beyond their strength.

“He will be great, and will be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God will give to him the throne of his ancestor David.” (1:32)

Here the promise to David in 2 Samuel 7:12–16 is taken up. At the same time v. 35 goes beyond it: he is “Son of God”, in a way no king of Israel ever was.

“Mary said to the angel, “How can this be, since I am a virgin?”” (1:34)

The Greek says “since I do not know a man”, as the Malayalam POC does. St Augustine and others took her question to mean that she had resolved to remain a virgin (in substance). Luke’s text does not say so directly, but what it clearly teaches is that the child’s conception is the work of the Spirit, without a human father (CCC 496–497).

“The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.” (1:35)

The promised Spirit of Isaiah 44:3 comes down here. Father, Son and Spirit are all named in this one scene.

“For nothing will be impossible with God.” (1:37)

An echo of what was said about Sarah: “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” (Genesis 18:14). Last Sunday’s Genesis story was the same promise.

“Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.”” (1:38)

“Servant” here means a slave woman, a title of humility and also of honour, like the “servants of the Lord” of Israel’s history. God does not take flesh without being welcomed.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Father willed the Incarnation to be preceded by the consent of the predestined mother (Lumen Gentium 56; CCC 488, 494). St Irenaeus, quoted there, says that the knot of Eve’s disobedience was untied by Mary’s obedience. St Ephrem the Syrian sings that death entered through Eve’s ear, and that through Mary’s ear the Word entered (in substance). St Bernard, in his fourth homily in praise of the Virgin Mother, pictures all creation waiting for her answer: “Answer quickly, O Virgin”. The Catechism sees in the angel’s greeting the Church’s growing understanding that Mary was “full of grace” from the first moment of her life (CCC 490–491).

Questions people ask

Did the donkey really speak?

The Church has given no ruling on this detail. The Church asks us first to consider what kind of writing we are reading (Dei Verbum 12). 2 Peter 2:16 mentions the event, and many readers take it as told. Others read the story as a vivid popular narrative with a touch of humour. Either way the teaching is the same: the prophet who thinks that he sees everything is corrected by the humblest of creatures, and he can say only what God gives him to say.

Is “full of grace” a good translation, and does this verse teach the Immaculate Conception?

“Favored one” and “full of grace” both translate one Greek word, which says that Mary has been and remains filled with God’s favour. The verse alone does not state the doctrine. The Church, reflecting on it over the centuries, came to understand that to be so graced for this mission, Mary was redeemed from the moment of her conception (CCC 490–492), and Pope Pius IX defined this in 1854. The feast comes this Tuesday, 8 December.

Could Mary have said no?

Yes. The Church insists that her consent was real: she was not used as an instrument but cooperated by faith and obedience (Lumen Gentium 56; CCC 494). The fullness of grace did not lessen her humanity. It set her fully able to answer God, as the angel’s courteous greeting and her careful question show.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the East Syriac year, Suvara means the announcement, and its Sundays are built around God’s messages to Zechariah, Mary and Joseph. Today’s Gospel is the heart of the season, and the Twenty-five Day Fast continues around it. The East Syriac tradition loves the Fathers’ poetry on this mystery. St Ephrem’s picture of the ear is a good one for us, who come to the Holy Qurbana first to listen: Mary heard, consented and bore the Word, and we hear the same Word proclaimed, and receive him at the altar. The Bema, the place of the word in the East Syriac tradition, reminds us that a Church must first be a listening Church.

For study and teaching

  • Balaam was a professional seer who could not see what his donkey saw. What might God be trying to show me through someone or something I look down on?
  • Isaiah promises the Spirit to a people who feel that they have no future. Where is the “dry ground” in my life or my family?
  • Paul, in prison, asks not for release but for a door for the word. What would I ask for if I were in his place?
  • Mary’s question and her consent stand side by side. What am I asking God at this time, and what would my “let it be” sound like?
