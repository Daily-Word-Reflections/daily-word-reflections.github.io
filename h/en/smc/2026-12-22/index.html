Word for the day

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” Galatians 4:4–5

Reflection

Paul gives the shortest Christmas creed: “When the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman.” The eternal Son receives a human mother; and through him we receive adoption, so that the Spirit in our hearts cries “Abba! Father!” We are no longer slaves but children and heirs.

John's Prologue continues: John the Baptist “came as a witness to testify to the light.” The true light came to his own, “and his own people did not accept him. But to all who received him… he gave power to become children of God.” Christmas is a choice: to receive or to refuse.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells the Galatians: when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children (Galatians 4:4–5).

Born of a woman. God did not drop from the sky as an adult. He chose a mother, a womb, a birth, a childhood. He entered our history at a particular time and place: in the days of Caesar Augustus, in Bethlehem of Judea. The fullness of time was the moment God chose.

And the purpose: so that we might receive adoption as children. Because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, Abba! Father! (Galatians 4:6). The Son became a son of Mary so that we might become sons and daughters of God.

In the Gospel John introduces John the Baptist: there was a man sent from God, whose name was John. He came as a witness to testify to the light. He himself was not the light (John 1:6–8). Then the sad line: he came to what was his own, and his own people did not accept him (John 1:11).

But the Gospel continues: to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave power to become children of God (John 1:12). Christmas is a question addressed to each of us: will I receive him?

Every family in Kerala is busy preparing: stars, cribs, cakes, new clothes. Good. But let us not be like Bethlehem, which had no room. Let us make room: a quiet hour of prayer, a good confession, reconciliation with someone. Then, receiving him, we will become what he came to make us: children of God. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus came so that we could be God's children. When you pray Our Father, remember that God is your Father!

Prayer

Father, in the fullness of time you sent your Son, born of a woman, to make us your children. Let me receive him and live as your child. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Three days before Christmas, both readings ask how the Son came and what his coming does to us. Paul says it in one sentence: God sent his Son, born of a woman, so that we might be adopted. John says it with an image: the true light came into the world, and those who received him became children of God. The two readings arrive at the same word: children.

Galatians 4:1–7 · Born of a woman, so that we might be children

Setting.

Paul writes to Gentile Christians in Galatia who are being urged by other teachers to take on circumcision and the whole Mosaic law. He has just told them that all who belong to Christ are “Abraham’s offspring, heirs according to the promise” (3:29). Now he explains what an heir is, with a picture from family law that everyone in his world understood.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the young heir owns everything, yet lives under guardians until the date his father has set.
  • v. 3: so were we, under “the elemental spirits of the world”.
  • vv. 4–5: the date arrives: God sends his Son.
  • vv. 6–7: God sends the Spirit of his Son, and the slave becomes a child and an heir.

Key words.

  • plērōma tou chronou, the fullness of time: Time is pictured as a vessel that God fills to the brim. The coming of the Son is not an accident of history but the date the Father set (4:2).
  • huiothesia, adoption: A legal word from the Greek and Roman world: an adopted son had the full rights of a son. Christ is Son by nature; we become children by grace, through him.
  • Abba, Father: An Aramaic word for father, kept untranslated by the Greek-speaking Church. Jesus prays with it in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36), and Paul uses it again in Romans 8:15.

Verse by verse.

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law,” (4:4)

“Sent” means that the Son was already with the Father before he was born. “Born of a woman” means that he is truly one of us. This is the only mention of Jesus’ mother in Paul’s letters, and she stands at the centre of the great turning point of time. “Born under the law” means that he lived as a son of Israel.

“in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” (4:5)

He takes our condition so that we may receive his. The word “redeem” recalls the price paid to release a slave.

“God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!”” (4:6)

God sends twice: the Son, then the Spirit of the Son. It is the Spirit who cries “Abba” within us. Christian prayer is the voice of the Son in us, speaking to his Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Galatians 4:4–5 as the heart of the Good News: God has visited his people (CCC 422). Vatican II opens its teaching on Our Lady with this very verse (Lumen Gentium 52). The Catechism sees in this passage the joint mission of the Son and the Spirit (CCC 689), and it recalls the teaching of the Fathers, St Irenaeus among them, that the Son of God became the son of man so that we might become children of God (CCC 460).

John 1:6–13 · He came to witness to the light

Setting.

The reading comes from the Prologue of John’s Gospel (1:1–18). The great poem about the Word has just said that “the light shines in the darkness” (1:5). Then, without warning, it turns to a man with a name and a task. The fourth Gospel never calls him “the Baptist”. For this Gospel his whole identity is to be a witness.

Structure.

  • vv. 6–8: John is sent as a witness; he is not the light.
  • v. 9: the true light was coming into the world.
  • vv. 10–11: the world and his own people do not receive him.
  • vv. 12–13: those who receive him are born of God.

Key words.

  • martyria, witness, testimony: A word from the law court, and a favourite of this Gospel. A witness speaks of what he knows and points away from himself. The later word “martyr” comes from this root.
  • phōs alēthinon, the true light: “True” here means real and final. Every other light is a lamp lit from this one. Jesus later calls John “a burning and shining lamp” (5:35).
  • exousia, power, right: Those who receive him are given the right and the authority to become God’s children. It is a gift, not an achievement.

Verse by verse.

“He himself was not the light, but he came to testify to the light.” (1:8)

The whole of John’s greatness lies in that “not”. He later says, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (3:30).

“He came to what was his own, and his own people did not accept him.” (1:11)

The Word came to the world he made and to the people he had prepared. The verse describes the sadness of every refusal, not the guilt of one nation: see the question below.

“But to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave power to become children of God, who were born, not of blood or of the will of the flesh or of the will of man, but of God.” (1:12–13)

A note on the text. Almost all Greek manuscripts have the plural, “who were born”, meaning believers. A few early Latin witnesses, with St Irenaeus and Tertullian, have the singular, “who was born”, meaning Christ, and some modern versions have followed it. The plural is the accepted reading; the Virgin birth stands on Matthew and Luke, not on this verse.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine loved the contrast between John and Jesus: John is the voice, Christ is the Word; the voice passes and the Word remains (Sermon 293, in substance). The Catechism uses John 1:12–13 when it teaches that to become children of God we must be “born from above” (CCC 526).

Questions people ask

Who are “his own people”? Does John blame the Jews?

No. Mary, Joseph, the shepherds, John himself and all the apostles were Jews, and they received him. The verse speaks of the human refusal that the world as a whole shares (1:10). The Church teaches that the Jewish people as such are not to be blamed for the rejection and death of Christ (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Abba is an Aramaic word, and Syriac, the language of our liturgical heritage, is a form of Aramaic. When the Syro-Malabar Church calls God “Father”, she stands very close to the speech in which Jesus himself first said it.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that I am “no longer a slave but a child”. What in my prayer still sounds like a slave speaking to a master?
  • John the witness points away from himself. In my family or parish, whom do my words and actions point towards?
