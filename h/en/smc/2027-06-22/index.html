Word for the day

“Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” 1 Peter 4:16

Reflection

Saint Paul knew about tents. Luke tells us that in Corinth he lodged with Aquila and Priscilla, “and they worked together, by trade they were tentmakers” (Acts 18:3). So when he writes to the same Corinthians about death and eternal life, he reaches for the image he knew best: “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” A tent is good, but it is temporary; it is pitched for a journey and folded at the end. Our bodies are such tents. They grow tired, they are wounded, and one day they are taken down. Paul does not despise the tent; he groans in it, “longing to be clothed with our heavenly dwelling”, not to be stripped bare but to be “further clothed, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.” This is the Christian hope: not escape from the body but its transformation in the resurrection. And the pledge of it is already given: “He who has prepared us for this very thing is God, who has given us the Spirit as a guarantee.” The Greek word means a first instalment, the advance payment that promises the rest. The Spirit poured out at Pentecost is God's down payment on our eternal home. Therefore, says Paul, “we are always confident… for we walk by faith, not by sight.”

The Gospel turns to a lamp. “Is a lamp brought in to be put under the bushel basket, or under the bed, and not on the lampstand?” Every Kerala home once had its nilavilakku, lit at dusk, set high in the front room so that its light would reach every corner. No one lights a lamp to hide it. The faith given to us at baptism, when a lighted candle was placed in our parents' hands, is not meant to be hidden under the bed of private feeling. “For there is nothing hidden, except to be disclosed; nor is anything secret, except to come to light.” Jesus adds a warning about how we listen: “Pay attention to what you hear; the measure you give will be the measure you get, and still more will be given you.” Those who give themselves generously to the word receive more light; those who hold back find even the little they have growing dim.

Today the First Set calendar honours two men who would not put their lamp under a basket, though it cost them their lives: St John Fisher and St Thomas More, martyrs of England in 1535. When King Henry VIII broke with Rome and had himself declared Supreme Head of the Church in England, the oath that was demanded of his subjects touched both his second marriage and the authority of the Pope. John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester, a learned and austere pastor, refused it; he was the only bishop of England to stand firm. While he lay in the Tower of London, Pope Paul III named him a cardinal. He was beheaded on 22 June 1535. It is told that on the way to execution he opened his New Testament and his eyes fell on the words, “And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent” (John 17:3). Thomas More was a layman: a lawyer, a scholar, a loving husband and father, the friend of the king and once his Lord Chancellor, famous for his wit and for his book Utopia. He did not seek martyrdom. For more than a year in the Tower he kept silent and used every honest means the law allowed. But he would not swear against his conscience, and he was condemned for treason on false testimony and beheaded on 6 July 1535, declaring, according to the reports of the time, that he died the king's good servant, but God's first. Pope Pius XI canonised them together in 1935, and in 2000 St John Paul II named Thomas More patron of statesmen and politicians.

The readings of their commemoration describe their road. Peter writes to Christians facing hostility: “Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal that is taking place among you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you. But rejoice insofar as you are sharing Christ’s sufferings”. He draws a sharp line: let none of you suffer as a criminal, “Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” Fisher and More were not rebels; they honoured the king in everything that was the king's. They suffered only for the name. And Jesus had warned the Twelve: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves… you will be dragged before governors and kings because of me”. More was wise as a serpent in his careful silence and innocent as a dove in his refusal to lie. Both found that the promise held: “what you are to say will be given to you at that time; for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” In our own time, Christians in many lands, and in some places in our own country, pay a price for their faith; Christians in public life in India face daily pressures to bend their conscience. Today's martyrs remind them, and us, that a well-formed conscience is a lamp that must not be hidden, and that the earthly tent may be taken down, but the house from God is eternal. “The one who endures to the end will be saved.”

Homily outline

Theme: “Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.”

The tent of the body and the house from God, the lamp on the lampstand, and two martyrs who served their king but God first.

1. A house not made with hands

  • The tentmaker's image.
  • We groan, longing to be further clothed.
  • The Spirit as a guarantee.
“For I know that my Redeemer lives, and that at the last he will stand upon the earth;” Job 19:25
“For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” 2 Corinthians 5:1

Apply: Visit or call someone who is seriously ill this week, and pray with them for the hope of the resurrection.

2. The lamp on the lampstand

  • The lamp of baptism.
  • Nothing hidden that will not come to light.
  • The measure you give.
“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.” Psalm 119:105
“He said to them, “Is a lamp brought in to be put under the bushel basket, or under the bed, and not on the lampstand?” Mark 4:21

Apply: Let your faith be seen in one concrete act at work, school or in public this week.

3. The king's good servant, but God's first

  • Fisher, the faithful bishop.
  • More, the honest layman.
  • The Spirit who speaks before governors and kings.
“But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods and we will not worship the golden statue that you have set up.” Daniel 3:18
“But Peter and the apostles answered, “We must obey God rather than any human authority.” Acts 5:29

Apply: Examine where you are pressed to act against conscience, and ask St Thomas More for courage to say no with kindness.

Our body is a tent, but God has given us his Spirit as a pledge of an eternal house.

Like Sts John Fisher and Thomas More, let us set our lamp on the lampstand, serve our country well and God first.

Take-home resolution: This week, pray for the dying and the persecuted, let your faith be seen in one act, and refuse one thing that goes against your conscience.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul was a tentmaker, and he tells us that our body is like a tent: good, but not permanent. “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens” (2 Corinthians 5:1).

God has already given us the Holy Spirit as a guarantee, like an advance payment, that we will have that eternal house. So we are confident, and we walk by faith, not by sight.

Jesus says that no one lights a lamp to hide it under a basket. Our faith is a lamp. It is meant to be seen, in our homes, at our work, in our words and choices.

Today we remember two men who did not hide their lamp: St John Fisher, a bishop, and St Thomas More, a husband, father and lawyer. When King Henry VIII demanded an oath against their conscience and against the unity of the Church, they refused. Both were beheaded in 1535. Thomas More said he died the king's good servant, but God's first.

Saint Peter says: if you suffer as a Christian, do not be ashamed, but glorify God because you bear this name. May we love our country and serve it well, but put God first. And when our earthly tent is folded, may we find the house that God has prepared for us. Amen.

For children

Children, would you hide a lamp under your bed? No! Jesus says our faith is like a lamp. Today we remember St Thomas More, a father who loved to laugh, and St John Fisher, a bishop. They chose God first, even when the king was angry. Let your light shine today by being honest and kind!

Prayer

Lord God, you have given us the Holy Spirit as the guarantee of an eternal home not made with hands. While we live in this tent of the body, teach us to walk by faith and not by sight. Lord Jesus, you sent your apostles like sheep among wolves and promised them your Spirit when they stood before governors and kings: as you gave St John Fisher and St Thomas More the courage to keep faith and conscience even unto death, give us the grace to set our lamp on the lampstand, to serve our country faithfully and you above all. Strengthen those who suffer for your name today, and bring us all to the house of the Father. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.
