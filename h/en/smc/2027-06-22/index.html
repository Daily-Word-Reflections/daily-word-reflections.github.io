Word for the day

“Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” 1 Peter 4:16

Reflection

Saint Paul knew about tents. Luke tells us that in Corinth he lodged with Aquila and Priscilla, “and they worked together, by trade they were tentmakers” (Acts 18:3). So when he writes to the same Corinthians about death and eternal life, he reaches for the image he knew best: “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” A tent is good, but it is temporary; it is pitched for a journey and folded at the end. Our bodies are such tents. They grow tired, they are wounded, and one day they are taken down. Paul does not despise the tent; he groans in it, “longing to be clothed with our heavenly dwelling”, not to be stripped bare but to be “further clothed, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.” This is the Christian hope: not escape from the body but its transformation in the resurrection. And the pledge of it is already given: “He who has prepared us for this very thing is God, who has given us the Spirit as a guarantee.” The Greek word means a first instalment, the advance payment that promises the rest. The Spirit poured out at Pentecost is God's down payment on our eternal home. Therefore, says Paul, “we are always confident… for we walk by faith, not by sight.”

The Gospel turns to a lamp. “Is a lamp brought in to be put under the bushel basket, or under the bed, and not on the lampstand?” Every Kerala home once had its nilavilakku, lit at dusk, set high in the front room so that its light would reach every corner. No one lights a lamp to hide it. The faith given to us at baptism, when a lighted candle was placed in our parents' hands, is not meant to be hidden under the bed of private feeling. “For there is nothing hidden, except to be disclosed; nor is anything secret, except to come to light.” Jesus adds a warning about how we listen: “Pay attention to what you hear; the measure you give will be the measure you get, and still more will be given you.” Those who give themselves generously to the word receive more light; those who hold back find even the little they have growing dim.

Today the First Set calendar honours two men who would not put their lamp under a basket, though it cost them their lives: St John Fisher and St Thomas More, martyrs of England in 1535. When King Henry VIII broke with Rome and had himself declared Supreme Head of the Church in England, the oath that was demanded of his subjects touched both his second marriage and the authority of the Pope. John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester, a learned and austere pastor, refused it; he was the only bishop of England to stand firm. While he lay in the Tower of London, Pope Paul III named him a cardinal. He was beheaded on 22 June 1535. It is told that on the way to execution he opened his New Testament and his eyes fell on the words, “And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent” (John 17:3). Thomas More was a layman: a lawyer, a scholar, a loving husband and father, the friend of the king and once his Lord Chancellor, famous for his wit and for his book Utopia. He did not seek martyrdom. For more than a year in the Tower he kept silent and used every honest means the law allowed. But he would not swear against his conscience, and he was condemned for treason on false testimony and beheaded on 6 July 1535, declaring, according to the reports of the time, that he died the king's good servant, but God's first. Pope Pius XI canonised them together in 1935, and in 2000 St John Paul II named Thomas More patron of statesmen and politicians.

The readings of their commemoration describe their road. Peter writes to Christians facing hostility: “Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal that is taking place among you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you. But rejoice insofar as you are sharing Christ’s sufferings”. He draws a sharp line: let none of you suffer as a criminal, “Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” Fisher and More were not rebels; they honoured the king in everything that was the king's. They suffered only for the name. And Jesus had warned the Twelve: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves… you will be dragged before governors and kings because of me”. More was wise as a serpent in his careful silence and innocent as a dove in his refusal to lie. Both found that the promise held: “what you are to say will be given to you at that time; for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” In our own time, Christians in many lands, and in some places in our own country, pay a price for their faith; Christians in public life in India face daily pressures to bend their conscience. Today's martyrs remind them, and us, that a well-formed conscience is a lamp that must not be hidden, and that the earthly tent may be taken down, but the house from God is eternal. “The one who endures to the end will be saved.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul was a tentmaker, and he tells us that our body is like a tent: good, but not permanent. “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens” (2 Corinthians 5:1).

God has already given us the Holy Spirit as a guarantee, like an advance payment, that we will have that eternal house. So we are confident, and we walk by faith, not by sight.

Jesus says that no one lights a lamp to hide it under a basket. Our faith is a lamp. It is meant to be seen, in our homes, at our work, in our words and choices.

Today we remember two men who did not hide their lamp: St John Fisher, a bishop, and St Thomas More, a husband, father and lawyer. When King Henry VIII demanded an oath against their conscience and against the unity of the Church, they refused. Both were beheaded in 1535. Thomas More said he died the king's good servant, but God's first.

Saint Peter says: if you suffer as a Christian, do not be ashamed, but glorify God because you bear this name. May we love our country and serve it well, but put God first. And when our earthly tent is folded, may we find the house that God has prepared for us. Amen.

For children

Children, would you hide a lamp under your bed? No! Jesus says our faith is like a lamp. Today we remember St Thomas More, a father who loved to laugh, and St John Fisher, a bishop. They chose God first, even when the king was angry. Let your light shine today by being honest and kind!

Prayer

Lord God, you have given us the Holy Spirit as the guarantee of an eternal home not made with hands. While we live in this tent of the body, teach us to walk by faith and not by sight. Lord Jesus, you sent your apostles like sheep among wolves and promised them your Spirit when they stood before governors and kings: as you gave St John Fisher and St Thomas More the courage to keep faith and conscience even unto death, give us the grace to set our lamp on the lampstand, to serve our country faithfully and you above all. Strengthen those who suffer for your name today, and bring us all to the house of the Father. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two pairs of readings are given today. In the weekday readings Paul speaks of our earthly body as a tent and of the eternal house God has prepared, and Jesus says that a lamp is not lit to be hidden. For Sts John Fisher and Thomas More, martyred in England in 1535, Peter tells Christians not to be surprised at the fiery ordeal, and Jesus sends his disciples out like sheep among wolves. The two pairs meet in these martyrs: men who walked by faith and not by sight, and who would not hide their light even before a king.

2 Corinthians 5:1–7 · A house not made with hands

Setting.

Paul has been describing the hardships of his ministry: afflicted but not crushed, carrying in the body the death of Jesus (4:8–10). He has just said that our slight momentary affliction is preparing an eternal weight of glory (4:17). Now he explains the ground of his hope: what awaits us beyond this mortal life.

Structure.

  • v. 1: if this earthly tent is destroyed, we have an eternal building from God.
  • vv. 2–4: we groan, longing to be further clothed, so that the mortal may be swallowed up by life.
  • v. 5: God has given the Spirit as a guarantee.
  • vv. 6–7: so we are confident, walking by faith, not by sight.

Key words.

  • skēnos, tent: Paul was a tentmaker (Acts 18:3) and knew how quickly a tent is taken down. The body in this life is a temporary dwelling, not our final home.
  • arrabōn, guarantee: A business word for a first payment that guarantees the rest. The Holy Spirit given to us now is the pledge of the life to come (also 1:22; Ephesians 1:14).

Verse by verse.

“For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” (5:1)

Paul does not despise the body; he looks forward to its transformation. The Christian hope is not escape from the body but resurrection.

“so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.” (5:4)

Death is not the last word. Life swallows death, as Paul also says in 1 Corinthians 15:54.

“for we walk by faith, not by sight.” (5:7)

A short sentence that describes the whole Christian life on earth.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of Christian death, quotes Paul’s desire to be away from the body and at home with the Lord (5:8; CCC 1005). The Church prays for the faithful departed with this hope: death is the end of our earthly pilgrimage, and for those who die in Christ it is the passage to the eternal house God has prepared.

Mark 4:21–25 · The lamp on the lampstand

Setting.

These sayings follow the parable of the sower and its explanation (4:1–20). Jesus has spoken of the word sown in different kinds of soil. Now he adds two short parables about light and measure: the word is meant to be revealed, and those who listen well will receive more.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–23: the lamp is not put under a basket; what is hidden will be revealed.
  • vv. 24–25: the measure you give; to those who have, more will be given.

Key words.

  • lychnos, lamp: A small clay oil lamp. The Greek of 4:21 says literally “Does the lamp come…?”, and many interpreters hear in it a hint of Christ himself, the light who has come into the world.
  • modios, bushel basket: A grain measure of about nine litres. Putting it over a lamp would put the light out.

Verse by verse.

“Is a lamp brought in to be put under the bushel basket, or under the bed, and not on the lampstand?” (4:21)

The answer is obvious. The kingdom, now hidden like a seed, is meant to shine for all.

“Pay attention to what you hear; the measure you give will be the measure you get, and still more will be given you.” (4:24)

Here the measure is the measure of attention. The more generously we listen to the word, the more God gives.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the laity are called to make Christ known by the witness of their lives, shining with faith, hope and charity, like a light in the world (in substance, Lumen Gentium 31, 35). A faith kept entirely private, hidden under the basket, has forgotten what it is for.

1 Peter 4:12–19 · If you suffer as a Christian

Setting.

First Peter is addressed to Christians scattered through the provinces of Asia Minor (1:1), who were facing insults and hostility from their neighbours because of their faith. Tradition places its writing at Rome, which the letter calls “Babylon” (5:13), shortly before or during the persecution under Nero.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal; rejoice to share Christ’s sufferings.
  • vv. 14–16: blessed if insulted for the name of Christ; suffer as a Christian, not as a wrongdoer.
  • vv. 17–19: judgment begins with the household of God; entrust yourselves to the faithful Creator.

Key words.

  • pyrōsis, fiery ordeal: Literally a burning, as in a smelter’s furnace where gold is refined (1:7). Suffering is pictured as a test that purifies.
  • Christianos, Christian: The name occurs only three times in the New Testament (Acts 11:26; 26:28; here). It was probably first used by outsiders; Peter tells believers to bear it with honour.

Verse by verse.

“But rejoice insofar as you are sharing Christ’s sufferings” (4:13)

The Christian does not seek suffering, but when it comes for Christ’s sake, it is a sharing in his own Passion.

“Yet if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” (4:16)

What the world counts shame, faith counts glory.

“let those suffering in accordance with God’s will entrust themselves to a faithful Creator, while continuing to do good.” (4:19)

Two things: trust God, and keep doing good. Martyrs do not curse their persecutors.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls martyrdom the supreme witness to the truth of the faith: the martyr bears witness to Christ who died and rose, to whom he is united by charity (CCC 2473). The Church has carefully kept the records of those who gave their lives for Christ (CCC 2474).

Matthew 10:16–22 · Sheep among wolves

Setting.

Jesus has chosen the Twelve and is sending them out on mission (10:1–15). In this part of his instructions he looks further ahead, to the persecutions his disciples will face before councils, governors and kings.

Structure.

  • v. 16: sheep among wolves: wise as serpents, innocent as doves.
  • vv. 17–18: they will be handed over and brought before governors and kings as a testimony.
  • vv. 19–20: do not worry what to say; the Spirit of your Father will speak.
  • vv. 21–22: division in families; hated for my name; the one who endures will be saved.

Key words.

  • phronimos, wise: Prudent, shrewd in practical matters. The disciple is not naive; he uses good sense.
  • akeraios, innocent: Literally “unmixed”, without any blend of guile. Prudence must never become cunning.

Verse by verse.

“so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” (10:16)

St Thomas More used every legal means to avoid giving the king grounds to condemn him, and kept silent where he could; but he would not take an oath against his conscience. That is the serpent and the dove together.

“for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” (10:20)

Many martyrs, simple people among them, have answered their judges with a wisdom that was not their own.

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” (10:22)

Endurance is not mere stubbornness. It is faithfulness kept to the end, by grace.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees this promise fulfilled in the martyrs of every age. St John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester, and St Thomas More, Lord Chancellor of England, refused the oath demanded by King Henry VIII, which implied rejecting the authority of the Pope, and would not accept the king as supreme head of the Church in England. Fisher was beheaded on 22 June 1535 and More on 6 July. They were canonised together in 1935, and in 2000 St John Paul II declared St Thomas More patron of statesmen and politicians.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Sts John Fisher and Thomas More are saints of the whole Church, and their witness speaks to every Catholic in public life. In our own day many Christians in many lands suffer for their faith; we pray for them, and for those who wrong them, as the martyrs themselves prayed. Thomas More is said to have declared on the scaffold that he died the king’s good servant, but God’s first.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “we walk by faith, not by sight.” Where in my life do I most need to walk by faith right now?
  • Thomas More was wise as a serpent and innocent as a dove. How can I be faithful to my conscience at work or in public life without being either naive or cunning?
