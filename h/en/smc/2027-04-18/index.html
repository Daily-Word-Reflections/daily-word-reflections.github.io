Word for the day

“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” John 16:22

Reflection

The first reading was spoken to a people in exile, far from a ruined Jerusalem. The prophet begins with a burst of praise: “Sing for joy, O heavens, and exult, O earth; break forth, O mountains, into singing! For the LORD has comforted his people, and will have compassion on his suffering ones.” But Zion does not feel comforted. She says what many of us have said in the dark: “The LORD has forsaken me, my Lord has forgotten me.” God answers with one of the tenderest sentences in the Bible: “Can a woman forget her nursing child, or show no compassion for the child of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands.” A mother's love is the strongest human love we know, and God says his love is stronger still. In the Easter season the image of the palms takes on new meaning. On the evening of the Resurrection the Risen Lord “showed them his hands and his side” (John 20:20), and to Thomas he said, “Put your finger here and see my hands” (John 20:27). Our names are written in those wounded hands. Then Isaiah describes a reversal no one could imagine. Zion, who thought herself “bereaved and barren, exiled and put away”, suddenly finds her house too small: “The place is too crowded for me; make room for me to settle.” She asks in wonder, “Who has borne me these?” This is the fruitfulness of the Church, the mother who receives children from every nation. On this World Prayer Day for Vocations we remember that every priest, every religious sister and brother, every missionary is a child the Lord has given to his Church. Kerala has given many such children to the whole world, and today, when fewer young people answer, Isaiah's promise holds: “those who wait for me shall not be put to shame.”

In Acts, the deacon Philip has preached Christ in Samaria, and “there was great joy in that city” (Acts 8:8). The apostles in Jerusalem send Peter and John. “The two went down and prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Spirit (for as yet the Spirit had not come upon any of them; they had only been baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus). Then Peter and John laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit.” The Church has always seen here the gift of the Spirit that completes the grace of Baptism; in the Eastern tradition the anointing with holy Myron is closely joined to Baptism itself. It also shows the bond between a new community and the apostles: the Samaritans are not left on their own but joined to the one Church through those whom Christ sent. Then a dark figure steps forward. Simon, who had practised magic and been admired as a great power, sees what happens through the laying on of hands and offers money: “Give me also this power so that anyone on whom I lay my hands may receive the Holy Spirit.” Peter's answer is fierce: “May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain God's gift with money! You have no part or share in this, for your heart is not right before God.” From Simon's name the Church calls the buying or selling of spiritual things simony. The lesson goes deeper than money. A vocation to the priesthood or religious life is not a career to be purchased, a family status to be secured, or a power to be used. It is a gift and a call, received freely and given freely: “You received without payment; give without payment” (Matthew 10:8). Peter still leaves the door open: “Repent therefore of this wickedness of yours, and pray to the Lord,” and Simon asks for prayer. Even here, mercy is not shut out.

Paul tells the Ephesians the truth about where we all began: “You were dead through the trespasses and sins in which you once lived, following the course of this world.” Not weak or confused, but dead, unable to raise ourselves. Then the two most hopeful words of the letter: “But God”. “But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ.” Paul interrupts himself to make sure we do not miss the point: “by grace you have been saved”. And he goes further: God “raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus.” The Resurrection is not only something that happened to Jesus on the first Easter; in Baptism it has already begun in us. We are seated with him, so that “in the ages to come he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus.” Simon the magician wanted to buy what can only be received. Paul reminds us that the whole of salvation is a gift. Every vocation begins here: not with my plan for my life, but with God's kindness, which made me alive when I was dead.

The Gospel takes us back to the Last Supper. Jesus says, “A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.” The disciples do not understand: “What does he mean by this ‘a little while’? We do not know what he is talking about.” We understand it now because we live after Easter. The first little while was the time from Thursday night to the tomb; the second little while ended on the morning of the Resurrection. Jesus does not hide the pain: “Very truly, I tell you, you will weep and mourn, but the world will rejoice; you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” He does not say the pain will be replaced by joy, but turned into it, as the labour of a mother becomes the joy of a child. “When a woman is in labor, she has pain, because her hour has come. But when her child is born, she no longer remembers the anguish because of the joy of having brought a human being into the world.” The Fathers saw in these words the Church giving birth to new children through the Cross, and Christian tradition has also thought of the Blessed Virgin Mary standing by the Cross, where her Son gave her to the beloved disciple as his mother (John 19:26–27). Then comes the promise for every disciple: “So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” A vocation is not without pain; priests and religious know the labour of their ministry. But it carries a joy that no one can take away, because it is the joy of the Risen Lord. And Jesus adds an invitation: “Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.” On this day the Church asks, as Jesus told her to ask, that the Lord of the harvest will send labourers into his harvest (Matthew 9:38).

Homily outline

Theme: “So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.”

The God who cannot forget us, the gift that cannot be bought, the mercy that makes the dead alive, and the sorrow that turns into joy.

1. I will not forget you

  • Zion's complaint.
  • Stronger than a mother's love.
  • Names on wounded palms.
“Can a woman forget her nursing child, or show no compassion for the child of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you.” Isaiah 49:15
“And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Matthew 28:20

Apply: When you feel forgotten this week, look at your palm and pray: my name is written on your hands.

2. The gift that cannot be bought

  • The laying on of hands.
  • Simon's silver.
  • Freely received, freely given.
“Ho, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and you that have no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.” Isaiah 55:1
“May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain God's gift with money!” Acts 8:20

Apply: Let go of any way you use holy things for honour or advantage.

3. But God, rich in mercy

  • Dead through trespasses.
  • Made alive together with Christ.
  • Seated with him in the heavenly places.
“Thus says the Lord GOD to these bones: I will cause breath to enter you, and you shall live.” Ezekiel 37:5
“But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” Ephesians 2:4–5

Apply: Thank God today for one way he has raised you from a kind of death.

4. Your pain will turn into joy

  • A little while.
  • The woman in labour and the Mother at the Cross.
  • A joy no one can take.
“Weeping may linger for the night, but joy comes with the morning.” Psalm 30:5
“So you have pain now; but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” John 16:22

Apply: Pray every day this week for vocations, and bring one sorrow to the Lord to be turned into joy.

God never forgets his people; he gives his Spirit freely, raises the dead to life, and turns our sorrow into the joy of Easter.

On this day of prayer for vocations, we ask the Lord of the harvest for labourers who will give freely what they have freely received.

Take-home resolution: This week pray daily for a vocation from your family or parish, thank a priest or sister who serves you, and give one sorrow to the Risen Lord.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, Zion in exile said: the LORD has forsaken me, my Lord has forgotten me. And God answered: can a woman forget her nursing child? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands (Isaiah 49:15–16). On Easter evening the Risen Lord showed his disciples his hands. Our names are written there.

Isaiah says that Zion, who thought she was barren, will find her house too crowded with children. That is the Church, our mother. Today, the World Prayer Day for Vocations, we thank God for the priests, sisters and missionaries he has given us, and we ask him for more.

In Acts, Peter and John lay their hands on the new believers of Samaria, and they receive the Holy Spirit. Then Simon the magician offers money for that power. Peter says: may your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain God's gift with money! A vocation is not a career to buy, nor a family honour to collect. It is a gift and a call, freely received and freely given.

Paul tells us that we were dead in our sins. But God, who is rich in mercy, made us alive together with Christ. By grace we have been saved. Every vocation, and every Christian life, begins with that gift.

In the Gospel Jesus says: you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy. Like a mother in labour, who forgets the pain when her child is born. Mary stood by the Cross in that hour, and on Easter morning her sorrow was turned into joy. Jesus promises us: no one will take your joy from you.

So today let us ask, as he told us to ask. Let us pray every day for vocations from our families. Parents, do not be afraid if God calls your son or daughter. Young people, listen; he may be calling you. And all of us, let us bring our sorrows to him, and let him turn them into joy. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, God says: even if a mother forgot her baby, I will never forget you. Your name is written on God's hands! You are never alone.

Today we pray for priests and sisters. Maybe Jesus is calling you to be one of them one day! Say a little prayer: Jesus, what do you want me to be?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you have written our names on the palms of your wounded hands, and you never forget us. Pour out on your Church the Holy Spirit, whom no one can buy, and keep us from treating any holy thing as a possession or a career. Rich in mercy, you made us alive when we were dead and seated us with you in the heavenly places. Turn our sorrows into joy, and give us the joy that no one can take from us. Lord of the harvest, send labourers into your harvest: call from our families and parishes good priests, holy religious and faithful missionaries, and give them generous hearts to answer. With the Blessed Virgin Mary, who stood by your Cross and rejoiced in your Resurrection, we give glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this fourth Sunday of the Resurrection, kept as the World Prayer Day for Vocations, the readings move from sorrow to joy. Isaiah hears Zion say, “The Lord has forsaken me,” and God answers that a mother may forget her nursing child, “yet I will not forget you.” In Acts, the Holy Spirit is given in Samaria through the laying on of the apostles' hands, and Simon learns that God's gift cannot be bought. Ephesians tells those who were dead in sin that God, rich in mercy, has made them alive and seated them with Christ. In John, Jesus promises that the disciples' pain will turn into joy, like the joy of a mother when her child is born.

The thread is a gift no one can earn or seize: God's faithful love, the Spirit, salvation by grace, a joy no one can take away. Vocations belong to the same order: a call is received, never bought. The calendar notes the fuller passages Ephesians 1:15–2:7 and John 16:16–33; the exegesis follows the readings as listed.

Isaiah 49:13–23 · I will not forget you

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC, after Jerusalem and its Temple had been destroyed. Many scholars attribute these chapters to a prophet of the exile writing in the tradition of Isaiah. They open with the words “Comfort, O comfort my people” (40:1).

In our passage the city of Zion, Jerusalem, is pictured as a woman: a wife who thinks herself abandoned and a mother who has lost her children. God answers her complaint, and the answer grows into a vision of the city crowded with returning children, carried by the nations.

Structure.

  • v. 13: a call to heaven and earth to rejoice: the Lord has comforted his people.
  • v. 14: Zion's complaint: forsaken, forgotten.
  • vv. 15–16: God's answer: more faithful than a mother; Zion inscribed on his palms.
  • vv. 17–21: the ruins filled with children; the bereaved mother astonished.
  • vv. 22–23: the nations carry Zion's sons and daughters home; those who wait for the Lord shall not be put to shame.

Key words.

  • raḥămîm, compassion: The Hebrew word for compassion is related to reḥem, womb. In v. 15 the two meet: “show no compassion for the child of her womb”. God's mercy is described with the most tender bond we know.
  • ḥāqaq, inscribe, engrave: To cut into a surface so that it cannot be erased. God has not merely written Zion's name down; he has engraved it on his own hands.
  • ’ōmēn, foster father: A guardian who carries a child (compare Numbers 11:12, where Moses complains that he must carry the people “as a nurse carries a sucking child”). In v. 23 kings become foster fathers to Zion's children.

Verse by verse.

“For the Lord has comforted his people, and will have compassion on his suffering ones.” (49:13)

The song of joy comes before the complaint. God's comfort is announced first, as Easter is announced to disciples still in grief.

“The Lord has forsaken me, my Lord has forgotten me.” (49:14)

Scripture lets Zion say this aloud. Complaint, too, can be prayer, as in the Psalms.

“Can a woman forget her nursing child, or show no compassion for the child of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you.” (49:15)

God does not deny that human love can fail. He says that his own love does not.

“See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands; your walls are continually before me.” (49:16)

Christians can hardly hear this verse without thinking of the risen Lord, who showed his disciples his hands (John 20:20, 27).

“Who has borne me these?” (49:21)

The barren mother finds herself surrounded by children she did not expect. It is a picture of the Church gathered from the nations.

“those who wait for me shall not be put to shame.” (49:23)

The image before it, of kings bowing and licking the dust, is the court language of the ancient world for complete submission. The promise at the end is the point: waiting on God is never wasted.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verses 14–15 to show that God's love for his people is stronger than a mother's love for her children (CCC 219), and it teaches that God's parental tenderness can be expressed by the image of motherhood, though God is neither man nor woman (CCC 239; 370). Pope John Paul I said in a brief Angelus of 1978 that God is our Father, and even more our Mother (in substance). The Church has also seen in the crowded city of vv. 18–23 a picture of herself, the Mother of many children, gathered from all peoples.

Acts 8:14–25 · God's gift cannot be bought

Setting.

After the death of Stephen, a persecution scattered the believers from Jerusalem (8:1). Philip, one of the Seven chosen in Acts 6:5, went down to Samaria and proclaimed the Messiah there. Many believed and were baptised, among them Simon, a man who had amazed the city with magic (8:9–13).

Samaritans and Jews were long divided over the place of worship and the Scriptures (compare John 4:9, 20). That the word of God was received in Samaria was a great step outward, as the risen Lord had foretold: “in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth” (1:8).

Structure.

  • vv. 14–17: the apostles send Peter and John; prayer and the laying on of hands; the Spirit is received.
  • vv. 18–19: Simon offers money for the power to give the Spirit.
  • vv. 20–23: Peter's rebuke and call to repentance.
  • v. 24: Simon asks for prayer.
  • v. 25: the apostles preach in many Samaritan villages on the way home.

Key words.

  • epetithesan tas cheiras, they laid their hands: The laying on of hands is a gesture of blessing and of handing on in Scripture. Here it is joined to prayer for the gift of the Spirit.
  • dōrea, gift: A gift given without payment. Luke uses it of the Holy Spirit in Peter's Pentecost sermon (2:38). By its nature a gift cannot be bought.
  • cholē pikrias, gall of bitterness: The phrase recalls Deuteronomy 29:18, a root that bears “poisonous and bitter growth”: a heart turned away from God. The POC renders the sense as deep resentment.

Verse by verse.

“prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Spirit” (8:15)

The apostles begin with prayer. The Spirit is asked for, not produced.

“Then Peter and John laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit.” (8:17)

John, who once wanted fire from heaven on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54), now brings the Holy Spirit to Samaritans.

“May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain God’s gift with money!” (8:20)

From Simon's name comes the word simony: buying or selling spiritual things.

“your heart is not right before God.” (8:21)

Peter goes to the root. The money is a symptom; the heart that wants to possess holy power is the illness.

“Pray for me to the Lord, that nothing of what you have said may happen to me.” (8:24)

Luke leaves the story open. Simon at least asks for the apostles' prayer.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism reads this passage as a witness to the sacrament later called Confirmation: from the time of the apostles, the gift of the Spirit that completes the grace of Baptism was given by the laying on of hands (CCC 1288; 1315). It names Simon's sin: simony is the buying or selling of spiritual things (CCC 2121). St Irenaeus counted Simon as the father of heresies (Against Heresies 1.23), and St Cyril of Jerusalem warned those preparing for Baptism that Simon came to the water and was baptised but was not enlightened, because his heart was not open (Procatechesis 2, in substance).

Ephesians 2:1–7 · But God, rich in mercy

Setting.

Ephesians is one of the letters whose authorship is discussed: tradition and many scholars hold it to be Paul's, written from prison, while others think it was written by a disciple in his name. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

In chapter 1 the letter has blessed God for choosing, adopting and redeeming us, and has prayed that we may know the power that raised Christ from the dead (1:19–20). Now it shows that the same power has worked in us: the God who raised Christ has raised us, who were dead.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: what we were: dead in trespasses, following the world and the evil one, children of wrath.
  • v. 4: the turn: “But God”.
  • vv. 5–6: what God has done: made alive, raised up, seated with Christ.
  • v. 7: why: to show the riches of his grace in the ages to come.

Key words.

  • plousios en eleei, rich in mercy: God's wealth is mercy. Pope John Paul II took the title of his encyclical on the mercy of God, Dives in Misericordia (1980), from this verse.
  • syn-, together with: Three verbs in vv. 5–6 begin with the Greek prefix for “with”: made alive with, raised with, seated with. What happened to Christ at Easter has happened to us with him.
  • chariti este sesōsmenoi, by grace you have been saved: The verb is in the perfect tense: a past act whose effect lasts. Salvation is God's gift; the letter repeats it in v. 8.

Verse by verse.

“You were dead through the trespasses and sins” (2:1)

Not merely weak or sick, but dead. The dead cannot raise themselves.

“But God, who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us” (2:4)

Two words that turn the whole story. Everything after them is God's work.

“made us alive together with Christ” (2:5)
“and raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus,” (2:6)

The verbs are past. In Baptism our life is already joined to the risen Lord, though we still await its full revealing (Colossians 3:3–4).

“the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus.” (2:7)

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent drew on verse 3, “by nature children of wrath”, in its teaching on the state of humanity after Adam's sin (Session 6, chapter 1). The Catechism teaches that our justification comes from the grace of God, the undeserved help he gives us to respond to his call (CCC 1996). Easter is the feast of this passage: the Church baptises in the season of the Resurrection because Baptism is our rising with Christ (Romans 6:4).

John 16:16–24 · Your pain will turn into joy

Setting.

These words belong to the Last Discourse, spoken by Jesus at the supper on the night before he died (John 13–17). He has told his disciples that he is going to the Father and that the Advocate will come (16:5–15). Now he speaks of a short absence and a return.

The Church reads these words in the season of the Resurrection because they were fulfilled at Easter: the disciples did not see him while he lay in the tomb, and then they saw him again, and their grief became joy (20:20).

Structure.

  • v. 16: the riddle: “a little while”.
  • vv. 17–19: the disciples' puzzled questions, and Jesus' awareness of them.
  • v. 20: weeping and joy.
  • v. 21: the image of a woman in labour.
  • v. 22: “I will see you again”: a joy no one can take.
  • vv. 23–24: asking the Father in Jesus' name.

Key words.

  • mikron, a little while: The phrase occurs seven times in vv. 16–19. The disciples cannot understand it; the reader knows it means the three days between death and Resurrection, and also the whole time until the Lord comes again.
  • lypē, pain, sorrow: Grief and pain of body and heart. The same word is used of the woman in labour (v. 21): the sorrow is real, but it is the sorrow of a birth.
  • aiteō / erōtaō, ask: Verse 23 uses two different verbs: “you will ask (erōtaō, question) nothing of me”, but “if you ask (aiteō, request) anything of the Father”. On that day the disciples will no longer need to question him; they will be able to ask the Father directly in his name.

Verse by verse.

“A little while, and you will no longer see me, and again a little while, and you will see me.” (16:16)
“you will have pain, but your pain will turn into joy.” (16:20)

Not replaced by joy, but turned into it. The same Cross that caused the grief becomes the source of the joy.

“When a woman is in labor, she has pain, because her hour has come.” (16:21)

“Hour” is the word John uses for the Lord's Passion (12:23; 13:1). The prophets compared the birth of God's new people to a woman in labour (Isaiah 26:17; 66:7–9). Many interpreters also think here of the Mother of Jesus, whom he calls “Woman” at the Cross (19:26), and of the new family born there.

“but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” (16:22)

Earlier he said “you will see me”; now, “I will see you”. The joy rests not on our sight of him, but on his faithful gaze upon us.

“Ask and you will receive, so that your joy may be complete.” (16:24)

Prayer in his name is the place where Easter joy grows.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine read the “little while” as the whole of this present age, short when seen from its end: now we grieve because we long for the Lord and do not yet see him, but soon we shall see him, and there no one will take our joy away (Tractates on John 101, in substance). The Catechism teaches that there is no other way of Christian prayer than Christ, and that our prayer has access to the Father only if we pray in the name of Jesus (CCC 2664).

Questions people ask

Why had the Samaritans not received the Holy Spirit when they were baptised?

Luke does not explain it fully. He shows that the new community in Samaria was joined to the apostles of Jerusalem, so that the Church remained one, and that the gift of the Spirit came through their prayer and the laying on of their hands. The Catechism sees here the origin of the sacrament that completes Baptism (CCC 1288; 1315). In the Eastern Churches, including the Syro-Malabar Church, this anointing is given together with Baptism, and the oil used is consecrated by the Major Archbishop, which keeps the link with the apostles (CCC 1290–1292).

Does God really compare himself to a mother?

Yes. Isaiah does so here and again in 66:13. God is neither man nor woman; he is pure spirit, and the perfections of both father and mother are found in him (CCC 239; 370). Scripture and the Church call God Father, as Jesus taught us, and the image of a mother shows the tenderness of that same love.

If we ask anything in Jesus' name, why are some prayers not answered as we hope?

To ask in his name is to ask as he would ask, in union with his will. The Catechism speaks honestly of the trial of prayer that seems not to be heard, and reminds us with James that we may ask wrongly (CCC 2735–2737). The Father always gives what is truly good, and above all the Holy Spirit (Luke 11:13).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar Church keeps this Sunday, like the universal Church, as the World Prayer Day for Vocations. Our families have given many priests and religious to the Church in India and far beyond. Isaiah's barren mother, astonished at her children, and Simon's attempt to buy what can only be received, together teach the right spirit: a vocation is a gift received in prayer, and for priests through the laying on of hands; it is never a career to be acquired.

In our tradition the newly baptised child is anointed with the holy oil (Mooron) at the same celebration, so that the gift of the Spirit that Peter and John brought to Samaria is given at the very start of Christian life. The Queen of Heaven is prayed until Pentecost; with it we greet Mary, the Mother whose sorrow at the Cross was turned into the joy of the Resurrection.

For study and teaching

  • Zion said, “The Lord has forgotten me.” When have I felt forgotten by God, and what does it mean to me that my name is written on his hands?
  • Simon wanted to own the power of the Spirit. In what ways can I treat holy things, roles in the parish or the Church itself as something I possess?
  • Ephesians says that I have been raised and seated with Christ. How would my week be different if I believed this was already true of me?
  • What sorrow in my family could the Lord turn into joy? Whom in my family or parish will I pray for this week, that they may hear and answer God's call?
