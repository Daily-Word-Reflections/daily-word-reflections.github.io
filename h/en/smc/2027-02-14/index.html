Word for the day

““Everyone then who hears these words of mine and acts on them will be like a wise man who built his house on rock.” Matthew 7:24

Reflection

The first reading sounds at first like a list of names and numbers, with one refrain tolling like a bell: “Thus all the days of Jared were nine hundred sixty-two years; and he died.” Methuselah lives longest of all, nine hundred sixty-nine years, “and he died.” One name breaks the pattern. “Enoch walked with God; then he was no more, because God took him.” He did not walk with God in a desert or a monastery: he “walked with God after the birth of Methuselah three hundred years, and had other sons and daughters.” His holiness was lived in an ordinary household. Sirach calls him “an example of repentance to all generations”, and Hebrews says that “he had pleased God.” At the end of the passage Lamech names his son Noah, saying, “Out of the ground that the LORD has cursed this one shall bring us relief from our work and from the toil of our hands.” Even under the long shadow of death God keeps hope alive. In this second week the Great Fast shows its real goal: not a list of things given up, but a life that walks with God.

In the second reading the priests carrying the ark come up out of the Jordan, and as soon as their feet touch dry ground the river returns and overflows its banks as before. Israel camps at Gilgal, and Joshua sets up the twelve stones taken from the riverbed. He gives the reason: “When your children ask their parents in time to come, ‘What do these stones mean?’ then you shall let your children know, ‘Israel crossed over the Jordan here on dry ground.’” The stones exist so that the story will be told, “so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the LORD is mighty.” Our Lenten customs are stones of this kind: the ash of the Vibhoodi, the abstinence kept by the whole house, the Way of the Cross on Fridays, the Puthen Pana sung in the evenings. When a child asks why we do not eat fish now, the answer is the story of Christ. Paul tells the baptised that they too have crossed a river. Everyone serves someone: “you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness.” “But now that you have been freed from sin and enslaved to God, the advantage you get is sanctification. The end is eternal life.” Then comes the great contrast between what we earn and what we are given: “For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.”

The Gospel is the ending of the Sermon on the Mount, and it is sobering: “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father in heaven.” Some will claim to have prophesied, cast out demons and done mighty works in his name, and he will answer, “I never knew you.” Then Jesus tells of two builders. The same rain falls on both houses, the same floods come, the same winds beat against them. The only difference is the foundation. The house of the one who hears and acts “did not fall, because it had been founded on rock”; the house of the one who hears and does not act falls, “and great was its fall!” Kerala knows what floods do to houses. The storms of illness, loss and temptation come to every home; what we build on is decided before they arrive. The Great Fast is digging time: hearing the word and doing it, until our life rests on Christ the rock. Today the Latin Church also honours Sts Cyril and Methodius, the brothers who gave the Slav peoples the Scriptures and the liturgy in their own language, so that the word might be both heard and lived.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the first reading is a long list of names, and after almost every name the same words: and he died. But one man is different: Enoch walked with God; then he was no more, because God took him (Genesis 5:24). He walked with God while raising sons and daughters, in an ordinary home.

That is the goal of the Great Fast. Not simply to give up fish and meat, but to walk with God, day after day, in our homes and at our work.

Israel crossed the Jordan and set up twelve stones, so that when the children asked, what do these stones mean?, the parents would tell them the story of God's mighty hand (Joshua 4:21–22). Our Lenten customs are such stones. When our children ask why we fast, let us tell them about Jesus.

Paul reminds us that everyone serves someone. For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord (Romans 6:23). Sin pays wages; God gives gifts.

Jesus warns us: not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father (Matthew 7:21). The two houses face the same storm. The difference is the foundation: the one who hears and acts has built on rock.

This week, let us choose one word of Jesus and do it. Let us dig down to the rock while the sky is clear. Then, when the storms come, our house will stand. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a house on the rock and a house on the sand. When the rain came, only the house on the rock stood strong!

When we listen to Jesus and do what he says, we are building on the rock. What will you do this week?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, rock of our salvation, you brought us through the waters of baptism into the land of your grace. Teach us to walk with God like Enoch, to tell our children what our Lenten stones mean, and to choose the free gift of eternal life rather than the wages of sin. Let us not only call you Lord, but do the will of your Father, so that our house may stand when the storms come. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Second Sunday of the Great Fast the readings ask what a life stands on. Genesis lists long lives that all end the same way, with one name that breaks the pattern: “Enoch walked with God”. In Joshua, Israel comes up out of the Jordan and sets up stones so that children will ask what they mean. Paul tells those who crossed the waters of baptism that everyone serves someone, and that sin pays in death but God gives life as a gift. Jesus closes the Sermon on the Mount with two houses and one storm. The line runs from walking, to crossing, to serving, to building, and its key words are walked, remember, gift and rock.

Genesis 5:19–31 · Enoch walked with God

Setting.

Chapter 5 is a genealogy, running in ten generations from Adam to Noah through Seth. Israel's neighbours also had lists from before the flood: the Sumerian King List gives reigns of enormous length. In Genesis the list has a rhythm: so-and-so lived, had children, and “he died”. It is a sermon on what sin brought, and on the fact that God's blessing to “be fruitful” continues through it. The numbers differ in the Hebrew, Samaritan and Greek texts, so they should not be pressed as a plain record.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: Jared, nine hundred sixty-two years; “and he died”.
  • vv. 21–24: Enoch, the seventh from Adam (Jude 14), and no “he died”.
  • vv. 25–27: Methuselah, the longest of all, and “he died”.
  • vv. 28–31: Lamech, and the naming of Noah.
  • ¶ The refrain makes Enoch stand out. His three hundred sixty-five years also match the days of the solar year, and many commentators think this is no accident: a complete life.

Key words.

  • hithhallēk ’et-hā’ĕlōhîm, walked with God: The verb means to walk to and fro, habitually. It is used again of Noah (6:9). The Greek Old Testament reads “pleased God”, which Sirach 44:16 and Hebrews 11:5 echo, and the POC's “ദൈവത്തിനു പ്രിയങ്കരനായി” stands close to that sense.
  • lāqaḥ, took: An ordinary verb for “take”, used again of Elijah, whom the Lord would take away (2 Kings 2:3). The text does not explain where Enoch went.
  • nōaḥ, Noah: Lamech's saying (v. 29) plays on a Hebrew verb for comfort or relief. A name becomes a prayer.

Verse by verse.

“Thus all the days of Jared were nine hundred sixty-two years; and he died.” (5:20)

The refrain is a bell. It carries out the word of 3:19, “to dust you shall return”, which was spoken over us on Vibhoodi. Great age does not change the last word.

“Enoch walked with God after the birth of Methuselah three hundred years, and had other sons and daughters.” (5:22)

Enoch's holiness is lived at home among children. Nothing here speaks of a desert or a cave. The same is offered to a father and mother in a Kerala house during the Fast.

“Enoch walked with God; then he was no more, because God took him.” (5:24)

The verse leaves the mystery alone. Later Jewish writers built a large literature around Enoch, and Jude 14–15 quotes from one such book. What matters is the first half of the sentence. Walking comes before being taken.

“he named him Noah, saying, “Out of the ground that the Lord has cursed this one shall bring us relief from our work and from the toil of our hands.”” (5:29)

These are the only spoken words in the chapter. A tired father looks at his son and hopes. The curse of 3:17 is remembered, and hope for relief runs ahead toward one who is greater than Noah.

How the Church reads it.

Sirach calls Enoch “an example of repentance to all generations” (44:16), which is why he suits this season so well. Hebrews places him in its list of faith: “he had pleased God” (11:5). St Irenaeus notes that Enoch pleased God without circumcision (in substance): the friendship of God is older than any sign. The Fast is a return to that first thing: to walk with God, step by step, in the house where we live.

Joshua 4:15–24 · What do these stones mean?

Setting.

After Moses' death Joshua leads Israel across the Jordan into the land. Chapters 3–4 tell the crossing as a liturgy more than a military campaign: priests carry the ark into the river, the waters stand still, the people cross, and twelve stones are taken from the riverbed. Many scholars think that the book reached its form long after the events, and that Israel's own later worship at Gilgal lies behind this telling. The reading is the close of the crossing.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–18: at God's word the priests come up, and the river returns.
  • v. 19: the date and the camp at Gilgal.
  • vv. 20–22: the stones and the children's question.
  • vv. 23–24: Jordan and Red Sea together; the purpose: all peoples, and fear of the Lord.

Key words.

  • ’ărôn habbərît, ark of the covenant: The sign of God's presence with his word. The waters stay back as long as it stands in the river: the crossing is God's doing.
  • gilgāl, Gilgal: The name is usually linked with a word for circle or rolling, and is often explained as a circle of stones. Here Israel will also keep the Passover (5:10).
  • yabbāšâ, dry ground: The word of Genesis 1:9, “let the dry land appear”, and of the Red Sea (Exodus 14:16). The God who made the land appear at creation makes it appear again for his people.

Verse by verse.

“The people came up out of the Jordan on the tenth day of the first month,” (4:19)

This is the date when the Passover lamb was to be chosen (Exodus 12:3). The new life in the land begins with a Passover calendar.

“When your children ask their parents in time to come, ‘What do these stones mean?’” (4:21)

Israel teaches by provoking a child's question, as at the Passover (Exodus 12:26). The stones do not speak by themselves; parents have to tell the story. That is what families do when a child asks why there is no meat this week.

“For the Lord your God dried up the waters of the Jordan for you until you crossed over, as the Lord your God did to the Red Sea, which he dried up for us until we crossed over,” (4:23)

“For you... for us”: the speaker merges the generations. Those who crossed the Red Sea are mostly dead, yet the story is “ours”. This is how Scripture remembers, and how the Church does in the Holy Qurbana.

“so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the Lord is mighty,” (4:24)

The miracle is not for Israel's pride. God's acts are meant to be seen by all peoples. So the reading looks beyond any one nation.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the crossing of the Jordan, by which God's people received the land, is an image of eternal life, and it is among the figures of Baptism (CCC 1222). Origen, in his homilies on Joshua, reads the crossing as a figure of Baptism (in substance). In the Greek Bible, Joshua's name is the same as the name of Jesus, and the Fathers made much of it: Jesus leads us through the water into the promise. The Great Fast was the ancient time when catechumens prepared for Baptism at Easter, so these verses speak directly to the season.

Romans 6:15–23 · The wages and the gift

Setting.

Paul has just said that “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (5:20). He knows how this will be heard: then let us go on sinning. He answers that objection at 6:1 with Baptism, “buried with him” (6:4), and answers it here again at v. 15 with the image of slavery. Slavery was everywhere in the Roman world, and some of his hearers in Rome were likely slaves themselves. The image is meant to be startling.

Structure.

  • v. 15: the objection.
  • v. 16: everyone serves someone.
  • vv. 17–18: you were slaves of sin; thanks be to God.
  • v. 19: Paul apologises for his human image; he asks for your members.
  • vv. 20–22: compare the two harvests.
  • v. 23: wages and gift.

Key words.

  • doulos, slave: The word Paul uses of himself in 1:1. There is no neutral, unowned human life: the choice is whom to obey.
  • typos didachēs, form of teaching: Many scholars think that this refers to the instruction given before Baptism. The Christian is “handed over” to the teaching, as a metal is to a mould.
  • opsōnia / charisma, wages / gift: Opsōnia was a term for a soldier's pay. Charisma is a gift of grace. Sin pays what it owes, while God gives what nobody earned (even our merit comes from his grace: CCC 2008).
  • hagiasmos, sanctification: Holiness as a process and a harvest. It is the fruit that grows in those belonging to God (vv. 19, 22).

Verse by verse.

“What then? Should we sin because we are not under law but under grace? By no means!” (6:15)

Grace does not loosen the moral life; it makes it possible. To be “under grace” does not mean that nothing matters. It means that for the first time we are able to obey.

“But thanks be to God that you, having once been slaves of sin, have become obedient from the heart to the form of teaching to which you were entrusted,” (6:17)

“From the heart” is the point. The Fast is a time of outward works, and Paul shows what they are for. Inner obedience is a thing to thank God for, because it is his work.

“I am speaking in human terms because of your natural limitations.” (6:19)

Paul knows that the image limps. God is no slave-driver. He uses the image in order to say that belonging is total.

“For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.” (6:23)

This verse is a gift to preachers. St Augustine noticed its surprise: Paul could have said that the wages of righteousness is eternal life, but he chose to say that it is a gift (in substance). We earn death. We can only be given life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that there is no true liberty except in the service of what is good and just, and that the choice to disobey is an abuse of liberty that leads to the slavery of sin (CCC 1733). On death as the consequence of sin, see CCC 1008. This is why the Church places the works of the Fast beside Baptism: each act of penance is a small daily return to what happened at the font. At every Holy Qurbana we receive the gift that verse 23 names.

Matthew 7:21–27 · Rock and sand

Setting.

These are the last words of the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), and Matthew then says the crowds were astounded (7:28). The closing warning is addressed to people who already know Jesus and call him Lord, and it applies especially to the religious.

Structure.

  • v. 21: not saying, but doing.
  • vv. 22–23: “On that day”: great deeds, and “I never knew you”.
  • vv. 24–25: the wise builder.
  • vv. 26–27: the foolish builder.
  • ¶ The two houses are the same in everything: same words heard, same rain, same wind. The foundation alone differs, and it cannot be seen until the storm.

Key words.

  • kyrie kyrie, Lord, Lord: The doubled address is respectful and earnest. The title itself is right, and Jesus does not reject it. It simply is not enough.
  • anomia, evildoers: Literally “you who work lawlessness”. The words echo Psalm 6:8. In Matthew the failure is to neglect the will of the Father.
  • phronimos / mōros, wise / foolish: The same pair as the ten bridesmaids (25:1–13). In Matthew, wisdom means hearing Jesus' word and doing it.

Verse by verse.

“Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father in heaven.” (7:21)

Jesus is the judge in this sentence, and this is an astonishing claim from a teacher on a hillside. “My Father” occurs here for the first time on his lips in Matthew's Gospel, and doing the Father's will is what he himself will pray for in Gethsemane.

“Then I will declare to them, ‘I never knew you; go away from me, you evildoers.’” (7:23)

Their gifts may have been real. Prophecy and miracles can be done in Jesus' name by those who do not love him. To “know” here means the relationship of the covenant, and that is what they lack.

“Everyone then who hears these words of mine and acts on them will be like a wise man who built his house on rock.” (7:24)

“These words of mine”: the whole Sermon. Jesus places his own teaching as the foundation, where Israel's teachers would place God's law. It is a claim about who he is.

“The rain fell, the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house, but it did not fall,” (7:25)

In the dry valleys of Palestine a winter storm can send a sudden torrent through a place that looked safe. Readers in Kerala need no explanation. The wise man is not spared the storm. Both houses take the same rain.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the prayer of faith consists not only in saying “Lord, Lord”, but in disposing the heart to do the Father's will (CCC 2611), and that the New Law demands a decisive choice between “the two ways”, to put the Lord's words into practice (CCC 1970). The Second Vatican Council warns that a person who remains in the Church in body but does not persevere in charity is not saved (Lumen Gentium 14). As St James says, “be doers of the word” (1:22). St Augustine reads the rock as Christ himself (in substance), as Paul does in 1 Corinthians 10:4.

Questions people ask

How could people live for nine hundred years?

Genesis is not giving a medical record. Other peoples of the ancient East recorded vast ages before the flood, and the numbers differ among the ancient copies of Genesis itself. The Church does not require us to count them as modern statistics. Their teaching lies elsewhere: in the blessing that continues, in the death that comes to all, and in the one who walked with God.

Did Enoch go to heaven before Christ?

The text says only that “God took him”, and Hebrews 11:5 says that he “did not experience death”. Scripture does not say where he went, and the Church has defined nothing on it. What the New Testament does say is that “No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended from heaven” (John 3:13), and the Catechism teaches that Christ alone opens that way to us (CCC 661).

Paul says that we are saved by gift, and Jesus says we must do the will of the Father. Do they disagree?

No. Both speak of one life. Paul says that the source is grace, and he insists in today's epistle that those who receive it become obedient “from the heart”. Jesus speaks of the fruit by which that life is known. The Church holds them together as “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6; see CCC 1814–1816).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

This is the Second Sunday of the Great Fast, the Sauma Ramba. Through the first week the calendar has read Genesis and Joshua together with Romans and the Sermon on the Mount, and Genesis has moved from creation to the line of Seth. In the Syriac tradition, Aphrahat the Persian Sage taught in his treatise on fasting that a pure heart is a better fast than abstaining from bread and water alone (in substance). The Fast is the digging of a foundation: this is what the ash, the abstinence and the Friday Way of the Cross are for. As the stones at Gilgal do, these customs prompt children to ask, and we are to tell them.

For study and teaching

  • Enoch walked with God while raising a family. Which ordinary part of my day could become a place of walking with God?
  • What are the “stones” in our home and parish that make children ask? Do we tell them the story?
  • Paul says that everyone serves someone. Looking at how I actually spend my time and money, whom do I serve?
  • If a storm came this week, what would my house be found to be standing on? What one thing from the Sermon on the Mount will I act on in this Fast?
