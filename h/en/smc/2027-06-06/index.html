Word for the day

“Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” Luke 6:36

Reflection

On the Fourth Sunday of the Apostles the readings move from the altar to the whole of life: to the courtroom, the market, the playing field and the place where we meet our enemies. The first reading continues Moses' farewell on the plains of Moab. He remembers how he organised the people in the wilderness and appointed judges, and what he told them: “Give the members of your community a fair hearing, and judge rightly between one person and another, whether citizen or resident alien. You must not be partial in judging: hear out the small and the great alike; you shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God’s.” Three things stand out. Justice must reach the outsider as well as the citizen. The poor man's case must be heard as carefully as the powerful man's. And the judge must not be afraid, because he judges in God's place and will answer to God. Every Christian who holds even a small authority hears these words: the teacher who marks answer papers, the officer who processes a file, the panchayat member, the parish committee, parents settling a quarrel between their children. Kerala today has hundreds of thousands of workers from other states; the resident alien of Deuteronomy is the migrant labourer on our building sites, who must receive his wages and his rights as fairly as anyone else. The Season of the Apostles shows us a Church that organised itself in the same spirit: when the Greek-speaking widows were neglected in the daily distribution, the apostles appointed seven men to see that they received justice (Acts 6:1–6).

Isaiah's words are among the sharpest in the whole Bible. He addresses Jerusalem with the names of the two most notorious cities of the Old Testament: “Hear the word of the Lord, you rulers of Sodom! Listen to the teaching of our God, you people of Gomorrah!” Then God speaks of the very worship that the people thought pleased him: “What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? … I cannot endure solemn assemblies with iniquity… When you stretch out your hands, I will hide my eyes from you; even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood.” God is not against sacrifice and prayer; he commanded them. He is against worship that leaves life untouched, festivals celebrated with the money of the cheated, prayers lifted by hands that oppress. A great parish feast, with illuminations and fireworks, means little to God if the workers who built the stage were not paid, or if a family in the parish has no food. Then come the commands: “Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your doings from before my eyes; cease to do evil, learn to do good; seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow.” And, unexpectedly, the most tender invitation: “Come now, let us argue it out, says the Lord: though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be like snow; though they are red like crimson, they shall become like wool.” God's anger is the anger of a lover who wants his people back. The door of the sacrament of Reconciliation is that invitation made concrete: come now, and the scarlet will become white.

In the Epistle Paul explains how he lives as an apostle. Though he owed no one anything, he made himself everyone's servant in order to win them: “To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews… To the weak I became weak, so that I might win the weak. I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” He did not change the Gospel, but he changed himself, his habits, his way of speaking, his food and his customs, so that nothing in him would block the Gospel. The apostles who carried the faith to the nations did the same. Mar Thoma's children in India lived for centuries as Indians in culture, Christians in faith and Oriental in worship: they kept the customs of their land, its dress and its family life, and they kept the faith of the apostle. Then Paul turns to sport, which the Corinthians loved, for their city hosted the Isthmian Games: “Do you not know that in a race the runners all compete, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may win it. Athletes exercise self-control in all things; they do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable one.” Children going back to school this week, students preparing for entrance examinations, young people training for a match know what discipline costs. Paul asks the same seriousness for the soul: a rule of prayer, fasting on the days the Church asks, mastery of the tongue and the eyes, “so that after proclaiming to others I myself should not be disqualified.”

The Gospel is from Luke's Sermon on the Plain, which the calendar gives in a longer form beginning with the choice of the Twelve apostles. Jesus has just named his apostles, and the first thing he teaches them is the hardest: “But I say to you that listen, Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you.” Four commands, each going further than the last: love in the heart, good in action, blessing on the lips, and prayer before God for the one who harms us. Then Jesus gives the rule that sums up the law: “Do to others as you would have them do to you.” And he asks three questions that strip away our self-satisfaction: “If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners love those who love them.” Christian love begins where ordinary decency ends. Finally Jesus gives the reason: “Your reward will be great, and you will be children of the Most High; for he is kind to the ungrateful and the wicked. Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” We do not love our enemies because they deserve it, but because God has loved us when we did not deserve it. The Indian Church has seen this Gospel lived in our own time. When Blessed Rani Maria, a Franciscan Clarist sister from near Perumbavoor, was murdered in Madhya Pradesh in 1995, her family forgave the man who killed her, and her sister visited him in prison and called him brother. Mercy did what punishment alone could not do: it changed his heart. Justice in Deuteronomy, a washed heart in Isaiah, discipline in Paul and mercy in Luke belong together. The disciple of Jesus is just, clean of heart, disciplined and, above all, merciful as the Father is merciful.

Homily outline

Theme: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.”

On the Fourth Sunday of the Apostles: impartial justice, worship with clean hands, the discipline of the race, and mercy like the Father's.

1. Hear out the small and the great alike

  • Judges in God's place.
  • The citizen and the resident alien.
  • Not intimidated by anyone.
“You must not be partial in judging: hear out the small and the great alike; you shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God’s.” Deuteronomy 1:17
“My brothers and sisters, do you with your acts of favoritism really believe in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ?” James 2:1

Apply: Give the same fair hearing to everyone you deal with this week, and pay every worker fully and on time.

2. Wash yourselves; learn to do good

  • Rulers of Sodom.
  • Worship with unjust hands.
  • Scarlet becomes snow.
“learn to do good; seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow.” Isaiah 1:17
“leave your gift there before the altar and go; first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.” Matthew 5:24

Apply: Before next Sunday's Qurbana, make peace with one person or make a good confession.

3. Run so as to win

  • All things to all people.
  • The Way of Thomas in India.
  • The imperishable wreath.
“but those who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength, they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary, they shall walk and not faint.” Isaiah 40:31
“Do you not know that in a race the runners all compete, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may win it.” 1 Corinthians 9:24

Apply: Keep one spiritual discipline this week as faithfully as an athlete keeps training.

4. Merciful as your Father is merciful

  • Love, do good, bless, pray.
  • The golden rule.
  • Children of the Most High.
“The Lord passed before him, and proclaimed, “The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness,” Exodus 34:6
“Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” Luke 6:36

Apply: Pray by name each day this week for someone who has hurt you.

The disciple of Jesus is just in judgment, clean of hands, disciplined in spirit and merciful as the Father.

Mercy can turn an enemy into a brother, as it did for Blessed Rani Maria's family.

Take-home resolution: This week, judge fairly, wash your hands of injustice, keep one discipline, and bless someone who has hurt you.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's readings take our faith out of the church and into daily life. Moses tells the judges of Israel: give a fair hearing to the citizen and the stranger; hear out the small and the great alike; do not be afraid of anyone, for the judgment is God's (Deuteronomy 1:16–17).

Each of us judges others: parents, teachers, employers, officers, committee members. Do we give the poor man the same hearing as the rich man? Do we pay the migrant worker on our building site the full wage on time? Justice is the first form of love.

Through Isaiah God says something shocking: I cannot bear your feasts and prayers when your hands are full of injustice. Wash yourselves; learn to do good; defend the orphan and the widow. Then he says: come, let us argue it out; though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be like snow (Isaiah 1:18). God's door is always open to the one who repents.

Saint Paul says: I have become all things to all people, that I might save some (1 Corinthians 9:22). He compares the Christian to an athlete who trains hard for a prize. Athletes discipline themselves for a medal that fades; we run for a crown that lasts for ever. Let us bring that same discipline to prayer, fasting and self-control.

And in the Gospel Jesus tells his apostles: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you (Luke 6:27–28). It is easy to love those who love us. Jesus asks more: be merciful, just as your Father is merciful (Luke 6:36).

Blessed Rani Maria's family forgave the man who killed her, and he became like a brother to them. Mercy changed him. This week let us name in prayer someone who has hurt us, and ask God to bless them. Then we will truly be children of the Most High. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: love even those who are unkind to you, and pray for them. That is hard! But Jesus helps us.

Remember the golden rule: treat others the way you want them to treat you. Today, say a little prayer for someone who was not nice to you.

Prayer

Lord God, judge of all the earth, you told the judges of Israel to hear the small and the great alike, and you told your people to wash their hands of injustice before they lift them in prayer. Make us just in every judgment and clean of heart; though our sins are like scarlet, make them white as snow. Give us the discipline of athletes who run for the imperishable wreath, and the humility of your apostle who became all things to all. Lord Jesus, you prayed for those who crucified you: teach us to love our enemies, to bless those who curse us, and to be merciful as our Father is merciful. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
