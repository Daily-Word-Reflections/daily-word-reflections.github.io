Word for the day

“Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” Luke 6:36

Reflection

On the Fourth Sunday of the Apostles the readings move from the altar to the whole of life: to the courtroom, the market, the playing field and the place where we meet our enemies. The first reading continues Moses' farewell on the plains of Moab. He remembers how he organised the people in the wilderness and appointed judges, and what he told them: “Give the members of your community a fair hearing, and judge rightly between one person and another, whether citizen or resident alien. You must not be partial in judging: hear out the small and the great alike; you shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God’s.” Three things stand out. Justice must reach the outsider as well as the citizen. The poor man's case must be heard as carefully as the powerful man's. And the judge must not be afraid, because he judges in God's place and will answer to God. Every Christian who holds even a small authority hears these words: the teacher who marks answer papers, the officer who processes a file, the panchayat member, the parish committee, parents settling a quarrel between their children. Kerala today has hundreds of thousands of workers from other states; the resident alien of Deuteronomy is the migrant labourer on our building sites, who must receive his wages and his rights as fairly as anyone else. The Season of the Apostles shows us a Church that organised itself in the same spirit: when the Greek-speaking widows were neglected in the daily distribution, the apostles appointed seven men to see that they received justice (Acts 6:1–6).

Isaiah's words are among the sharpest in the whole Bible. He addresses Jerusalem with the names of the two most notorious cities of the Old Testament: “Hear the word of the Lord, you rulers of Sodom! Listen to the teaching of our God, you people of Gomorrah!” Then God speaks of the very worship that the people thought pleased him: “What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? … I cannot endure solemn assemblies with iniquity… When you stretch out your hands, I will hide my eyes from you; even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood.” God is not against sacrifice and prayer; he commanded them. He is against worship that leaves life untouched, festivals celebrated with the money of the cheated, prayers lifted by hands that oppress. A great parish feast, with illuminations and fireworks, means little to God if the workers who built the stage were not paid, or if a family in the parish has no food. Then come the commands: “Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your doings from before my eyes; cease to do evil, learn to do good; seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow.” And, unexpectedly, the most tender invitation: “Come now, let us argue it out, says the Lord: though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be like snow; though they are red like crimson, they shall become like wool.” God's anger is the anger of a lover who wants his people back. The door of the sacrament of Reconciliation is that invitation made concrete: come now, and the scarlet will become white.

In the Epistle Paul explains how he lives as an apostle. Though he owed no one anything, he made himself everyone's servant in order to win them: “To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews… To the weak I became weak, so that I might win the weak. I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” He did not change the Gospel, but he changed himself, his habits, his way of speaking, his food and his customs, so that nothing in him would block the Gospel. The apostles who carried the faith to the nations did the same. Mar Thoma's children in India lived for centuries as Indians in culture, Christians in faith and Oriental in worship: they kept the customs of their land, its dress and its family life, and they kept the faith of the apostle. Then Paul turns to sport, which the Corinthians loved, for their city hosted the Isthmian Games: “Do you not know that in a race the runners all compete, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may win it. Athletes exercise self-control in all things; they do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable one.” Children going back to school this week, students preparing for entrance examinations, young people training for a match know what discipline costs. Paul asks the same seriousness for the soul: a rule of prayer, fasting on the days the Church asks, mastery of the tongue and the eyes, “so that after proclaiming to others I myself should not be disqualified.”

The Gospel is from Luke's Sermon on the Plain, which the calendar gives in a longer form beginning with the choice of the Twelve apostles. Jesus has just named his apostles, and the first thing he teaches them is the hardest: “But I say to you that listen, Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you.” Four commands, each going further than the last: love in the heart, good in action, blessing on the lips, and prayer before God for the one who harms us. Then Jesus gives the rule that sums up the law: “Do to others as you would have them do to you.” And he asks three questions that strip away our self-satisfaction: “If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you? For even sinners love those who love them.” Christian love begins where ordinary decency ends. Finally Jesus gives the reason: “Your reward will be great, and you will be children of the Most High; for he is kind to the ungrateful and the wicked. Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” We do not love our enemies because they deserve it, but because God has loved us when we did not deserve it. The Indian Church has seen this Gospel lived in our own time. When Blessed Rani Maria, a Franciscan Clarist sister from near Perumbavoor, was murdered in Madhya Pradesh in 1995, her family forgave the man who killed her, and her sister visited him in prison and called him brother. Mercy did what punishment alone could not do: it changed his heart. Justice in Deuteronomy, a washed heart in Isaiah, discipline in Paul and mercy in Luke belong together. The disciple of Jesus is just, clean of heart, disciplined and, above all, merciful as the Father is merciful.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's readings take our faith out of the church and into daily life. Moses tells the judges of Israel: give a fair hearing to the citizen and the stranger; hear out the small and the great alike; do not be afraid of anyone, for the judgment is God's (Deuteronomy 1:16–17).

Each of us judges others: parents, teachers, employers, officers, committee members. Do we give the poor man the same hearing as the rich man? Do we pay the migrant worker on our building site the full wage on time? Justice is the first form of love.

Through Isaiah God says something shocking: I cannot bear your feasts and prayers when your hands are full of injustice. Wash yourselves; learn to do good; defend the orphan and the widow. Then he says: come, let us argue it out; though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be like snow (Isaiah 1:18). God's door is always open to the one who repents.

Saint Paul says: I have become all things to all people, that I might save some (1 Corinthians 9:22). He compares the Christian to an athlete who trains hard for a prize. Athletes discipline themselves for a medal that fades; we run for a crown that lasts for ever. Let us bring that same discipline to prayer, fasting and self-control.

And in the Gospel Jesus tells his apostles: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you (Luke 6:27–28). It is easy to love those who love us. Jesus asks more: be merciful, just as your Father is merciful (Luke 6:36).

Blessed Rani Maria's family forgave the man who killed her, and he became like a brother to them. Mercy changed him. This week let us name in prayer someone who has hurt us, and ask God to bless them. Then we will truly be children of the Most High. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: love even those who are unkind to you, and pray for them. That is hard! But Jesus helps us.

Remember the golden rule: treat others the way you want them to treat you. Today, say a little prayer for someone who was not nice to you.

Prayer

Lord God, judge of all the earth, you told the judges of Israel to hear the small and the great alike, and you told your people to wash their hands of injustice before they lift them in prayer. Make us just in every judgment and clean of heart; though our sins are like scarlet, make them white as snow. Give us the discipline of athletes who run for the imperishable wreath, and the humility of your apostle who became all things to all. Lord Jesus, you prayed for those who crucified you: teach us to love our enemies, to bless those who curse us, and to be merciful as our Father is merciful. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s readings ask what kind of people God’s people should be. Moses charges the judges of Israel to hear the small and the great alike, because judgment belongs to God. Isaiah, continuing where last Sunday’s reading left off, tells Jerusalem that God cannot bear worship offered by hands full of blood: wash yourselves, learn to do good, defend the orphan and the widow, and though your sins are like scarlet they shall be like snow. Paul makes himself a slave to all to win them for Christ, and trains like an athlete for an imperishable wreath. And in Luke, Jesus asks the most demanding thing of all: love your enemies, be merciful as your Father is merciful.

The thread that joins them is the character of God himself. He shows no partiality; he wants justice more than sacrifices; he spares nothing to save; he is kind to the ungrateful and the wicked. In the Season of the Apostles the Church hears that those sent in Christ’s name must reflect the face of the Father.

Deuteronomy 1:16–18 · Hear the small and the great alike

Setting.

Moses is giving his first great speech in the plains of Moab, retelling the journey from Horeb. Last Sunday we heard God’s command to leave the mountain. Before telling of the departure, Moses recalls how he shared the burden of leadership: the people had grown so numerous that he could not bear them alone, so he took wise and experienced men from each tribe and set them as leaders and judges (1:9–15). The story of this appointment, made on the advice of his father-in-law Jethro, is told in Exodus 18:13–26.

Today’s verses are Moses’ charge to those judges. In a small community where everyone knew everyone, and where the powerful could easily press the weak, these words set out the basic principles of fair judgment.

Structure.

  • v. 16: hear the cases fairly and judge rightly between brothers, and between an Israelite and a resident alien.
  • v. 17a: no partiality: hear the small and the great alike; do not fear anyone, for the judgment is God’s.
  • v. 17b: cases too hard for you, bring to me.
  • v. 18: Moses gave the people all the commands they were to keep.

Key words.

  • šiptem ṣedeq, judge rightly: Literally “judge with righteousness”. Ṣedeq is the right order God wants among his people, not only correct procedure but what is truly just.
  • gēr, resident alien: A foreigner living among Israel without land or family protection. The Law repeatedly commands care for the stranger, “for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (Deuteronomy 10:19).
  • lō’ takkîrû pānîm, you must not be partial: Literally “do not recognise faces”: do not let the face of the person, rich or poor, powerful or weak, decide the case. The New Testament uses the same idea when it says God shows no partiality (Romans 2:11).

Verse by verse.

“Give the members of your community a fair hearing, and judge rightly between one person and another, whether citizen or resident alien.” (1:16)

The stranger is given the same hearing as the citizen. Justice that protects only one’s own is not God’s justice.

“You must not be partial in judging: hear out the small and the great alike” (1:17)

The small are named first. In practice it is the poor whose voice is easily ignored.

“you shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God’s.” (1:17)

A judge acts in God’s name, not in the name of the powerful. This gives courage, and also humility: the judge too will be judged.

“Any case that is too hard for you, bring to me, and I will hear it.” (1:17)

Even those with authority must know their limits and seek help. Good leadership is shared and humble.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism defines justice as the constant and firm will to give God and neighbour their due, and quotes the Law: “You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbour” (CCC 1807; Leviticus 19:15). The Letter of James warns Christians against showing partiality in the assembly itself, honouring the rich and dishonouring the poor (James 2:1–9). The Church’s social teaching asks all who hold authority to serve the common good and to give special care to the poor and the weak (CCC 1902–1903, 2448).

Isaiah 1:10–20 · Wash yourselves; learn to do good

Setting.

Isaiah’s first chapter is a kind of overture to the whole book. Last Sunday we heard its opening: a people wounded from head to foot, Jerusalem left like a hut in a field, saved from becoming like Sodom only because God kept a few survivors (1:1–9). Isaiah now takes up the name of Sodom and turns it into a sharp address to the leaders and people of Jerusalem.

The setting is the Temple, crowded with sacrifices and festivals. Isaiah himself had received his call in that Temple (6:1–8), so he is not attacking worship as such. He attacks worship cut off from justice: prayers lifted by hands stained with the blood of the oppressed.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the summons: hear the word of the Lord, you rulers of Sodom, you people of Gomorrah.
  • vv. 11–15: God refuses sacrifices, offerings, festivals and even prayers from hands full of blood.
  • vv. 16–17: nine commands: wash, be clean, remove evil, cease to do evil, learn to do good, seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow.
  • vv. 18–20: the offer: let us argue it out; scarlet sins made white; the choice between obedience and rebellion.

Key words.

  • tôrâ, teaching: Usually translated “law”, but its first meaning is instruction, the guidance a parent or teacher gives. Isaiah calls the prophetic word “the teaching of our God”.
  • niwwākĕḥâ, let us argue it out: A term of the law court: let us settle the case, reason it out together. The POC renders it “let us be reconciled”, which is where God wants the case to end.
  • mišpāṭ, justice: Right judgment in practice, especially for those who have no one to defend them. The orphan and the widow are the test of a society’s justice.

Verse by verse.

“Hear the word of the Lord, you rulers of Sodom! Listen to the teaching of our God, you people of Gomorrah!” (1:10)

A shocking address to the holy city. The Church hears it as spoken first to herself whenever her worship and her life part company.

“What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? says the Lord” (1:11)

God commanded sacrifices in the Law. What he rejects is the idea that they can replace a just life.

“even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood.” (1:15)

Raised hands were the posture of prayer. The same hands have harmed the weak. God will not look at hands he sees doing evil.

“Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your doings from before my eyes; cease to do evil, learn to do good; seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow.” (1:16–17)

The washing God wants is a changed life. “Learn” is important: doing good is a skill, practised and grown.

“Come now, let us argue it out, says the Lord: though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be like snow” (1:18)

The court case ends in an offer of mercy. Scarlet and crimson were fast dyes that could not be washed out; God promises to do what no washing can.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that outward sacrifice, to be genuine, must express spiritual sacrifice, and that the prophets often denounced sacrifices not from the heart or not joined to love of neighbour (CCC 2100). The Church has always heard in verse 18 the promise fulfilled in Baptism and in the sacrament of Penance, where God washes away sins we could never remove ourselves. St Justin Martyr quoted verses 16 to 20 when he explained Christian Baptism to the emperor, calling it the washing by which believers are made new (in substance, First Apology 61).

1 Corinthians 9:19–27 · Run so as to win the imperishable wreath

Setting.

Chapters 8 to 10 of the letter deal with food offered to idols, which some Corinthians ate without scruple, troubling the consciences of others. In chapter 9 Paul gives his own example: as an apostle he had rights, including the right to be supported by the community, but he gave them up so as not to hinder the Gospel (9:1–18).

In today’s verses he explains the principle behind his whole life: he adapts himself to everyone in order to win them for Christ. Then he turns to an image the Corinthians knew well. The Isthmian Games, second only to the Olympic Games, were held every two years near Corinth; the winner received a wreath of pine or celery that soon withered.

Structure.

  • v. 19: though bound to no one, Paul has made himself a slave to all.
  • vv. 20–22a: to Jews as a Jew, to those outside the law as one outside it, to the weak as weak.
  • vv. 22b–23: all things to all people, to save some; all for the sake of the Gospel.
  • vv. 24–25: in a race only one wins the prize; athletes train for a perishable wreath, we for an imperishable one.
  • vv. 26–27: Paul does not run aimlessly or box at the air; he disciplines his body so as not to be disqualified.

Key words.

  • ennomos Christou, under Christ’s law: Paul is not lawless. He is bound by the law of Christ, the law of love (Galatians 6:2). His flexibility is in customs, never in faith or morals.
  • stephanos, wreath: The victor’s crown of leaves at the games. Paul contrasts it with the crown of eternal life (2 Timothy 4:8; James 1:12).
  • hypōpiazō, I punish (my body): A boxing term, literally “to strike under the eye”. Paul uses it as a vivid picture of self-discipline, not of hatred for the body.

Verse by verse.

“For though I am free with respect to all, I have made myself a slave to all, so that I might win more of them.” (9:19)

Paul’s freedom is used for service. This is the pattern of Christ, who took the form of a slave (Philippians 2:7).

“To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews.” (9:20)

Paul was himself a Jew and kept Jewish customs when it helped his mission (Acts 21:26). He did not regard the practices of his people with contempt.

“I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” (9:22)

Not changing the message, but changing the manner. A missionary learns the language, food and customs of those he serves.

“Run in such a way that you may win it.” (9:24)

Not every runner wins at the games, but in God’s race all who run faithfully can receive the prize. The point is to run in earnest.

“Athletes exercise self-control in all things; they do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable one.” (9:25)

If athletes discipline themselves for a few leaves, how much more should Christians for eternal life?

“I punish my body and enslave it, so that after proclaiming to others I myself should not be disqualified.” (9:27)

Even the great apostle does not presume on his salvation. Preaching to others does not excuse the preacher from his own conversion.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has taken Paul’s “all things to all people” as a charter for inculturation: the Gospel must take flesh in each people’s culture while remaining whole and unchanged (Redemptoris Missio 52–54). The Catechism teaches that the way of perfection passes by way of the cross, that there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle, and that spiritual progress involves ascesis and mortification (CCC 2015). St John Chrysostom, preaching on this chapter, explains that Paul became all things not by changing his faith but by stooping to each person’s weakness out of love, as Christ did for us (in substance, Homilies on 1 Corinthians).

Luke 6:27–36 · Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful

Setting.

Luke’s Sermon on the Plain (6:17–49) is shorter than Matthew’s Sermon on the Mount, but shares much of it. Jesus has come down from the mountain where he chose the Twelve (6:12–16) and stands on a level place with a great crowd. He has just spoken four blessings for the poor, the hungry, those who weep and those who are hated, and four woes for the rich, the full, those who laugh and those who are praised (6:20–26).

Now he turns to “you that listen”. The love he commands is not a feeling but a decision to do good, even to those who do harm.

Structure.

  • vv. 27–28: love your enemies, do good, bless, pray.
  • vv. 29–30: the cheek, the coat, giving and not demanding back.
  • v. 31: the golden rule.
  • vv. 32–34: three questions: what credit is it to love, do good and lend only to those who return it?
  • vv. 35–36: love, do good, lend expecting nothing; you will be children of the Most High; be merciful as your Father is merciful.

Key words.

  • agapate tous echthrous, love your enemies: Agapē is love that wills the good of the other. Jesus does not ask us to feel affection for those who hurt us, but to want and do their good.
  • charis, credit: The word usually means grace or favour. “What charis is that to you?” asks what is gracious, or God-like, about loving only those who love us.
  • oiktirmōn, merciful: Compassionate, moved with pity. Where Matthew says “Be perfect” (5:48), Luke says “Be merciful”: the perfection of the Father is his mercy.

Verse by verse.

“But I say to you that listen, Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you” (6:27)

Four commands climb from inner attitude to action: love, do good, bless, pray. Prayer for an enemy is where love often has to begin.

“bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you.” (6:28)

Jesus lived this on the cross: “Father, forgive them” (23:34), and Stephen followed him (Acts 7:60).

“If anyone strikes you on the cheek, offer the other also” (6:29)

A blow on the cheek was above all an insult. Jesus asks his disciples not to answer insult with insult, breaking the chain of revenge (see the question below).

“Do to others as you would have them do to you.” (6:31)

Many teachers taught a form of this rule. Jesus puts it in its most active form and sets it within love of enemies.

“But love your enemies, do good, and lend, expecting nothing in return. Your reward will be great, and you will be children of the Most High; for he is kind to the ungrateful and the wicked.” (6:35)

The reason is God himself. Children resemble their Father; God sends his kindness even on those who do not thank him.

“Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” (6:36)

This verse sums up the whole passage. Mercy is not one virtue among others; it is the family likeness of God’s children.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ died out of love for us while we were still enemies, and that the Lord asks us to love as he does, even our enemies (CCC 1825). It explains that “be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” cannot be kept by imitating God from outside, but only by a vital participation in his love, and that Christian prayer extends to the forgiveness of enemies, transfiguring the disciple into the likeness of the Master (CCC 2842–2844). The Didache, one of the oldest Christian writings, begins its way of life with these words: bless those who curse you, pray for your enemies, love those who hate you (in substance, Didache 1). Pope Francis took “Merciful like the Father” from this verse as the motto of the Jubilee of Mercy (Misericordiae Vultus 13–14).

Questions people ask

When Jesus says “offer the other also”, does it mean we must accept abuse and never defend ourselves or others?

No. Jesus forbids revenge and the answering of insult with insult, not the protection of life and justice. When he was struck before the high priest, he asked, “If I have spoken rightly, why do you strike me?” (John 18:23). The Church teaches that legitimate defence is allowed, and is even a grave duty for those responsible for others (CCC 2263–2265). A person suffering abuse is not asked by the Gospel to remain in danger. Forgiveness heals the heart; it does not require us to call evil good or to leave the weak unprotected.

In Isaiah, does God reject worship and sacrifice?

No. The same God commanded the sacrifices in the Law, and Isaiah received his call in the Temple (6:1–8). What God rejects is worship used as a cover for injustice. The prophets join the two together, as Jesus does when he tells us to be reconciled with our brother before offering our gift at the altar (Matthew 5:23–24). Our worship, above all the Eucharist, is meant to make our lives just and merciful.

Was Paul being two-faced when he became “all things to all people”?

No. Paul never changed the Gospel; he firmly resisted even Peter when he thought the truth of the Gospel was at stake (Galatians 2:11–14). What he changed was his manner: customs, food, ways of speaking, so that nothing unnecessary would stand between people and Christ. Love made him flexible in small things so as to be faithful in great ones.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syro-Malabar Holy Qurbana the Peace is exchanged before the Anaphora begins, as we prepare to offer the gifts. Jesus’ command to love our enemies gives that gesture its full weight: we cannot share the peace of Christ with those near us while refusing to forgive. This Sunday we pray especially for Christians who suffer for their faith in many lands, and for those who have wronged them, that both may come to know the mercy of the Father.

Paul’s “all things to all people” also describes the long history of our Church. The St Thomas Christians kept the apostolic faith while living fully within the culture of Kerala; Fr Placid Podipara described them as Indian in culture, Christian in faith and Oriental in worship (in substance). In the Season of the Apostles we give thanks for that gift and pray for our missionaries who learn new languages and ways so that others may meet Christ.

For study and teaching

  • Moses told the judges to hear the small and the great alike. Whose voice in my family, parish or workplace is easily ignored, and how can I make sure it is heard?
  • Isaiah says “learn to do good”. What is one good act I could practise this week until it becomes a habit?
  • Paul trained like an athlete for an imperishable wreath. What discipline in prayer, fasting or self-control would help me run better?
  • Jesus asks us to pray for those who abuse us. Who is the person I find hardest to pray for, and will I name them before God today?
