Word for the day

“He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes and inherit a seat of honor. For the pillars of the earth are the Lord’s, and on them he has set the world.” 1 Samuel 2:8

Reflection

Yesterday we honoured grandparents, and today the Church gives us the grandparents of Jesus. The Bible does not name the parents of the Virgin Mary. Their names, Joachim and Anne, come to us from an early Christian writing of the second century, the Protoevangelium of James, which is not part of Scripture but which the Church has treasured for its picture of the family into which Mary was born. By tradition, then, Joachim and Anne were an older couple who had long prayed for a child. The Eastern Churches kept their feast from early times, and the West followed. The name Anne is the Greek form of Hannah, which means grace, and in Kerala it lives on in the name Annamma, borne by countless grandmothers, while Joachim, in its Malayalam form Yoakim, is the name of many a grandfather. We honour them as the saints who handed on faith to the one who gave the Saviour to the world.

The Church puts the song of Hannah on their lips. Hannah, long barren, prayed in the temple at Shiloh, and when her son Samuel was born she sang: “My heart exults in the Lord; my strength is exalted in my God.” It is the song that Mary will sing again in the Magnificat. “The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble gird on strength. Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread, but those who were hungry are fat with spoil. The barren has borne seven.” And above all: “He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes and inherit a seat of honor.” God is the one who reverses the world's order. The couple who, according to tradition, bore the shame of childlessness became the grandparents of the Messiah.

Isaiah continues the theme in his word to Jerusalem: “Sing, O barren one who did not bear; burst into song and shout, you who have not been in labor! For the children of the desolate woman will be more than the children of her that is married, says the Lord.” Then comes the command that every grandmother and grandfather needs: “Enlarge the site of your tent, and let the curtains of your habitations be stretched out; do not hold back.” The family of faith grows wider than anyone could have imagined, because the Lord says, “with everlasting love I will have compassion on you.” The letter to the Hebrews shows the same pattern in Abraham and Sarah. Of Abraham the letter says that “he considered him faithful who had promised,” and that “from one person, and this one as good as dead, descendants were born.” Faith is trust in the one who keeps his promise when our own strength is gone.

Matthew's Gospel opens with a list of names: “An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” It is a long chain of ordinary people, some famous, some not, some sinners, who handed on a promise from one generation to the next. Jesus did not fall from the sky; he was born into a family and a history. Joachim and Anne belong to that chain. In every Kerala home, the names of grandparents, the lamp lit at dusk, the Our Father taught at a grandmother's knee are the links of the same chain. And the chain ends in the verse that matters most: “Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah.”

The weekday readings add a sober word. James says to the rich: “Your riches have rotted, and your clothes are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have rusted, and their rust will be evidence against you.” The wages kept back by fraud “cry out.” In Luke, Jesus says it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God. The disciples ask in despair, “Then who can be saved?” and he answers, “What is impossible for mortals is possible for God.” The same tradition that names Joachim also remembers him as a rich man, but one who gave his gifts to the poor and to the Temple, and who, when he was humiliated for having no child, did not turn bitter but fasted and prayed. Riches do not save, and neither does poverty by itself; the grace of God saves, and it asks for a heart that gives and trusts. Peter says: “Look, we have left our homes and followed you.” Jesus answers that no one who has left house or family for the kingdom “will not get back very much more in this age, and in the age to come eternal life.” Grandparents are the best witnesses of this: a long life given away in love comes back as a great family and a rich old age.

Full homily

Dear friends, yesterday we honoured grandparents and the elderly. Today the Church remembers the most famous grandparents in the world: Saint Joachim and Saint Anne, the parents of the Virgin Mary and the grandparents of Jesus. The Bible does not give their names. They come from an early Christian tradition. But the Church has loved them for centuries.

The first reading gives us the song of Hannah, a woman who could not have a child and who prayed with all her heart. When God gave her a son she sang: “He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap” (1 Samuel 2:8). Mary would sing a song just like this in the Magnificat. According to the old tradition, Joachim and Anne also prayed for a child for a long time. God heard them.

The letter to the Hebrews tells us of Abraham and Sarah, who were too old to have children, and yet God made them parents of a great people because they trusted his promise (Hebrews 11:11–12). Faith means trusting the one who keeps promises, even when our own strength is gone.

The Gospel is a list of names: “An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah” (Matthew 1:1). It tells us that Jesus was born into a human family, with ancestors and grandparents. In our families, too, faith comes down through the generations: the grandmother who lights the lamp, the grandfather who takes us to church, the parents who teach us to pray. We are links in that chain.

The weekday readings add a warning. Saint James tells the rich: “Your riches have rotted” (James 5:2). And Jesus says what is impossible for us is possible for God (Luke 18:27). Let us not trust money. Let us thank God for our grandparents, pray for them, and hand on the faith to our children. Saints Joachim and Anne, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know who Mary's mummy and daddy were? Their names were Joachim and Anne, and they were Jesus' grandparents! They loved God and prayed a lot. Today, hug your grandparents, or if they are in heaven, say a little prayer: ‘Thank you, God, for my grandparents.’

Prayer

Lord God, you gave your Son a mother and a family, and you chose Joachim and Anne to hand on the faith to the Virgin Mary. Bless our grandfathers and grandmothers, living and departed. Reward their patience, their prayers and the hope they kept for us. Make us grateful for the chain of faith that has reached us, and let us pass it on to our children. Teach us, like Hannah, to pour our hearts before you, and, like Abraham and Sarah, to trust your promise when our own strength is spent. Keep us from the love of money that rots, and give us hearts that give. Through the prayers of Saint Joachim and Saint Anne, grant to the old and the lonely a peaceful home. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The weekday readings speak about wealth. James warns the rich who have kept back the wages of their workers: their riches are rotting, and the cry of the labourers has reached the Lord of hosts. In Luke, Jesus says it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God, and when the disciples ask who then can be saved, he answers that what is impossible for people is possible for God. Today the Church also remembers Saints Joachim and Anne, the parents of the Virgin Mary according to tradition, and offers readings for them about God who gives life to the barren: Hannah’s song, Isaiah’s “Sing, O barren one”, Abraham and Sarah in the Letter to the Hebrews, and the genealogy of Jesus in Matthew. The two sets meet in one truth: what people cannot do, God can, and he lifts up the poor and the lowly.

James 5:1–6 · The cry of the labourers

Setting.

The Letter of James is addressed to Jewish Christians “in the Dispersion” (1:1). Its writer is James, whom tradition identifies with the “brother of the Lord”, the leader of the Church in Jerusalem; some scholars think it was written in his name a little later. The letter is full of concern for the poor. Here, in the manner of the prophets, James turns to the rich landowners and speaks of judgment. He is not condemning wealth in itself but wealth gained by fraud and kept at the cost of others.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the rich must weep and wail; their riches rot, their gold rusts, and the rust will bear witness against them.
  • v. 4: the wages kept back by fraud cry out; the cry of the harvesters has reached the Lord of hosts.
  • vv. 5–6: lives of luxury; hearts fattened for a day of slaughter; the righteous one condemned and killed.

Key words.

  • ololyzō, to wail, howl: The word of the prophets for the cry of anguish at coming judgment (for example Isaiah 13:6). James speaks like Amos and Isaiah.
  • misthos, wages: The pay of labourers. The Law commanded that a hired worker be paid on the same day (Leviticus 19:13; Deuteronomy 24:14–15), because he is poor and depends on it.
  • kyrios sabaōth, Lord of hosts: Used in the New Testament only here and in Romans 9:29. It recalls the Lord who commands heaven’s armies and takes the side of the oppressed.

Verse by verse.

“Your riches have rotted, and your clothes are moth-eaten.” (5:2)

In the ancient world wealth was stored as grain, garments and metal. James says these treasures are already perishing, and what is hoarded is lost.

“Listen! The wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, cry out” (5:4)

The wages themselves cry out, as Abel’s blood cried from the ground (Genesis 4:10). God hears what no judge heard.

“You have condemned and murdered the righteous one, who does not resist you.” (5:6)

It may mean the poor and innocent, who cannot defend themselves in court. Christians have also heard in it an echo of the Righteous One, Christ (Acts 3:14; 7:52).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that a just wage is the legitimate fruit of work, and that to refuse or withhold it can be a grave injustice (CCC 2434, citing this letter and Leviticus 19:13, Deuteronomy 24:14–15, James 5:4). It lists the injustice done to wage earners among the sins that cry to heaven (CCC 1867). Passages such as this one are among the roots of the Church’s social teaching on the dignity of work.

Luke 18:25–30 · What is impossible for mortals is possible for God

Setting.

The passage follows the story of a rich ruler who asked what he must do to inherit eternal life and went away sad when Jesus asked him to sell his possessions and give to the poor (18:18–24). Jesus looks at him and says how hard it is for the wealthy to enter the kingdom. The camel and the needle’s eye is a deliberate exaggeration, a picture of what is humanly impossible. The idea that “the Needle’s Eye” was a low gate in Jerusalem is a later story with no support in the ancient texts.

Structure.

  • v. 25: a camel through the eye of a needle is easier than a rich person entering the kingdom.
  • vv. 26–27: “Then who can be saved?” “What is impossible for mortals is possible for God.”
  • vv. 28–30: Peter: “Look, we have left our homes and followed you.” Jesus promises much more in this age and eternal life in the age to come.

Key words.

  • adynata / dynata, impossible / possible: The answer turns on these two words. Salvation is not earned by human strength, whether rich or poor, but given by God’s power.
  • sōthēnai, to be saved: The question of the crowd is the question of every hearer. For Luke, salvation is already present in Jesus, and the poor and sinners are among the first to receive it.
  • pollaplasiona, many times more: Luke’s word for what is received in return. Mark has “a hundredfold” and adds “with persecutions” (10:30). What the disciple receives is not riches but a larger family, the community of believers.

Verse by verse.

“it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God.” (18:25)

The danger is not money as such but the heart that clings to it. Riches can fill the heart so that there is no room for God and neighbour.

“What is impossible for mortals is possible for God.” (18:27)

This is the Gospel in one line. The rich person can be saved, as can the poor, because grace can open what human effort cannot.

“there is no one who has left house or wife or brothers or parents or children, for the sake of the kingdom of God, who will not get back very much more in this age, and in the age to come eternal life.” (18:29–30)

The reward is promised both now and then. Those who give up their own security for the Gospel find a family of faith and the joy of belonging to Christ.

How the Church reads it.

St Clement of Alexandria wrote a whole treatise on this passage, Who Is the Rich Man That Is Saved? He taught in substance that what Jesus asks us to renounce is the passion for riches, not the possession of goods, and that a rich person can be saved by using wealth to help others. The Catechism teaches that poverty of heart is a requirement of the Gospel: the Lord asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544–2547), while those called to the evangelical counsels give up everything to follow him (CCC 915–916). It also teaches love for the poor as the sign of authentic discipleship (CCC 2443–2446).

1 Samuel 2:1–10 · Hannah’s prayer

Setting.

Hannah, wife of Elkanah, was barren and prayed at the sanctuary of Shiloh for a child. God gave her Samuel, and she brought him to serve the Lord (1:1–2:11). This song is her thanksgiving. Its themes are the reversal of fortunes: the strong are broken, the weak are raised, the poor are seated with princes. The name Anne is the Greek form of Hannah, which means “favour, grace”.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: joy in the Lord, the Holy One and Rock.
  • vv. 3–8: the proud are brought down; the barren bears seven; the Lord raises the poor from the dust.
  • vv. 9–10: the Lord guards the faithful; the Lord judges the ends of the earth and exalts his anointed.

Key words.

  • ṣûr, rock: A name for God as the unshakable refuge of his people (compare Deuteronomy 32:4).
  • mĕšîḥô, his anointed: The last word of the song. In its first sense it is the king of Israel; the Church also hears the Messiah, whom Mary’s own song, the Magnificat, will recall.

Verse by verse.

“The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble gird on strength.” (2:4)

God’s way overturns human strength. The weak are not helpless because the Lord is their strength.

“He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap” (2:8)

This is the verse behind many psalms and the Magnificat: Mary says he has “lifted up the lowly” (Luke 1:52).

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always seen Hannah’s prayer as the model for Mary’s Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55); the two songs share the same movement of praise for a God who raises the lowly (CCC 2619). Today Saint Anne, who tradition says was granted a child after long waiting, is honoured as a woman of the same faith.

Isaiah 54:1–8 · Sing, O barren one

Setting.

These verses come from the second part of Isaiah, addressed to the exiles in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ. Jerusalem, left desolate, is pictured as a barren woman and a forsaken wife. God now calls her to sing and to enlarge her tent, for her children will be more than ever. The “Maker” who is her husband has not abandoned her for good.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: sing, enlarge your tent; your descendants will fill the nations.
  • vv. 4–6: do not fear; your Maker is your husband; the Lord calls you back like a wife forsaken.
  • vv. 7–8: for a brief moment I abandoned you; with everlasting love I will have compassion on you.

Key words.

  • gō’ēl, redeemer: The close relative who rescues a family member from slavery or debt (Ruth 4). God takes on that role for his people.
  • raḥămîm / ḥesed ‘ôlām, compassion / everlasting love: Raḥămîm comes from the word for womb: the mother’s tenderness. The same Lord who seems silent for a moment loves with a love that does not end.

Verse by verse.

“Enlarge the site of your tent, and let the curtains of your habitations be stretched out” (54:2)

Faith prepares room before the gift arrives. Hope enlarges the heart.

“For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you.” (54:7)

The shadows are real but brief. God’s final word is not abandonment but the gathering of his people.

How the Church reads it.

The Church applies verse 1 to the barren who become mothers by God’s grace: Sarah, Hannah, Elizabeth, and Saint Anne. Saint Paul applies the same verse to the Jerusalem above, our mother (Galatians 4:26–27). The Catechism teaches that God’s power is shown in the weakness and lowly estate of those he chooses (CCC 64, 489, on the holy women of the Old Testament who prepared for Mary).

Hebrews 11:8–12 · By faith Abraham and Sarah

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews, written to Jewish Christians who were tempted to lose heart, presents a long list of people of faith in chapter 11. Abraham and Sarah stand at the head: they trusted the promise of a land and a child when both seemed impossible. The reading is chosen for Joachim and Anne because they too, according to tradition, waited long for a child.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–10: Abraham obeys the call, lives in tents as a stranger, and looks for the city whose builder is God.
  • vv. 11–12: by faith Abraham, too old and with Sarah barren, received power of procreation; from one man, as good as dead, came descendants like the stars.

Key words.

  • pistis, faith: In Hebrews, faith is trust that holds fast to what is promised though it is not yet seen (11:1).
  • technitēs kai dēmiourgos, architect and builder: The two words for the maker of the city. Abraham looks for a city that human hands did not found.

Verse by verse.

“By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to set out for a place that he was to receive as an inheritance; and he set out, not knowing where he was going.” (11:8)

Faith moves before the road is clear. Abraham is the pattern of obedient trust.

“because he considered him faithful who had promised.” (11:11)

The reason for hope is not our strength but the faithfulness of God who promises.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to Abraham as the model of faith in his obedience to the call (CCC 144–147) and to Sarah, whose child Isaac was born by God’s promise (CCC 706). The Church holds up these ancestors in faith to the families who wait on God. Saints Joachim and Anne are remembered in the Church as a couple of the same patient trust.

Matthew 1:1–16 · The genealogy of Jesus the Messiah

Setting.

Matthew opens his Gospel with the family tree of Jesus, arranged in three sets of fourteen generations (v. 17), from Abraham to David, from David to the exile in Babylon, and from the exile to the Messiah. In Hebrew letters the name David adds up to fourteen, a clue to the pattern. Jesus is presented as son of Abraham, the father of the people, and son of David, the king.

Unusually for a Jewish genealogy, four women are named: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and “the wife of Uriah” (Bathsheba). The list ends with a break in the pattern: Joseph is “the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born”. It is Mary, not Joseph, through whom Jesus is born, and Matthew goes on to tell of his conception by the Holy Spirit (1:18–25). The names of Joachim and Anne do not appear in Scripture; they come from the second-century Protoevangelium of James, an early writing the Church does not receive as Scripture, and tradition has kept the names.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the title: the genealogy of Jesus Christ, son of David, son of Abraham.
  • vv. 2–6: from Abraham to David, including Tamar, Rahab and Ruth.
  • vv. 7–11: from Solomon to the exile in Babylon.
  • vv. 12–16: from the exile to Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born.

Key words.

  • biblos geneseōs, book of origins: The same words as the opening of Genesis in the Greek Bible (Genesis 2:4; 5:1). Matthew begins a new genesis, a new creation, in Christ.
  • christos, Messiah: The Greek for “anointed”. The first verse names Jesus as the Messiah, son of David.

Verse by verse.

“An account of the genealogy of Jesus the Messiah, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” (1:1)

In one line, Matthew places Jesus in the whole story of Israel. The promise to Abraham and the promise to David meet in him.

“and Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called the Messiah.” (1:16)

The pattern “father of” breaks here. Joseph is not named as Jesus’ father but as Mary’s husband: Jesus is born of Mary.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the genealogy as proof that God’s promises are kept through history, including through sinners and foreigners. The Catechism teaches that Mary, the daughter of Zion, is the one in whom the long preparation of the Old Covenant reaches its goal (CCC 489). The Church honours the parents of the Virgin Mary in this tradition of those who handed on faith, and Mary’s own descent from David has been the common teaching of Christians from early times.

Questions people ask

Who were Saints Joachim and Anne?

The Gospels do not name the parents of the Virgin Mary. The names Joachim and Anne come from the Protoevangelium of James, written in the second century. The Church does not treat this book as Scripture or as a reliable historical source for its details, but the names have been honoured in the East from early centuries and in the West from the Middle Ages, and 26 July is kept for them. They are remembered as the grandparents of Jesus according to tradition, and as an example of faithful parents who handed on the faith.

Does the Gospel say the rich cannot be saved?

No. Jesus says it is hard, not that it is impossible, and he says that all things are possible for God. Zacchaeus, a rich man, was saved when he gave half his goods to the poor and repaid those he had cheated (Luke 19:1–10). The danger is the heart that clings to riches and forgets God and neighbour.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Saints Joachim and Anne remind the Syro-Malabar family of the part grandparents play in handing on the faith. In many Kerala homes it is the grandmother who teaches the first prayers and the grandfather who leads the evening prayer, and these are among the things the parish and the eparchy are glad to protect. The readings of the day also ask how a family uses its goods: the wages owed to workers, the generosity of the home, and the trust in God’s power that is greater than our plans.

For study and teaching

  • James says the wages held back “cry out” to the Lord. Do I pay a fair wage and a fair price, and do I listen to the workers whose labour I use?
  • Jesus says that what is impossible for people is possible for God. What do I think is impossible in my life, and can I place it in his hands?
  • Hannah, Sarah and Anne waited long for a child. What am I waiting for, and how do I wait in faith?
