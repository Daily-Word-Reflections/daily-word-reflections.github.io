Word for the day

“He said to her, “Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease.”” Mark 5:34

Reflection

Today's Gospel is a story inside a story. Jesus is on his way to the house of Jairus, whose little daughter is dying, and a great crowd is pressing in on him. Into that crowd comes a woman nobody notices. Mark describes her in a few heavy lines: she “had been suffering from hemorrhages for twelve years. She had endured much under many physicians, and had spent all that she had; and she was no better, but rather grew worse.” Twelve years of bleeding meant twelve years of exhaustion, and, under the law of purity in Leviticus, twelve years of being kept at a distance from worship and from the touch of others. Her money is gone, her health is going, and her place in the community has quietly disappeared. She is the kind of person a crowd walks past. Yet she has heard about Jesus, and hearing has become hope: “If I but touch his clothes, I will be made well.” She does not ask for attention. She only reaches out from behind, in the crush of bodies, and touches the edge of his cloak.

“Immediately her hemorrhage stopped; and she felt in her body that she was healed of her disease.” She could have slipped away, healed and unseen. But Jesus stops. “Immediately aware that power had gone forth from him, Jesus turned about in the crowd and said, ‘Who touched my clothes?’” The disciples find the question almost foolish: everyone is touching him. But there is a difference between the crowd that presses and the one who touches in faith. Many brush against Jesus; one person reaches for him. Jesus will not let her go home with a healed body and a hidden heart. He wants to meet her. And so she comes “in fear and trembling, fell down before him, and told him the whole truth.” Then comes the word that she will remember for the rest of her life: “Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease.” She came as a nameless case; she leaves as a daughter. The healing of her body is followed by the healing of her place among the people of God. This is what the Holy Qurbana is meant to be for us: not a crowd pressing in, but a touch of faith, and a word of peace that sends us home as sons and daughters.

Paul's letter opens with a blessing that could be the woman's song: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction with the consolation with which we ourselves are consoled by God.” Paul wrote these words after a time of great suffering in Asia, when he despaired even of life (2 Corinthians 1:8). He does not pretend that the apostles are spared trouble. He says rather that consolation is a gift that flows on: God consoles us so that we can console others. The comfort we receive is not meant to stop with us, any more than the woman's healing was meant to stay hidden. In this Season of the Apostles we remember that the Church was born among sufferings, and that the apostles became channels of a consolation greater than their own. Every Christian who has been comforted by God in grief, illness or failure has something to give to someone else who is now walking that same road.

June has come to Kerala with the monsoon and with the opening of schools. The rain beats on the roofs, children set out with new bags and old fears, parents count fees and worry about the year ahead. The Gospel today is a quiet encouragement for all who feel lost in the crowd: the sick who have tried many remedies, the families who have spent all they had on treatment, the student who feels invisible in a large class, the elderly who are seldom touched or visited. Jesus notices the one touch of faith in the crowd. He turns, he looks, he calls by name. And he sends his disciples, the apostles of every age, to be the same kind of presence: to notice, to stop, and to console with the consolation they themselves have received.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's Gospel tells of a woman who was sick for twelve years. She had gone to many doctors and spent everything she had, and she only grew worse. Because of her illness she was also kept away from others. She was the kind of person a crowd walks past.

But she had heard about Jesus. And she said to herself: “If I but touch his clothes, I will be made well” (Mark 5:28). She reached out from behind and touched his cloak, and immediately she was healed.

Then Jesus stopped. “Who touched my clothes?” The disciples said: everyone is touching you! But there is a difference between pressing around Jesus and touching him with faith. Many were near him; one person reached for him. Every Sunday many of us come close to the altar. Do we press around him like a crowd, or do we reach out to him in faith?

The woman came trembling and told him the whole truth. And Jesus said: “Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace.” She came as a nameless sick woman; she went home as a daughter. Jesus heals not only the body but our place in God's family.

Saint Paul calls God “the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation”, who consoles us so that we may console others (2 Corinthians 1:3–4). The comfort God gives us is not meant to stop with us. In these days of rain and new school years, let us look for the person no one notices, and let us pass on the consolation we have received. Amen.

For children

Children, a sick lady was so sure of Jesus' power that she thought: if I just touch his clothes, I will be well. And she was! Jesus stopped and looked for her, because he wanted to be her friend, not only her doctor. Jesus sees you too, even in a big crowd or a big new classroom. And you can help him by being kind to the child nobody notices.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you turned in the crowd to find the woman who touched your cloak in faith, and you sent her home as a daughter, healed and at peace. Look on all who are weary of illness, on families who have spent everything for treatment, and on those who feel unseen. Give us faith to reach out to you, especially in the Holy Qurbana. Father of mercies and God of all consolation, comfort us in every affliction, so that we may comfort others with the consolation we have received. Holy Spirit, make us apostles of consolation in our homes, schools and parishes. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the comfort of God reaching people who suffer. Paul blesses “the God of all consolation”, who comforts him in every trouble so that he can pass that comfort on to others. In Mark, a woman who has suffered for twelve years reaches out in faith, touches the cloak of Jesus and is healed, and then hears him call her “Daughter”. In the Season of the Apostles the Church learns that the consolation of Christ is meant to flow on: the one who has been comforted becomes a comforter.

2 Corinthians 1:3–7 · The God of all consolation

Setting.

Second Corinthians was written after a painful time between Paul and the community at Corinth: a hurtful visit, a severe letter written “with many tears” (2:4), and news of a terrible affliction he had suffered in the province of Asia (1:8). Instead of the usual thanksgiving after the greeting, Paul begins with a blessing of God, in the Jewish form “Blessed be God…”. The word “consolation” and its verb occur ten times in these five verses.

Structure.

  • v. 3: the blessing: God is the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation.
  • v. 4: God comforts us so that we may comfort others with the same comfort.
  • v. 5: as Christ’s sufferings overflow to us, so does consolation through Christ.
  • vv. 6–7: Paul’s sufferings and comfort are for the Corinthians; they share both, and his hope for them is firm.

Key words.

  • paraklēsis, consolation, comfort, encouragement: From the verb “to call to one’s side”. It is the comfort of someone who stands beside us, not only kind words. The same root gives Paraklētos, the name Jesus gives the Holy Spirit (John 14:16).
  • thlipsis, affliction: Literally “pressure, crushing”. Paul uses it for real hardship: danger, opposition, despair (1:8). God does not always remove the pressure; he comforts within it.
  • ta pathēmata tou Christou, the sufferings of Christ: Paul does not say his sufferings are like Christ’s; he says Christ’s sufferings overflow into his life. The apostle lives the life of his Lord, the Cross included.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation,” (1:3)

“Father of mercies” is a Jewish way of praying, still heard in synagogue prayer. Paul joins it to the name of Jesus: the merciful God is known as the Father of our Lord.

“who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction” (1:4)

Comfort is never a private possession. What God gives us in our trouble becomes the gift we can hand to another.

“For just as the sufferings of Christ are abundant for us, so also our consolation is abundant through Christ.” (1:5)

Two overflowings, and the second matches the first. Those who share the Cross are not left without the comfort of the Risen Lord.

How the Church reads it.

Pope St John Paul II, in his letter on human suffering Salvifici Doloris (1984), dwells on Paul’s words about sharing the sufferings of Christ: human suffering, joined to Christ, is drawn into the work of redemption and becomes a source of strength for others (in substance, Salvifici Doloris 20–24). The Catechism teaches the same about the sick: by grace, suffering can be united to the saving Passion of Christ (CCC 1521). The Church’s works of mercy, among them to comfort the afflicted, are the shape of this reading in daily life (CCC 2447).

Mark 5:25–34 · Daughter, your faith has made you well

Setting.

Jesus is on his way to the house of Jairus, a leader of the synagogue whose little daughter is dying (5:21–24). A large crowd presses around him. Mark sets one story inside another: the healing of the woman is told in the middle of the raising of Jairus’ daughter. Both concern a “daughter”, and the number twelve joins them: the woman has suffered for twelve years, and the girl is twelve years old (5:42).

Under the Law of Moses a woman with a flow of blood was ritually unclean, and so was anything she touched (Leviticus 15:25–27). This was a matter of worship, not of guilt, but for twelve years it would have kept her apart from the Temple and from much of ordinary life.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–26: twelve years of suffering, doctors, poverty, and no cure.
  • vv. 27–28: she has heard of Jesus; she comes from behind and touches his cloak.
  • v. 29: at once the bleeding stops, and she knows she is healed.
  • vv. 30–32: Jesus knows that power has gone out from him and looks for the one who touched him.
  • vv. 33–34: she tells the whole truth; he calls her “Daughter” and sends her away in peace.

Key words.

  • dynamis, power: The power of God at work in Jesus. Mark says it “had gone forth from him”: the healing is not magic in the cloak but the living power of the Lord.
  • sesōken, has made you well, has saved you: The Greek verb sōzō means both to heal and to save. The woman is healed in her body, and she is also given salvation and peace.
  • thygatēr, daughter: In the Gospels this woman is the one individual Jesus addresses as “daughter” (Matthew and Luke tell the same healing). A woman who came hidden and alone is given a family name in public.

Verse by verse.

“She had heard about Jesus, and came up behind him in the crowd and touched his cloak,” (5:27)

She comes from behind, perhaps afraid that her uncleanness would be noticed. Her faith is humble and hidden, but it is real.

“Immediately aware that power had gone forth from him, Jesus turned about in the crowd and said, “Who touched my clothes?”” (5:30)

Jesus does not ask because he must find a culprit. He wants to meet her. A healing received in secret is to become a face-to-face encounter.

“He said to her, “Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease.”” (5:34)

“Go in peace” is a Hebrew blessing; the priest Eli sent Hannah away with the same words (1 Samuel 1:17). Her body was already healed; now her shame is healed too, and she is restored before everyone.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the sacraments are “powers that come forth” from the Body of Christ, which is ever-living and life-giving (CCC 1116), using the words of this story. What the woman received by touching his cloak, the Church receives in the sacraments. St Augustine, preaching on this miracle, contrasts the crowd that pressed on Jesus with the woman who touched him: the crowd presses, faith touches (in substance). Many crowd around Christ; few reach out to him in faith.

Questions people ask

Was the woman wrong to touch Jesus when the Law said she was unclean?

Jesus never blames her. The Law of uncleanness was about fitness for worship, not about sin. In Jesus the movement is reversed: her uncleanness does not pass to him; his holiness and healing pass to her. That is why he calls her forward, not to shame her, but so that everyone may see that she is clean and a daughter of God.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says God comforts us so that we may comfort others. Whom has God comforted through me, and to whom could I pass on his comfort this week?
  • The woman came from behind, then stood before Jesus and told him the whole truth. What hidden need could I bring to him openly in prayer?
