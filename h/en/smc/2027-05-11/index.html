Word for the day

“So it is with those who store up treasures for themselves but are not rich toward God.” Luke 12:21

Reflection

A man in the crowd interrupts Jesus with a family quarrel: “Teacher, tell my brother to divide the family inheritance with me.” Anyone who has lived in Kerala knows how familiar that sentence sounds. A few cents of land, a share in the ancestral house, a dispute over who looked after the parents: brothers stop speaking, cousins go to court, and sometimes the quarrel outlives everyone who began it. Jesus refuses to become the judge of the case: “Friend, who set me to be a judge or arbitrator over you?” He is not indifferent to justice; he goes to the root of the problem: “Take care! Be on your guard against all kinds of greed; for one's life does not consist in the abundance of possessions.” The word for life here is not the life of the body but life itself, the real life of a person. A house can be divided fairly and the heart still be poisoned by greed. Jesus wants to heal the heart.

Then he tells the parable of the rich man whose land produced abundantly. Notice how he speaks: “What should I do, for I have no place to store my crops?” “I will pull down my barns and build larger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods. And I will say to my soul, Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry.” He talks only to himself. There is no one else in his plans: no God to thank, no neighbour to share with, no poor man at his gate. Then God speaks for the first time, and calls him by his true name: “You fool! This very night your life is being demanded of you. And the things you have prepared, whose will they be?” The answer is painfully simple: someone else's, perhaps the very brothers who will quarrel over them. Jesus ends: “So it is with those who store up treasures for themselves but are not rich toward God.” To be rich toward God is to let our goods pass through our hands into his: in thanksgiving, in almsgiving, in support for the Church and the poor. In these days before Pentecost, the Holy Spirit is the true treasure we ask for; and he is given not to those who fill their barns but to those who empty their hands.

Paul deals with a different question in Corinth, but the root is the same. Some Christians knew that idols are nothing, and so they ate meat sacrificed to idols without a second thought. Paul agrees that “food will not bring us close to God”. But he adds: “take care that this liberty of yours does not somehow become a stumbling block to the weak.” A believer whose conscience is still fragile might see a strong Christian eating in an idol's temple and be led back into idolatry. “So by your knowledge those weak believers for whom Christ died are destroyed.” The phrase cuts deep: the weak brother is one for whom Christ died. Knowledge that ignores him is not wisdom but pride; earlier in the same chapter Paul says, “Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up” (1 Corinthians 8:1). His conclusion is generous: “Therefore, if food is a cause of their falling, I will never eat meat, so that I may not cause one of them to fall.” Our rights, like our riches, are given us for others. A Christian who drinks heavily in front of a young person, who mocks the simple faith of an old woman, or who boasts of his knowledge while his neighbour stumbles, has forgotten that his brother is someone for whom Christ died.

Full homily

Dear friends, a man in the crowd asks Jesus, “Teacher, tell my brother to divide the family inheritance with me.” How often we hear such quarrels in our own families: over land, over the house, over money.

Jesus does not take sides. He goes to the root: “Take care! Be on your guard against all kinds of greed; for one's life does not consist in the abundance of possessions.” A property can be divided fairly, and still the heart can be full of greed. Jesus wants to heal the heart.

Then he tells the story of a rich man. His land gave a great harvest, and he said: I will build bigger barns, and then I will rest, eat, drink and be merry. He spoke only to himself. No God, no neighbour, no poor man in his plans. And God said: “You fool! This very night your life is being demanded of you.” All his grain was left behind.

Paul tells the Corinthians: be careful that your liberty does not become a stumbling block to the weak. The weak brother is someone “for whom Christ died”. “Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up.” Our rights and our riches are given to us for others.

In these days before Pentecost we ask for the greatest treasure, the Holy Spirit. He fills not full barns but open hands. Let us empty one barn this week, make peace in our families, and be rich toward God. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told about a man who kept all his grain for himself in big barns and never shared. God called him foolish!

Jesus wants us to be ‘rich toward God’. How? By sharing! This week, share one of your toys, snacks or pencils with someone, and you will be rich toward God.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you became poor for our sake, so that by your poverty we might become rich. Guard us against every kind of greed. Heal the quarrels in our families over land and money, and teach us that our life does not consist in our possessions. Make us rich toward God by sharing what we have with the poor. Give us a love that builds up, so that we never cause a weaker brother or sister to stumble, remembering that you died for them. Send us your Holy Spirit, the true treasure, and fill our empty hands. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two things that can turn us in on ourselves: knowledge and possessions. In Corinth some Christians knew that idols are nothing and used that knowledge without caring for weaker believers; Paul says that love must govern what we know. In Luke a man asks Jesus to settle an inheritance, and Jesus tells of a rich man who spoke only to himself and forgot God. In both readings the question is the same: whom do my gifts serve?

1 Corinthians 8:8–13 · Do not become a stumbling block to the weak

Setting.

In a city like Corinth much of the meat sold in the market came from animals sacrificed in pagan temples, and temples had dining rooms where people held family and social meals. The Corinthians had asked Paul about this food (8:1). Some reasoned that, since “no idol in the world really exists” (8:4), they could eat anything anywhere.

Paul agrees that the idols are nothing. But he begins with a warning: “Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up” (8:1). Later he will forbid taking part in sacrificial meals as such (10:20–21). Here his concern is the brother or sister whose faith is still fragile.

Structure.

  • v. 8: food does not bring us closer to God, or take us further away.
  • v. 9: take care that your liberty does not trip up the weak.
  • v. 10: an example: someone sees you eating in an idol’s temple.
  • vv. 11–12: the weak destroyed by your knowledge; to sin against them is to sin against Christ.
  • v. 13: Paul’s own resolve: I will never eat meat if it makes one fall.

Key words.

  • exousia, liberty: Literally a right or an authority. The Corinthians were proud of their right. Paul does not deny it, but in the next chapter he shows how he himself gave up his rights for the sake of the Gospel (9:12).
  • syneidēsis, conscience: The inner judgment by which a person knows right and wrong. A weak conscience is not a wicked one; it is one not yet fully formed, and it can be wounded.
  • oikodomeō, encouraged: In v. 10 the Greek says literally that the weak conscience will be “built up” to eat idol food. Paul turns the Corinthians’ own word against them: your example will “build up” your brother, all the way to his ruin.

Verse by verse.

“But take care that this liberty of yours does not somehow become a stumbling block to the weak.” (8:9)

A stumbling block is a stone on the path that makes someone fall. What is harmless for me may be the stone my brother trips over.

“So by your knowledge those weak believers for whom Christ died are destroyed.” (8:11)

Paul measures the worth of a weak believer by the price paid for him: the death of Christ. No Christian is unimportant.

“Therefore, if food is a cause of their falling, I will never eat meat, so that I may not cause one of them to fall.” (8:13)

Just before, Paul says that to sin against them is to “sin against Christ” (8:12). It is the lesson Paul learned on the Damascus road: Christ and his members are one.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes v. 12 among the rules that guide conscience: charity always proceeds by way of respect for one’s neighbour and his conscience (CCC 1789). Paul gives the same teaching in Romans: “Do not let what you eat cause the ruin of one for whom Christ died” (Romans 14:15). The principle reaches beyond food: to drink, to speech, to what we post and share. Liberty is a gift of Christ, and love decides how it is used.

Luke 12:13–21 · The parable of the rich fool

Setting.

Jesus is on his long journey to Jerusalem (9:51), and the crowd has gathered “by the thousands” (12:1). He has just been speaking of fearless witness and of God’s care for those who trust him (12:4–12). A man interrupts with a family quarrel.

Teachers of the law were sometimes asked to settle such disputes, and the law of Moses gave rules for inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17). The man is not asking Jesus to judge fairly; he has already decided what Jesus should say.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the request about the inheritance.
  • v. 14: Jesus refuses to be made a judge.
  • v. 15: the warning against all kinds of greed.
  • vv. 16–19: the parable: a great harvest, bigger barns, a conversation with his own soul.
  • v. 20: God speaks: “You fool!”
  • v. 21: the lesson: rich toward God.

Key words.

  • pleonexia, greed: Literally “having more”, the desire for more and more. The Letter to the Colossians calls greed idolatry (Colossians 3:5): what we cannot stop wanting has become our god.
  • aphrōn, fool: In the Bible a fool is not a person without intelligence; this man is clever with his crops. The fool is the one who lives as if God did not exist: “Fools say in their hearts, ‘There is no God’” (Psalm 14:1).
  • psychē, soul, life: The rich man speaks to his own psychē, “Soul, you have ample goods”. That night his psychē, his life, is demanded of him. The same word frames the story: he planned everything for his soul except the soul itself.

Verse by verse.

“for one’s life does not consist in the abundance of possessions.” (12:15)

Jesus does not say that possessions are evil. He says that life is not made of them. We are more than what we own.

“And I will say to my soul, Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry.” (12:19)

He speaks only to himself. In his speech there is no God, no neighbour, no family: only “I”, “my” and “my soul”. Sirach describes the same man, who says, “I have found rest, and now I shall feast on my goods!” and does not know how soon he will die (Sirach 11:19).

“You fool! This very night your life is being demanded of you. And the things you have prepared, whose will they be?” (12:20)

The Greek says literally “they are demanding your soul”, an indirect way of speaking of God’s action. The man worried about room for his crops; he never asked where his soul would go.

How the Church reads it.

St Basil the Great preached on this parable to the rich of his city: the bread you keep belongs to the hungry, and the coat in your chest to the one who has none (in substance, Homily on “I will pull down my barns”). The Catechism teaches that the tenth commandment forbids greed and the desire to amass earthly goods without limit (CCC 2536). To be “rich toward God” is to store up treasure in heaven (Matthew 6:20), and the way to heaven passes through the hands of the poor.

Questions people ask

Is it wrong to save money or plan for the future?

No. Prudence is a virtue, and Scripture praises Joseph, who stored grain in Egypt so that the land would not perish in the famine (Genesis 41:36). The rich man’s fault is not planning but planning only for himself, as if he were the owner of his life and of his goods. The question Jesus asks is not “Do you save?” but “For whom, and toward whom, do you live?”

For study and teaching

  • Is there something I have a right to do that might cause a weaker brother or sister to stumble? How would love change my choice?
  • If my life were demanded of me tonight, what would I be “rich toward God” in?
