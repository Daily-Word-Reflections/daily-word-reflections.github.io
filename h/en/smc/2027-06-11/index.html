Word for the day

“for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith. And a great many people were brought to the Lord.” Acts 11:24

Reflection

Today the Church keeps the feast of St Barnabas, whom the Acts of the Apostles calls an apostle together with Paul (Acts 14:14), though he was not one of the Twelve. His real name was Joseph. He was a Levite from Cyprus, and the apostles gave him a new name, Barnabas, “which means ‘son of encouragement’” (Acts 4:36). We first meet him in the earliest days of the Church in Jerusalem, when he sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet. It is a small gesture, but it tells us who he is: a man who gives without holding back, and who trusts the community of the apostles. All through Acts he lives up to his name. Wherever Barnabas appears, someone is encouraged, welcomed, trusted, or given a second chance.

The clearest example is Saul. After his conversion on the road to Damascus, the disciples in Jerusalem were afraid of him; they remembered the persecutor and did not believe he had changed. “But Barnabas took him, brought him to the apostles, and described for them how on the road he had seen the Lord” (Acts 9:27). Barnabas risked his own reputation to vouch for a former enemy. Without that act of trust, the Church might have kept at a distance the greatest missionary she has ever had. Today's reading shows him again in the same role. When the Gospel reaches Gentiles in Antioch, the church in Jerusalem sends Barnabas to see what is happening. “When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced, and he exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast devotion; for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” He does not come as an inspector looking for faults, but as a brother looking for grace, and when he finds it he rejoices. Then he does something humble and wise: he goes to Tarsus to look for Saul, and brings him to Antioch, and the two of them teach there for a whole year. “It was in Antioch that the disciples were first called ‘Christians.’”

From Antioch the Holy Spirit sends them out. “While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.’ Then after fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off” (Acts 13:2–3). Barnabas and Paul go first to Cyprus, Barnabas' own island, and then into Asia Minor. In this Season of the Apostles we see the Church's mission born from worship, fasting and the laying on of hands: not from a human plan, but from the Spirit speaking in a praying community. The Epistle tells how, years later, Paul and Barnabas went up to Jerusalem to lay before the leaders the gospel they preached among the Gentiles, and how “James and Cephas and John, who were acknowledged pillars, recognized the grace that had been given to me, they gave to Barnabas and me the right hand of fellowship.” The unity of the Church, with Peter among the pillars, guarded the truth of the Gospel. And the leaders asked only one thing: “that we remember the poor.” Barnabas, who had sold his field for the poor, would not have needed reminding.

Barnabas was not without weakness. Paul tells that at Antioch “even Barnabas was led astray” for a time when some held back from eating with Gentile believers (Galatians 2:13). And Acts honestly records that Paul and Barnabas had a sharp disagreement over John Mark, who had left them in the middle of their first journey; “they parted company; Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus” (Acts 15:39). Barnabas gave Mark a second chance, as he had once given Saul a first one. Later Paul himself would write, “Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful in my ministry” (2 Timothy 4:11). The encourager had been right about his cousin. Tradition holds that Barnabas died a martyr in Cyprus, at Salamis.

The Gospel is the Lord's warning to those he sends: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” The apostles will be handed over and hated, but they will not be alone: “it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” And the psalm of the first reading gives the apostle's song: “O give thanks to the Lord, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples.” The weekday Gospel of this Fourth Friday, the raising of Jairus' daughter, adds a word that Barnabas lived: “Do not fear, only believe” (Mark 5:36). Every parish needs a Barnabas: someone who sees grace and rejoices, who welcomes the newcomer and the convert, who speaks for the person others are afraid of, and who gives a second chance to those who have failed. Today let us ask him to make us sons and daughters of encouragement.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate St Barnabas. His name was Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, but the apostles called him Barnabas, “son of encouragement” (Acts 4:36). He sold his field and gave the money to the apostles for the poor. Wherever he went, people were encouraged.

When Saul was converted, the Christians in Jerusalem were afraid of him. But Barnabas took him by the hand, brought him to the apostles and spoke for him (Acts 9:27). Without Barnabas, the Church might have kept Saul, the future Saint Paul, at a distance.

In today's reading the Church sends Barnabas to Antioch, where many Gentiles have believed. He does not come to find faults. “When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced” (Acts 11:23). Scripture says, “he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” Then he goes to look for Saul, and they teach together for a year. In Antioch the disciples were first called Christians.

While the church at Antioch was praying and fasting, the Holy Spirit said: “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul” (Acts 13:2). The mission of the Church is born at prayer. The apostles in Jerusalem gave them the right hand of fellowship, and asked only that they remember the poor (Galatians 2:9–10).

Barnabas also gave a second chance to young Mark, who had once left the mission. Paul later wrote that Mark was useful in his ministry (2 Timothy 4:11). Jesus warns that his apostles go out like sheep among wolves, but the Spirit of the Father speaks through them (Matthew 10:16–20). Tradition says Barnabas died a martyr in Cyprus.

Every family and every parish needs a Barnabas. Let us look for God's grace in others and rejoice in it; let us welcome the newcomer and the one others fear; let us give a second chance; and let us remember the poor. St Barnabas, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Barnabas was called the son of encouragement because he always made people feel brave and loved. When everyone was afraid of Saul, Barnabas became his friend.

This week, be a Barnabas! Sit with the child who is alone, say something kind to someone who is sad, and help a friend who made a mistake to try again.

Prayer

Lord God, you chose St Barnabas, a good man full of the Holy Spirit and of faith, to encourage the young Church, to welcome Saul, to rejoice in your grace among the Gentiles, and to carry the Gospel to the nations. By his prayers make us sons and daughters of encouragement: generous with the poor, welcoming to newcomers, quick to see your grace, slow to judge, and ready to give a second chance. Send labourers into your harvest, keep them wise and innocent among dangers, and let your Spirit speak through them. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Barnabas Shleeha the readings follow an apostle’s life from praise to mission to suffering. The song from Chronicles calls God’s people to make known his deeds among the nations. In Acts, Barnabas is sent to Antioch, rejoices at the grace of God he finds there, fetches Saul from Tarsus, and in that city the disciples are first called Christians. In Galatians, Paul goes up to Jerusalem with Barnabas, and the pillars of the Church give them the right hand of fellowship, asking only that they remember the poor. And in Matthew, Jesus sends his apostles like sheep among wolves, promising the Spirit of the Father to speak through them. Barnabas, the “son of encouragement”, shows what an apostle looks like: generous, joyful, quick to see God’s grace in others, and faithful under trial.

1 Chronicles 16:8–12 · Make known his deeds among the peoples

Setting.

The Books of Chronicles retell Israel’s history with special care for worship. In chapter 15 David brings the ark of the covenant into Jerusalem with music and joy. In chapter 16 the ark is set in its tent, offerings are made, and David appoints Levites to give thanks and praise. On that day a psalm of thanksgiving is sung (16:8–36).

The psalm is made of parts of three psalms found in the Psalter: verses 8–22 match Psalm 105:1–15, verses 23–33 match Psalm 96, and verses 34–36 come from Psalm 106. Today’s verses are its opening call to praise.

Structure.

  • v. 8: give thanks, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples.
  • vv. 9–10: sing, tell his wonderful works, glory in his holy name.
  • vv. 11–12: seek the Lord and his presence; remember his works and judgments.

Key words.

  • hôdû, give thanks: From a root that means both to thank and to confess. To give thanks is to proclaim publicly what God has done.
  • ‘ălîlōtāyw, his deeds: God’s acts in history: the call of Abraham, the Exodus, the gift of the land. Israel’s praise tells a story.
  • biqšû pānāyw, seek his presence: Literally “seek his face”. With the ark now in Jerusalem, Israel can come before God in worship; the call is to seek him “continually”.

Verse by verse.

“O give thanks to the Lord, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples.” (16:8)

Praise that stays inside Israel is not enough; God’s deeds are to be told to the nations. The verse is a seed of mission.

“Glory in his holy name; let the hearts of those who seek the Lord rejoice.” (16:10)

Joy is the mark of those who seek God. Barnabas, too, “rejoiced” when he saw God’s grace (Acts 11:23).

“Seek the Lord and his strength, seek his presence continually.” (16:11)

Not a single act but a lifelong search. Even those who have found God keep seeking him.

“Remember the wonderful works he has done, his miracles, and the judgments he uttered,” (16:12)

Remembering is the root of praise. In the Holy Qurbana the Church remembers the greatest of God’s works, the death and Resurrection of Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Church prays these words also in the Psalter, as Psalm 105. The Catechism teaches that praise is the form of prayer that recognises most directly that God is God, giving him glory for his own sake (CCC 2639), and that the Psalms, sung by Christ and the Church, remain the great school of prayer (CCC 2585–2589). The call to “make known his deeds among the peoples” is fulfilled when the apostles proclaim the Gospel to all nations.

Acts 11:21–26 · A good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith

Setting.

After the martyrdom of Stephen, believers scattered by persecution went as far as Phoenicia, Cyprus and Antioch (11:19). At Antioch, the great city of Syria on the river Orontes, some men from Cyprus and Cyrene began to proclaim the Lord Jesus to Greeks as well, not only to Jews (11:20). The church in Jerusalem sends Barnabas to see what is happening.

Barnabas has appeared before. His name was Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus; the apostles called him Barnabas, “son of encouragement”, and he sold a field and laid the money at their feet (4:36–37). When the disciples in Jerusalem were afraid of the newly converted Saul, it was Barnabas who brought him to the apostles and vouched for him (9:27).

Structure.

  • v. 21: the hand of the Lord is with them; many believe and turn to the Lord.
  • v. 22: Jerusalem sends Barnabas to Antioch.
  • vv. 23–24: he sees the grace of God, rejoices, encourages all to remain faithful; he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.
  • vv. 25–26: he fetches Saul from Tarsus; a year of teaching; the disciples first called Christians at Antioch.

Key words.

  • parekalei, he exhorted, he encouraged: The verb of Barnabas’ own name, “son of encouragement” (paraklēsis). He does exactly what his name says.
  • agathos, good: Luke rarely calls anyone “good”. Barnabas’ goodness shows in his joy at others’ success and his generosity in bringing Saul to share the work.
  • Christianoi, Christians: A Latin-style ending added to Christos: “those who belong to Christ”. The name was probably given by outsiders, who saw that these people were always speaking of Christ.

Verse by verse.

“News of this came to the ears of the church in Jerusalem, and they sent Barnabas to Antioch.” (11:22)

The mother church keeps communion with the new community. Barnabas, a Greek-speaking Jew from Cyprus, was well suited to a mixed church.

“When he came and saw the grace of God, he rejoiced, and he exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast devotion;” (11:23)

He did not come to inspect suspiciously but to recognise God’s work. To see grace in others and rejoice is itself a grace.

“for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith.” (11:24)

The same was said of Stephen (6:5). Holiness, not cleverness, made Barnabas fruitful.

“Then Barnabas went to Tarsus to look for Saul,” (11:25)

Barnabas does not keep the work for himself. He goes looking for the gifted man others had forgotten, and the history of the Church changes.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours Barnabas as an apostle, a title Luke himself gives him (Acts 14:14), though he was not one of the Twelve. With Paul he was set apart by the Holy Spirit and sent on the first missionary journey (13:2–3). Pope Benedict XVI, in his Wednesday catechesis on Barnabas, Silas and Apollos (2007), described Barnabas as a man of encouragement and reconciliation who believed in Paul when others doubted, and who later gave a second chance to John Mark (in substance; see Acts 15:36–39). Ancient tradition, not Scripture, says he died a martyr in Cyprus.

Galatians 2:1–10 · The right hand of fellowship

Setting.

Paul is defending the Gospel he preached to the Galatians. Some teachers were insisting that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses. Paul shows that his Gospel came from Christ by revelation (1:12) and that the leaders in Jerusalem recognised it.

He tells of a visit to Jerusalem “after fourteen years”, with Barnabas and Titus, a Gentile believer. Many scholars think this is the meeting described in Acts 15, often called the Council of Jerusalem; others connect it with the famine-relief visit of Acts 11:30. Either way, the outcome is the same: the Gentiles need not be circumcised, and the apostles are united.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Paul goes up with Barnabas and Titus, by revelation, and lays his Gospel before the leaders.
  • vv. 3–5: Titus is not compelled to be circumcised; Paul did not yield to false believers.
  • v. 6: the leaders added nothing to his Gospel.
  • vv. 7–9: they recognise that Paul is sent to the Gentiles as Peter to the circumcised; James, Cephas and John give the right hand of fellowship.
  • v. 10: only one request: remember the poor.

Key words.

  • anethemēn, I laid before them: Paul sets his Gospel before the leaders for their judgment. He is sure of his call, yet he seeks communion with those who were apostles before him.
  • styloi, pillars: James, Cephas (Peter) and John are called pillars of the Church, as of a temple. The image recalls that the Church is God’s house, built on the apostles (Ephesians 2:20).
  • dexias koinōnias, the right hands of fellowship: A handshake of agreement and partnership. Koinōnia is communion: one faith, one Church, different fields of mission.

Verse by verse.

“Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus along with me.” (2:1)

Barnabas stands beside Paul at this decisive moment. The fourteen years are counted either from Paul’s conversion or from his first visit to Jerusalem (1:18); scholars differ.

“in order to make sure that I was not running, or had not run, in vain.” (2:2)

Paul’s Gospel did not depend on human approval, but its fruit depended on communion. A Gospel cut off from the Church would be running in vain.

“and when James and Cephas and John, who were acknowledged pillars, recognized the grace that had been given to me, they gave to Barnabas and me the right hand of fellowship” (2:9)

The pillars recognise “grace”, not merely ability. Unity in the Church is the recognition of God’s gifts in one another.

“They asked only one thing, that we remember the poor, which was actually what I was eager to do.” (2:10)

Care for the poor is the one condition added to the Gospel. Paul later organised a great collection for the poor of Jerusalem (Romans 15:25–27; 2 Corinthians 8–9).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ built his Church on the apostles, with Peter at their head, and that their communion continues in the bishops with the successor of Peter (CCC 880–881). Peter’s place among the apostles was given him by Christ (Matthew 16:18), and Paul here seeks communion with him. The Second Vatican Council points to the Church’s mission to the nations as born at Pentecost and carried on by the apostles in communion (Ad Gentes 4–5). The one request, to remember the poor, is still the mark of every true mission (CCC 2443–2444).

Matthew 10:16–22 · Sheep in the midst of wolves

Setting.

Matthew 10 is Jesus’ great instruction to the Twelve as he sends them out (10:1–5). The first part concerns their mission in Galilee. From verse 16 the horizon widens: Jesus speaks of councils and governors, kings and Gentiles, persecution and family division. These words look ahead to the whole mission of the Church after Easter.

Jesus speaks of being flogged “in their synagogues”. In the first decades, disputes between the followers of Jesus and other Jews were disputes within the one people of Israel; the apostles themselves were Jews. These words are about the cost of witness, never a reason to blame the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4).

Structure.

  • v. 16: sheep among wolves; wise as serpents, innocent as doves.
  • vv. 17–18: councils, synagogues, governors and kings: a testimony.
  • vv. 19–20: do not worry what to say; the Spirit of your Father will speak in you.
  • vv. 21–22: division in families; hated because of my name; the one who endures to the end will be saved.

Key words.

  • phronimoi, wise, shrewd: Practical, clear-sighted good sense. Disciples are not to be naïve about danger.
  • akeraioi, innocent: Literally “unmixed”, pure, without hidden motives. Wisdom without innocence becomes cunning; innocence without wisdom becomes foolishness.
  • martyrion, testimony, witness: The word from which “martyr” comes. To stand before rulers for Christ’s sake is itself to bear witness to him.

Verse by verse.

“See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” (10:16)

“I am sending you”: the mission begins in Christ. The sheep are not left alone; the Shepherd sends them and goes with them.

“When they hand you over, do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say; for what you are to say will be given to you at that time;” (10:19)

Not a reason to neglect preparation, but a promise for the hour of trial.

“for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” (10:20)

The Spirit who came at Pentecost speaks in witnesses. Stephen, “full of the Holy Spirit”, is the first example (Acts 7:55).

“But the one who endures to the end will be saved.” (10:22)

Perseverance is a grace to be asked for daily. The end is not defeat but salvation.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that martyrdom is the supreme witness to the truth of the faith, bearing witness to Christ who died and rose, and that the Church has carefully kept the records of those who went to the end to witness to their faith (CCC 2473–2474). It teaches that all Christians are called to bear witness, and that the Holy Spirit is the principal agent of the Church’s mission (CCC 852). St John Chrysostom, preaching on these verses, notes the wonder that Christ sends sheep among wolves and yet the sheep overcome the wolves, so long as they remain sheep (in substance, Homilies on Matthew 33).

Questions people ask

Barnabas was not one of the Twelve. Why is he called an apostle?

“Apostle” means one who is sent. Luke calls Paul and Barnabas apostles (Acts 14:14) because they were sent by the Holy Spirit and the church of Antioch on mission (Acts 13:2–3). The Twelve have a unique place as the foundation of the Church, with Peter at their head; Barnabas and Paul are apostles in the wider sense, honoured by the Church with the same title.

Who first called the disciples “Christians”?

Acts says only that the disciples were first called Christians at Antioch (11:26). The form of the word suggests it was coined by outsiders, perhaps as a nickname. Believers came to wear it with honour: “if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace” (1 Peter 4:16).

Did Paul refuse the authority of Peter and the other apostles?

No. Paul insists that his Gospel came from Christ, but he goes up to Jerusalem to lay it before Peter, James and John, and he values their recognition. The Church teaches that Peter’s authority comes from Christ (Matthew 16:18; CCC 881), and Paul’s visit shows that the Gospel is lived in communion with Peter and the apostles.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar calendar keeps the feast of Mar Barnabas Shleeha in the Season of the Apostles, the season that remembers the Church’s first mission. Antioch, where Barnabas taught and where the disciples were first called Christians, became one of the great centres of early Christianity in Syria, and the starting point of the mission to the nations (Acts 13:1–3). Our Church, the Church of the Mar Thoma Nasranis, belongs to that wider Syriac family of Churches.

Barnabas teaches every parish the ministry of encouragement. He saw grace in a new community, believed in Saul when others feared him, and gave John Mark a second chance. On his feast, a family or parish group might ask: whom can we encourage this week, and whose gifts have we overlooked?

For study and teaching

  • The psalm says, “make known his deeds among the peoples.” What has God done in my life that I could tell someone this week?
  • Barnabas “saw the grace of God” and rejoiced. Whose goodness have I been slow to notice or to praise?
  • The pillars asked Paul and Barnabas only to remember the poor. How does my parish or family remember the poor in a regular way?
  • Jesus promises that the Spirit of the Father will speak in his witnesses. When have I needed courage to speak of Christ, and how can I prepare by prayer?
