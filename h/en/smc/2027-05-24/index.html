Readings

  • Gospel John 11:17–27 · Jesus, the Resurrection and the Life

Word for the day

“Jesus said to her, “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live,”” John 11:25

Reflection

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison, and he writes as a father: “Only let us hold fast to what we have attained. Brothers and sisters, join in imitating me, and observe those who live according to the example you have in us.” It may sound bold to say ‘imitate me’, but Paul has just told them that he himself is still running, pressing on towards the goal (Philippians 3:12–14). He does not offer himself as a finished saint, but as a runner who has not stopped. Faith is passed on this way, from life to life. Every one of us learned to pray from someone: a mother at the evening prayer, a grandfather with his rosary, a catechism teacher, a priest. The Season of the Apostles reminds us that the Church was built on witnesses whose lives could be copied. The question for us is simple: if someone younger imitated my faith, would they come closer to Christ?

Then Paul's voice breaks: “For many live as enemies of the cross of Christ; I have often told you of them, and now I tell you even with tears.” He describes them without hatred, with grief: “Their end is destruction; their god is the belly; and their glory is in their shame; their minds are set on earthly things.” These seem to be not pagans far away but baptized people who have made comfort and appetite their god. The danger is close to every one of us. Against it Paul sets one of the most beautiful lines of the New Testament: “But our citizenship is in heaven, and it is from there that we are expecting a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ.” Philippi was a Roman colony, proud of its citizenship; Paul tells them they belong to a greater city. And the Saviour we await “will transform the body of our humiliation that it may be conformed to the body of his glory.” Our bodies, weak and ageing, are not prisons to escape but seeds of the resurrection. The Catechism quotes these words when it teaches how we will rise: Christ will change our lowly body to be like his glorious body (CCC 999).

The Gospel shows that hope at the graveside. Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. Martha goes out to meet Jesus and speaks with the honesty of grief: “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him.” Jesus says, “Your brother will rise again.” Martha answers with the faith of a devout Jewish woman of her time: “I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.” And Jesus brings the last day into the present: “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?” Resurrection is not only an event at the end; it is a Person standing before her. Martha's answer is one of the great confessions of the Gospel: “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” In the Season of the Apostles, when the Church goes out to proclaim the Risen Lord, Martha teaches us the heart of that proclamation. Every Christian funeral in our parishes, with its prayers and its incense and its candles, repeats her words. And every time we visit a grieving family, we bring the same Lord to the door, asking quietly: “Do you believe this?”

Full homily

Dear friends, today Paul writes from prison to the Christians of Philippi: “join in imitating me.” He is not boasting. He is a runner still pressing on, and he asks them to run with him.

Then he weeps for those who live as enemies of the Cross, whose god is the belly and whose minds are set on earthly things. And he reminds us: “our citizenship is in heaven.” We belong to God's kingdom, and we wait for the Saviour who will transform our weak bodies to be like his glorious body.

In the Gospel Lazarus has died. Martha runs to meet Jesus and says honestly, “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” Jesus does not scold her. He says, “Your brother will rise again.”

Martha thinks of the last day, but Jesus says, “I am the resurrection and the life.” The resurrection is not only a future day; it is a Person, and he is here.

Jesus asks her, “Do you believe this?” Martha answers, “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God.”

Today he asks the same question of each of us, especially those who are grieving. Let us answer with Martha, and live as citizens of heaven. Amen.

For children

Children, Martha was sad because her brother Lazarus had died. But Jesus told her, ‘I am the resurrection and the life.’ That means Jesus is stronger than death!

When someone we love goes to heaven, we can be sad, but we can also say like Martha: ‘Yes, Lord, I believe!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are the resurrection and the life. At Bethany you met Martha in her grief and drew from her the confession of faith; meet us also in our sorrows, and make us say with her, Yes, Lord, I believe. Keep us from making our belly our god and setting our minds on earthly things. Remind us that our citizenship is in heaven, and teach us to live so that others may imitate us and come to you. When our bodies grow weak and our days end, transform the body of our humiliation into the likeness of your glorious body, and raise us up to live with the Father and the Holy Spirit for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The second week of the Season of the Apostles opens with two readings about the future God has prepared for our bodies. Paul tells the Philippians that their citizenship is in heaven and that Christ will transform the body of our humiliation to be like his glorious body. At Bethany, Jesus tells a grieving Martha, “I am the resurrection and the life,” and she answers with a full confession of faith.

Philippians 3:16–21 · Our citizenship is in heaven

Setting.

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison; where he was held, whether in Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea, is still debated. Philippi was a Roman colony (Acts 16:12), and its people were proud to be citizens of Rome, far away. In chapter 3 Paul has warned against those who insisted on circumcision for Gentile believers, and he has told of counting all things loss for Christ (3:7–8). Now he warns against another danger: a life governed by appetite.

Structure.

  • v. 16: hold fast to what you have attained.
  • v. 17: imitate me, and watch those who live by our example.
  • vv. 18–19: with tears: enemies of the cross, whose god is the belly.
  • vv. 20–21: our citizenship is in heaven; the Saviour will transform our bodies.

Key words.

  • politeuma, citizenship, commonwealth: The word the people of a Roman colony would have used of their belonging to Rome. Christians belong to a homeland in heaven, and they live in this world as its citizens.
  • koilia, belly: “Their god is the belly” describes people ruled by their appetites. Scholars debate whom Paul has in mind; what is clear is that he warns of a life without the cross.
  • symmorphon, conformed: The same root as in 3:10, “becoming like him in his death”. Those who share the form of Christ’s cross will share the form of his glory.

Verse by verse.

“Brothers and sisters, join in imitating me, and observe those who live according to the example you have in us.” (3:17)

Paul is not proud; he has just said he has not yet reached the goal (3:12). He offers himself as a model of one who runs toward Christ. Faith is learned by watching faithful people.

“For many live as enemies of the cross of Christ; I have often told you of them, and now I tell you even with tears.” (3:18)

Paul’s warning comes with tears, not anger. He weeps for those who reject the cross.

“But our citizenship is in heaven, and it is from there that we are expecting a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ.” (3:20)

In the Roman world “saviour” and “lord” were titles given to the emperor. Paul gives them to Jesus, who will come not from Rome but from heaven.

“He will transform the body of our humiliation that it may be conformed to the body of his glory,” (3:21)

Not a “vile body”, as older translations put it, but a lowly body, weak and mortal. God does not throw away our bodies; he will glorify them.

How the Church reads it.

The early Letter to Diognetus describes Christians in Paul’s spirit: they live on earth, but they are citizens of heaven, and they obey the laws while surpassing them by their lives. The Catechism teaches that in the resurrection Christ will raise our bodies, transformed into the likeness of his glorious body (CCC 999–1000). The hope of heaven does not take us out of the world; the Second Vatican Council teaches that it should rather urge us to work for a better earth (Gaudium et Spes 39).

John 11:17–27 · I am the resurrection and the life

Setting.

Lazarus of Bethany, the brother of Martha and Mary, was a friend Jesus loved (11:3, 5). Jesus delayed two days before going to Judea, where his life was in danger (11:6–8). Bethany was a village on the Mount of Olives, about three kilometres from Jerusalem; the Greek says fifteen stadia. The raising of Lazarus is the seventh and greatest of the signs in John’s Gospel, and it leads the authorities to resolve to put Jesus to death (11:53).

The “Jews” who had come to console the sisters are their neighbours and friends from Jerusalem, carrying out the work of mercy of comforting mourners. In this chapter many of them come to believe (11:45).

Structure.

  • vv. 17–19: four days in the tomb; many come to console.
  • vv. 20–22: Martha goes to meet Jesus: “Lord, if you had been here.”
  • vv. 23–24: “Your brother will rise again”; “on the last day”.
  • vv. 25–26: “I am the resurrection and the life… Do you believe this?”
  • v. 27: Martha’s confession: the Messiah, the Son of God.

Key words.

  • tetartaios, four days: Lazarus has been dead four days. A later Jewish tradition held that the soul stayed near the body for three days; after four days death was beyond doubt. The sign will be undeniable.
  • anastasis, resurrection: Martha believes in the resurrection of the dead at the last day, as many Jews of her time did, especially the Pharisees (Acts 23:8). Jesus brings that hope into the present: the resurrection is a person, and he is here.
  • egō eimi, I am: The fifth of the seven “I am” sayings with an image in John (bread, light, gate, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine). It echoes the name of God revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14).

Verse by verse.

“When Jesus arrived, he found that Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days.” (11:17)

Jesus seems to arrive too late. In John, God’s timing is never late; it serves a greater sign (11:4).

“Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” (11:21)

Martha speaks with the honesty of a friend. Her grief and her faith stand side by side, and Jesus does not rebuke her.

“Jesus said to her, “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live,” (11:25)

Textual note: a few early witnesses, among them a papyrus of the third century, lack the words “and the life”. Nearly all manuscripts have them, and the Church reads them. Those who believe will die in body, yet live; and those who live in him will never die the death that separates from God.

“Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” (11:27)

Martha’s confession is as full as Peter’s at Caesarea Philippi (Matthew 16:16). It is almost the very confession for which John wrote his Gospel (20:31).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that Jesus links faith in the resurrection to his own person: “I am the resurrection and the life” (CCC 994). The Church reads this Gospel at funerals and prays in her liturgy that for the faithful life is changed, not ended. Martha, often remembered for being busy (Luke 10:40), is here the one who makes the great confession; the Church honours Martha, Mary and Lazarus together as saints (29 July in the Roman calendar).

Questions people ask

If Jesus is the resurrection, why do believers still die?

Jesus does not promise that believers will escape bodily death; Lazarus himself would die again. He promises that death no longer has the last word: those who believe in him, “even though they die, will live”. The Catechism teaches that for those who die in Christ’s grace, death is a participation in his death, so that they may also share his Resurrection (CCC 1006, 1010).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “our citizenship is in heaven.” In what everyday choices does my true homeland show?
  • Martha brought both her grief and her faith to Jesus. What sorrow do I need to bring to him honestly today?
