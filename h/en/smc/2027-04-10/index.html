Readings

  • Epistle Colossians 3:14–17 · Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts
  • Gospel Luke 24:36–43 · Jesus appears to his disciples

Word for the day

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” Luke 24:39

Reflection

Paul's letter to the Colossians describes the new life of those who have been raised with Christ in Baptism. Just before today's passage he tells them to put on, like new clothes, “compassion, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience,” and to forgive one another as the Lord has forgiven them. Then comes the garment that holds all the others together: “Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony.” Love is the belt that keeps the other virtues from falling off. Then Paul gives three gifts for a Christian home and community. The first: “let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, to which indeed you were called in the one body. And be thankful.” The word ‘rule’ is the word for an umpire in the games: when we are pulled in different directions, the peace of Christ is to make the call. The second: “Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly.” Not visit occasionally, but dwell, live in the house, have its own room. The third: “with gratitude in your hearts sing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs to God.” A Christian family that prays together, reads the Gospel together and sings together is a small church. Kerala Christian homes have long kept this at dusk, when the lamp is lit and the family gathers for evening prayer: that moment is Colossians 3 lived out. Saturday is also the day the Church honours the Blessed Virgin Mary, who kept all the words of her Son and pondered them in her heart (Luke 2:19): she is the model of the word of Christ dwelling richly in a home.

Paul ends with a rule that covers all of life: “And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” There is nothing too ordinary to be done in the name of Jesus: cooking, studying, driving, nursing, ploughing. And everything is to be done with thanksgiving. The Greek word for giving thanks is the root of the word Eucharist; the Syriac word Qurbana means the offering. A Christian life is meant to become one long Qurbana: an offering of everything to the Father, through the Son, with thanks.

In the Gospel we are back on Easter evening in Jerusalem. The two disciples from Emmaus are telling their story when “Jesus himself stood among them and said to them, ‘Peace be with you.’” The disciples were terrified: “They were startled and terrified, and thought that they were seeing a ghost.” Jesus answers with a tenderness that is almost humorous: “Why are you frightened, and why do doubts arise in your hearts? Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” Then, because they are still disbelieving for joy, he asks, “Have you anything here to eat?” “They gave him a piece of broiled fish, and he took it and ate in their presence.” Luke insists on the body because the faith of the Church depends on it. The Resurrection is not a beautiful idea, a memory, or a vision; the One who was crucified is alive in his own body, glorified and no longer subject to death, yet the same body that bears the wounds. That is why Christians bury their dead with reverence and wait for the resurrection of the body. And the One who ate fish with his friends gives himself to us as food at every Holy Qurbana: his true Body and Blood, risen and living.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells the Colossians: Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony. Love is the belt that holds all the other virtues in place.

He gives three gifts for a Christian home: let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts; let the word of Christ dwell in you richly; and sing psalms, hymns and spiritual songs to God with gratitude.

When a Kerala family lights the lamp at dusk and gathers to pray, read the Gospel and sing, that is Colossians 3 lived out. And Paul adds: whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks. Our whole life can become a Qurbana, an offering with thanks.

In the Gospel the Risen Lord stands among his disciples and says, Peace be with you. They think he is a ghost. He says: Look at my hands and my feet; touch me and see; a ghost does not have flesh and bones. And he eats a piece of fish before them.

Our faith is not in a ghost or an idea. Jesus is risen in his own body, glorified, still bearing his wounds. And the same Lord gives us his true Body and Blood at the altar.

Tonight, let us light the lamp, pray and sing together at home, and let his peace rule in our hearts. Alleluia! Amen.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you stood among your frightened disciples and said, Peace be with you; you showed them your hands and your feet and ate before them. Strengthen our faith in your true Resurrection and in the resurrection of the body. Clothe us with love above all, let your peace rule in our hearts, and let your word dwell in our homes richly, so that with grateful hearts we may sing to the Father and do everything in your name. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The risen Lord stands among his frightened disciples and says, “Peace be with you.” Paul, writing to the Colossians, prays that this peace of Christ may rule in their hearts, and that the word of Christ may dwell in them richly as they sing and give thanks. The Gospel shows that the peace is not an idea but a person: Jesus with real hands and feet, who eats before them. The words to watch are peace, thankful and flesh and bones.

Colossians 3:14–17 · Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts

Setting.

Colossae was a small town in the Lycus valley in Asia Minor. The letter tells the believers that they have been raised with Christ and must now “seek the things that are above” (3:1). Paul then describes the new life as a change of clothes: take off the old self, put on the new (3:9–12). Today’s verses are the crowning garment and what follows from it.

Many scholars think that Colossians was written by a disciple of Paul in his name; others hold that Paul wrote it himself, perhaps with a co-worker. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

Structure.

  • v. 14: above all, love, which binds everything together.
  • v. 15: the peace of Christ as the umpire of the heart; be thankful.
  • v. 16: the word of Christ dwelling richly; teaching and singing.
  • v. 17: do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks.

Key words.

  • syndesmos tēs teleiotētos, bond of perfection: Love is the belt that holds all the other virtues in place, so that together they make one whole. Without love the other virtues fall apart.
  • brabeuetō, let it rule: A word from the games: the umpire who decides. When the heart is divided, the peace of Christ is to give the decision.
  • eucharistoi, thankful: Thanks is named three times in these four verses (vv. 15, 16, 17). The Greek word is the root of “Eucharist”, the great thanksgiving of the Church.

Verse by verse.

“Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony.” (3:14)

The Greek has no new verb here; “clothe yourselves” carries on from v. 12. Love is the last and outer garment that holds the rest together.

“And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, to which indeed you were called in the one body. And be thankful.” (3:15)

This peace is not only inner calm. We are called to it “in the one body”, the Church; it is peace among brothers and sisters.

“with gratitude in your hearts sing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs to God.” (3:16)

Singing is one of the ways the word of Christ lives in a community. The early Church prayed the psalms and also composed new hymns to Christ.

“And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” (3:17)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Eucharist is above all thanksgiving to the Father, a blessing by which the Church expresses her gratitude for all his benefits (CCC 1359–1360). Paul’s words also describe the Church’s prayer: the word, the psalms, the hymns and thanksgiving all meet in the Liturgy. In the Holy Qurbana the priest greets the people more than once with the risen Lord’s own words, “Peace be with you”, so that his peace may rule among us.

Luke 24:36–43 · See that it is I myself

Setting.

It is still Easter Sunday evening in Luke’s account. The two disciples have returned from Emmaus and are telling how they knew the Lord in the breaking of the bread (24:35). The eleven and those with them have just said, “The Lord has risen indeed, and he has appeared to Simon!” (24:34). And while they are talking, he is there.

Structure.

  • v. 36: Jesus stands among them: “Peace be with you.”
  • vv. 37–38: fear: they think they see a ghost.
  • vv. 39–40: hands and feet: “Touch me and see.”
  • vv. 41–43: joy mixed with disbelief; Jesus eats a piece of fish.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: The everyday greeting shalom, now spoken by one who has passed through death. In the risen Lord’s mouth it is the gift of reconciliation.
  • pneuma, ghost: The disciples think they are seeing a spirit without a body. Jesus corrects them: the Resurrection is not a vision or a ghost story.
  • sarka kai ostea, flesh and bones: Jesus insists on the reality of his body. It is the same body that was crucified, now glorified.

Verse by verse.

“While they were talking about this, Jesus himself stood among them and said to them, “Peace be with you.”” (24:36)

A few ancient manuscripts lack the words “and said to them, ‘Peace be with you’”, but almost all have them, and they match John 20:19.

“Look at my hands and my feet; see that it is I myself. Touch me and see; for a ghost does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.” (24:39)

The wounds show who he is. The risen Lord does not leave behind his Passion; he carries it into glory.

“While in their joy they were disbelieving and still wondering” (24:41)

An honest line. Joy can be so great that it is hard to believe. Faith grows slowly, and the Lord is patient with it.

“and he took it and ate in their presence.” (24:43)

He does not need food; he eats for them, to show that he is truly alive. Peter later preaches that the apostles “ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead” (Acts 10:41).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the risen Jesus showed the disciples that he was not a ghost, invited them to touch him, and ate with them, and that his body is the same body that suffered and died, now risen with new properties (CCC 645). Christ rose with his own body, and so shall we (CCC 999). St Ignatius of Antioch, writing on his way to martyrdom, defends this against those who said the Lord only seemed to have a body: he was in the flesh even after the Resurrection, and the apostles touched him and believed (Letter to the Smyrnaeans 3, in substance).

Questions people ask

If his body was real, how could he suddenly stand among them, or come in through locked doors (John 20:19)?

The risen body of Jesus is real, but it is no longer bound by space and time in the way our bodies are. It is a glorified body, sharing in the life of the Spirit. It is not a return to earthly life, as with Lazarus (CCC 645–646). This is why the disciples sometimes did not recognize him at first, and why he could come and go as he willed.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that the peace of Christ should be the umpire in my heart. Which decision before me now needs that umpire?
  • Jesus showed his wounds to frightened friends. How can my own healed wounds help someone else to believe?
