Word for the day

“And everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or children or fields, for my name's sake, will receive a hundredfold, and will inherit eternal life.” Matthew 19:29

Reflection

Today's readings speak of inheritance. In Egypt, crushed by slavery, Israel hears God's promise: “I will take you as my people, and I will be your God… I will bring you into the land… I will give it to you for a possession.” The promised land was never only a territory; it was life with God.

The Servant in Isaiah feels he has laboured in vain, “yet surely my cause is with the LORD, and my reward with my God.” Peter writes to scattered Christians that they have been born anew “into an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you.” The Church is the people already on the way to that inheritance.

In the Gospel the disciples are shocked: if the rich can hardly enter the Kingdom, who can be saved? Jesus answers, “For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.” Then Peter asks what they will receive for leaving everything. Jesus promises thrones, a hundredfold, and eternal life. The apostles, on whom the Church is founded, will share in Christ's judgement and glory.

For Kerala Christians, whose families have often been measured by land and property, this Gospel is searching. Our deepest inheritance is not the family property but the faith received from the apostle Thomas and handed down for centuries. What we leave for Christ is never lost; it returns a hundredfold.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, in Kerala we know a lot about inheritance: land, houses, gold, and sometimes long family disputes over them. Today God speaks to us about a different inheritance.

In Egypt the Israelites were slaves. God said to Moses: “I will take you as my people, and I will be your God. I will bring you into the land I promised.” The land was a gift, but the greater gift was God himself: “I will be your God.”

St Peter writes to Christians who had lost homes and property: God has given you new birth “into an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you.” No one can steal it, divide it, or take it to court.

In the Gospel Jesus says it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the Kingdom. The disciples are shocked. Jesus says, “For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.” And to those who leave everything for him he promises a hundredfold and eternal life.

So let us ask ourselves today: what am I leaving to my children? If we leave them land without faith, we leave them poor. If we leave them faith, we leave them an inheritance that will never fade. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what an inheritance is? It's something precious your parents and grandparents give you.

Jesus said the most precious gift is not money or land, but God himself and heaven, which no one can take away.

Let's thank God for our faith, the best gift from our families. Say together: “Jesus, you are my treasure!”

Prayer

Father of mercy, you have given us new birth into a living hope and an inheritance kept in heaven. Free us from all that holds us back from following your Son, and make us faithful to the faith we have received, for all things are possible with you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the second Sunday of Qudash Edtha the readings speak of an inheritance. In Exodus, God promises slaves in Egypt a land “for a possession”. In Isaiah, the Servant who seems to have labored in vain knows that his reward is with God. Peter tells scattered Christians that they have been born anew into an inheritance kept in heaven. In Matthew, Jesus promises those who leave everything for his name a hundredfold and eternal life. The season looks at the Church as God's house; today's readings show what that house is for: a people God has made his own, on the way to an inheritance that cannot fade.

The key words are promise, possession and reward. Each reading also has a time of testing before the gift: brick-making in Egypt, hidden years in a quiver, “various trials”, and the hard saying about riches.

Exodus 6:1–8 · “I am the Lord”: the promises of deliverance

Setting.

Moses has gone to Pharaoh for the first time, and things have got worse. Pharaoh orders the Israelites to make bricks without being given straw, and the Israelite foremen blame Moses (Exodus 5). Moses turns to God with a bitter question: “Why did you ever send me?” (5:22). Chapter 6 is God's answer. It does not begin with an explanation but with God's name and a chain of promises.

Many scholars see 6:2–8 as a second account of Moses' call, parallel to the burning bush in chapter 3, from another strand of Israel's tradition. Placed here, after failure, it carries a message: God's plan is not defeated by the first setback.

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Now you shall see”: God's answer to Moses' complaint.
  • vv. 2–3: “I am the Lord”: the name, and the God of the patriarchs.
  • vv. 4–5: the covenant with the fathers; God hears the groaning of the slaves.
  • vv. 6–8: the message for Israel: a chain of “I will” promises, closed again by “I am the Lord”.
  • ¶ The passage is framed by the same words, “I am the Lord” (vv. 2 and 8, and again in v. 6). Everything promised in between rests on who God is.

Key words.

  • ’ănî YHWH, I am the Lord: The sacred name revealed to Moses at the bush (3:14–15). Out of reverence Jews do not pronounce it; they say Adonai, “the Lord”, and the Greek and Latin Bibles and our own translations do the same. It is the name of a God who is present and faithful.
  • ’ēl šadday, God Almighty: The name by which God was known to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Genesis 17:1; 35:11). Its original meaning is uncertain; the ancient translations rendered it “Almighty”.
  • gā’al, to redeem: The verb behind the gō’ēl, the next of kin who buys back a relative sold into slavery or recovers his land (Leviticus 25:25, 47–49). God acts as Israel's next of kin: these slaves are his family.
  • môrāšāh, possession: Something received to hold as a heritage. The land is a gift, not a prize won by Israel's own strength.

Verse by verse.

“I have also heard the groaning of the Israelites whom the Egyptians are holding as slaves, and I have remembered my covenant.” (6:5)

In the Bible, for God to “remember” is not to recall something forgotten; it is to act on a promise. The groaning of the slaves and the promise to the fathers meet in God's memory.

“I will free you from the burdens of the Egyptians and deliver you from slavery to them. I will redeem you with an outstretched arm” (6:6)

The “outstretched arm” is the image of God's power at work in the Exodus (Deuteronomy 4:34; 26:8).

“I will take you as my people, and I will be your God.” (6:7)

This is the heart of the covenant, a formula repeated through the prophets (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:28) and at the very end of the Bible (Revelation 21:3). The land is given so that a people may belong to God. Jewish tradition links the four verbs of vv. 6–7 (bring out, deliver, redeem, take) with the four cups of wine at the Passover meal.

“I will bring you into the land that I swore to give to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; I will give it to you for a possession.” (6:8)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the revelation of the divine name to Moses proved to be the fundamental one for both the Old and the New Covenants, and that the name expresses God's faithfulness in spite of human sin (CCC 204, 211). The memory of the Exodus is the pattern of the Christian memorial: when the Church remembers God's saving deeds in the liturgy, they become present (CCC 1363). The New Testament says that Christ “redeemed” us (Galatians 3:13; 1 Peter 1:18–19): in the Incarnation God became our next of kin in the flesh.

Isaiah 49:1–7 · A light to the nations

Setting.

This is the second of the four Servant Songs in the Book of Consolation (Isaiah 42:1–9; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12). The setting is the end of the Babylonian exile. Here the Servant himself speaks, to the coastlands and the peoples far away.

Who is the Servant? In v. 3 he is called “Israel”, yet in v. 5 his mission is to bring Israel back to God. He is Israel as God meant it to be: one who carries the people's vocation and serves the people. The New Testament sees this figure fulfilled in Jesus.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the call from the womb; the hidden weapon; “You are my servant, Israel”.
  • v. 4: the Servant's discouragement, and his trust.
  • vv. 5–6: the mission widened: not only the tribes of Jacob but the end of the earth.
  • v. 7: the despised one before whom kings will stand up.

Key words.

  • ‘ebed, servant: A word of honour as well as of service: Moses and David are each called the Lord's servant. The Servant belongs wholly to God.
  • pe‘ullāh, reward, wages: The pay a worker receives at the end of the day (v. 4). The Servant leaves the account of his labour with God.
  • ’ôr gôyim, light to the nations: God's salvation is not for Israel alone. Simeon sings these words over the child Jesus (Luke 2:32), and Paul and Barnabas take them as their own command (Acts 13:47).

Verse by verse.

“The Lord called me before I was born, while I was in my mother’s womb he named me.” (49:1)

Jeremiah heard the same call (Jeremiah 1:5), and Paul uses this language of his own vocation (Galatians 1:15). A vocation begins in God's choice, before any merit.

“he made me a polished arrow, in his quiver he hid me away.” (49:2)

A sharpened arrow is kept in the quiver until the moment it is needed. Hidden years are not wasted years.

“I have labored in vain, I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity; yet surely my cause is with the Lord, and my reward with my God.” (49:4)

The honest cry of every apostle who sees little fruit. The answer is not success but trust: the outcome belongs to God.

“I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth.” (49:6)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the characteristics of the Messiah are revealed above all in the Servant Songs, which proclaim the meaning of Jesus' Passion (CCC 713). The Second Vatican Council opened its constitution on the Church with the words “Christ is the light of nations”, a light that shines on the face of the Church (Lumen Gentium 1). On a Sunday of Qudash Edtha this is the Church's mission: to carry that light to the end of the earth.

1 Peter 1:3–7 · An inheritance kept in heaven

Setting.

1 Peter is addressed to “the exiles of the Dispersion” in five provinces of Asia Minor (1:1). Most of them were converts from paganism, living as a small minority and suffering suspicion and slander for their faith. The letter is sent from “Babylon” (5:13), a code name for Rome.

Today's passage is the opening blessing. It follows the Jewish form of prayer that begins “Blessed be God” (the berakah). Many scholars hear in the letter the echo of teaching given at baptism: new birth, hope, testing and joy.

Structure.

  • v. 3: blessing of the Father, who has given us new birth into a living hope.
  • v. 4: the inheritance and its three qualities.
  • v. 5: God's power guards us until the salvation to be revealed.
  • vv. 6–7: joy in trials; faith tested like gold.

Key words.

  • anagennaō, to give new birth: To beget again. The same idea as being born “from above” (John 3:3–5) and the “water of rebirth” (Titus 3:5). The Christian life begins with a birth that God gives.
  • klēronomia, inheritance: The Greek Old Testament uses this word for the land promised to Israel. Peter now uses it for the gift kept in heaven.
  • aphthartos, amiantos, amarantos, imperishable, undefiled, unfading: Three words that each begin with the Greek “not”. The last gave its name to the amaranth, the flower thought never to fade. The promised land could be ravaged, defiled or lost; this inheritance cannot.
  • phroureō, to protect, to guard: A military word: a garrison guarding a town. While the inheritance is kept in heaven, the heirs are guarded on earth.

Verse by verse.

“By his great mercy he has given us a new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,” (1:3)

Hope is “living” because its foundation is a living person, the risen Christ.

“and into an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you,” (1:4)
“more precious than gold that, though perishable, is tested by fire” (1:7)

Gold was refined in the furnace to remove its impurities. Trials do not destroy faith; they show that it is real. Notice the link with today's Gospel: gold perishes, faith remains.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that through baptism we are delivered from sin and reborn as children of God (CCC 1213), and that hope responds to the desire for happiness that God has placed in every heart (CCC 1818). Because of its many baptismal themes, 1 Peter is read on the Sundays of Easter in one year of the Roman lectionary, the season when the Church welcomes the newly baptized. What we receive at the font is not a reward for this life but the pledge of the life to come.

Matthew 19:23–30 · Who then can be saved?

Setting.

The passage follows directly on the rich young man (19:16–22), who asked what good deed would bring eternal life and went away grieving, for he had many possessions. Jesus now speaks to his disciples about what they have just seen.

Many people of the time took wealth as a sign of God's blessing (compare Job 42:10–12; Deuteronomy 28:1–14). That is why the disciples are astonished: if the blessed rich cannot easily enter, who can?

Structure.

  • vv. 23–24: the hard saying: the camel and the needle.
  • vv. 25–26: “Who can be saved?” “For God all things are possible.”
  • v. 27: Peter's question: “What then will we have?”
  • vv. 28–29: a double promise: thrones for the Twelve; a hundredfold and eternal life for all who leave things for his name.
  • v. 30: the great reversal.

Key words.

  • kamēlos, camel: The largest animal in Palestine passing through the smallest opening: a deliberate picture of the impossible, in Jesus' typical style of exaggeration (compare the log in the eye, 7:3–5).
  • palingenesia, renewal of all things: Literally “new birth” or “regeneration”. In the New Testament the word appears only here and in Titus 3:5. It speaks of the world made new at the end, the same hope as Peter's “new birth”.
  • hekatontaplasiona, a hundredfold: An overflowing return. Mark's version adds “with persecutions” (Mark 10:30): the reward includes the cross.

Verse by verse.

“it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God.” (19:24)

Matthew, who usually says “kingdom of heaven”, here writes “kingdom of God”. The two mean the same: “heaven” is a reverent way of saying “God”.

“For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.” (19:26)

An echo of the angel's word to Mary (Luke 1:37) and of God's word to Abraham (Genesis 18:14). Salvation is always God's gift; a rich man too can be saved by grace, as Zacchaeus was (Luke 19:1–10).

“you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.” (19:28)

The Twelve are the foundation of the renewed people of God. “Judging” here also carries the Old Testament sense of ruling and leading, as the judges of Israel did.

“And everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or children or fields, for my name’s sake, will receive a hundredfold, and will inherit eternal life.” (19:29)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone, and that detachment from riches is necessary for entering the Kingdom (CCC 2544, 2556). It sees in the Twelve, with Peter at their head, the structure the Lord gave to his community: representing the twelve tribes of Israel, they are the foundation stones of the new Jerusalem (CCC 765). Clement of Alexandria, in his homily “Who is the rich man that shall be saved?”, teaches that the Lord does not condemn possessions themselves but the soul's attachment to them; riches are to be used for others (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was there a gate in Jerusalem called “the Eye of the Needle”?

This explanation is popular, but there is no ancient evidence for such a gate; it appears only in much later writers. A few late manuscripts read kamilos, “rope”, instead of kamēlos, “camel”, probably to soften the saying. Jesus means the impossible, and then says that God does the impossible (19:26).

If God is called “the Lord” in Genesis, why does Exodus 6:3 say the patriarchs did not know this name?

Genesis does use the name, even in God's own words to the patriarchs (Genesis 15:7; 28:13). Exodus 6:3 means that they had not yet known the full meaning of the name. That meaning is shown in the Exodus: the Lord is the God who delivers and redeems. Scholars also note that these books join different strands of tradition.

Must every Christian leave home and family?

Some are called to leave everything literally, as priests and religious do. All are called to put Christ first and to hold everything for his name's sake (CCC 2544–2545). The Gospel does not abandon families; in the family of the Church they are received back “a hundredfold”.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Qudash Edtha is the last season of the East Syriac liturgical year; its Sundays look at the Church as God's dwelling and as the bride on her way to the Kingdom. Today's Gospel promises thrones to the Twelve. For the St Thomas Christians this has a family meaning: our Church traces its faith to one of those Twelve, the apostle Thomas, who left everything and, as our tradition holds, came to India. In the Holy Qurbana we already receive the pledge of the inheritance Peter speaks of, kept in heaven and given to us at the altar.

For study and teaching

  • Exodus 6 begins after failure and complaint. When my efforts for God seem to make things worse, which of the “I will” promises do I most need to hear?
  • Isaiah's Servant says, “I have labored in vain”, yet leaves his reward with God. Where do I need to leave the results with God?
  • Peter calls our inheritance “imperishable, undefiled, and unfading”. What perishable things compete with it in my heart?
  • What has my family received “a hundredfold” through faith, and what might Jesus be asking me to leave for his name?
