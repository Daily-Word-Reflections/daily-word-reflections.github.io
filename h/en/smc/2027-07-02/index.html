Word for the day

“Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” Matthew 10:40

Reflection

Today the Church remembers a large and nameless band: the Seventy Disciples of Jesus. “After this the Lord appointed seventy others and sent them on ahead of him in pairs to every town and place where he himself intended to go. He said to them, ‘The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.’” Notice the word “others”. Jesus had already sent the Twelve; now he sends seventy more, ordinary disciples whose names the Gospel does not give. The mission of the Church does not belong only to bishops and priests; it belongs to every baptised person (CCC 863). The number echoes the seventy elders on whom God put the spirit of Moses, when Moses said, “Would that all the Lord’s people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his spirit on them!” Our East Syriac tradition counts Mar Addai among the Seventy and tells that he was sent to Edessa, and Mar Mari after him; their names are on the Anaphora of Addai and Mari with which we celebrate the Holy Qurbana. So the Seventy are not far away from us: every Sunday we pray with words that come from their mission.

The first reading is for the thirsty. The exiles of Israel were far from home, poor and parched, and God speaks to them: “When the poor and needy seek water, and there is none, and their tongue is parched with thirst, I the Lord will answer them, I the God of Israel will not forsake them. I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys; I will make the wilderness a pool of water, and the dry land springs of water.” Before this, God had said, “do not fear, for I am with you.” The Seventy go out into a dry world with only that promise. In Kerala this week the rain is falling on dry wells and paddy fields, and the image is easy to see: where the Gospel arrives, springs open. Jesus himself stood on the last day of the festival and cried, “Let anyone who is thirsty come to me, and let the one who believes in me drink.” The Seventy carry that cry from house to house.

In the second reading Paul stands up in the synagogue at Antioch in Pisidia, having been sent off by the Church with fasting and prayer and the laying on of hands, and he begins not with himself but with the history of God's faithfulness: “The God of this people Israel chose our ancestors and made the people great during their stay in the land of Egypt, and with uplifted arm he led them out of it.” Forty years in the wilderness, the judges, Samuel, Saul, and then David, “a man after my heart, who will carry out all my wishes.” And at the end: “Of this man’s posterity God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised.” Paul speaks as a son of Israel to his own people, and the Church remembers that she draws her life from that holy root. A good missionary tells first what God has done, and only then what we must do.

The Epistle is for those who are afraid, and every missionary is afraid at times. “We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.” Then Paul asks the questions that make a coward brave: “If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us, will he not with him also give us everything else?” And the great question: “Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?” Jesus had said that he was sending the Seventy “like lambs into the midst of wolves”; Paul's answer is the answer of those who have come back with joy: “No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” Today is also the First Friday, when the Church thinks of the open heart of the Lord, from which “at once blood and water came out”. That heart is the love from which, as Paul says, “neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation” can separate us (CCC 478).

The Gospel tells the price and the pay of discipleship. The price: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” Jesus does not ask us to love our families less; the Catechism reminds us that he asks us to put him first, and then we love them rightly (CCC 2232). “Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” The pay: not great wages but small welcomes. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” The Seventy needed hosts, people who would say “Peace to this house!” and open a door, and the Lord counts the host as a partner in the mission. “And whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple, truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward.” In Kerala the first thing a guest receives is a glass of water; Jesus says that this ordinary kindness is seen in heaven. The whole world is evangelised, in the end, by a thousand glasses of water and a few brave people who walk to the next village.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers the Seventy Disciples of Jesus. We do not know their names, and that is part of the message. Jesus did not send only the Twelve; he sent seventy others. The mission is for all who are baptised, and that means you and me.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few” (Luke 10:2). Jesus sends them two by two, with peace on their lips: “Peace to this house!” They did not have much to take with them. They had a message, a companion and a promise.

The promise is in the other readings. God says through Isaiah, “I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys” (Isaiah 41:18). Wherever the Gospel arrives, dry hearts open like wells in the rains. And Paul says, “If God is for us, who is against us?” and “nothing in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:31, 39). Today is the First Friday: let us bring our fears to the open heart of Jesus.

Then Jesus tells us the price and the pay. The price: love him above all, and take up the cross. The pay: not gold, but welcome. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me,” and a cup of cold water given in his name will not lose its reward (Matthew 10:40, 42).

Our own prayer at the Qurbana comes from Mar Addai and Mar Mari, disciples whom our tradition counts among the Seventy. Today let us be like them: let us pray for labourers, let us welcome the stranger, and let us carry the peace of Christ to one house. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus sent seventy friends to tell people about God, two by two. They said, ‘Peace to this house!’ You can do that too: be a friend who brings peace.

Jesus also said that even a glass of cold water given to someone in his name is a great gift. This week give a glass of water, a smile and a kind word to someone who is tired or sad!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sent seventy disciples ahead of you, two by two, to every place where you meant to go. Send labourers into your harvest, and give us hearts open to be sent and doors open to welcome. Open rivers in the dry places of our time; keep us in your love, from which nothing can separate us; and teach us to love you above all and to give a cup of cold water in your name. By the open heart that you showed us on the cross, make us missionaries of your mercy, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The four readings of this day speak of God who provides, calls and holds fast. In Isaiah the Lord turns the desert into a garden for the poor who have nothing but their thirst. In Acts Paul, preaching at Antioch of Pisidia, tells Israel’s story from the exodus to David as the road that leads to the Saviour, Jesus. In Romans, Paul sings of a love from which nothing can separate us, and in Matthew Jesus sends out his messengers and tells them what following him will cost and what it will gain. On the day when the Church remembers the seventy disciples whom Jesus sent before him (Luke 10:1), these readings ask what it means to be sent, and what is promised to those who receive the sent. The notes below take the Isaiah, Romans and Matthew readings.

Isaiah 41:17–20 · I will open rivers on the bare heights

Setting.

These verses belong to the part of Isaiah (chapters 40 to 55) that many scholars date to the time of the Babylonian exile, and that Catholic tradition has always read as part of the one book of Isaiah. The exiles feel forgotten; the Lord answers through the prophet that he is about to act, as at the Exodus, leading his people home. In 41:8–16 God has just told Israel, “You are my servant, I have chosen you and not cast you off”. Our passage turns that comfort into a picture: water in the desert and trees in the wasteland.

Structure.

  • v. 17: the poor and needy seek water, find none; the Lord will answer and will not forsake them.
  • vv. 18–19: rivers, fountains and pools in the wilderness; trees planted together in the desert.
  • v. 20: the purpose: that all may see and know that the Lord’s hand has done this.

Key words.

  • ‘ānî we’ebyôn, poor and needy: A pair of words used in the Psalms for those who have no resources but God. In Isaiah they stand for the whole exiled people, but the phrase has always been read more widely, of every person who knows his need.
  • ‘āzab, to forsake, to leave: The same verb is used in Isaiah 49:14 (“the Lord has forsaken me”), where the answer is again that God does not forget. Here the promise is stated as a promise from the Lord’s own mouth.
  • bārā’, to create: A verb used in the Old Testament only with God as the subject. The new rivers in the desert are a work of creation, not a lucky change in the weather.

Verse by verse.

“When the poor and needy seek water, and there is none, and their tongue is parched with thirst, I the Lord will answer them, I the God of Israel will not forsake them.” (41:17)

The thirst is real, a picture drawn from the desert road between Babylon and Judah, but it also stands for a deeper need. The answer is a person: “I the Lord”. The promise is not first of water but of presence.

“I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys” (41:18)

Rivers on bare heights is a startling picture, for water runs down to the valleys and not along the hilltops. God does not only supply what is lacking; he makes the dry places themselves a source of life.

“so that all may see and know, all may consider and understand, that the hand of the Lord has done this” (41:20)

Four verbs for knowing pile up. The purpose of the miracle is that people come to know the Lord who has acted. The same thought runs through the Gospel of John, where the signs are given so that people may believe.

How the Church reads it.

From the Church’s earliest days, the water that flows in the wilderness has been read as a sign of the Spirit and of baptism. Jesus speaks of “rivers of living water” in John 7:37–39, and the Catechism teaches that the prophets’ promises of water find their fulfilment in the Spirit poured out in Christ (CCC 694, in substance). Many Christian readers have seen in the trees of many kinds planted together (verse 19) a picture of the Church, gathering people from every nation under the one Lord.

Romans 8:28–39 · Who will separate us from the love of Christ?

Setting.

This is the climax of the first great section of Romans (chapters 1 to 8). Paul has shown that all, Jew and Gentile, need the grace of God, that justification comes through faith in Christ, and that the Holy Spirit gives the baptised the life of sons and daughters of God (8:14–17). He has also said that creation itself groans in hope (8:19–23). Now he gathers it all into a statement of confidence: God works through everything, even through suffering, for the good of those who love him.

Verses 29–30 are sometimes read as a chain, as if God sorted people ahead of time. Paul is writing a song of assurance to Christians who are being pressed, not a lecture on who is saved and who is lost. The Church reads them in that spirit.

Structure.

  • v. 28: all things work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.
  • vv. 29–30: God’s plan in five steps: foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified.
  • vv. 31–34: four questions (who is against us, who will bring a charge, who is to condemn), and one answer: God, who gave his Son, and Christ, who died, was raised and intercedes.
  • vv. 35–37: tribulations cannot separate us from Christ’s love; we are more than conquerors.
  • vv. 38–39: a list of powers that cannot separate us from the love of God in Christ.

Key words.

  • synergei, works together: The verb has the sense of working with others. Some ancient manuscripts add “God” as the subject (“God works all things together for good”), and the Malayalam Catholic Bible follows that reading. Either way the thought is of an active providence, not a blind fate.
  • proōrisen, predestined: Literally “marked out beforehand”. Paul’s goal is stated at once: to be conformed to the image of the Son. Predestination in the Church’s teaching is a plan for glory, not a doom for anyone.
  • hypernikōmen, we are more than conquerors: One word in Greek: “we over-conquer”. Paul does not say that suffering is absent or pleasant, only that the victory is already won through the one who loved us.

Verse by verse.

“We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.” (8:28)

The verse is not a claim that everything that happens is good. It says that God can bring good out of everything, even evil, for those who love him. The “good” is explained in the next verse: being made like the Son.

“If God is for us, who is against us?” (8:31)

Paul does not deny that there are enemies. He says that no opponent counts when God has taken our side. The proof follows at once: God gave up his own Son for us all (verse 32).

“No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” (8:37)

The victory is “through him who loved us”, so it belongs to Christ and is shared with us. The martyrs have been the most faithful readers of this verse.

“nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (8:39)

The list reaches everything in the universe. “Love” here is God’s own love, which is the beginning of the whole chapter, as of the whole creation. Only we ourselves can turn away from it, which is why Paul’s letters also contain warnings.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in this chapter the whole Gospel of hope. The Catechism quotes Romans 8:28–30 in its teaching on how God’s grace calls us to a share in the divine life (CCC 2012), and teaches that God predestines no one to hell; that comes only from a person’s own turning away from God (CCC 1037, in substance). The Roman liturgy uses verses 31–39 for the Mass of martyrs, and many Christians in every age have taken them as their strength in trial.

Matthew 10:37–42 · The cost and the reward of following Jesus

Setting.

These verses end the Mission Discourse (Matthew 10), in which Jesus sends the twelve out to the lost sheep of the house of Israel (10:5–6) and tells them what to expect: hostility, trials, and the care of the Father who counts every sparrow (10:16–31). In 10:32–36 Jesus has said that following him may divide a family. Today’s verses finish the discourse with three steps: what Jesus asks (loving him above all and carrying the cross), what he promises (the one who loses life for his sake finds it), and what he gives to those who welcome his messengers.

Structure.

  • vv. 37–38: love for Jesus above family; the cross.
  • v. 39: to find one’s life is to lose it, and to lose it for his sake is to find it.
  • vv. 40–42: welcome of the messengers is welcome of Jesus and of the Father; the reward of the prophet, the righteous, and the one who gives a cup of water.

Key words.

  • axios, worthy: Literally “weighing as much as”. A person is worthy of Jesus when Jesus is given the weight that belongs to him. It is not a matter of merit earned but of the order of love.
  • stauros, cross: Matthew uses this word of the disciple’s way before the cross of Jesus has been lifted. The hearers would have known it as the Roman means of execution. Jesus speaks plainly of the cost.
  • misthos, reward: A word that means pay or wage, but used here for a gift from God that exceeds any deserving. Even a cup of water, given in the name of a disciple, has its reward.

Verse by verse.

“whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me” (10:38)

Jesus first mentions the cross here, long before the Passion. The Gospel was written after the Passion, and its readers understood: the disciple takes up what Jesus himself will carry.

“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” (10:39)

The Greek word for life, psychē, means the whole self. The paradox is the heart of the Gospel: a life held on to for its own sake is lost, a life given for Christ is found, and not only after death.

“whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple … truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward” (10:42)

The “little ones” are the humble disciples whom the world overlooks. The smallest kindness done to them is done to Christ (compare Matthew 25:40), and it is never lost.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always taken the first verses as the foundation of the vocation to follow Christ in a special way. The Catechism, speaking of the duties of children and parents, teaches that family ties, though precious, are not absolute, and that parents must respect and encourage the vocation of their children (CCC 2232, in substance). Verse 42 is one of the passages on which the Church bases the works of mercy (CCC 2447, in substance), and the cup of cold water has become a lasting image for the smallest gift done with love.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus really ask us to love our family less?

No. In the language of the Gospel, to “love more” than another is to give first place to Jesus, without removing the love owed to parents and children, which God himself commands in the Fourth Commandment. Jesus says this to people for whom following him may bring a break with a family that opposes the faith, and to every disciple who must remember that God comes first. Christian love for family is not lessened when God is first; it is purified and strengthened.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The seventy disciples named in Luke 10:1 are remembered today. The Church of the East counts among them Addai (Thaddaeus), the traditional apostle of Edessa and of Mesopotamia, whom Eusebius of Caesarea in his Church History calls one of the seventy. The ancient Anaphora of Addai and Mari, which the Syro-Malabar Church prays in the Holy Qurbana, carries the names of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, and so links every Qurbana on our altars with this early sending.

For study and teaching

  • Who are the “little ones” in my parish or neighbourhood to whom I could give a cup of cold water in Jesus’ name?
