Word for the day

“Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” Matthew 10:40

Reflection

Today the Church remembers a large and nameless band: the Seventy Disciples of Jesus. “After this the Lord appointed seventy others and sent them on ahead of him in pairs to every town and place where he himself intended to go. He said to them, ‘The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.’” Notice the word “others”. Jesus had already sent the Twelve; now he sends seventy more, ordinary disciples whose names the Gospel does not give. The mission of the Church does not belong only to bishops and priests; it belongs to every baptised person (CCC 863). The number echoes the seventy elders on whom God put the spirit of Moses, when Moses said, “Would that all the Lord’s people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his spirit on them!” Our East Syriac tradition counts Mar Addai among the Seventy and tells that he was sent to Edessa, and Mar Mari after him; their names are on the Anaphora of Addai and Mari with which we celebrate the Holy Qurbana. So the Seventy are not far away from us: every Sunday we pray with words that come from their mission.

The first reading is for the thirsty. The exiles of Israel were far from home, poor and parched, and God speaks to them: “When the poor and needy seek water, and there is none, and their tongue is parched with thirst, I the Lord will answer them, I the God of Israel will not forsake them. I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys; I will make the wilderness a pool of water, and the dry land springs of water.” Before this, God had said, “do not fear, for I am with you.” The Seventy go out into a dry world with only that promise. In Kerala this week the rain is falling on dry wells and paddy fields, and the image is easy to see: where the Gospel arrives, springs open. Jesus himself stood on the last day of the festival and cried, “Let anyone who is thirsty come to me, and let the one who believes in me drink.” The Seventy carry that cry from house to house.

In the second reading Paul stands up in the synagogue at Antioch in Pisidia, having been sent off by the Church with fasting and prayer and the laying on of hands, and he begins not with himself but with the history of God's faithfulness: “The God of this people Israel chose our ancestors and made the people great during their stay in the land of Egypt, and with uplifted arm he led them out of it.” Forty years in the wilderness, the judges, Samuel, Saul, and then David, “a man after my heart, who will carry out all my wishes.” And at the end: “Of this man’s posterity God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised.” Paul speaks as a son of Israel to his own people, and the Church remembers that she draws her life from that holy root. A good missionary tells first what God has done, and only then what we must do.

The Epistle is for those who are afraid, and every missionary is afraid at times. “We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.” Then Paul asks the questions that make a coward brave: “If God is for us, who is against us? He who did not withhold his own Son, but gave him up for all of us, will he not with him also give us everything else?” And the great question: “Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?” Jesus had said that he was sending the Seventy “like lambs into the midst of wolves”; Paul's answer is the answer of those who have come back with joy: “No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” Today is also the First Friday, when the Church thinks of the open heart of the Lord, from which “at once blood and water came out”. That heart is the love from which, as Paul says, “neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation” can separate us (CCC 478).

The Gospel tells the price and the pay of discipleship. The price: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me; and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” Jesus does not ask us to love our families less; the Catechism reminds us that he asks us to put him first, and then we love them rightly (CCC 2232). “Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” The pay: not great wages but small welcomes. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me.” The Seventy needed hosts, people who would say “Peace to this house!” and open a door, and the Lord counts the host as a partner in the mission. “And whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple, truly I tell you, none of these will lose their reward.” In Kerala the first thing a guest receives is a glass of water; Jesus says that this ordinary kindness is seen in heaven. The whole world is evangelised, in the end, by a thousand glasses of water and a few brave people who walk to the next village.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers the Seventy Disciples of Jesus. We do not know their names, and that is part of the message. Jesus did not send only the Twelve; he sent seventy others. The mission is for all who are baptised, and that means you and me.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few” (Luke 10:2). Jesus sends them two by two, with peace on their lips: “Peace to this house!” They did not have much to take with them. They had a message, a companion and a promise.

The promise is in the other readings. God says through Isaiah, “I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys” (Isaiah 41:18). Wherever the Gospel arrives, dry hearts open like wells in the rains. And Paul says, “If God is for us, who is against us?” and “nothing in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:31, 39). Today is the First Friday: let us bring our fears to the open heart of Jesus.

Then Jesus tells us the price and the pay. The price: love him above all, and take up the cross. The pay: not gold, but welcome. “Whoever welcomes you welcomes me,” and a cup of cold water given in his name will not lose its reward (Matthew 10:40, 42).

Our own prayer at the Qurbana comes from Mar Addai and Mar Mari, disciples whom our tradition counts among the Seventy. Today let us be like them: let us pray for labourers, let us welcome the stranger, and let us carry the peace of Christ to one house. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus sent seventy friends to tell people about God, two by two. They said, ‘Peace to this house!’ You can do that too: be a friend who brings peace.

Jesus also said that even a glass of cold water given to someone in his name is a great gift. This week give a glass of water, a smile and a kind word to someone who is tired or sad!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sent seventy disciples ahead of you, two by two, to every place where you meant to go. Send labourers into your harvest, and give us hearts open to be sent and doors open to welcome. Open rivers in the dry places of our time; keep us in your love, from which nothing can separate us; and teach us to love you above all and to give a cup of cold water in your name. By the open heart that you showed us on the cross, make us missionaries of your mercy, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.
