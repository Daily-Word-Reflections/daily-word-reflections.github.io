Word for the day

“And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” Colossians 3:17

Reflection

On the first day of May, when many nations honour working men and women, the Church turns to a working man of Nazareth. In 1955 Pope Pius XII placed a feast of St Joseph the Worker on this day, so that the workers of the world would have a patron and a model, and so that no one would think that the Church looks on labour from a distance. Our Syro-Malabar families know Joseph well. We call him Yauseppithavu, Father Joseph; we keep March as his month and Wednesday as his day; countless churches in Kerala bear his name, and many a family begins a new house or a new business with a prayer to him. Today's Gospel gives him the only title the people of Nazareth ever gave him: the carpenter. The word used in Matthew's Greek, tekton, means a craftsman, a builder who worked with wood and perhaps with stone, making ploughs and yokes, doors and roof beams for the village. Mark's Gospel gives the same title to Jesus himself: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary” (Mark 6:3). For about thirty years the Son of God lived in that workshop, learned the trade from Joseph, and earned his bread as the people around him did. The Church sees in this hidden life a great teaching: the greater part of the Saviour's life on earth was spent in ordinary work (Catechism 531–533).

The first reading takes us back to the beginning, to the words spoken to Adam after the first sin: “cursed is the ground because of you; in toil you shall eat of it all the days of your life”, and “By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread until you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; you are dust, and to dust you shall return.” Then the man is sent out of the garden “to till the ground from which he was taken.” It would be easy to conclude that work itself is a punishment. Scripture says otherwise. Before there was any sin, “The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). Work belonged to God's first plan for us; it is a share in his own care for creation. What sin brought was the thorns and thistles, the toil that wears us out, the sweat, the frustration, the work that seems never to bear fruit. The good news of Nazareth is that the Son of God entered into exactly this toil. The Second Vatican Council says that he worked with human hands and thought with a human mind (Gaudium et Spes 22). In the workshop of Joseph, sweat itself was taken up into the work of redemption. The farmer in Kuttanad, the woman at the tea estate in Munnar, the young nurse on a night shift in a hospital far from home: their sweat is not wasted when it is joined to his.

The second reading is the prayer of a king who knows that power and cleverness are not enough. Solomon asks God: “Send her forth from the holy heavens, and from the throne of your glory send her, that she may labor at my side, and that I may learn what is pleasing to you.” He admits frankly, “For the reasoning of mortals is worthless, and our designs are likely to fail”, and asks, “Who has learned your counsel, unless you have given wisdom and sent your holy spirit from on high?” Joseph was such a man of wisdom, though he never sat on a throne. The Gospels do not record a single word from him; they record his obedience. Four times God spoke to him in a dream, and each time he rose and did what he was told: he took Mary as his wife, he fled with the child to Egypt, he returned, he settled in Nazareth (Matthew 1:20–24; 2:13–23). His wisdom was not a theory; it laboured at his side. In these last days before the Ascension and Pentecost, Solomon's prayer is the prayer of the whole Church: send your holy spirit from on high. Colossians tells us what such wisdom looks like in a household: “Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony”, and, “whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” That one verse could be written over the door of the house at Nazareth, and over the door of every Christian home and workplace.

In the Gospel Jesus comes home to Nazareth and teaches in the synagogue, and the people who watched him grow up are astounded: “Where did this man get this wisdom and these deeds of power? Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary?” Familiarity blinds them; “they took offense at him.” Jesus answers, “Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house”, and Matthew adds a sad line: “he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” Without knowing it, the villagers paid Joseph his greatest honour: the One who stood before them had been known for years simply as his son. The weekday readings of this Fifth Saturday complete the picture. Hebrews says that Jesus, born of the tribe of Judah and not of the priestly tribe, is a priest “through the power of an indestructible life”; the carpenter's son is the eternal high priest who offers himself for us. And in John Jesus promises, “When the Advocate comes, whom I will send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who comes from the Father, he will testify on my behalf.” On this May Day we also remember the Church's long teaching on the dignity of work, from Pope Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum (1891) to St John Paul II's Laborem Exercens (1981), which says that work is for the human person, and not the person for work. We pray for those who have no work, for those who are paid unjustly, for the many workers from other states who build our houses in Kerala far from their families, and for our own sons and daughters who labour in the Gulf and across the world. St Joseph, patron of workers, pray for them all.

Homily outline

Theme: “And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.”

On May Day, with St Joseph, Patron of the Working Class: work in God's plan, toil redeemed by the carpenter's Son, and wisdom that labours at our side.

1. By the sweat of your face

  • Work in God's first plan.
  • Toil and thorns after the fall.
  • The Son of God enters our toil.
“By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread until you return to the ground” Genesis 3:19
“Anyone unwilling to work should not eat.” 2 Thessalonians 3:10

Apply: Do one task today that you have been avoiding, and offer its effort to Christ.

2. Is not this the carpenter's son?

  • The hidden years at Nazareth.
  • Joseph who taught a trade to the Son of God.
  • Familiarity that blinds.
“You shall eat the fruit of the labor of your hands; you shall be happy, and it shall go well with you.” Psalm 128:2
“Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary?” Matthew 13:55

Apply: Thank one ordinary worker today, a bus driver, a cleaner or a shopkeeper, and see Christ's dignity in them.

3. Do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus

  • Wisdom that labours at my side.
  • Clothed with love.
  • Work that becomes thanksgiving.
“that she may labor at my side, and that I may learn what is pleasing to you.” Wisdom 9:10
“And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” Colossians 3:17

Apply: Begin and end each working day this week with a short prayer to St Joseph.

The Son of God worked at Joseph's bench, and so every honest work can become holy.

Let us offer our labour in his name, pay and seek just wages, and pray for all who toil.

Take-home resolution: This week, offer your work each morning, thank one worker each day, and pray for those who labour far from home.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is May Day, the day many countries honour workers. The Church gives us a worker too: St Joseph, the carpenter of Nazareth, patron of the working class.

The first reading tells us that after the first sin God said to Adam: “By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread” (Genesis 3:19). But work itself is not a curse. Even before sin, God placed the man in the garden to till it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Sin brought the thorns, the tiredness and the frustration.

Then the Son of God came and worked with human hands. For many years Jesus worked in Joseph's workshop. In the Gospel the people of Nazareth say, “Is not this the carpenter’s son?” (Matthew 13:55). They meant it as an insult. For us it is a joy: our Saviour knows what it is to be tired after a day's work.

Joseph never speaks in the Gospels. He listens to God and obeys. Solomon prays in the second reading for wisdom to labour at his side (Wisdom 9:10). Joseph had that wisdom: quiet, faithful, hard-working. And St Paul tells us, “whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus” (Colossians 3:17).

Today let us pray for all workers: for those without work, for those paid unjustly, for workers from other states living among us, and for our own children working far from home. And let us offer our own work to the Lord, so that it becomes a prayer. St Joseph, pray for us. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus had a father on earth who was a carpenter: St Joseph. Jesus helped him to make tables and doors.

When you do your homework or help at home, do it for Jesus. Then your work becomes a prayer, just like the work in Joseph's workshop!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you lived for many years as the carpenter's son at Nazareth and worked with human hands. Through the prayers of St Joseph, patron of workers, make holy the labour of our hands and minds. Give work to those who have none, justice to those who are paid unfairly, rest to the weary, and strength to all who toil far from home. Teach us to do everything in your name, giving thanks to the Father through you, and send us from on high the Spirit of wisdom, whom you have promised. Amen. Alleluia!
