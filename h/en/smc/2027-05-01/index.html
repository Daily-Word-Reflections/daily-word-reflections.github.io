Word for the day

“And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” Colossians 3:17

Reflection

On the first day of May, when many nations honour working men and women, the Church turns to a working man of Nazareth. In 1955 Pope Pius XII placed a feast of St Joseph the Worker on this day, so that the workers of the world would have a patron and a model, and so that no one would think that the Church looks on labour from a distance. Our Syro-Malabar families know Joseph well. We call him Yauseppithavu, Father Joseph; we keep March as his month and Wednesday as his day; countless churches in Kerala bear his name, and many a family begins a new house or a new business with a prayer to him. Today's Gospel gives him the only title the people of Nazareth ever gave him: the carpenter. The word used in Matthew's Greek, tekton, means a craftsman, a builder who worked with wood and perhaps with stone, making ploughs and yokes, doors and roof beams for the village. Mark's Gospel gives the same title to Jesus himself: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary” (Mark 6:3). For about thirty years the Son of God lived in that workshop, learned the trade from Joseph, and earned his bread as the people around him did. The Church sees in this hidden life a great teaching: the greater part of the Saviour's life on earth was spent in ordinary work (Catechism 531–533).

The first reading takes us back to the beginning, to the words spoken to Adam after the first sin: “cursed is the ground because of you; in toil you shall eat of it all the days of your life”, and “By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread until you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; you are dust, and to dust you shall return.” Then the man is sent out of the garden “to till the ground from which he was taken.” It would be easy to conclude that work itself is a punishment. Scripture says otherwise. Before there was any sin, “The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). Work belonged to God's first plan for us; it is a share in his own care for creation. What sin brought was the thorns and thistles, the toil that wears us out, the sweat, the frustration, the work that seems never to bear fruit. The good news of Nazareth is that the Son of God entered into exactly this toil. The Second Vatican Council says that he worked with human hands and thought with a human mind (Gaudium et Spes 22). In the workshop of Joseph, sweat itself was taken up into the work of redemption. The farmer in Kuttanad, the woman at the tea estate in Munnar, the young nurse on a night shift in a hospital far from home: their sweat is not wasted when it is joined to his.

The second reading is the prayer of a king who knows that power and cleverness are not enough. Solomon asks God: “Send her forth from the holy heavens, and from the throne of your glory send her, that she may labor at my side, and that I may learn what is pleasing to you.” He admits frankly, “For the reasoning of mortals is worthless, and our designs are likely to fail”, and asks, “Who has learned your counsel, unless you have given wisdom and sent your holy spirit from on high?” Joseph was such a man of wisdom, though he never sat on a throne. The Gospels do not record a single word from him; they record his obedience. Four times God spoke to him in a dream, and each time he rose and did what he was told: he took Mary as his wife, he fled with the child to Egypt, he returned, he settled in Nazareth (Matthew 1:20–24; 2:13–23). His wisdom was not a theory; it laboured at his side. In these last days before the Ascension and Pentecost, Solomon's prayer is the prayer of the whole Church: send your holy spirit from on high. Colossians tells us what such wisdom looks like in a household: “Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony”, and, “whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” That one verse could be written over the door of the house at Nazareth, and over the door of every Christian home and workplace.

In the Gospel Jesus comes home to Nazareth and teaches in the synagogue, and the people who watched him grow up are astounded: “Where did this man get this wisdom and these deeds of power? Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary?” Familiarity blinds them; “they took offense at him.” Jesus answers, “Prophets are not without honor except in their own country and in their own house”, and Matthew adds a sad line: “he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” Without knowing it, the villagers paid Joseph his greatest honour: the One who stood before them had been known for years simply as his son. The weekday readings of this Fifth Saturday complete the picture. Hebrews says that Jesus, born of the tribe of Judah and not of the priestly tribe, is a priest “through the power of an indestructible life”; the carpenter's son is the eternal high priest who offers himself for us. And in John Jesus promises, “When the Advocate comes, whom I will send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who comes from the Father, he will testify on my behalf.” On this May Day we also remember the Church's long teaching on the dignity of work, from Pope Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum (1891) to St John Paul II's Laborem Exercens (1981), which says that work is for the human person, and not the person for work. We pray for those who have no work, for those who are paid unjustly, for the many workers from other states who build our houses in Kerala far from their families, and for our own sons and daughters who labour in the Gulf and across the world. St Joseph, patron of workers, pray for them all.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is May Day, the day many countries honour workers. The Church gives us a worker too: St Joseph, the carpenter of Nazareth, patron of the working class.

The first reading tells us that after the first sin God said to Adam: “By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread” (Genesis 3:19). But work itself is not a curse. Even before sin, God placed the man in the garden to till it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Sin brought the thorns, the tiredness and the frustration.

Then the Son of God came and worked with human hands. For many years Jesus worked in Joseph's workshop. In the Gospel the people of Nazareth say, “Is not this the carpenter’s son?” (Matthew 13:55). They meant it as an insult. For us it is a joy: our Saviour knows what it is to be tired after a day's work.

Joseph never speaks in the Gospels. He listens to God and obeys. Solomon prays in the second reading for wisdom to labour at his side (Wisdom 9:10). Joseph had that wisdom: quiet, faithful, hard-working. And St Paul tells us, “whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus” (Colossians 3:17).

Today let us pray for all workers: for those without work, for those paid unjustly, for workers from other states living among us, and for our own children working far from home. And let us offer our own work to the Lord, so that it becomes a prayer. St Joseph, pray for us. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus had a father on earth who was a carpenter: St Joseph. Jesus helped him to make tables and doors.

When you do your homework or help at home, do it for Jesus. Then your work becomes a prayer, just like the work in Joseph's workshop!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you lived for many years as the carpenter's son at Nazareth and worked with human hands. Through the prayers of St Joseph, patron of workers, make holy the labour of our hands and minds. Give work to those who have none, justice to those who are paid unfairly, rest to the weary, and strength to all who toil far from home. Teach us to do everything in your name, giving thanks to the Father through you, and send us from on high the Spirit of wisdom, whom you have promised. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the day the world keeps as Labour Day the Church remembers St Joseph, the working man of Nazareth, and today’s four readings form a short theology of work. Genesis tells how work, which God gave to the first man as a share in tending his garden, became toil and sweat after sin. In Wisdom, Solomon asks God to send wisdom to labour at his side, so that his work may please God. Colossians tells the baptized to do everything, in word or deed, in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks. In Matthew the people of Nazareth stumble over a carpenter’s son who teaches with wisdom. The words to watch are work, wisdom and name: the work that sin made heavy is lifted again when it is done with God’s wisdom and in the name of Jesus, as it was in the workshop of Joseph.

Genesis 3:17–19, 23–24 · By the sweat of your face

Setting.

Genesis 2–3 tells, in the language of story, the truth about where human beings stand before God. The man is formed from the dust of the ground and placed in the garden of Eden “to till it and keep it” (2:15). Work belongs to the good creation, before any sin. Then the man and the woman eat from the one tree God had forbidden, and in 3:14–19 God speaks to the serpent, to the woman and to the man in turn.

Today’s verses give the word to the man and then his sending out from the garden. The reading skips vv. 20–22: the naming of Eve, the garments of skin God makes for the couple, and God’s words about the tree of life. The garments matter: even in judgment God clothes those he must send away.

Structure.

  • v. 17: the ground is cursed because of the man; he will eat of it in toil.
  • v. 18: thorns and thistles; the plants of the field.
  • v. 19: sweat until the return to dust.
  • v. 23: the man sent out to till the ground from which he was taken.
  • v. 24: the cherubim and the flaming sword guard the way to the tree of life. ¶ The man is not cursed; the ground is. The task of tilling remains, but now it is hard, and death sets its limit.

Key words.

  • ’ădāmâ, ground: The man (’ādām) is taken from the ground (’ădāmâ), and the two words sound alike in Hebrew. The man and the soil belong together: he is made from it, he lives from it, he returns to it.
  • ‘iṣṣābôn, toil, pain: The same noun is used in 3:16 for the woman’s pain in childbearing and in 3:17 for the man’s toil. Both of the great tasks of human life, giving life and sustaining it, now carry sorrow.
  • kĕrûbîm, cherubim: Heavenly guardians. Their images later stand over the ark of the covenant (Exodus 25:18–22) and are woven into the curtain of the tabernacle. The way back to God’s presence is guarded, but it is not abolished.

Verse by verse.

“cursed is the ground because of you; in toil you shall eat of it all the days of your life;” (3:17)

Sin breaks the harmony between the man and the earth. Work does not become evil, but the earth no longer answers him easily.

“By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread until you return to the ground,” (3:19)

Bread is still given. The verse that pronounces judgment also promises that the man will eat; God does not abandon him to hunger.

“you are dust, and to dust you shall return.” (3:19)

The words spoken to Christians at the start of the Great Fast in many Churches. They humble, but they do not end the story: the dust is the same dust into which God first breathed life (2:7).

“therefore the Lord God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from which he was taken.” (3:23)

He leaves with a task. Outside Eden the man still works the ground; the vocation given in 2:15 continues in a harder place.

“and a sword flaming and turning to guard the way to the tree of life.” (3:24)

The way to the tree of life is guarded, not destroyed. The Fathers saw it reopened by Christ: the Cross is the new tree of life, and the Book of Revelation ends with the tree of life given to the redeemed (Revelation 22:2).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in the garden “work is not yet a burden”, but the collaboration of man and woman with God in perfecting the visible creation (CCC 378), and that after the Fall the harmony between the human being and creation is broken (CCC 400). The Church therefore never treats work as a punishment in itself. St John Paul II, in his encyclical Laborem Exercens (1981), reads Genesis as the first “gospel of work”: the toil and sweat are real, yet work remains a good thing for the human person, through which he becomes more human. The Catechism adds that by enduring the hardship of work in union with Jesus, the carpenter of Nazareth and the Crucified, a person collaborates with the Son of God in his work of redemption (CCC 2427).

Wisdom 9:10–18 · Send wisdom to labour at my side

Setting.

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, most likely by a Jewish teacher in Alexandria in Egypt in the last century or so before Christ. The writer speaks in the person of Solomon, the king famed for wisdom. The Catholic Church receives the book as Scripture; Jews and most Protestants do not include it in their canon.

Chapter 9 is Solomon’s prayer for wisdom, a meditative retelling of his prayer at Gibeon (1 Kings 3:6–9). He confesses that even the most gifted person is nothing without the wisdom that comes from God (9:6). Today’s verses ask God to send wisdom from his throne, and then reflect on how little mortals can know without her.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the petition: send her, that she may labour at my side.
  • vv. 11–12: what wisdom will do: guide, guard, make my works acceptable.
  • vv. 13–16: the limits of human reason: the body weighs down the soul; even earthly things are found with labour.
  • vv. 17–18: only God’s gift of wisdom and holy spirit sets human paths right.

Key words.

  • sophia, wisdom: In this book wisdom is pictured as a person, a woman who sits beside God’s throne (9:4) and is sent out to dwell with human beings. The New Testament uses this language of Christ, “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24).
  • kopiaō, to labour, to toil: Solomon asks that wisdom may “labor at my side” (9:10). He does not ask to be spared work, but to have a companion in it. A different word, ponos (toil), stands in v. 16: what is at hand “we find with labor”.
  • skēnos, tent: The body is called an “earthy tent” (9:15), a dwelling that can be folded up. St Paul uses the same image: “if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God” (2 Corinthians 5:1).

Verse by verse.

“Send her forth from the holy heavens, and from the throne of your glory send her, that she may labor at my side,” (9:10)

The prayer of every worker who wants his work to be more than effort. Wisdom here is not cleverness but the gift that makes work please God.

“Then my works will be acceptable,” (9:12)

Solomon’s “works” include his judging and building. Work becomes acceptable to God not by its size but by the wisdom that guides it.

“for a perishable body weighs down the soul, and this earthy tent burdens the thoughtful mind.” (9:15)

The writer uses the language of Greek thought, but he does not despise the body. He is describing how hard it is for creatures of dust to know heavenly things.

“Who has learned your counsel, unless you have given wisdom and sent your holy spirit from on high?” (9:17)

Wisdom and the holy spirit stand side by side. Christians hear in this verse a first light on the Spirit whom the Father sends, and in this season of Resurrection the Church is already looking toward Pentecost.

How the Church reads it.

St Joseph is the model of the worker who asked for wisdom. The Gospels never record a word he spoke; they record what he did, quickly and exactly, each time God made his will known (Matthew 1:24; 2:14, 21). St John Paul II, in Redemptoris Custos (1989), presents Joseph’s work as the daily means by which he served the mystery of the Incarnation, and says that human work was taken up into that mystery in the house of Nazareth. The Catechism counts wisdom first among the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1831).

Colossians 3:14–17 · Do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus

Setting.

The letter is written in the name of Paul to the Church at Colossae in Asia Minor. Many scholars think a disciple of Paul wrote it after his death; others hold that Paul wrote it, perhaps with the help of a co-worker such as Timothy, who is named with him in the opening (1:1). The Church receives it as part of the apostolic witness.

Just before this reading the baptized are told to clothe themselves with compassion, kindness, humility, meekness and patience (3:12). Today’s verses put the last garment on top of all the others: love. Then the passage turns to peace, to the word of Christ, to song, and finally to everything we do.

Structure.

  • v. 14: above all, love, which binds everything together.
  • v. 15: the peace of Christ as the umpire in the heart; be thankful.
  • v. 16: the word of Christ dwelling richly; teaching and singing.
  • v. 17: whatever you do, in word or deed, in the name of the Lord Jesus, with thanks. ¶ Thanksgiving appears in each of vv. 15, 16 and 17.

Key words.

  • syndesmos, bond: Love is the belt or ligament that holds the other virtues together “in perfect harmony”. Without it they fall apart.
  • brabeuō, to rule, to be umpire: The word for the judge at the games who awards the prize. The peace of Christ is to decide disputes in the heart and in the community.
  • eucharistos, thankful: From the family of words that gives us “Eucharist”. A Christian life is a life of thanksgiving, and its centre is the great Thanksgiving of the Holy Qurbana.

Verse by verse.

“Above all, clothe yourselves with love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony.” (3:14)

Love is not one virtue among the others but the one that makes the others whole.

“And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, to which indeed you were called in the one body.” (3:15)

Peace is not only a private calm. It is given to “the one body”, the Church.

“Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly;” (3:16)

The verb means to make one’s home. The word is to live in the community as in its own house, through reading, teaching and song.

“And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.” (3:17)

Nothing is left out: the carpenter’s bench, the kitchen, the office and the field. Work done in the name of Jesus becomes an offering.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the daily work of lay people, carried out in the Spirit, becomes a spiritual sacrifice acceptable to God through Jesus Christ, offered with the Lord’s Body in the Eucharist (Lumen Gentium 34). The Catechism takes up this teaching (CCC 901). St Joseph is the plainest example of a holy life made of ordinary work: the Church does not honour him for great deeds in public, but for faithful labour done for Jesus and Mary.

Matthew 13:53–58 · Is not this the carpenter’s son?

Setting.

Matthew 13 gathers seven parables of the kingdom. When Jesus has finished them he goes to his “hometown”, Nazareth in Galilee, a small village where everyone knew his family. Matthew follows Mark’s account (Mark 6:1–6) but tells it more briefly and gently.

In Mark the people ask, “Is not this the carpenter?” (Mark 6:3). Matthew writes “the carpenter’s son”. Both are true: Jesus learned his trade from Joseph and worked at it himself. St Justin Martyr, in the second century, says that Jesus made ploughs and yokes.

Structure.

  • vv. 53–54a: Jesus comes to his hometown and teaches in the synagogue.
  • vv. 54b–56: the people’s questions: where did he get this wisdom? Is he not one of us?
  • v. 57: they take offence; the saying about a prophet in his own country.
  • v. 58: few deeds of power because of their unbelief.

Key words.

  • tektōn, carpenter, craftsman: A worker in wood, and often in stone as well: a builder. In a Galilean village he would make doors, beams, tools and furniture. The Son of God spent most of his years on earth at this work.
  • patris, hometown, own country: The place of one’s father. The familiar can become a barrier: they think they know him already.
  • skandalizomai, to take offence, to stumble: The word for a stumbling block. They trip over the very ordinariness through which God had come close.

Verse by verse.

“Where did this man get this wisdom and these deeds of power?” (13:54)

They admit the wisdom and the power. Their difficulty is not the evidence but the source: he is too familiar to be from God.

“Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary?” (13:55)

Joseph is not named here but described by his work. He is remembered in his own village as a craftsman, and Jesus as his son.

“And are not his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?” (13:55)

In Hebrew and Aramaic “brother” can mean a close relative. The Church teaches that these are not other children of Mary: James and Joseph are sons of another Mary (compare Matthew 27:56; CCC 500).

“And he did not do many deeds of power there, because of their unbelief.” (13:58)

Mark says he “could do no deed of power there” (Mark 6:5). Matthew’s wording makes clear that the limit is not in Jesus’ power but in their refusal to receive it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that during the greater part of his life Jesus shared the condition of the vast majority of human beings: a daily life spent without evident greatness, a life of manual labour (CCC 531), and that the hidden life at Nazareth allows everyone to enter into fellowship with Jesus by the most ordinary events of daily life (CCC 533). Pope Pius XII instituted the feast of St Joseph the Worker in 1955, so that the first of May, a day of labour across the world, would also be kept as a day to honour the dignity of work in the light of Nazareth. Pope Francis, in Patris Corde (2020), calls Joseph a working father who teaches the value, dignity and joy of earning one’s bread.

Questions people ask

Is work a punishment for sin?

No. Before any sin the man is placed in the garden “to till it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). Sin did not create work; it made work hard, uncertain and mixed with pain (CCC 378, 400). In Christ even the hardship of work can be joined to his own offering and become a share in redemption (CCC 2427).

Did Jesus have brothers and sisters?

The Gospels speak of his “brothers and sisters”, but in the Bible these words are used for cousins and close relatives as well (for example, in the Hebrew of Genesis 13:8 Lot, Abraham’s nephew, is called his brother; NRSV kindred). The Church has always confessed that Mary remained a virgin, and the Catechism notes that James and Joseph, named here among Jesus’ “brothers”, are the sons of another Mary (CCC 499–500).

Why did the people of Nazareth reject him?

Not because the signs were lacking. They saw his wisdom and heard of his power. They could not accept that God would come so close, in a neighbour, a worker, a man whose family they knew. The Gospel warns every believer: familiarity with holy things can become a reason not to receive them.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St Joseph, whom our tradition calls Mar Yausep, is dear to Syro-Malabar families, and many of our parishes bear his name. His feast as patron of workers falls today on the Saturday of the Fifth Week of Resurrection, so the joy of Easter surrounds it. In a land where many work in fields, in fishing, in building, and far from home in the Gulf and beyond, the feast is a reminder that the Church honours the work of every person and asks for just wages and humane conditions.

Pope Leo XIII, in Rerum Novarum (1891), began the modern social teaching of the Church on work and the rights of workers. Today is a good day to pray for those without work, for migrant workers and their families, and for employers who must decide justly, entrusting all of them to the protection of Mar Yausep.

For study and teaching

  • Genesis says work became hard after sin. Where in my work do I feel the “thorns and thistles”, and how could I offer that hardship with Christ?
  • Solomon asked for wisdom to labour at his side. Do I pray before my work, and what would change if I did?
  • Colossians says “whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus.” Which part of my daily work have I kept apart from him?
  • The people of Nazareth could not see God in a familiar carpenter. Whom do I fail to respect because I think I already know them?
