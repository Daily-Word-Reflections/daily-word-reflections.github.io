Word for the day

“The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume.” John 12:3

Reflection

On the eve of Hosanna Sunday the Church sets two farewells side by side. Paul is on his way to Jerusalem and calls the elders of Ephesus to Miletus. He knows what is coming: “And now, as a captive to the Spirit, I am on my way to Jerusalem, not knowing what will happen to me there, except that the Holy Spirit testifies to me in every city that imprisonment and persecutions are waiting for me.” He is not afraid: “But I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the good news of God's grace.” He charges the elders to shepherd “the church of God that he obtained with the blood of his own Son”, and gives them a saying of Jesus found nowhere else in Scripture: “It is more blessed to give than to receive.” Then “he knelt down with them all and prayed. There was much weeping among them all; they embraced Paul and kissed him.” Paul walks toward Jerusalem as his Master did, with his face set and his heart full of love for those he leaves behind. Every priest who has left a parish, every parent who has watched a child leave for a distant country, knows something of that shore at Miletus.

The Gospel takes us to Bethany “six days before the Passover”. Lazarus, raised from the dead, sits at the table; Martha serves, as she always does; and Mary does something no one expected. She “took a pound of costly perfume made of pure nard, anointed Jesus' feet, and wiped them with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume.” A pound of pure nard was worth a year's wages. Judas objects that it should have been sold for the poor, but John tells us his heart was not with the poor. Jesus defends her: “Leave her alone. She bought it so that she might keep it for the day of my burial.” Mary has understood what the disciples have not: that he is going to die, and that love does not calculate at such a moment. Her gift is extravagant, and the whole house is filled with it. Tomorrow we will wave palms and cry Hosanna; this evening the Gospel asks what we will pour out at his feet in the coming week. In many homes the days before Pesaha are spent cleaning the house and preparing for the Pesaha appam. Let the house be filled too with the fragrance of prayer, forgiveness and generous love.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells the elders of Ephesus that he is going to Jerusalem as a captive to the Spirit. Prison and persecution wait for him. Yet he says: I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course (Acts 20:24).

He reminds them of a word of the Lord Jesus: it is more blessed to give than to receive (Acts 20:35). Then they kneel together, weep and embrace him on the shore.

Six days before the Passover, Mary of Bethany pours a pound of costly nard on Jesus' feet and wipes them with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume (John 12:3). Jesus says she has kept it for the day of his burial.

Tomorrow we wave palms and cry Hosanna. Tonight let us ask what we will pour out at his feet this week. Let our homes be filled with the fragrance of prayer and love. Amen.

For children

Children, Mary gave Jesus her most precious perfume, and the whole house smelled lovely. What is something special you can give to Jesus this week? Maybe your time in prayer, or a kind act for someone at home.

Lord Jesus Christ, six days before the Passover you sat at table in Bethany, and Mary poured out her costly perfume at your feet. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the eve of Hosanna Sunday, two farewells. Paul, on his way to Jerusalem and expecting chains, says goodbye to the elders of Ephesus with tears and tells them to care for the flock. At Bethany Mary pours out costly perfume on the feet of Jesus, who says it is for his burial. Both Paul and Mary know that a road is ending in Jerusalem, and both spend without counting.

Acts 20:22–38 · Paul’s farewell at Miletus

Setting.

Paul is ending his third missionary journey, probably around AD 57, and hurrying to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost (20:16). He does not go back to Ephesus, where he had spent about three years, but sends for its elders to come to Miletus, a port some 50 km to the south (20:17). This is the only speech in Acts that Paul addresses to Christian leaders, and it reads like a last testament.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: where he is going: bound by the Spirit, ready to give his life.
  • vv. 25–27: none of them “will ever see my face again”; he has kept nothing back.
  • vv. 28–31: keep watch over the flock; wolves will come.
  • v. 32: he commends them to God.
  • vv. 33–35: his own example: he worked with his hands and helped the weak.
  • vv. 36–38: prayer and tears at the ship.

Key words.

  • episkopos, overseer: In v. 17 these men are called elders (presbyteroi), and in v. 28 overseers. At this early stage the two words overlap. The offices of bishop and presbyter became distinct as the Church’s ministry took its settled form, already clear in St Ignatius of Antioch.
  • poimainō, to shepherd: To feed and to guard. The elders share the work of the Good Shepherd (John 10:11; 1 Peter 5:2–4).
  • paratithemai, to commend: The word of v. 32. It is the verb Jesus uses on the Cross in Luke: “into your hands I commend my spirit” (Luke 23:46). Paul gives his people to God, because they were never his own.

Verse by verse.

“But I do not count my life of any value to myself, if only I may finish my course” (20:24)

The image of a runner, as in 1 Corinthians 9:24–27 and 2 Timothy 4:7. His life is worth something only as the means of finishing what he has been given to do.

“Keep watch over yourselves and over all the flock, of which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to shepherd the church of God that he obtained with the blood of his own Son.” (20:28)

First “yourselves”: a shepherd who does not watch himself cannot watch others. A note on the text: the Greek at the end can mean “with the blood of his own” (that is, his own Son) or “with his own blood”, and some manuscripts read “the church of the Lord”. The NRSV and the POC take it differently. All agree on the price.

“remembering the words of the Lord Jesus, for he himself said, ‘It is more blessed to give than to receive.’” (20:35)

This saying of Jesus is not in any of the four Gospels. Acts has preserved it for us. Paul has just shown what it means: he worked with his hands so as to give.

“When he had finished speaking, he knelt down with them all and prayed.” (20:36)

Grown men weep and embrace (v. 37). The Church’s leaders are also friends. This warmth is part of what ministry should be.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes this verse when it speaks of the apostles entrusting the flock to those who would follow them (Lumen Gentium 20; see also CCC 861). The same teaching stands behind every bishop and priest in the Church today. For the rest of us, the passage is a mirror: a good shepherd tells the whole truth (v. 27), does not love money (v. 33), and cares for the weak (v. 35).

John 12:1–8 · The house was filled with fragrance

Setting.

“Six days before the Passover” begins the countdown in John to the Lord’s death. Bethany is close to Jerusalem (11:18). In the house, the people of the last chapter are together: Martha serving, Lazarus at table, and Mary. John’s readers know the leaders have already resolved to kill Jesus (11:53).

This Mary is the sister of Martha and Lazarus. The Gospels do not identify her with Mary Magdalene or with the sinful woman of Luke 7; that merger became common in the West after St Gregory the Great, and the Churches of the East have generally kept them distinct.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the setting: a dinner at Bethany.
  • v. 3: Mary’s act.
  • vv. 4–6: Judas’s objection, and John’s comment.
  • vv. 7–8: Jesus’ reply.

Key words.

  • nardos pistikē, pure nard: Nard is a fragrant oil from a plant of the Himalayan region, carried to the Mediterranean at great cost. The exact meaning of pistikē is uncertain; it is usually taken as “genuine, pure”. The word is found only here and in Mark 14:3.
  • litra, pound: A Roman pound, about a third of a kilogram. That is a large amount of perfume to pour out at one time. The POC has “a bottle”.
  • triakosia dēnaria, three hundred denarii: A denarius was a day’s pay for a labourer (Matthew 20:2), so this was close to a year’s wages. Judas has done the sum correctly.

Verse by verse.

“The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume.” (12:3)

Washing feet was a servant’s task. Mary anoints them with perfume and wipes them with her hair. In a few days Jesus will wash the feet of his disciples (13:5). St Augustine says that the whole world has since been filled with the good odour of Christ (in substance; compare 2 Corinthians 2:15).

“Why was this perfume not sold for three hundred denarii and the money given to the poor?” (12:5)

A good argument used for a bad reason, as John notes (v. 6). The disciple who looks most concerned for the poor is the one stealing from them. John does not let us use the poor as a pretext.

“She bought it so that she might keep it for the day of my burial.” (12:7)

A note on the text: this verse is hard. The older Greek manuscripts read “let her keep it for the day of my burial”, and the NRSV expands this. Some later manuscripts read “she has kept it”. The POC puts it differently again, “let her consider that she has done this for the day of my burial”. In every reading Jesus receives the act as an anointing for his burial.

“You always have the poor with you, but you do not always have me.” (12:8)

Jesus echoes Deuteronomy 15:11, which goes on to command an open hand to the poor. So this is no excuse for neglecting them. It is a statement about this moment. The Catechism uses the verse to teach that Jesus’ presence is to be recognized in the poor themselves (CCC 2449).

How the Church reads it.

In the same way, the Church has always held together costly beauty in the worship of Christ and generous care for the poor. The Catechism’s teaching on the poor (CCC 2443–2449) sits beside its teaching on sacred art and worship, and neither cancels the other. St John Chrysostom could praise gifts for the altar and in the same breath tell his people not to neglect Christ in the poor (in substance). The perfume and the alms come from one love.

Questions people ask

Is Mary of Bethany the same person as Mary Magdalene?

The Gospels treat them as different women: Mary of Bethany lives near Jerusalem with Martha and Lazarus, and Mary Magdalene comes from Galilee (Luke 8:2). The Western tradition for some centuries joined them. Today the Roman calendar itself keeps separate days for them (22 and 29 July), and the Eastern traditions did not join them.

For study and teaching

  • What would it mean for me to give something costly to the Lord this coming Week of the Passion, without counting?
  • Who is the “weak” person I am called to help (Acts 20:35), and what is the work of my own hands that could help them?
