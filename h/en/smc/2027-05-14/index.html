Word for the day

“And they cast lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” Acts 1:26

Reflection

Today the Church remembers an apostle about whom Scripture tells us one story only, and that story happens in these very days. Between the Ascension and Pentecost the eleven return to Jerusalem and go up to the upper room, and Luke tells us that “All these were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus, as well as his brothers” (Acts 1:14). The next verse begins: “In those days Peter stood up among the believers (together the crowd numbered about one hundred twenty persons)”. So the choice of Matthias is made by a praying Church, gathered around the Mother of the Lord, waiting for the Spirit. Peter, who had denied his Master three times and been forgiven by the lakeshore, now acts as the shepherd Jesus made him. He does not hide the painful wound: Judas “was numbered among us and was allotted his share in this ministry.” The place left empty by betrayal must be filled, because the Twelve are not a random committee. Jesus had chosen twelve, as Israel had twelve tribes, and had promised: “you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel” (Matthew 19:28). The new Israel needs its twelve foundations before the Spirit comes.

Peter states what an apostle must be: “one of the men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us”. And then the heart of the matter: “one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” An apostle is first of all a witness, someone who has walked with Jesus and seen him risen. Two names are proposed: Joseph called Barsabbas, also known as Justus, and Matthias. Then the community prays: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen”. Notice the words: not whom we prefer, but whom you have chosen. They cast lots, an old way in Israel of leaving the decision to God (“The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is the LORD’s alone,” Proverbs 16:33), and the lot falls on Matthias. It is the last time Scripture shows the Church choosing by lot; after Pentecost the Spirit will guide her through prayer, discernment and the laying on of hands. Joseph Justus, not chosen, disappears from the story without a word of complaint, a quiet lesson for every one of us who is passed over. Ancient tradition is uncertain about where Matthias later preached and died; Eusebius records the opinion that he had been one of the seventy disciples, and a tradition holds that his relics rest in Trier in Germany, where an abbey bears his name. We know little about him; we know the one thing that matters: he was chosen by the Lord to witness to the Resurrection.

The first reading for the feast explains how God chooses. Samuel comes to Bethlehem to anoint a king among the sons of Jesse. He sees Eliab, tall and impressive, and thinks, surely this is the one. “But the LORD said to Samuel, ‘Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature, because I have rejected him; for the LORD does not see as mortals see; they look on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.’” Seven sons pass by, and none is chosen. “Are all your sons here?” “There remains yet the youngest, but he is keeping the sheep.” The boy nobody thought to call is the one God has chosen; Samuel anoints him, “and the spirit of the LORD came mightily upon David from that day forward.” The pattern is clear: God looks on the heart, chooses the one who seems least, and then gives his Spirit. Paul writes the same truth to the proud Corinthians: “Consider your own call, brothers and sisters: not many of you were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth. But God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong”. Why? “so that no one might boast in the presence of God.” Our worth is not in our talents but in Christ, “who became for us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification and redemption”.

The Gospel lets us hear Jesus praying with joy: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants”. The fishermen of Galilee, the tax collector, the zealot, and now Matthias, were such infants: not learned men, but men to whom the Father had revealed the Son. Then comes the invitation that every apostle has carried to the ends of the earth: “Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart”. The apostolic office is not a crown but a yoke shared with Christ; bishops, priests and every baptised person who serves learn from the gentle and humble heart of Jesus or they do not learn at all. Our Syro-Malabar Church calls herself the Church of the Mar Thoma Christians, proud of her apostolic origin. Today's feast reminds us that apostolic faith rests not on our pride but on God's choice and on the witness to the Resurrection handed down from those first Twelve. The weekday readings echo the same call: “Let what you heard from the beginning abide in you” (1 John 2:24), and “the one who endures to the end will be saved” (Matthew 24:13). Matthias stayed with Jesus from the beginning, and endured to the end.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we keep the feast of St Matthias, Mar Mathias Shleeha. We know only one story about him, and it happened in these very days, between the Ascension and Pentecost, when the apostles were praying in the upper room with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14).

Judas had betrayed the Lord, and one of the twelve places was empty. Peter stood up and said that someone who had walked with Jesus from the beginning must become “a witness with us to his resurrection” (Acts 1:22). That is what an apostle is: a witness of the Risen Lord.

They proposed two men, Joseph Justus and Matthias, and they prayed: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen” (Acts 1:24). Not whom we like, but whom you have chosen. The lot fell on Matthias. And Joseph Justus went home without complaint. Both are lessons for us.

Samuel thought the tall Eliab must be God's choice, but God said: “the LORD looks on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). The youngest boy, out with the sheep, was anointed. Paul says the same: God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong, so that no one may boast before God (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

Jesus thanks the Father for revealing these things to infants, and he invites: “Come to me, all you that are weary… and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). The apostles were simple men who learned from his gentle and humble heart. Our Church, the Church of Mar Thoma, received the faith from such apostles.

Let us pray before our decisions, as the first Church did. Let us not judge by appearance. And as we wait with Mary for the Holy Spirit, let us be witnesses of the Resurrection, each in our own place. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, there were twelve apostles, but one left Jesus. So the others prayed with Mother Mary and asked God to choose a new apostle. God chose Matthias!

God does not look at how tall or strong or clever we are. He looks at our heart. Let us keep our heart full of love for Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you chose the Twelve to be witnesses of your Resurrection, and when one place was left empty you filled it through the prayer of your Church gathered with your Mother. Through the prayers of Mar Mathias Shleeha and of Mar Thoma our father in faith, keep our Church faithful to the apostolic faith. You look on the heart and not on appearance: purify our hearts, teach us to accept your choices without envy, and give us your gentle and humble heart. As we wait with Mary for the Holy Spirit, make us witnesses of your Resurrection in our homes and our work. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Mathias Shleeha the four readings tell one story: God chooses, and he does not choose as we would. In Bethlehem Samuel looks at the tallest of Jesse’s sons and is told that the Lord looks on the heart; the youngest, out with the sheep, is anointed. In the upper room the believers pray to the Lord who knows every heart, and the lot falls on Matthias, a faithful man of whom Scripture says nothing else. Paul reminds the Corinthians that God chose the weak and the low so that no one might boast. And Jesus thanks his Father for revealing the mysteries of the Kingdom to infants. The words to watch are chosen and heart. Two days before Pentecost, the Church is made ready by restoring the Twelve.

1 Samuel 16:4–13 · The Lord looks on the heart

Setting.

King Saul has disobeyed the word of the Lord, and Samuel has been told that the kingdom will be given to another (15:26–28). Samuel grieves for Saul, but God sends him to Bethlehem, to the house of Jesse, with a horn of oil (16:1). The journey is dangerous, since anointing a new king while Saul lives could be taken as treason; so Samuel goes under the cover of offering a sacrifice (16:2–3).

Bethlehem is a small town of Judah. Jesse is the grandson of Ruth the Moabite (Ruth 4:17). From this house, and from this town, the Messiah will one day be born (Micah 5:2; Matthew 2:6).

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5: Samuel at Bethlehem; the elders tremble; the sacrifice prepared.
  • vv. 6–7: Eliab, and the Lord’s word: not by appearance, but by the heart.
  • vv. 8–10: seven sons pass by; none is chosen.
  • v. 11: “Are all your sons here?” The youngest is keeping the sheep.
  • vv. 12–13: David is brought in and anointed; the spirit of the Lord comes upon him.

Key words.

  • māšaḥ, to anoint: To pour oil on a person and set him apart for God. The one anointed is the māšîaḥ, the “anointed one”, which comes into Greek as Christos. Every time we say “Christ” or “Messiah” we remember this horn of oil.
  • lēbāb, heart: In Hebrew the heart is not only feeling but the centre of the person: thought, will and decision. God sees the whole person from within.
  • haqqāṭān, the youngest: Literally “the small one”. The youngest son, given the sheep to mind, is the least likely heir. Scripture again and again passes over the eldest: Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, and now David.

Verse by verse.

“the Lord does not see as mortals see; they look on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart.” (16:7)

This is the key verse of the reading, and of the feast. The believers in Acts will pray with the same faith: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart.”

““There remains yet the youngest, but he is keeping the sheep.”” (16:11)

Jesse did not even think to call him. The future shepherd of Israel is found among the flocks, and Christ, born in David’s town, will call himself the good shepherd (John 10:11).

“for we will not sit down until he comes here.” (16:11)

The Hebrew speaks of sitting down, or gathering round, for the sacrificial meal; the POC renders it “we will not eat”. Nothing begins until the one God has chosen is present.

“and the spirit of the Lord came mightily upon David from that day forward.” (16:13)

The anointing with oil is the outward sign; the gift of the Spirit is what it means. The same pattern returns at the Jordan, when the Spirit comes upon Jesus (Luke 3:22; 4:18).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads David’s anointing as a figure of Christ, the Son of David, anointed with the Holy Spirit (Acts 10:38). The Catechism explains that “Christ” means anointed, and that in Israel those consecrated for a mission from God were anointed in his name: kings, priests and, at times, prophets (CCC 436). On this feast the reading also teaches about every call: God’s choice does not follow rank or appearance, and those who choose ministers in the Church must look, as far as human beings can, at the heart.

Acts 1:15–26 · The lot fell on Matthias

Setting.

The scene lies between the Ascension and Pentecost. The eleven apostles, with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus and his brothers, are in the upper room devoting themselves to prayer (1:13–14). Before the Spirit comes, Peter rises to fill the place left empty by Judas.

The number twelve matters. Jesus chose the Twelve as a sign of the twelve tribes of Israel (Luke 22:30), the whole people of God gathered again. The Church that will be born at Pentecost must stand on that full foundation. When the apostle James is later killed (12:2), no one is chosen to replace him: the Twelve had to be complete once, as witnesses, at the beginning.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: Peter rises among about one hundred twenty believers; Scripture had to be fulfilled concerning Judas.
  • vv. 18–19: an aside by Luke on Judas’ death and the Field of Blood.
  • v. 20: two verses from the Psalms.
  • vv. 21–22: the condition: a witness from the baptism of John to the Ascension.
  • vv. 23–26: two are proposed; prayer; the lot falls on Matthias.

Key words.

  • klēros, share, lot: Judas “was allotted his share” (v. 17), literally his lot, and at the end “the lot fell on Matthias” (v. 26). The same word frames the whole reading. From it comes our word “clergy”: those whose lot is the Lord’s service.
  • episkopē, position of overseer: The word of Psalm 109:8 in its Greek form. It is related to episkopos, overseer, from which comes “bishop”. Luke sees in the apostolic office a charge of oversight that must be handed on.
  • kardiognōstēs, knower of hearts: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart” translates this single Greek word, found in the New Testament only here and in 15:8. It is the prayer of 1 Samuel 16:7 on Christian lips.

Verse by verse.

“In those days Peter stood up among the believers (together the crowd numbered about one hundred twenty persons)” (1:15)

Peter, who denied the Lord three times, now leads the community. The number one hundred twenty is ten times twelve: a small Israel gathered round the Twelve.

“one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” (1:22)

The first task of an apostle is to witness to the Resurrection. Matthias had walked with Jesus from the Jordan to the Mount of Olives, though the Gospels never once mention his name. Faithfulness unseen is not faithfulness unknown to God.

““Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen” (1:24)

They do not ask God to bless their choice; they ask him to show his. The prayer probably addresses the Lord Jesus, who chose the first Twelve (1:2).

“And they cast lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” (1:26)

Casting lots was an old way of leaving a decision to God (Proverbs 16:33). After Pentecost the Church never uses it again in Acts; she will choose with prayer, laying on of hands and the guidance of the Spirit (6:6; 13:2–3).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Lord Jesus gave his community a structure, choosing the Twelve with Peter as their head; representing the twelve tribes of Israel, they are the foundation stones of the new Jerusalem (CCC 765). The Second Vatican Council sees in this election the beginning of apostolic succession: the apostles took care to appoint successors, so that the mission entrusted to them would continue to the end of the world (Lumen Gentium 20). St John Chrysostom notes that Peter, though first, acts together with all, and leaves the choice to God in prayer (in substance, Homilies on Acts 3). Of Matthias’ later life we know little for certain. Eusebius reports that he was said to be one of the seventy disciples (Luke 10:1); later traditions place his preaching in various lands.

1 Corinthians 1:26–30 · God chose what is weak

Setting.

The Christians of Corinth were divided into parties, each boasting of a favourite teacher (1:12), and some prized eloquence and human wisdom. Paul answers with the message of the Cross, which looks like foolishness to the world but is the power of God (1:18). Now he asks them to look at themselves: their own calling proves his point.

Structure.

  • v. 26: consider your call: not many wise, powerful or of noble birth.
  • vv. 27–28: God chose the foolish, the weak, the low and despised, things that are not.
  • v. 29: the purpose: that no one might boast before God.
  • v. 30: all is from God in Christ Jesus, who became our wisdom, righteousness, sanctification and redemption.

Key words.

  • klēsis, call: Not first a vocation to a work but God’s call into Christ. Paul uses the word for the whole community: each of them was called by name.
  • exelexato, chose: The verb is repeated three times in two verses: God chose, God chose, God chose. It is the language of Israel’s election (Deuteronomy 7:6–7), not because they were many but because the Lord loved them.
  • kauchaomai, to boast: A key word in Paul’s letters. The boasting he forbids is self-reliance before God. In the next verse he quotes Jeremiah 9:24: let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.

Verse by verse.

“not many of you were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth.” (1:26)

“Not many”, not “none”: there were people of standing at Corinth too, such as Erastus, the city treasurer (Romans 16:23). But most were ordinary people, labourers, freed slaves, households of small means.

“God chose what is low and despised in the world, things that are not, to reduce to nothing things that are” (1:28)

“Things that are not” are people the world counts as nobodies. God creates from nothing, and in his Church he raises up from nothing.

“He is the source of your life in Christ Jesus, who became for us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification and redemption” (1:30)

Christ is not only our teacher of wisdom; he is himself our wisdom. All four gifts come to us not as achievements but in union with him.

How the Church reads it.

This reading suits the feast of a little-known apostle. The Church did not choose the most famous of the hundred and twenty; she prayed, and God chose Matthias. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter, marvels that the world was won not by philosophers or orators but by fishermen and tentmakers (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that all in the Church are called to holiness (Lumen Gentium 39–40): God’s call is not reserved for the gifted, and every gift is his.

Matthew 11:25–30 · Revealed to infants

Setting.

Chapter 11 is a chapter of mixed response. John the Baptist sends from prison to ask whether Jesus is the one to come (11:2–3); some call Jesus a glutton and drunkard (11:19); the towns of Galilee where most of his miracles were done do not repent (11:20–24). At this low point Jesus turns to his Father in thanksgiving. The welcome he did not find among the important he finds among the little ones.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–26: thanksgiving: hidden from the wise, revealed to infants.
  • v. 27: the Father and the Son know each other; the Son reveals the Father.
  • vv. 28–30: the invitation: come to me, take my yoke, find rest.

Key words.

  • nēpioi, infants: Little children, too young to speak well. Here it means the humble and simple, those without learning or standing, like the fishermen who followed him.
  • epiginōskei, knows: A strong form of the verb “to know”: full, intimate knowledge. Only the Son knows the Father in this way, and he shares that knowledge with whom he chooses.
  • anapausis, rest: Not idleness but the rest that God gives, like the Sabbath rest. Jesus’ words recall Jeremiah 6:16: “and find rest for your souls.”

Verse by verse.

“I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants” (11:25)

Jesus does not despise learning; he rejoices that God’s gift does not depend on it. Pride closes the heart that learning ought to open.

“and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him.” (11:27)

Here Matthew sounds like John’s Gospel. The Son’s choice is the source of every apostolic call, Matthias’ included.

““Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest.” (11:28)

Many hear an echo of Wisdom’s invitation in Sirach: “Draw near to me, you who are uneducated” (Sirach 51:23). Jesus speaks as Wisdom in person.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws on this thanksgiving to describe Jesus’ prayer: his exclamation “Yes, Father!” reveals the depth of his heart, his adherence to the Father’s good pleasure (CCC 2603). St Augustine often contrasts the proud wise of this world with the little ones to whom Christ reveals himself, and says that God’s mysteries are open to the humble (in substance). On the feast of an apostle this Gospel says where every mission begins: not in our talent, but in the Son’s will to reveal the Father through those he chooses.

Questions people ask

Acts says Judas fell headlong and burst open; Matthew says he hanged himself. Which is right?

The two accounts differ, and the Church has never hidden it. Matthew tells that Judas hanged himself and that the priests bought a potter’s field with the silver (Matthew 27:3–10); Luke, in an aside, tells that a field was acquired with the reward and that Judas fell headlong (Acts 1:18). Since ancient times readers have suggested that both memories can be held together, and that both preserve the name “Field of Blood”. What both teach is the same: the betrayal ended in ruin, and the place Judas left had to be filled.

Why did the apostles use lots? Is that not leaving things to chance?

For them it was the opposite of chance. They first set the conditions, chose two worthy men and prayed; then they let God decide between them. “The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is the Lord’s alone” (Proverbs 16:33). After Pentecost, with the Spirit given, Acts never again shows lots being cast.

Was Matthias a full apostle, or only a replacement?

A full apostle. Luke says he “was added to the eleven apostles”, and from the next chapter the Twelve stand together (2:14). The Church keeps his feast as an apostle. Paul was also called an apostle by the Risen Lord, but in another way, as the apostle to the nations; the Twelve were the witnesses from the beginning.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Our tradition calls the apostles Shleeha, “the sent ones”, and the Syro-Malabar Church, proud of its descent from Mar Thoma Shleeha, honours every one of the Twelve as a father in faith. In the Creed of the Holy Qurbana we confess one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church. Mar Mathias reminds us that the apostolic Church is not only the famous apostles, but also the faithful man chosen in prayer, of whom only one scene is told.

The feast falls two days before Pentecost, which opens the Season of the Apostles. The Twelve are complete again, gathered with Mary in prayer, waiting for the Spirit. This is a good day to pray for our bishops and priests, for those discerning a vocation, and for those who choose and form them, that they may look at the heart as the Lord does.

For study and teaching

  • Samuel almost anointed the wrong son because he looked at appearance. Where do I judge people by what I see rather than by their heart?
  • Matthias followed Jesus for years without being named. What faithful service in my parish goes unseen, and how can I honour it?
  • Paul says God chose the weak to shame the strong. How does that change the way I see my own weakness?
  • The apostles prayed, “Show us which one you have chosen.” How do I bring my own big decisions to God in prayer?
