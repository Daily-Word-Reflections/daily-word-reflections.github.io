Word for the day

“And they cast lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles.” Acts 1:26

Reflection

Today the Church remembers an apostle about whom Scripture tells us one story only, and that story happens in these very days. Between the Ascension and Pentecost the eleven return to Jerusalem and go up to the upper room, and Luke tells us that “All these were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus, as well as his brothers” (Acts 1:14). The next verse begins: “In those days Peter stood up among the believers (together the crowd numbered about one hundred twenty persons)”. So the choice of Matthias is made by a praying Church, gathered around the Mother of the Lord, waiting for the Spirit. Peter, who had denied his Master three times and been forgiven by the lakeshore, now acts as the shepherd Jesus made him. He does not hide the painful wound: Judas “was numbered among us and was allotted his share in this ministry.” The place left empty by betrayal must be filled, because the Twelve are not a random committee. Jesus had chosen twelve, as Israel had twelve tribes, and had promised: “you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel” (Matthew 19:28). The new Israel needs its twelve foundations before the Spirit comes.

Peter states what an apostle must be: “one of the men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us”. And then the heart of the matter: “one of these must become a witness with us to his resurrection.” An apostle is first of all a witness, someone who has walked with Jesus and seen him risen. Two names are proposed: Joseph called Barsabbas, also known as Justus, and Matthias. Then the community prays: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen”. Notice the words: not whom we prefer, but whom you have chosen. They cast lots, an old way in Israel of leaving the decision to God (“The lot is cast into the lap, but the decision is the LORD’s alone,” Proverbs 16:33), and the lot falls on Matthias. It is the last time Scripture shows the Church choosing by lot; after Pentecost the Spirit will guide her through prayer, discernment and the laying on of hands. Joseph Justus, not chosen, disappears from the story without a word of complaint, a quiet lesson for every one of us who is passed over. Ancient tradition is uncertain about where Matthias later preached and died; Eusebius records the opinion that he had been one of the seventy disciples, and a tradition holds that his relics rest in Trier in Germany, where an abbey bears his name. We know little about him; we know the one thing that matters: he was chosen by the Lord to witness to the Resurrection.

The first reading for the feast explains how God chooses. Samuel comes to Bethlehem to anoint a king among the sons of Jesse. He sees Eliab, tall and impressive, and thinks, surely this is the one. “But the LORD said to Samuel, ‘Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature, because I have rejected him; for the LORD does not see as mortals see; they look on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.’” Seven sons pass by, and none is chosen. “Are all your sons here?” “There remains yet the youngest, but he is keeping the sheep.” The boy nobody thought to call is the one God has chosen; Samuel anoints him, “and the spirit of the LORD came mightily upon David from that day forward.” The pattern is clear: God looks on the heart, chooses the one who seems least, and then gives his Spirit. Paul writes the same truth to the proud Corinthians: “Consider your own call, brothers and sisters: not many of you were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth. But God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong”. Why? “so that no one might boast in the presence of God.” Our worth is not in our talents but in Christ, “who became for us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification and redemption”.

The Gospel lets us hear Jesus praying with joy: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants”. The fishermen of Galilee, the tax collector, the zealot, and now Matthias, were such infants: not learned men, but men to whom the Father had revealed the Son. Then comes the invitation that every apostle has carried to the ends of the earth: “Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart”. The apostolic office is not a crown but a yoke shared with Christ; bishops, priests and every baptised person who serves learn from the gentle and humble heart of Jesus or they do not learn at all. Our Syro-Malabar Church calls herself the Church of the Mar Thoma Christians, proud of her apostolic origin. Today's feast reminds us that apostolic faith rests not on our pride but on God's choice and on the witness to the Resurrection handed down from those first Twelve. The weekday readings echo the same call: “Let what you heard from the beginning abide in you” (1 John 2:24), and “the one who endures to the end will be saved” (Matthew 24:13). Matthias stayed with Jesus from the beginning, and endured to the end.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we keep the feast of St Matthias, Mar Mathias Shleeha. We know only one story about him, and it happened in these very days, between the Ascension and Pentecost, when the apostles were praying in the upper room with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14).

Judas had betrayed the Lord, and one of the twelve places was empty. Peter stood up and said that someone who had walked with Jesus from the beginning must become “a witness with us to his resurrection” (Acts 1:22). That is what an apostle is: a witness of the Risen Lord.

They proposed two men, Joseph Justus and Matthias, and they prayed: “Lord, you know everyone’s heart. Show us which one of these two you have chosen” (Acts 1:24). Not whom we like, but whom you have chosen. The lot fell on Matthias. And Joseph Justus went home without complaint. Both are lessons for us.

Samuel thought the tall Eliab must be God's choice, but God said: “the LORD looks on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). The youngest boy, out with the sheep, was anointed. Paul says the same: God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong, so that no one may boast before God (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

Jesus thanks the Father for revealing these things to infants, and he invites: “Come to me, all you that are weary… and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). The apostles were simple men who learned from his gentle and humble heart. Our Church, the Church of Mar Thoma, received the faith from such apostles.

Let us pray before our decisions, as the first Church did. Let us not judge by appearance. And as we wait with Mary for the Holy Spirit, let us be witnesses of the Resurrection, each in our own place. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, there were twelve apostles, but one left Jesus. So the others prayed with Mother Mary and asked God to choose a new apostle. God chose Matthias!

God does not look at how tall or strong or clever we are. He looks at our heart. Let us keep our heart full of love for Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you chose the Twelve to be witnesses of your Resurrection, and when one place was left empty you filled it through the prayer of your Church gathered with your Mother. Through the prayers of Mar Mathias Shleeha and of Mar Thoma our father in faith, keep our Church faithful to the apostolic faith. You look on the heart and not on appearance: purify our hearts, teach us to accept your choices without envy, and give us your gentle and humble heart. As we wait with Mary for the Holy Spirit, make us witnesses of your Resurrection in our homes and our work. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!
