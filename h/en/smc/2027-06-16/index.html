Word for the day

“But law came in, with the result that the trespass multiplied; but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more,” Romans 5:20

Reflection

In the fifth chapter of Romans Paul sets the whole of human history between two men. “Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death came through sin, and so death spread to all because all have sinned…” The one man is Adam. Through his disobedience sin entered our race like a poison entering the bloodstream, and with sin came death, the separation that God never willed. The Church reads in this passage the revealed basis of her teaching on original sin: we are born into a wounded humanity, deprived of the holiness in which Adam was created, and every personal sin adds to the wound. But Paul's purpose is not to frighten us with Adam; it is to dazzle us with Christ. Adam, he says, “is a type of the one who was to come.” He is like a rough sketch of the true Man. And the comparison is not equal. Again and again Paul writes “much more”: if many died through one man's trespass, “much more surely have the grace of God” and his gift “abounded for the many.” Then the great parallel: “Therefore just as one man’s trespass led to condemnation for all, so one man’s act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all. For just as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous.” Adam reached for the fruit of the tree to become like God; Christ, who is God, stretched out his arms on the tree of the Cross in obedience. Our own Mar Aprem, whom we remembered last week, loved to sing of the two trees, the tree of Eden and the tree of Golgotha, and of the Second Adam who opened again the gate of Paradise.

Then Paul writes one of the most consoling sentences in the Bible: “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.” God's grace is not a small repair that barely covers the damage. It overflows. No sin of ours, no history of a family, no darkness of a nation is deeper than the grace of Christ. Paul will immediately warn that this is no excuse to go on sinning (Romans 6:1–2); grace that abounds is grace that changes us. But it means that no one in the parish, in the prison or in the hospital bed is beyond hope.

The Gospel tells the same story as a parable. Jesus speaks in the Temple, in the last days before his Passion, to the chief priests, the scribes and the elders who had questioned his authority (Mark 11:27). “A man planted a vineyard, put a fence around it, dug a pit for the wine press, and built a watchtower; then he leased it to tenants and went to another country.” His hearers knew Isaiah's song of the vineyard, in which the vineyard is God's people (Isaiah 5:1–7). The owner sends servant after servant to collect his share of the fruit; they are beaten, insulted, killed. The patience of the owner is almost foolish. “He had still one other, a beloved son. Finally he sent him to them, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’” The word “beloved” is the word the Father spoke over Jesus at the Jordan and on the mountain (Mark 1:11; 9:7). But the tenants reason: “This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.” Jesus is telling his own story, and he knows how it will end. Yet he ends with the psalm: “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord’s doing, and it is amazing in our eyes.” The greatest sin ever committed, the rejection and killing of the Son, became the cornerstone of our salvation. Here is Paul's “where sin increased, grace abounded” in the shape of a story. The parable was spoken to particular leaders at a particular hour, and Mark says that the crowd was on Jesus' side; the Church has always refused to turn it into a verdict on the Jewish people, then or now (Nostra Aetate 4). It is addressed to us, the tenants of the vineyard today. Our parish, our family, our own life are leased to us by the Owner, who still sends his messengers looking for fruit. As our Syro-Malankara brothers and sisters begin the Apostles' Fast today, the question is for all of us: what fruit will the Owner find?

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul tells the whole story of humanity in two men. Through Adam, sin and death came into the world. Through Jesus Christ, grace and life came. We all share Adam's wound; we can all share Christ's healing.

And the two are not equal. Paul says again and again, “much more.” Grace is much stronger than sin. “Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (Romans 5:20). There is no sin so great that the grace of Christ cannot overcome it.

In the Gospel Jesus tells a story about a vineyard. The owner sends servant after servant to collect the fruit, and the tenants beat them and kill them. At last he sends his beloved son, and they kill him too. Jesus is speaking about himself, the beloved Son of the Father, who will soon be put to death.

But God does not let the story end there. “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Mark 12:10). The worst thing human beings ever did, killing the Son of God, became the foundation of our salvation. That is what grace does: it turns even death into life.

Today we are the tenants of God's vineyard: our family, our parish, our own life. The Owner is looking for fruit. Let us give him the fruit of prayer, forgiveness and charity, and if we have sinned, let us run to his grace, which is always greater. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a man who sent his own dear son to his vineyard, and bad workers killed him. Jesus was talking about himself. But God made something wonderful out of it: Jesus rose again and saved us! God can bring good even out of bad things. And God's love is always bigger than our sins.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings tell the story of salvation in a few strokes. Paul sets two men side by side: Adam, through whose disobedience sin and death entered the world, and Christ, through whose obedience grace and life overflow for all. In Mark, Jesus tells of a vineyard owner who sends servant after servant and at last his beloved son, whom the tenants kill; yet the rejected stone becomes the cornerstone. Where human refusal is great, God’s faithfulness is greater: “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.”

Romans 5:12–21 · Where sin increased, grace abounded

Setting.

In the first chapters of Romans Paul has shown that all, Jews and Gentiles, need God’s saving justice, and that it is given through faith in Christ (3:21–26). In the first verses of chapter 5 he spoke of peace with God and of God’s love poured into our hearts. Now he looks at the whole of human history through two figures, Adam and Christ.

The passage is one of the most important in Scripture for the Church’s teaching on original sin. Paul’s sentences are long and sometimes broken off (v. 12 begins a comparison that is completed only in v. 18), because he is reaching for something greater than words: the abundance of grace.

Structure.

  • v. 12: through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin, and death spread to all.
  • vv. 13–14: sin and death reigned even before the law; Adam is a type of the one to come.
  • vv. 15–17: the gift is not like the trespass: “much more” the grace of the one man Jesus Christ.
  • vv. 18–19: one man’s trespass, condemnation for all; one man’s obedience, justification and life for all.
  • vv. 20–21: the law came in; where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.

Key words.

  • eph’ hō, because: In verse 12 the NRSV reads “because all have sinned”. The old Latin translation read “in whom (in Adam) all have sinned”, and St Augustine built on it. Modern scholars mostly read “because”, but the Church’s teaching does not depend on one phrase: the whole passage teaches that all are affected by Adam’s sin and all need Christ (vv. 18–19).
  • typos, type: A pattern or imprint, like the mark a seal leaves. Adam is a “type of the one who was to come”: as one man’s act affected all, so does Christ’s, but in the opposite direction and far more.
  • hyperperisseuō, to abound all the more: Paul uses a strengthened verb: grace did not only match sin, it overflowed beyond it. This is the heart of the passage.

Verse by verse.

“just as sin came into the world through one man, and death came through sin, and so death spread to all because all have sinned” (5:12)

Paul speaks of death not only as the end of the body but as separation from God. Sin is not only each person’s own fault; it is a power that entered the world and spread.

“Adam, who is a type of the one who was to come.” (5:14)

From the beginning God’s plan pointed to Christ. The first man already foreshadows the new Adam (1 Corinthians 15:22, 45).

“For just as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous.” (5:19)

“The many” in Hebrew style means all, as verse 18 says. Christ’s obedience, “to the point of death” (Philippians 2:8), undoes Adam’s disobedience.

“but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (5:20)

This is no excuse to sin, as Paul says at once (6:1–2). It is the reason no sinner need despair.

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent, in its decree on original sin, appealed to this passage (Session 5, 1546). The Catechism teaches that all are implicated in Adam’s sin, as St Paul affirms (CCC 402), and that original sin is a sin “contracted” and not “committed”: a state, not a personal act (CCC 404). It also recalls the words of St Leo the Great: Christ’s inexpressible grace gave us blessings better than those the demon’s envy had taken away (CCC 412). The Church’s teaching on original sin is never without the Good News: it is the reverse side of the truth that Jesus is the Saviour of all (CCC 389).

Mark 12:1–12 · The stone the builders rejected

Setting.

Jesus has entered Jerusalem and cleansed the Temple. The chief priests, the scribes and the elders have asked him by what authority he acts (11:27–33). He answers with this parable, and they understand that it is spoken against them (12:12).

The opening words echo Isaiah’s song of the vineyard (Isaiah 5:1–7), where the vineyard is the house of Israel and God is the owner who planted it with care. In Jesus’ parable, the vineyard is still God’s and still good; the problem is with the tenants entrusted with it. Vineyards were often owned by people who lived far away and let them to tenants for a share of the harvest.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the vineyard planted with care and let to tenants.
  • vv. 2–5: servant after servant sent for the fruit, beaten, insulted, killed.
  • vv. 6–8: last of all the beloved son; killed and thrown out of the vineyard.
  • v. 9: the owner will come and give the vineyard to others.
  • vv. 10–11: Psalm 118: the rejected stone has become the cornerstone.
  • v. 12: the leaders understand, and want to arrest him, but fear the crowd.

Key words.

  • agapētos, beloved: The same word the Father’s voice used at Jesus’ baptism and transfiguration: “my Son, the Beloved” (1:11; 9:7). Jesus tells his own story: he is not one more servant but the only Son.
  • klēronomos, heir: The tenants think that by killing the heir they can take the inheritance. Sin always imagines that God’s gifts can be seized by getting rid of God.
  • kephalē gōnias, cornerstone: Literally “head of the corner”: the main stone that holds two walls together, or the top stone of an arch. The early Church used this psalm verse again and again of the Risen Christ (Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“A man planted a vineyard, put a fence around it, dug a pit for the wine press, and built a watchtower” (12:1)

Every detail shows the owner’s care. God has given his people everything they need to bear fruit.

“He had still one other, a beloved son. Finally he sent him to them, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’” (12:6)

The owner’s hope seems almost foolish. It is the foolishness of God’s love, which sends the Son even knowing what will happen.

“He will come and destroy the tenants and give the vineyard to others.” (12:9)

The vineyard is not destroyed; its care is given to new tenants. Many interpreters see here the leadership of God’s people entrusted to the Twelve and to those who follow them. That is a warning to every Church leader in every age.

“‘The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (12:10)

Jesus quotes Psalm 118:22–23, sung at the great feasts. The parable ends not with the death of the son but with his vindication: a hint of the Resurrection.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, describing the images of the Church, says that the Church is God’s vineyard and cultivated field, and that the true vine is Christ (CCC 755); it adds that the Lord compared himself to the stone rejected by the builders which became the cornerstone (CCC 756). The Church reads this parable as addressed first to herself and her pastors. The Second Vatican Council teaches that what happened in Christ’s Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews of that time, nor against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4; see CCC 597).

Questions people ask

Does this parable mean that God rejected the Jewish people?

No. Mark says that the leaders understood the parable was told against them, and that they feared the crowd (12:12): the people are not the target. The vineyard itself is not condemned; it remains God’s. St Paul, a Jew who loved his people, insists that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). The Church teaches that the Jews are not to be presented as rejected or accursed by God (Nostra Aetate 4).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.” Where in my life or my family do I most need to believe this?
  • The owner sent servant after servant and finally his beloved son. What fruit is God asking of the “vineyard” entrusted to me: my family, my work, my parish?
