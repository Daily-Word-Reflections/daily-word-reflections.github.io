Word for the day

“But law came in, with the result that the trespass multiplied; but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more,” Romans 5:20

Reflection

In the fifth chapter of Romans Paul sets the whole of human history between two men. “Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death came through sin, and so death spread to all because all have sinned…” The one man is Adam. Through his disobedience sin entered our race like a poison entering the bloodstream, and with sin came death, the separation that God never willed. The Church reads in this passage the revealed basis of her teaching on original sin: we are born into a wounded humanity, deprived of the holiness in which Adam was created, and every personal sin adds to the wound. But Paul's purpose is not to frighten us with Adam; it is to dazzle us with Christ. Adam, he says, “is a type of the one who was to come.” He is like a rough sketch of the true Man. And the comparison is not equal. Again and again Paul writes “much more”: if many died through one man's trespass, “much more surely have the grace of God” and his gift “abounded for the many.” Then the great parallel: “Therefore just as one man’s trespass led to condemnation for all, so one man’s act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all. For just as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous.” Adam reached for the fruit of the tree to become like God; Christ, who is God, stretched out his arms on the tree of the Cross in obedience. Our own Mar Aprem, whom we remembered last week, loved to sing of the two trees, the tree of Eden and the tree of Golgotha, and of the Second Adam who opened again the gate of Paradise.

Then Paul writes one of the most consoling sentences in the Bible: “where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.” God's grace is not a small repair that barely covers the damage. It overflows. No sin of ours, no history of a family, no darkness of a nation is deeper than the grace of Christ. Paul will immediately warn that this is no excuse to go on sinning (Romans 6:1–2); grace that abounds is grace that changes us. But it means that no one in the parish, in the prison or in the hospital bed is beyond hope.

The Gospel tells the same story as a parable. Jesus speaks in the Temple, in the last days before his Passion, to the chief priests, the scribes and the elders who had questioned his authority (Mark 11:27). “A man planted a vineyard, put a fence around it, dug a pit for the wine press, and built a watchtower; then he leased it to tenants and went to another country.” His hearers knew Isaiah's song of the vineyard, in which the vineyard is God's people (Isaiah 5:1–7). The owner sends servant after servant to collect his share of the fruit; they are beaten, insulted, killed. The patience of the owner is almost foolish. “He had still one other, a beloved son. Finally he sent him to them, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’” The word “beloved” is the word the Father spoke over Jesus at the Jordan and on the mountain (Mark 1:11; 9:7). But the tenants reason: “This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.” Jesus is telling his own story, and he knows how it will end. Yet he ends with the psalm: “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord’s doing, and it is amazing in our eyes.” The greatest sin ever committed, the rejection and killing of the Son, became the cornerstone of our salvation. Here is Paul's “where sin increased, grace abounded” in the shape of a story. The parable was spoken to particular leaders at a particular hour, and Mark says that the crowd was on Jesus' side; the Church has always refused to turn it into a verdict on the Jewish people, then or now (Nostra Aetate 4). It is addressed to us, the tenants of the vineyard today. Our parish, our family, our own life are leased to us by the Owner, who still sends his messengers looking for fruit. As our Syro-Malankara brothers and sisters begin the Apostles' Fast today, the question is for all of us: what fruit will the Owner find?

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul tells the whole story of humanity in two men. Through Adam, sin and death came into the world. Through Jesus Christ, grace and life came. We all share Adam's wound; we can all share Christ's healing.

And the two are not equal. Paul says again and again, “much more.” Grace is much stronger than sin. “Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (Romans 5:20). There is no sin so great that the grace of Christ cannot overcome it.

In the Gospel Jesus tells a story about a vineyard. The owner sends servant after servant to collect the fruit, and the tenants beat them and kill them. At last he sends his beloved son, and they kill him too. Jesus is speaking about himself, the beloved Son of the Father, who will soon be put to death.

But God does not let the story end there. “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Mark 12:10). The worst thing human beings ever did, killing the Son of God, became the foundation of our salvation. That is what grace does: it turns even death into life.

Today we are the tenants of God's vineyard: our family, our parish, our own life. The Owner is looking for fruit. Let us give him the fruit of prayer, forgiveness and charity, and if we have sinned, let us run to his grace, which is always greater. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a man who sent his own dear son to his vineyard, and bad workers killed him. Jesus was talking about himself. But God made something wonderful out of it: Jesus rose again and saved us! God can bring good even out of bad things. And God's love is always bigger than our sins.

Prayer

Lord God, through one man's disobedience sin and death entered the world, and through the obedience of your Son, the Second Adam, grace and life have abounded for all. You planted your vineyard with love and sent your servants and at last your beloved Son, whom we rejected; yet you made the rejected stone the cornerstone of our salvation. Where sin has increased in our lives, let your grace abound all the more. Make us faithful tenants of your vineyard, giving you the fruit you seek. Bless our Syro-Malankara brothers and sisters in their fast. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
