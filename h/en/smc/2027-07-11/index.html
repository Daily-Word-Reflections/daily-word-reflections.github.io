Word for the day

“But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him.” Luke 15:20

Reflection

The first reading is part of Moses' last sermon, and it begins with a question that reaches across the whole history of the world: “For ask now about former ages, long before your own, ever since the day that God created human beings on the earth; ask from one end of heaven to the other: has anything so great as this ever happened or has its like ever been heard of?” Moses asks whether any people has ever heard the voice of a god speaking out of a fire and lived, and whether any god has ever gone to take a nation for himself “by trials, by signs and wonders, by war, by a mighty hand and an outstretched arm”, as the Lord did in Egypt. Then the reason is given in one sentence, which the calendar rightly calls the eternal love of God: “And because he loved your ancestors, he chose their descendants after them.” Israel was not chosen because it was great, but because it was loved. The commandments that follow, “for your own well-being and that of your descendants after you”, come after the love, as a father's rule comes after the father's embrace.

In Isaiah the prophet looks beyond a time of judgment: “On that day the branch of the Lord shall be beautiful and glorious.” The Church has always heard in the word Branch the promised king of David's line (compare Jeremiah 23:5), and in the survivors in Zion, “everyone who has been recorded for life in Jerusalem”, the holy people he gathers. God cleanses Jerusalem “by a spirit of judgment and by a spirit of burning”, and then, as in the Exodus, he creates over Mount Zion “a cloud by day and smoke and the shining of a flaming fire by night”. The last verse is a picture for every monsoon in Kerala: over all the glory there will be “a canopy. It will serve as a pavilion, a shade by day from the heat, and a refuge and a shelter from the storm and rain.” This is the Church in the season of Kaitha, which begins in the heat and the heavy rains of July: a tent that God himself spreads over his people. (The longer reading begins with the woes on the proud daughters of Zion, and the calendar's heading remembers only the promise.)

Paul's Epistle is about confidence. “Not that we are competent of ourselves to claim anything as coming from us; our competence is from God, who has made us competent to be ministers of a new covenant, not of letter but of spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” He compares the ministry of Moses, whose face shone with a glory that Israel could not bear to look at, with the ministry of the Spirit. Paul does not despise the first: it “came in glory”. He says that when a greater glory appears, the first looks pale beside it, as a lamp beside the sun. This is not a rejection of the Jewish people or their covenant; the Church teaches that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29), and Paul himself was a son of that covenant. His point is that what Jeremiah promised has come: the law written on hearts by the Holy Spirit (Jeremiah 31:33). And so, he says, “we act with great boldness”. The minister of this covenant does not shine by himself; he lets the Spirit give life.

Then comes the Gospel, the most famous of all parables, which the calendar calls the parable of the prodigal son, though it is better called the parable of the father. It is the third of three: a lost sheep, a lost coin, a lost son. St Luke tells us why Jesus told them: the Pharisees and scribes were grumbling, “This fellow welcomes sinners and eats with them.” The younger son asks for his share while his father is alive, which is as good as saying, I wish you were dead. He goes to a distant country, spends everything, and ends up feeding pigs, the lowest place a Jewish young man can imagine, wishing he could eat what the pigs eat. Then comes the turning: “when he came to himself.” He rehearses a speech: “I have sinned against heaven and before you.” But before he can say it, the father, who has been watching the road, sees him “while he was still far off”, is “filled with compassion”, runs (an old man does not run in an Eastern village), embraces and kisses him. The robe, the ring and the sandals are the signs of a son, not of a servant; the fatted calf is for a feast. “For this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found!”

The parable has a second half, and Jesus meant it for his critics. The elder son, who has stayed, comes in from the field, hears the music, and is angry and will not go in. And the father goes out again, as he went out to the younger, and pleads with him: “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But we had to celebrate and rejoice, because this brother of yours was dead and has come to life; he was lost and has been found.” The elder son says “this son of yours”; the father answers “this brother of yours”. The parable ends without telling us whether he went in. That is not an accident: the open door is held out to everyone who ever thought of himself as faithful and cannot bear the joy of mercy. Many Kerala families know both sons: one who went to a distant land and came home, and one who stayed and carried the house. Both are loved. The Church answers the father's call in the sacrament of Reconciliation, where the robe is given again, and in the Holy Qurbana, which is the feast of the fatted calf.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Moses asks Israel: has anything so great as this ever happened? And he gives the answer: God loved your ancestors, and he chose their children after them (Deuteronomy 4:37). Before there is any law, there is love. Before we do anything, we are loved. That is the first message of today.

The prophet Isaiah says that God will spread over his people a canopy, “a shade by day from the heat, and a refuge and a shelter from the storm and rain” (Isaiah 4:6). In these July days, when the rain is heavy in Kerala and the sun is hot between the clouds, we know what a good roof means. The Church is that roof, which God himself has put over us.

Saint Paul says that we have a new covenant, not of the letter but of the Spirit: “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life” (2 Corinthians 3:6). God does not want us only to obey rules. He wants to write his love on our hearts.

And the Gospel gives us the picture of that love, in the father who runs. The younger son asks for his share and goes away and loses everything. But he comes to himself and says, ‘I will go back to my father.’ And before he even finishes his speech, the father sees him far off, is filled with compassion, runs, embraces and kisses him. He gives him the best robe, a ring and sandals and holds a feast. “This son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found!” (Luke 15:24).

But there is also the elder son, who is angry. He has been working faithfully, and he cannot bear the party. The father goes out to him as well: “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours” (Luke 15:31). We do not know if he went in. That is the question left for us.

Whether we are the younger or the elder, the Father is waiting at the gate. This week, if you have been far away, go back, and go to confession. If you have stayed, go out and welcome your brother and sister. Let the lamp be lit for those who have not yet come home. Amen.

For children

Children, in today's story a father had two sons. One went away and lost everything, but when he came back the father ran to hug him! God is like that father. If you do something wrong, do not hide. Say sorry and go to him, and he will run to meet you. And if your brother or sister is forgiven, be happy for him or her too, as the father wanted the elder son to be. Let us all come to the party!

Prayer

Father of mercies, who chose your people because you loved them, and who watch the road for each of your children, we thank you for your Son, who has told us what you are like. Bring home all who are far away, and give us the courage to say, ‘Father, I have sinned.’ Clothe us again with the robe of your Spirit, put on our hand the ring of your covenant, and seat us at the table of your Son. Teach those of us who have stayed at home to share your joy, and to welcome our brothers and sisters without grudging. Spread over your Church the canopy of your protection against the heat and the storm, and write your law on our hearts. We ask this through Jesus Christ, your Son, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The four readings of this Sunday tell of a God who first loves, then speaks, and who gives shelter and a place at the table. In Deuteronomy, Moses asks whether any people has ever been loved and chosen like Israel, and gives the reason in one sentence: “because he loved your ancestors”. In Isaiah, after judgment and cleansing, a cloud by day and a flaming fire by night become a canopy and a shelter over Zion. In Second Corinthians, Paul says that the ministry of the Spirit, the new covenant, brings a glory greater than the glory on Moses’ face, and he speaks boldly because of that hope. And in Luke, the parable of the prodigal son shows the Father running down the road to meet a child who has been lost. (The calendar gives the longer forms, Isaiah 3:16–4:6, 2 Corinthians 3:4–18 and Luke 15:4–32; the notes below follow the verses printed for the day.) In Kaitha, when the Church looks at the fruit of the apostles’ sowing, the Sunday asks what fruit the Father most wants: that his children come home.

Deuteronomy 4:32–40 · Because he loved your ancestors

Setting.

Deuteronomy is Moses’ great farewell address to Israel on the plains of Moab, before the people cross the Jordan. In the first discourse (1:6–4:40) Moses recalls what God has done and warns Israel not to forget it. At Horeb the people heard God’s voice out of the fire but saw no form (4:12, 15). That is why they must not make an image of God.

Verses 32–40 are the climax of this first discourse, and they lead straight into the Ten Commandments (chapter 5) and the Shema (6:4–5). The people are asked to look back across all history and to look at the whole earth, and to ask whether anything like this has ever happened.

Structure.

  • vv. 32–34: two questions about the voice from the fire and the rescue from Egypt: nothing like it has ever been heard.
  • vv. 35–36: the purpose: that Israel may know that the Lord is God, and there is no other.
  • vv. 37–38: the reason: God loved the ancestors and chose their descendants, bringing them out by his presence.
  • vv. 39–40: the response: know today and take it to heart; keep the commandments, for your good.

Key words.

  • ’āhab, he loved: The key word of verse 37. Israel’s election is rooted in love, not merit; Moses says the same in 7:7–8 and 9:4–6, where Israel is told that it is not chosen for its own righteousness.
  • bāḥar, he chose: God’s choosing is not a choice against others but a choice for the sake of all. Israel is chosen to be a people through whom the knowledge of God reaches the world.
  • wahăšēbōtā ’el-lĕbābekā, take it to heart (literally, return it to your heart): In Hebrew thinking the heart is the place of memory and decision. To “take to heart” is to let a truth shape one’s life.

Verse by verse.

“For ask now about former ages, long before your own, ever since the day that God created human beings on the earth;” (4:32)

Moses invites the people to test his claim against all of history. He is not afraid of the question, because the love of God is a fact of experience.

“To you it was shown so that you would acknowledge that the Lord is God; there is no other besides him.” (4:35)

God’s great deeds are not for display but for knowledge. In the Bible, to know God is to trust and to love him.

“And because he loved your ancestors, he chose their descendants after them.” (4:37)

The heart of the reading. The covenant begins not with Israel’s goodness but with God’s love, as a father’s love begins before a child can do anything.

“So acknowledge today and take to heart that the Lord is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath; there is no other.” (4:39)

“Today” is an important word in Deuteronomy. Every generation stands at Horeb afresh and is asked to say yes.

“Keep his statutes and his commandments, which I am commanding you today for your own well-being and that of your descendants after you,” (4:40)

The commandments are given for good, not as a burden. They follow love as the rules of the house follow the father’s embrace.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Deuteronomy’s words about the one God as the root of her own faith, and the Catechism begins its teaching on God with the Shema and the confession that the Lord is one (CCC 200–202). It teaches that God chose Israel out of love and that his covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (CCC 218, 839; Romans 11:29). The Catechism says that the Ten Commandments are given as a gift of the God who first saved and then gave the law (CCC 2060–2061): the order of Moses’ words, love first and commandments after, is the order of the whole Gospel.

Isaiah 4:2–6 · A canopy over all the glory

Setting.

The verses end a long section of warning in Isaiah 2–4. The prophet has spoken of the pride of Jerusalem and its wealthy women (3:16–4:1), and of a coming judgment that will leave only a remnant. Then comes a sudden turn: “On that day” the Lord will make something new and beautiful grow.

Verses 5–6 recall the Exodus. In the wilderness the Lord led the people by a pillar of cloud by day and fire by night (Exodus 13:21–22) and filled the tent of meeting with his glory (Exodus 40:34–38). Isaiah promises the same presence, now over the whole of Mount Zion.

Structure.

  • v. 2: the branch of the Lord, and the fruit of the land, beautiful and glorious for the survivors.
  • v. 3: those who remain are called holy, written down for life.
  • v. 4: the Lord washes away the filth and the bloodstains by a spirit of judgment and burning.
  • vv. 5–6: cloud by day, fire by night, and a canopy that is shade, refuge and shelter.

Key words.

  • ṣemaḥ, branch, shoot: A plant springing up. Later prophets use the word for the promised king from David’s line (Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 3:8). Here it may mean the fruitfulness of the land; the Church has also heard in it a word about the Messiah.
  • kol-hakkātûb laḥayyîm, recorded for life: A register of the living, like the “book” of Exodus 32:32 and Daniel 12:1. Those who remain are held in God’s memory, each by name.
  • ḥuppâ, canopy: The word used elsewhere for a bridal canopy (Joel 2:16; Psalm 19:5). God’s glory over Zion is like the canopy of a wedding: shelter, joy and belonging.

Verse by verse.

“On that day the branch of the Lord shall be beautiful and glorious,” (4:2)

After judgment, growth. God does not end his story with ruin.

“Whoever is left in Zion and remains in Jerusalem will be called holy, everyone who has been recorded for life in Jerusalem,” (4:3)

Holiness here is a gift from God, given by being cleansed and counted. It is not first of all an achievement.

“Then the Lord will create over the whole site of Mount Zion and over its places of assembly a cloud by day and smoke and the shining of a flaming fire by night. Indeed over all the glory there will be a canopy.” (4:5)

The old presence returns, and “creates”: a verb used elsewhere of God’s creating. The assembly itself, the people gathered to worship, is covered by God’s glory.

“It will serve as a pavilion, a shade by day from the heat, and a refuge and a shelter from the storm and rain.” (4:6)

A beautiful end: the glory of God is not terrifying here but sheltering. It protects from sun by day and from storm by night.

How the Church reads it.

In the Christian tradition the cloud and fire are signs of the Holy Spirit: the Catechism speaks of the cloud and light as symbols by which God reveals himself and veils himself (CCC 697), and of fire as the symbol of the transforming energy of the Spirit (CCC 696). The Church sees the promise of shelter fulfilled in the Church, where God’s presence rests over those who gather in his name. At Pentecost the Spirit came as fire on those who were gathered (Acts 2:3).

2 Corinthians 3:4–12 · Ministers of a new covenant

Setting.

Paul is defending his ministry against those who say he needs letters of recommendation or a stronger claim. His answer is the Corinthians themselves, “a letter of Christ” written “not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts” (3:2–3).

In 3:7–18 he compares his ministry with that of Moses, whose face shone when he came down from Sinai (Exodus 34:29–35) so that he covered it with a veil. Paul does not criticise Moses or the Law; he says that the covenant of Sinai was truly glorious. His point is that the new covenant is more glorious still, as Jeremiah and Ezekiel had promised (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–27). The Church has always read this as a fulfilment, not a rejection, of what God gave to Israel.

Structure.

  • vv. 4–6: our competence is from God, who made us ministers of a new covenant, of Spirit, not letter.
  • vv. 7–9: if the ministry of the stone tablets came with glory, the ministry of the Spirit and of justification has much more.
  • vv. 10–11: the earlier glory is surpassed by the greater, as the temporary by the lasting.
  • v. 12: because of such hope, great boldness.

Key words.

  • gramma / pneuma, letter / spirit: “Letter” is the written code taken by itself, without the Spirit’s power to keep it. It is not the Law that is bad (Romans 7:12) but the situation of a law that tells what is right and cannot give strength to do it.
  • doxa, glory: The weight and radiance of God’s presence. Paul reasons from the lesser to the greater: if the earlier covenant had glory, the new has far more.
  • parrēsia, boldness, openness: Plain speech and confidence before others and before God. Paul speaks plainly because the hope he proclaims has no veil.

Verse by verse.

“Not that we are competent of ourselves to claim anything as coming from us; our competence is from God,” (3:5)

The apostle’s confidence is not in himself. Whatever a minister of the Church does well is the work of God.

“who has made us competent to be ministers of a new covenant, not of letter but of spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.” (3:6)

Paul is not setting the Old Testament against the New. He says that the law without grace shows sin and cannot heal it, and that the Spirit is the gift that makes obedience possible.

“For if there was glory in the ministry of condemnation, much more does the ministry of justification abound in glory!” (3:9)

Paul grants that the earlier ministry was glorious. The new does not deny that glory; it is its fulfilment.

“Since, then, we have such a hope, we act with great boldness,” (3:12)

Christian boldness is the child of hope. We can speak plainly and live openly because God’s work is not hidden or passing.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Old Law is a preparation for the Gospel, and that the New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit given through faith in Christ and written on the heart (CCC 1964–1966). St Augustine, in his work The Spirit and the Letter, reads Paul’s words to mean that the law, without the help of grace, shows what we should do but cannot give the strength to do it; the Spirit pours love into the heart and so fulfils the law (in substance). The Church also holds, with Paul, that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29; CCC 121, 839).

Luke 15:11–32 · His father saw him and ran

Setting.

Luke 15 opens with a complaint: the Pharisees and the scribes grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them (15:1–2). Jesus answers with three parables: the lost sheep, the lost coin and the two sons. In each, something is lost, then found, and there is joy. The calendar gives the longer form, from 15:4, so that the three belong together.

In the Jewish world of Jesus’ day, asking for one’s inheritance while the father was alive was a grave breach of the family. To keep pigs, animals unclean for Jews, and to long to eat their food showed the lowest point of a Jewish son’s fall. Everything in the parable is meant to make the father’s welcome as surprising as possible.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–16: the younger son asks for his share, leaves, spends everything and feeds pigs.
  • vv. 17–19: he comes to himself and decides to return as a hired hand.
  • vv. 20–24: the father sees him far off, runs, embraces him, orders robe, ring, sandals and a feast.
  • vv. 25–30: the elder son comes in from the field, hears the music, is angry and refuses to go in.
  • vv. 31–32: the father’s plea: “you are always with me”; “we had to celebrate”.

Key words.

  • eis heauton elthōn, coming to himself: Literally “having come into himself”. Repentance begins when a person wakes from the dream of independence and sees the truth about himself.
  • esplanchnisthē, he was filled with compassion: The verb comes from splanchna, the inner organs, the gut. It is the deepest word of mercy in the Gospels, used of Jesus himself (Luke 7:13).
  • anezēsen, he has come to life: The same verb used of rising from the dead. The father says that his son was “dead” and “has come to life”, which is more than a return home.

Verse by verse.

“The younger of them said to his father, ‘Father, give me the share of the property that will belong to me.’ So he divided his property between them.” (15:12)

The father lets the child go. Love does not hold a person by force. The same freedom is what allows return.

“But when he came to himself he said, ‘How many of my father’s hired hands have bread enough and to spare, but here I am dying of hunger!” (15:17)

His first motive is hunger, not perfect love. God works with the repentance we have and makes it grow (see CCC 1453).

“But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him.” (15:20)

The father has been watching the road. An elder in that culture did not run. He runs, to reach his son before the village can shame him, and he kisses him before the speech is made.

“Then he became angry and refused to go in. His father came out and began to plead with him.” (15:28)

The father goes out a second time. He runs to the one who left, and he goes out to the one who stayed. His love is the same for both.

“Then the father said to him, ‘Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours.” (15:31)

The elder son has all he wanted, and has not known it. The parable ends without telling us if he comes in. The door is left open for the listener.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to this parable as the great description of conversion and repentance: the son’s misery, his return, and the father’s embrace, a picture of the “gift of reconciliation” in the sacrament of Penance (CCC 1439). St John Paul II, in his encyclical Dives in Misericordia, reflects at length on the parable as a revelation of mercy as love that restores dignity (nn. 5–6). The Church today reads the two sons as pictures of two temptations within every disciple: to leave the Father’s house, and to stay in it without joy. A number of early writers read the elder son as the Jewish people and the younger as the Gentiles; the Church does not lay the elder son’s fault on any people, and Paul says that God’s gifts to Israel are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). The father’s words, “all that is mine is yours”, are spoken to every disciple.

Questions people ask

Deuteronomy speaks of driving out nations to give Israel their land. How does the Church read such words?

With care. Moses himself says that Israel did not receive the land for its righteousness (Deuteronomy 9:4–6), and the same book calls for justice to the stranger (10:18–19). The Church teaches that the Old Testament has “imperfect and provisional” elements, and that all of Scripture is read in the light of Christ, who forbids violence and teaches love of enemies (Dei Verbum 15; CCC 122, 129). These verses can never justify force against any people today.

Is “the letter kills” a rejection of the Old Testament or of Jewish law?

No. Paul calls the law “holy and just and good” (Romans 7:12) and says here that it came with glory. “Letter” means the law without the grace to keep it, and the “Spirit” means the new gift of life. The Church holds that the Old Testament remains the Word of God and that God’s covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (CCC 121, 839).

Why does Jesus leave the end of the parable open?

The parable is addressed to those who grumbled at Jesus for welcoming sinners (15:1–2). The ending asks them, and each of us, what we will do: go in to the feast or stay outside. The father’s plea stands. The Gospel does not say what happened because it is asking the listener to answer.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Kaitha, the Syro-Malabar season in which the Church looks at the fruit of the apostles’ sowing, is also a season of watchfulness. The Father in today’s Gospel, who watches the road, is a good picture of the God for whom we watch. In every Holy Qurbana of the Anaphora of Addai and Mari the Church asks for the pardon of debts and the forgiveness of sins (in substance), and then gathers at the Lord’s table; like the younger son, we come home not as hired hands but as children.

The elder son’s question is one for every parish: do we welcome the one who has come back, with the joy of the Father, or do we stand outside? A family or prayer group might ask: is there someone in our parish or family who needs us to “go out” and plead with them to come in?

For study and teaching

  • Moses says: “because he loved your ancestors, he chose their descendants after them.” How have I experienced God’s love before I could do anything to earn it?
  • Isaiah promises a canopy of shelter from heat and storm. What is the storm in my life now, and how can I sit under the Lord’s shelter in prayer this week?
  • Paul says that the Spirit gives life where the letter alone cannot. Where in my life do I know what is right but need the strength of the Spirit to do it?
  • In the parable, is it easier for me to see myself as the younger son or the elder? What would it look like to go to the Father’s feast today?
