Word for the day

“But as for what was sown on good soil, this is the one who hears the word and understands it, who indeed bears fruit.” Matthew 13:23

Reflection

Paul thanks God that the Gospel “is bearing fruit and growing in the whole world,” and prays that the Colossians may “lead lives worthy of the Lord… bearing fruit in every good work.” The Church is God's field.

Jesus explains the sower: the same seed falls on the path, rocky ground, thorns and good soil. The difference lies in the hearing. “Blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear.” Good soil is a heart that hears, understands and keeps the word, and so bears fruit thirty, sixty and a hundredfold.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul thanks God that the Gospel is bearing fruit and growing in the whole world, and prays that the Colossians may lead lives worthy of the Lord, bearing fruit in every good work (Colossians 1:6, 10). The Church is God's field.

Jesus explains the parable of the sower: the same seed falls on the path, on rocky ground, among thorns and on good soil. The one who hears the word and understands it bears fruit, a hundredfold, sixtyfold, thirtyfold (Matthew 13:18–23). The difference is not in the seed but in the soil.

What kind of soil is my heart today? A path trodden hard by hurry, shallow rock, or thorns of worry? Let us pull out one thorn this week: one hour less on the phone, one hour more with the Word. Amen.

For children

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Fruit is the word of the day. Paul thanks God that the Gospel is “bearing fruit and growing” among the Colossians and prays that they may “bear fruit in every good work”. In the Gospel Jesus explains why he speaks in parables and interprets the parable of the sower: the seed is the same, the soil makes the difference. The seed bears fruit in the one who hears the word and understands it.

Colossians 1:3–11 · Bearing fruit and growing

Setting.

Colossae was a small town in the Lycus valley in Asia Minor (modern Turkey). Paul had not founded this Church himself; it was founded by Epaphras, his co-worker (1:7). Paul writes from prison, to strengthen the Colossians against teachings that were drawing them away from Christ's supremacy. The letter begins, as usual, with thanksgiving and prayer.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–8: thanksgiving for their faith, love and hope, and for the Gospel bearing fruit since Epaphras taught them.
  • vv. 9–11: Paul's prayer: knowledge of God's will, a worthy life, fruit in good works, strength and patience.

Key words.

  • pistis, agapē, elpis, faith, love, hope (vv. 4–5): The three theological virtues appear together, as in 1 Corinthians 13:13 and 1 Thessalonians 1:3. Here hope is “laid up for you in heaven”, like a treasure kept safe.
  • karpophoroumenon kai auxanomenon, bearing fruit and growing (vv. 6, 10): These words recall God's first blessing in Genesis 1:28, “Be fruitful and multiply”. The Gospel brings about a new creation.
  • epignōsis, knowledge (vv. 9–10): Not mere information but deep, personal knowledge of God, which shows itself in a way of life.

Verse by verse.

“Just as it is bearing fruit and growing in the whole world, so it has been bearing fruit among yourselves from the day you heard it and truly comprehended the grace of God.” (1:6)
“so that you may lead lives worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing to him, as you bear fruit in every good work and as you grow in the knowledge of God.” (1:10)

Knowledge and good works grow together. Knowing God leads to good works, and doing good leads to knowing God better.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith, hope and charity are the foundation of Christian moral life, infused by God to make us capable of acting as his children (CCC 1812–1813). Paul's prayer for the Colossians is a model of intercession: he thanks God first, then asks for wisdom and perseverance for others (CCC 2634–2638, in substance).

Matthew 13:10–23 · The seed on good soil

Setting.

Matthew 13 is the third of the five great discourses in Matthew's Gospel, a chapter of seven parables of the Kingdom. Jesus has just told the parable of the sower to the crowds from a boat (13:1–9). Now the disciples ask why he teaches this way. Opposition to Jesus has grown (ch. 11–12), and the parables both reveal and conceal: they open the Kingdom to those who are willing to come close and ask.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–17: why parables: to you it has been given to know; the prophecy of Isaiah 6:9–10; blessed are your eyes.
  • vv. 18–23: the explanation of the sower: the path, the rocky ground, the thorns, the good soil.

Key words.

  • mystēria, secrets (v. 11): The hidden plan of God now revealed in Jesus. It is “given”: understanding the Kingdom is a gift.
  • syniēmi, understand (vv. 13, 19, 23): A key word in Matthew 13. The good soil is the one who “hears the word and understands it”. Understanding in the Bible involves the heart and leads to obedience.
  • skandalizetai, falls away (v. 21): Literally “stumbles”. Trouble and persecution become a stumbling block for one without roots.

Verse by verse.

“But blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear.” (13:16)

The disciples are not cleverer than others; they are blessed because they stay with Jesus and ask him.

“But as for what was sown on good soil, this is the one who hears the word and understands it, who indeed bears fruit and yields, in one case a hundredfold, in another sixty, and in another thirty.” (13:23)

Even the good soil gives different harvests. God does not ask the same yield from everyone, but he asks for fruit.

How the Church reads it.

A note on the text. Mark says Jesus speaks in parables “in order that” they may look but not perceive (Mark 4:12); Matthew says “because” they do not perceive (13:13). Matthew stresses the people's own closed hearts. The Catechism says that the parables are like mirrors: will a person be hard soil or good earth? Through them Jesus invites people to the feast of the Kingdom, but he also asks for a radical choice (CCC 546).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus speak in parables to keep people out?

No. Parables call for a response. Those who come close and ask, like the disciples, receive the explanation. The closed heart, not God's wish, keeps a person outside (Matthew 13:15; 1 Timothy 2:4).
