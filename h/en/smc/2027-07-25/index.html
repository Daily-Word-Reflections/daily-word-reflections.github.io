Word for the day

“You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition.” Mark 7:8

Reflection

The Fourth Sunday of Kaitha puts side by side two things that people often think are rivals: the commandment of God and the traditions of men. The Gospel opens with a scene any Kerala household would know. The Pharisees and some scribes who had come from Jerusalem notice that the disciples eat without first washing their hands. Washing before meals, and washing cups, pots and bronze kettles, was a devout custom of pious Jews, and in itself a good one; Jesus himself does not condemn the custom. What he condemns is the use of such a custom to set aside what God has plainly said. Their question, “Why do your disciples not live according to the tradition of the elders?”, is answered with a sentence from Isaiah, who prophesied that this people “honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me.” We should hear this as a debate among the people of God, in which Jesus speaks as a Jewish teacher to other Jewish teachers, and as a warning to every community that has traditions, including ours.

Then Jesus gives a precise example, and it is about parents. “Moses said, ‘Honor your father and your mother.’” That is the fourth commandment of the first reading: “Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you in the land.” Some, however, had arranged that whatever support a son might have given his parents could be declared “Corban,” dedicated to God, and so withheld from the father and mother who needed it. It looked pious, and it killed a commandment. The lesson is plain: no offering, no donation, no ornament on the church and no vow can replace the daily care of an old father or mother. “Then you no longer permit doing anything for a father or mother, thus making void the word of God through your tradition.” On this Sunday, when many parishes and families remember grandparents and the elderly, the Gospel asks a very practical question: who is sitting alone in the back room of our house?

The first reading takes us to Horeb, where Moses recalls the ten words and the fire. “These words the Lord spoke with a loud voice to your whole assembly at the mountain, out of the fire, the cloud, and the thick darkness, and he added no more. He wrote them on two stone tablets.” The people were afraid, and said: “Today we have seen that God may speak to someone and the person may still live.” God's law is not a burden placed on us to catch us out. It is the voice of the living God out of the fire, spoken so that people may live. The commandment about parents stands at the head of the second table because the family is where every child first learns to hear a voice that asks for love and gives life in return.

Isaiah shows what happens when a people hears no voice at all. “The people did not turn to him who struck them, or seek the Lord of hosts.” The leaders led them astray, and the prophets who taught lies were the tail; the orphans and widows found no compassion; wickedness burned like a fire; “Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh.” Brother devours brother, and the fire of the sin of a people finally burns the people. It is a hard reading, and it is not a prophecy about other people only. Wherever a family fights over land and inheritance, wherever relatives stop speaking for years, we are in the world of Manasseh and Ephraim. The Lord's hand, says the prophet, “is stretched out still”: stretched out in judgment, and, as the Church reads it, still stretched out to call us back.

Paul's short passage in the second reading supplies the remedy: measure yourself by the Lord and not by one another. The Corinthians' rivals commended themselves and compared themselves with each other, which, says Paul, “does not show good sense.” He will boast only within the field that God has assigned to him, and he will not boast in the labours of others. The whole passage closes with a sentence from Jeremiah that he also uses in his first letter: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.” For the Church in Kerala the sentence is a good guard against the pride of old families, rich parishes, big feasts and fine buildings. “For it is not those who commend themselves that are approved, but those whom the Lord commends.” The Lord commends the one who honours a father and a mother, who keeps a clean heart, and who listens when God speaks from the fire.

Homily outline

Theme: “You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition.”

On the Fourth Sunday of Kaitha: the voice of God from the fire, Corban and the care of parents, and boasting only in the Lord.

1. The voice of God out of the fire

  • The Ten Words are spoken for life.
  • The fourth commandment opens the table of love.
  • Today we have seen that God may speak and a person may live.
“Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you in the land that the Lord your God is giving you.” Deuteronomy 5:16
“for indeed our God is a consuming fire.” Hebrews 12:29

Apply: Honour a parent or elder this week with one concrete act: a visit, a call, a meal together.

2. Corban and the commandment

  • Heart far from God, lips near.
  • A gift cannot replace the care of a father or mother.
  • Tradition must serve the word of God.
“The Lord said: Because these people draw near with their mouths and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me, and their worship of me is a human commandment learned by rote;” Isaiah 29:13
“You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition.” Mark 7:8

Apply: Ask whether any gift or custom of yours is a way of escaping a plain duty at home.

3. The fire that devours, and the one who boasts

  • Manasseh devoured Ephraim.
  • Compare yourself with the Lord, not with others.
  • The Lord commends the humble.
“Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh, and together they were against Judah. For all this his anger has not turned away; his hand is stretched out still.” Isaiah 9:21
““Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.”” 2 Corinthians 10:17

Apply: Make one move this week toward peace with a relative with whom you are not speaking.

God's commandments are his own voice, spoken for life, and they begin with honouring those who gave us life.

Let no tradition, gift or pride make the word of God void in us: let us boast only in the Lord.

Take-home resolution: This week, honour a parent or an elder in a concrete way, seek peace in the family, and keep the heart clean before the Lord.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Gospel brings us to the dinner table. Some Pharisees and scribes notice that the disciples of Jesus eat without washing their hands first. For them it was a matter of tradition, and a good tradition. But Jesus uses the occasion to teach something deeper.

He quotes Isaiah: “This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me” (Mark 7:6). It is possible to say all the prayers, to keep all the customs, and still have a heart that is far from God. And then Jesus says, “You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition” (Mark 7:8).

He gives an example. God said: “Honor your father and your mother.” But some people said, ‘What I would have given to my parents is Corban, it is dedicated to God,’ and so they gave nothing to their old parents. They looked pious, but they made void the word of God (Mark 7:10–13).

Brothers and sisters, this is very close to our life. A donation to the church is a good thing. A big feast is a good thing. But if our old father or mother sits alone in the back room, if no one has time to talk to them, then something is wrong. Today is the day we remember grandparents and the elderly. The first reading reminds us: “Honor your father and your mother… so that it may go well with you” (Deuteronomy 5:16).

Isaiah shows us what happens when people forget God and each other: “Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh” (Isaiah 9:21). Brother turns against brother. Let us ask where in our families there are such quarrels, and let us be the first to make peace.

And Saint Paul tells us how to live: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord” (2 Corinthians 10:17). Not in our family name, our money or our good works, but in the Lord. This week, let us honour our parents, keep our hearts clean, and boast only in him. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: honour your father and your mother. That means your grandparents too! Some grown-ups said they were too busy because they were giving to God, and Jesus said that was wrong. This week, give your grandparents a hug, tell them a story, or draw them a picture. That is a way of loving God, too!

Prayer

Lord God, you spoke to your people out of the fire and gave them your commandments that they might live. Teach us to honour our fathers and mothers, to care for them in their old age and to respect the elders of our parish and of every family. Keep our worship from becoming a habit of the lips while the heart is far from you. Heal every family in which brother is set against brother, and make us boast in you alone. Through the prayers of Sts Joachim and Anne, grandparents of your Son, bless the old people of our homes. Amen.
