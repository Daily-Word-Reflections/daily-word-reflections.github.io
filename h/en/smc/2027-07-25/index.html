Readings

  • Second Reading Isaiah 9:13–21 · A people who did not turn to the one who struck them

  • Gospel Mark 7:1–13 · Do not make void the word of God through your tradition

Word for the day

“You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition.” Mark 7:8

Reflection

The Fourth Sunday of Kaitha puts side by side two things that people often think are rivals: the commandment of God and the traditions of men. The Gospel opens with a scene any Kerala household would know. The Pharisees and some scribes who had come from Jerusalem notice that the disciples eat without first washing their hands. Washing before meals, and washing cups, pots and bronze kettles, was a devout custom of pious Jews, and in itself a good one; Jesus himself does not condemn the custom. What he condemns is the use of such a custom to set aside what God has plainly said. Their question, “Why do your disciples not live according to the tradition of the elders?”, is answered with a sentence from Isaiah, who prophesied that this people “honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me.” We should hear this as a debate among the people of God, in which Jesus speaks as a Jewish teacher to other Jewish teachers, and as a warning to every community that has traditions, including ours.

Then Jesus gives a precise example, and it is about parents. “Moses said, ‘Honor your father and your mother.’” That is the fourth commandment of the first reading: “Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you in the land.” Some, however, had arranged that whatever support a son might have given his parents could be declared “Corban,” dedicated to God, and so withheld from the father and mother who needed it. It looked pious, and it killed a commandment. The lesson is plain: no offering, no donation, no ornament on the church and no vow can replace the daily care of an old father or mother. “Then you no longer permit doing anything for a father or mother, thus making void the word of God through your tradition.” On this Sunday, when many parishes and families remember grandparents and the elderly, the Gospel asks a very practical question: who is sitting alone in the back room of our house?

The first reading takes us to Horeb, where Moses recalls the ten words and the fire. “These words the Lord spoke with a loud voice to your whole assembly at the mountain, out of the fire, the cloud, and the thick darkness, and he added no more. He wrote them on two stone tablets.” The people were afraid, and said: “Today we have seen that God may speak to someone and the person may still live.” God's law is not a burden placed on us to catch us out. It is the voice of the living God out of the fire, spoken so that people may live. The commandment about parents stands at the head of the second table because the family is where every child first learns to hear a voice that asks for love and gives life in return.

Isaiah shows what happens when a people hears no voice at all. “The people did not turn to him who struck them, or seek the Lord of hosts.” The leaders led them astray, and the prophets who taught lies were the tail; the orphans and widows found no compassion; wickedness burned like a fire; “Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh.” Brother devours brother, and the fire of the sin of a people finally burns the people. It is a hard reading, and it is not a prophecy about other people only. Wherever a family fights over land and inheritance, wherever relatives stop speaking for years, we are in the world of Manasseh and Ephraim. The Lord's hand, says the prophet, “is stretched out still”: stretched out in judgment, and, as the Church reads it, still stretched out to call us back.

Paul's short passage in the second reading supplies the remedy: measure yourself by the Lord and not by one another. The Corinthians' rivals commended themselves and compared themselves with each other, which, says Paul, “does not show good sense.” He will boast only within the field that God has assigned to him, and he will not boast in the labours of others. The whole passage closes with a sentence from Jeremiah that he also uses in his first letter: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.” For the Church in Kerala the sentence is a good guard against the pride of old families, rich parishes, big feasts and fine buildings. “For it is not those who commend themselves that are approved, but those whom the Lord commends.” The Lord commends the one who honours a father and a mother, who keeps a clean heart, and who listens when God speaks from the fire.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Gospel brings us to the dinner table. Some Pharisees and scribes notice that the disciples of Jesus eat without washing their hands first. For them it was a matter of tradition, and a good tradition. But Jesus uses the occasion to teach something deeper.

He quotes Isaiah: “This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me” (Mark 7:6). It is possible to say all the prayers, to keep all the customs, and still have a heart that is far from God. And then Jesus says, “You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition” (Mark 7:8).

He gives an example. God said: “Honor your father and your mother.” But some people said, ‘What I would have given to my parents is Corban, it is dedicated to God,’ and so they gave nothing to their old parents. They looked pious, but they made void the word of God (Mark 7:10–13).

Brothers and sisters, this is very close to our life. A donation to the church is a good thing. A big feast is a good thing. But if our old father or mother sits alone in the back room, if no one has time to talk to them, then something is wrong. Today is the day we remember grandparents and the elderly. The first reading reminds us: “Honor your father and your mother… so that it may go well with you” (Deuteronomy 5:16).

Isaiah shows us what happens when people forget God and each other: “Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh” (Isaiah 9:21). Brother turns against brother. Let us ask where in our families there are such quarrels, and let us be the first to make peace.

And Saint Paul tells us how to live: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord” (2 Corinthians 10:17). Not in our family name, our money or our good works, but in the Lord. This week, let us honour our parents, keep our hearts clean, and boast only in him. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: honour your father and your mother. That means your grandparents too! Some grown-ups said they were too busy because they were giving to God, and Jesus said that was wrong. This week, give your grandparents a hug, tell them a story, or draw them a picture. That is a way of loving God, too!

Prayer

Lord God, you spoke to your people out of the fire and gave them your commandments that they might live. Teach us to honour our fathers and mothers, to care for them in their old age and to respect the elders of our parish and of every family. Keep our worship from becoming a habit of the lips while the heart is far from you. Heal every family in which brother is set against brother, and make us boast in you alone. Through the prayers of Sts Joachim and Anne, grandparents of your Son, bless the old people of our homes. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

All four readings ask what really honours God: words and customs, or a heart that listens. In Deuteronomy, Moses recalls the day at Horeb when God spoke the commandments out of the fire, among them the command to honour father and mother. In Isaiah, a people struck by God’s correction will not turn back to him, and the whole society consumes itself. Paul, writing to the Corinthians, refuses to boast of what he has not done and gives the rule for every Christian: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.” In Mark, Jesus is asked why his disciples do not follow the tradition of the elders about washing, and he answers with a question of his own: how can a custom be used to set aside a commandment of God, above all the commandment to honour one’s parents? The lesson is not that tradition is bad, but that nothing, however venerable, may take the place of God’s word and of love for the neighbour closest to us.

Deuteronomy 5:16–24 · The commandments spoken out of the fire

Setting.

Deuteronomy presents itself as the farewell addresses of Moses on the plains of Moab, before Israel crosses the Jordan. In chapter 5 he gathers the people and recalls what happened at Horeb, the name Deuteronomy uses for Sinai: God himself spoke the ten words to the whole assembly. Today’s reading begins in the middle of the Decalogue, with the fourth commandment and the commandments about the neighbour, and goes on to the people’s fear at the voice of God.

This version of the Decalogue differs a little from the one in Exodus 20. The fourth commandment adds “as the Lord your God commanded you” and “that it may go well with you”, and the commandment on coveting uses a different word for the neighbour’s house than for his wife. Deuteronomy is preaching the law to a new generation: the words are not only history but a living address to “you”.

Structure.

  • v. 16: honour father and mother, with the promise of long life and good in the land.
  • vv. 17–21: the commandments about the neighbour: do not murder, commit adultery, steal, bear false witness, or covet.
  • v. 22: the Lord spoke these words from the fire, cloud and darkness, added no more, and wrote them on two tablets.
  • vv. 23–24: the leaders of the tribes come to Moses, astonished that they have heard God’s voice and still live.

Key words.

  • kabbēd, honour: From a root meaning “to be heavy”. To honour is to give weight to someone, to treat them as important. The word is also used of honouring God (Proverbs 3:9), so the fourth commandment stands on the border between our duty to God and our duty to people.
  • ’ăśeret haddĕbārîm, the ten words: The Hebrew name for the Decalogue (Deuteronomy 4:13; 10:4). They are “words” before they are “laws”: a voice that addresses a people with whom God has made a covenant.
  • kābôd, glory: In verse 24 the people say that God has shown them his “glory and greatness”. Glory is the weighty, radiant presence of God. The same root as “honour” appears here: God is honoured because he is truly weighty.

Verse by verse.

“Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you in the land that the Lord your God is giving you.” (5:16)

The commandment is the only one of the ten that carries a promise. Families that hand on respect and care from one generation to the next are the roots of a lasting society.

“These words the Lord spoke with a loud voice to your whole assembly at the mountain, out of the fire, the cloud, and the thick darkness, and he added no more.” (5:22)

“And he added no more” means that these ten words stand apart as the heart of the covenant. All other laws are explanations of them.

“He wrote them on two stone tablets, and gave them to me.” (5:22)

The words are written down so that they may be kept, taught and passed on. Written Scripture begins here with God’s own hand.

“Today we have seen that God may speak to someone and the person may still live.” (5:24)

Israel had feared that to hear God would be fatal. The surprise is that God’s voice gives life. This is already a sign of the Gospel, where the Word becomes flesh and lives among us.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism follows the fourth commandment through Deuteronomy’s wording. It teaches that the commandment is addressed first to children, but also speaks of the duties of parents, of grown children towards aged parents, and of citizens towards those in authority (CCC 2196–2200, 2214–2220). The promise of verse 16 is read as a sign that observing this commandment bears fruit in peace and in the common good (CCC 2200). The same Catechism teaches that the Ten Commandments are the words of God to his people, spoken before they are written, and that they are not set aside but fulfilled in Christ (CCC 2056–2063, 2074). Christians also hear verse 24 in the light of the Gospel: God speaks to us not out of fire and darkness, but through his Son, in whom the Word became flesh (Hebrews 1:1–2).

Isaiah 9:13–21 · They did not turn to the one who struck them

Setting.

Isaiah prophesied in Jerusalem in the eighth century before Christ. The northern kingdom, called Israel or Ephraim, was in crisis. Its kings had fought each other and had made and broken alliances with Syria and Assyria, and in 722 Samaria fell to the Assyrians. This section (9:8–10:4) is a poem in four stanzas, each ending with the same refrain: “For all this his anger has not turned away; his hand is stretched out still.” Today’s reading gives the second and third stanzas.

The point is not cruel punishment but a refusal to change. God had warned Israel through hardship, but the people did not turn to him. The poem shows how a society that will not turn to God begins to turn on itself.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–15: the people do not turn; the Lord cuts off head and tail, the elders and the lying prophets.
  • v. 16: those who lead the people lead them astray.
  • v. 17: no pity for the young, the orphans and the widows, since all are godless; the refrain.
  • vv. 18–19: wickedness burns like a fire through the land; no one spares another.
  • vv. 20–21: hunger that is never satisfied; Manasseh devours Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh; the refrain again.

Key words.

  • šûb, to turn back, return: The Hebrew verb for repentance, a turning around. The whole poem hangs on a refusal to “turn”. Isaiah’s own son was called Shear-jashub, “a remnant shall return” (7:3).
  • yādô nĕṭûyâ, his hand is stretched out: The refrain. It can mean a hand raised in judgment, but also a hand still held out, a sign that God has not yet withdrawn. The words are sad rather than final.
  • rō’š wĕzānāb, head and tail: A common Hebrew picture for the whole of society, from the highest to the lowest. Verse 15 explains it: the head is the elders and dignitaries, the tail the prophets who teach lies. (The NRSV notes that verse 15 may be a later explanation, as it interrupts the flow of the poem.)

Verse by verse.

“The people did not turn to him who struck them, or seek the Lord of hosts.” (9:13)

Suffering that does not lead to prayer only hardens. The prophet’s complaint is not that God punished but that the people did not hear him in it.

“for those who led this people led them astray, and those who were led by them were left in confusion.” (9:16)

The leaders are held to account first. The Church has always taught that those with authority will answer to God for those entrusted to them.

“for everyone was godless and an evildoer, and every mouth spoke folly.” (9:17)

When no one is left to speak truth, the poor and defenceless suffer most: the orphans and widows of the same verse.

“no one spared another.” (9:19)

The fire is the people’s own wickedness spreading. Sin does not stay private; it burns through a community.

“Manasseh devoured Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh, and together they were against Judah.” (9:21)

The two tribes descended from Joseph’s sons turn on each other. Division within the people of God is both a consequence and a cause of turning away from God.

How the Church reads it.

Christian tradition has read this poem as a warning about the effects of unrepented sin on whole societies. The Catechism describes “structures of sin”: social situations and institutions that result from and encourage personal sins (CCC 1869). It teaches that sin is never only private, because it wounds the unity of the Church and of the human family (CCC 1440, 1869). But the refrain also speaks of patience. Lines that sound only like judgment are read in the Church as a call to conversion, for the Lord does not desire the death of the wicked but that they turn and live (Ezekiel 18:23; CCC 1428–1429). The Church prays for her leaders, and for all who suffer because of the failures of others, and for those who caused them to suffer.

2 Corinthians 10:12–18 · Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord

Setting.

Paul wrote this letter, probably from Macedonia, around AD 55–56, to a Church in conflict with him. Newcomers had arrived in Corinth who praised themselves, carried letters of recommendation, and measured themselves against each other. Paul calls them, with some irony, “super-apostles” (11:5; 12:11). Many scholars think that chapters 10–13 come from a sharper letter written at a different time from chapters 1–9; the Church receives the letter as one inspired text.

Paul has to defend his ministry, and he does so reluctantly. His answer is not to put forward his own achievements but to speak of the limits that God has given him: he is the one who first brought the Gospel to Corinth, and he looks forward to going on to places where Christ has not yet been proclaimed.

Structure.

  • v. 12: Paul will not classify himself among those who commend themselves and compare themselves with one another.
  • vv. 13–14: his boasting stays within the field God has assigned to him, which includes Corinth.
  • vv. 15–16: he will not boast in the labours of others; he hopes that as faith grows, his work may go beyond Corinth.
  • vv. 17–18: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord”; it is not self-commendation but the Lord’s that counts.

Key words.

  • kauchaomai, to boast: The key word of 2 Corinthians, used some twenty times. It can mean sinful self-praise or glad pride in what God has done. The difference is where the boast is placed: in oneself, or in the Lord.
  • kanōn, limit, field, rule: Originally a measuring rod, then the sphere of work assigned to someone. From it comes our word “canon”. Paul lives within a measure given by God, not one he draws for himself.
  • synistēmi, to commend: To recommend someone, to put in a good light. Paul’s opponents commend themselves; he asks only for the commendation that comes from God.

Verse by verse.

“But when they measure themselves by one another, and compare themselves with one another, they do not show good sense.” (10:12)

Comparing ourselves with others is a rule that always misleads: we find someone worse to feel better, or someone better to feel worse.

“We, however, will not boast beyond limits, but will keep within the field that God has assigned to us” (10:13)

Each person has a vocation. Humility is not thinking little of oneself but knowing the task given by God.

““Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.”” (10:17)

Paul quotes Jeremiah 9:23–24, which he also quotes in 1 Corinthians 1:31. The Lord is the proper object of boasting, because every good work is his gift.

“For it is not those who commend themselves that are approved, but those whom the Lord commends.” (10:18)

The final judgment on a life belongs to God. We are asked to be faithful, not to be praised.

How the Church reads it.

The Church applies verse 17 to the teaching on merit. The Catechism teaches that the merit of a person before God arises from the fact that God has chosen to associate him with the work of his grace, so that our good works are first of all God’s gifts to us (CCC 2006–2011). St Augustine put it in a saying that has become classic: when God crowns our merits, he crowns his own gifts (in substance). The same verse sets the pattern for apostolic work: the Second Vatican Council teaches that the mission of the Church is carried on in obedience to Christ, who sends and gives the increase (Ad Gentes 2–5; 1 Corinthians 3:6–7). Paul’s plan to go on to “lands beyond you” has been read by the Church as the missionary instinct that never rests while there are people who have not heard the Gospel.

Mark 7:1–13 · You make void the word of God through your tradition

Setting.

Jesus is in Galilee. A group of Pharisees and scribes has come from Jerusalem, probably to examine him, and they notice that some of his disciples eat without first washing their hands. Mark adds an explanatory note for readers who are not Jewish (vv. 3–4): the Pharisees “and all the Jews” follow the tradition of the elders about washing. This is a generalisation for outsiders, not a statement about every Jew, and many Jews of that time did not observe these customs in the same way. Washing before eating was a pious practice, not a law of Moses for everyone; the Law asks for ritual washing mainly of the priests (Exodus 30:17–21). The Pharisees were sincere teachers who wished to extend the holiness of the Temple into daily life.

Jesus does not condemn the custom of washing or the elders as such. His reply goes to the heart of the matter: he accuses of hypocrisy any religion that honours God with the lips while the heart is far away, and he gives a concrete example in which a human custom has been used to excuse people from a commandment of God.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–5: the Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem ask why Jesus’ disciples do not follow the tradition of the elders.
  • vv. 6–8: Jesus quotes Isaiah 29:13: lips honour, hearts far away; human precepts taught as doctrines; you abandon the commandment of God for human tradition.
  • vv. 9–13: the example of Corban: Moses commands honour for father and mother, yet a vow dedicating goods to God is used to avoid supporting them; the word of God is made void.
  • ¶ The passage continues (vv. 14–23) with Jesus’ teaching to the crowd and the disciples on what truly defiles a person.

Key words.

  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: In Greek, a stage actor, one who plays a part. Jesus uses it for a religion of appearances, not for a whole people. The word aims at the gap between lips and heart that every believer must examine in himself.
  • paradosis, tradition: Literally “what is handed over”. It is a neutral word: Paul also uses it of the faith he hands on (1 Corinthians 11:2, 23; 15:3). The question is what is handed on, and whether it serves the word of God or replaces it.
  • qorbān, Corban: An Aramaic form of the Hebrew word for an offering brought to God (Leviticus 1:2). Mark explains it as “an offering to God”. The word has the same root as the Syriac qurbānā, the name of our Holy Qurbana.

Verse by verse.

“This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me” (7:6)

Jesus quotes Isaiah in the Greek (Septuagint) form. The Hebrew text speaks of a worship learned by rote. Either way, the fault is worship that does not come from the heart.

“You abandon the commandment of God and hold to human tradition.” (7:8)

Jesus names what is at stake: not tradition as such but the choice to put a human rule in the place of the divine commandment.

“For Moses said, ‘Honor your father and your mother’; and, ‘Whoever speaks evil of father or mother must surely die.’” (7:10)

He quotes the fourth commandment (Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16) together with the law in Exodus 21:17. Honour is serious: to curse a parent was a grave crime. The first reading of today stands behind this verse.

“thus making void the word of God through your tradition that you have handed on.” (7:13)

“Making void” is strong: a human rule can empty the word of God of its force. Jesus does not oppose the word to tradition, but warns that tradition must serve the word.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always asked how to hold together Jesus’ criticism of “human tradition” and her own reverence for Tradition. The answer given by the Second Vatican Council is that Sacred Tradition, which comes from the apostles, is the word of God handed on by the Holy Spirit and is inseparable from Scripture (Dei Verbum 9–10; CCC 80–82). The Catechism distinguishes this one Tradition from the “theological, disciplinary, liturgical or devotional traditions” of local Churches, which can change and which must always serve it (CCC 83). The fourth commandment that Jesus defends is taught in the Catechism as a duty that continues into adult life: grown children owe their parents material and moral support in old age, illness, loneliness and distress (CCC 2218). Jesus’ words about the Corban vow show that no religious gift, however pious, may be used to avoid this duty. Early Christian writers also applied this Gospel against the plea of “custom”: St Cyprian, quoting an earlier writer, said that custom without truth is only the antiquity of error (in substance, Letter 74).

Questions people ask

Did Jesus condemn the Pharisees or the Jewish people for washing their hands?

No. Jesus himself came as a Jew who kept the Law, and Mark’s note about “all the Jews” is an outsider’s generalisation, not a statement about every Jew. Washing was a sincere pious custom of some groups, and Jesus does not forbid it. He criticises the use of any custom to excuse oneself from a commandment of God. The Church teaches that the Jewish people remain beloved by God (Romans 11:28–29; Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839), and that no blame may be laid on the Jews as a people (CCC 597–598). The warnings of this Gospel belong to every believer’s heart, and to Christians no less than to anyone.

If Jesus criticised tradition, why does the Catholic Church keep Tradition?

Jesus criticised a human rule that cancelled God’s commandment. The Church’s Sacred Tradition is something else: the word of God that Christ gave to the apostles and that the Holy Spirit keeps alive in the Church. It never contradicts Scripture and is always measured by it (Dei Verbum 9–10). Customs of local Churches, such as devotions and some rules, are kept or changed with the same test Jesus gives: do they help people to love God and neighbour, or do they empty the commandment?

What if it is hard to honour a parent who has been unkind or unjust?

To honour is not the same as to obey every command. A child is never bound to obey a command to sin (Acts 5:29), and in cases of abuse the Church encourages seeking safety and help. But honour includes gratitude for the gift of life, respect, prayer and, where needed, practical care in old age. Many people find that to forgive and to pray for a difficult parent is itself a way of honouring the commandment, and the Church asks pastors to accompany such families with patience.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Season of Kaitha, the summer season of the Syro-Malabar calendar, the Church turns after the Apostles’ season to a long stretch of teaching on how to live the Gospel in ordinary time, and today’s readings give a clear rule of life. The Syro-Malabar Church has received a rich inheritance from the Thomas Christians: family prayer, reverence for elders and for the priest, and the blessing of parents on children. This is a living tradition of the kind Jesus does not reject, and it is worth cherishing, together with the call to examine whether the heart is in it. Many Kerala families today are separated by distance, with sons and daughters working far away from ageing parents. The fourth commandment asks that care for them be more than a money transfer, and that visits, calls and prayer be part of it.

The word “Corban” in the Gospel is the same root as qurbānā, the offering we make at the altar. In the Holy Qurbana we offer ourselves with the gifts of bread and wine; the Gospel today asks that no offering to God be a way of closing our hand to the neighbour, beginning with our own father and mother.

For study and teaching

  • Moses reminds the people that they heard the voice of God and lived. How do I hear God’s word in the Holy Qurbana, and what do I do with it afterwards?
  • The poem in Isaiah ends in people turning on each other. What divisions in my family, parish or community need the prophet’s call to turn back?
  • Paul measures himself by the “field” God gave him, and not by comparison with others. In what way do I compare myself with others, and how could I boast in the Lord instead?
  • Jesus accuses people of honouring God with the lips while the heart is far away. What custom of mine, however good, could be keeping me from a commandment of God, especially honour to my parents?
