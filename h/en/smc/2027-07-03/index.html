Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!” Jesus said to him, “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.”” John 20:28–29

Reflection

“Dukhrana” is a Syriac word that means remembrance. Each year on 3 July our Church remembers Mar Thoma, the Apostle Thomas, whom we call our father in faith. According to the East Syriac tradition, this is the day on which his relics were solemnly brought to Edessa, and so the day became his memorial; in the Syro-Malabar Church it is an important feast and a day of obligation. It is also the last day of the Season of the Apostles, and the season could not close with a better name. Thomas is the apostle whom the Gospel of John knows best after Peter and the beloved disciple. Three times he speaks. When Jesus decided to return to Judea, where his enemies were waiting, it was Thomas who said to the others, “Let us also go, that we may die with him.” At the Last Supper, when Jesus said “you know the way”, it was Thomas who said what the others were thinking: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” And on the second Sunday of Easter he spoke the greatest words in the Gospel. We have been unfair to Thomas by calling him only the doubter. He is the brave one, the honest one, and the one who confessed.

The Gospel is one of the most loved passages of John. Thomas was not with the others on Easter evening when Jesus came, and the ten told him, “We have seen the Lord.” He answered, “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” Thomas asked for no more than the others had already received, since Jesus had “showed them his hands and his side” on the first evening. And Jesus did not refuse. “A week later his disciples were again in the house, and Thomas was with them.” The Lord came again, “Although the doors were shut”, and said, “Peace be with you.” Then he turned to Thomas by name and gave him what he had asked: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” The Gospel does not say that Thomas touched. It says that he answered, and what he answered is the climax of the whole book: “My Lord and my God!” The Gospel began with “the Word was God”; now a disciple says it to the face of the risen Jesus. The Catechism notes that even in the presence of the risen Lord some disciples doubted, and that the Lord's patient dealing with their doubt is part of the Church's faith (CCC 644). Then comes a blessing for us: “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” That is the Indian Church, and every church after the apostles: “Although you have not seen him, you love him; and even though you do not see him now, you believe in him and rejoice with an indescribable and glorious joy.”

The Epistle tells us what the apostles are to the Church: “So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone. In him the whole structure is joined together and grows into a holy temple in the Lord; in whom you also are built together spiritually into a dwelling place for God.” The Church is apostolic because she is founded on the apostles, and because the teaching they handed on is kept in her by their successors, the bishops (CCC 857). For us in India this is not abstract. Our community has always said that it received the faith directly from an apostle. According to tradition, Thomas came by sea to Muziris, near Kodungallur, in the year 52, preached to Jews and others on the Malabar coast, baptised, and founded communities at Kodungallur, Palayoor, Kokkamangalam, Kottakkavu, Niranam, Nilackal and Kollam, and then travelled further to the east coast, where he was martyred at Mylapore about the year 72; his tomb is venerated in the San Thome Basilica at Chennai. Historians weigh the sources with care, and the tradition has been held with unbroken love by the Thomas Christians for nearly two thousand years: in the ancient Acts of Thomas, in the memory of the Church of the East, in the songs and customs of our families. The Orthodox, Mar Thoma and Malankara Churches honour the same apostle, and today we rejoice with them in the one Lord whom Thomas confessed.

Why does the Church read Genesis on this day? The calendar chooses a passage about a garden in the east: “And the Lord God planted a garden in Eden, in the east; and there he put the man whom he had formed.” In the middle of the garden stands “the tree of life”; “A river flows out of Eden to water the garden”; and “The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it.” The Bible ends where it begins: in the last book, “On either side of the river is the tree of life with its twelve kinds of fruit… and the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations”, and the risen Lord promises, “To everyone who conquers, I will give permission to eat from the tree of life that is in the paradise of God.” Our East Syriac fathers, above all Saint Ephrem, loved to sing of this paradise, and of the Church as the garden that God plants again. On Easter morning Mary Magdalene thought the risen Lord was the gardener. Thomas was given a garden to till and keep: a garden in the east, watered by rivers, where the tree of the cross would stand and where the leaves of the tree would heal people. The rivers of the Malabar coast meet the sea near Kodungallur, and by tradition the preaching of the apostle there was the first stream of the river of the Gospel in our land.

The second reading is from the book of Proverbs, the voice of Wisdom speaking to a child: “Hear, my child, and accept my words, that the years of your life may be many. I have taught you the way of wisdom; I have led you in the paths of uprightness.” And: “Keep hold of instruction; do not let go; guard her, for she is your life.” The Thomas Christians have done exactly this. For centuries, without a ruling power to protect them and often without many books, they kept hold of the instruction handed down to them: the Qurbana, the Creed, the sign of the cross, the fasts, the family prayer, and the memory of Thomas, whom they called the Way of Thomas, Mar Thoma Margam. The sage's last verse is the promise for those who keep to the path: “But the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day.” Thomas once asked, “How can we know the way?” and Jesus answered, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” We owe to the honest question of Thomas one of the greatest sayings of the Lord.

The weekday readings of the Seventh Saturday also have something to say on this feast. Paul, in the first letter to the Corinthians, speaks to the married and the unmarried about the practical shape of Christian faith in a household. “To the married I give this command, not I but the Lord, that the wife should not separate from her husband… and that the husband should not divorce his wife.” Where one partner is not yet a believer, he says that the believing partner should not cast the other aside: “For the unbelieving husband is made holy through his wife, and the unbelieving wife is made holy through her husband.” The Catechism uses this verse for the task of the Catholic spouse in a marriage with a person of another faith (CCC 1637). Paul's last word is a gentle one: “It is to peace that God has called you. Wife, for all you know, you might save your husband. Husband, for all you know, you might save your wife.” The Gospel takes us back to the beginning, to the same Genesis: “Have you not read that the one who made them at the beginning ‘made them male and female,’ and said, ‘For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh’? So they are no longer two, but one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate.” Jesus names the hardness of heart which made Moses permit divorce, and says, “from the beginning it was not so”; the Church holds the indissolubility of marriage as a gift of Christ and also accompanies with mercy those whose marriages have been wounded (CCC 1614–1615, 1640). Faith came to India through families, and it is handed on by families still. The garden of Eden was a home before it was a Church, and the Church has always been a household.

On this feast the Church prays that the faith of Thomas, tested and then confessed, be ours. Many Syro-Malabar people today go on pilgrimage to the churches that tradition links with the apostle, or sing the old songs of Mar Thoma. But the best Dukhrana is a daily one: to say at the elevation in the Qurbana, in our own heart, “My Lord and my God”, and then to carry the same Lord to those who have not yet seen. Thomas is the apostle of India and of the Church of the East; the Syro-Malabar Church today carries his mission to every state of India and to many countries of the world. May the Apostle who said “Let us also go” make us willing to go.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the Dukhrana of Mar Thoma, Saint Thomas the Apostle, our father in faith. The word Dukhrana means remembrance. Today the Church remembers him, and we remember that the faith of our families and our parish comes, according to our oldest tradition, from an apostle of Jesus.

Thomas is often called the doubter, but he was much more. When Jesus went toward danger, Thomas said to the others, “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (John 11:16). When he did not understand, he asked: “How can we know the way?” (John 14:5). He was brave, and he was honest.

On Easter evening Thomas was not with the others. They told him, “We have seen the Lord.” He said that he could not believe unless he saw the wounds. A week later Jesus came again, through closed doors, and said, “Peace be with you.” Then Jesus turned to Thomas: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe” (John 20:27). Thomas did not need to touch. He said, “My Lord and my God!” This is the highest confession in the whole Gospel.

And Jesus said: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe” (John 20:29). That is us. We have not seen Jesus with our eyes. But we have seen the faith of our parents, and we have received the Body of the Lord in the Qurbana. Saint Paul says that we are built on the foundation of the apostles, with Christ as the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20). Thomas is one of those foundation stones for our Church in India.

The calendar also gives us the Garden of Eden and the Book of Proverbs. In Eden God puts the man in a garden “to till it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). Thomas was given a garden in the east to till and keep. And Proverbs says, “Keep hold of instruction; do not let go; guard her, for she is your life” (Proverbs 4:13). Our ancestors kept hold of the faith for nearly two thousand years. What are we keeping hold of, and what are we handing on?

Today in the Saturday readings, Jesus and Saint Paul speak about marriage and peace in the home. The faith came to India in families and lives in families. Let us pray for our homes: “It is to peace that God has called you” (1 Corinthians 7:15). And let us say this week, at the elevation of the Holy Qurbana, the prayer of Thomas: “My Lord and my God.” Amen.

For children

Children, today is the feast of Saint Thomas, who came to our India with the good news of Jesus. Thomas was brave and he asked honest questions. When Jesus came back after Easter, Thomas said the most beautiful words: ‘My Lord and my God!’

You can say those words, too! When you see the priest lift the Body of Jesus at the Holy Qurbana, say in your heart: ‘My Lord and my God!’ And thank God for the people who taught you to pray.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you came through closed doors and showed the wounds of your love to your disciple Thomas, and he answered, “My Lord and my God!” Give us the faith of Thomas: courage to go with you even to death, honesty to ask the way, and humility to adore. We thank you for the apostle who, according to tradition, brought the Gospel to our land, and for all the parents, teachers and ancestors who have handed it on. Keep us on the Way, make us strong in your peace, and send us as witnesses to those who have not yet seen. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Dukhrana of Mar Thoma, the Church’s great memorial of the apostle of the East, the four readings of the feast build one picture: a house, a road and a confession. Genesis opens with God planting a garden in the east and setting the man in it to till and keep it. Proverbs shows a father leading his child along the way of wisdom, a path that grows brighter until full day. In Ephesians, Paul says that the Gentiles are no longer strangers but citizens and members of the household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone. And in John, Thomas, who has asked for proof, receives it, and finds in it more than he asked: “My Lord and my God!”

Together they say what the Church believes about the apostles: they are not the source of the faith but the foundation on which Christ builds, and the road they open leads to the Lord whom they confess. The Thomas Christians of India have always remembered their own apostle in just this way, as the one through whom the light came to them.

The day also has the readings of the Seventh Saturday of the Apostles, 1 Corinthians 7:8–16 (Paul on peace in marriage and in families where one partner is not a believer) and Matthew 19:1–12 (Jesus on marriage and on celibacy for the kingdom). The notes below follow the Dukhrana readings.

Genesis 2:8–17 · God plants a garden in Eden, in the east

Setting.

Genesis 2:4b to 3:24 is the second account of creation, told in a different style from the seven days of chapter 1, and many scholars attribute it to an older source than the first. Here God forms the man from the dust (2:7), then plants a garden, and sets the man in it. The story is told with the simple pictures of ancient storytelling (God as gardener, trees, rivers), and its purpose is to say who God is, who the human being is, and what the first relationship was meant to be.

The verses read today are the planting of the garden and the first commandment. They come before the creation of the woman (2:18–24) and before the sin (chapter 3). Nothing here has gone wrong yet. It is a picture of what God intended: a home, work, a river of life, and one limit, which makes the gift a relationship and not only a possession.

Structure.

  • v. 8: God plants a garden in Eden, in the east, and puts the man there.
  • v. 9: trees good for sight and food; in the middle, the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
  • vv. 10–14: the river that goes out of Eden and divides into four: Pishon, Gihon, Tigris, Euphrates.
  • v. 15: the man is put in the garden to till it and keep it.
  • vv. 16–17: the one commandment: eat of every tree except one; to eat of it is death.

Key words.

  • gan ‘ēden, garden of Eden: “Eden” is often connected with a word for delight. In the Greek Old Testament the garden is called paradeisos, a Persian word for an enclosed park, and this is the word that came into Christian use for the place of God’s presence (see Luke 23:43).
  • ‘ābad ûshāmar, to till and to keep: The two verbs of verse 15. The first means to work, and also to serve; the second means to guard. The same pair is used elsewhere for the priests’ service in the sanctuary (Numbers 3:7–8), which is why many readers see the garden as the first sanctuary, and the human being as given a calling, not only a job.
  • ‘ēts haḥayyîm, tree of life: In Proverbs wisdom is called “a tree of life” to those who hold her (3:18). The tree stands for life that comes only from God. At the end of the Bible it appears again, by the river of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 22:2).

Verse by verse.

“And the Lord God planted a garden in Eden, in the east; and there he put the man whom he had formed.” (2:8)

God is shown as one who plants, who makes a place for his creature before asking anything of him. “In the east” is said from the writer’s side: Eden lies towards the sunrise. For the Churches of the Syriac East the word has its own warmth.

“A river flows out of Eden to water the garden, and from there it divides and becomes four branches.” (2:10)

The river of life flows out from God’s garden into the whole world. Pishon and Gihon cannot now be identified, while the Tigris and the Euphrates are known. The point is that life, from its source in Eden, reaches all lands. Some Fathers saw in the four rivers a figure of the four Gospels and, more widely, of the grace that flows from the Church.

“The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it.” (2:15)

The man is not placed in the garden to be idle. Work, in the first picture, is part of the gift: a share in God’s own care for the world. Pope Francis, in Laudato Si’ 67, explains that “till” means to cultivate and “keep” means to protect and preserve.

“You may freely eat of every tree of the garden; but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat” (2:16–17)

The commandment begins with an enormous permission: every tree but one. The single limit shows that the man is a creature who receives life, and does not make it. The tree is not a trap: it marks the difference between God, who decides what is good and evil, and the human being who must trust him.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the garden as a picture of the friendship with God for which we were made. The Catechism teaches that the first man was created good and placed in friendship with his Creator, and that in this state of original justice he was to “till and keep” the garden (CCC 374–379, in substance). The Fathers of the East gave the garden a special love. St Ephrem’s Hymns on Paradise describe it as a place of light, fragrance and fruit, which the Church on earth already foreshadows, and which Christ has opened again (in substance). Many Fathers saw in the river of Eden a first sign of the water of baptism, and in the tree of life a figure of the cross and of the Eucharist.

Proverbs 4:10–18 · Keep hold of instruction, for she is your life

Setting.

Proverbs chapters 1 to 9 form a long introduction to the book, made of speeches in which a father (or teacher) speaks to his “child” and urges him to follow wisdom and to avoid folly. Today’s passage is one of these speeches. It is not a set of rules but an appeal, full of the language of the road: walking, running, stumbling, turning aside.

Wisdom in this book is more than clever advice. It is the way of living that fits the world as God made it, and in the later books of the Old Testament it comes to be spoken of almost as a person at God’s side (Proverbs 8; Wisdom 7–9). The New Testament sees that wisdom fulfilled in Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–13: the teacher’s promise: long life, a path that is clear, and a command: keep hold of instruction, for she is your life.
  • vv. 14–17: the other road: the way of the wicked, who cannot sleep unless they have done wrong, and whose food and drink is violence.
  • v. 18: the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day.

Key words.

  • derek, way, road: The commonest word for the road in the Old Testament, and also for a manner of life. “The two ways”, of life and of death, is a theme found throughout the Bible, and the first Christians called themselves “the Way” (Acts 9:2).
  • mûsār, instruction, discipline: The teaching that shapes a person, sometimes with correction. It is not only knowledge but formation, which is why the teacher asks the child to “keep hold” of it as of life itself.
  • nōgah, brightness: A shining that increases. The verse uses it of the sunrise which grows till noon. The righteous life is a road on which the light grows, not a single flash.

Verse by verse.

“When you walk, your step will not be hampered; and if you run, you will not stumble.” (4:12)

The teacher’s promise is not that the road is level but that the walker will not be caught. Those who walk in wisdom, even in difficulty, are not thrown.

“Keep hold of instruction; do not let go; guard her, for she is your life.” (4:13)

Instruction is spoken of as a person (“her”) and as life itself. The Hebrew word for “life” is in the plural, hinting at fullness of life. Whoever holds on to wisdom holds on to what makes a human being truly alive.

“For they eat the bread of wickedness and drink the wine of violence.” (4:17)

Bread and wine are the daily food and the festive drink. Here wickedness and violence have become the very food of those who follow them: they live on it. The verse warns that evil, once chosen, becomes a habit that feeds itself.

“But the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day.” (4:18)

This is the hopeful end of the passage. The life lived in the way of God does not remain in the half-light but grows towards noon. The image fits the history of Israel as well as the life of every believer, and the Church has seen in it the light of Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in the “way” of Proverbs a call that Christ fulfils: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6), spoken in answer to a question from Thomas, “how can we know the way?” (14:5). Because of this, the early Christians called their faith the Way, and the Thomas Christians of India long spoke of their faith as Mar Thoma Margam, the Way of Thomas. The Church also reads Proverbs as a school of everyday holiness, in which the teacher’s patience is the pattern of the patience of parents and priests with those who are growing in faith, and she sees in Wisdom a first sketch of Christ, whom Paul calls “the power of God and the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24).

Ephesians 2:19–22 · The household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles

Setting.

The letter to the Ephesians speaks of the Church as the one body of Christ in which Jews and Gentiles are made one. Many scholars hold that Paul himself wrote it, while others think it was written by a close follower in his tradition; the Church receives it as Scripture either way. In 2:11–18 the writer has described how Christ has broken down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile, and made peace through the cross. Our verses draw the conclusion with the pictures of a city, a household and a building.

Three images follow one another. First comes citizenship in a city (“citizens with the saints”), then family (“members of the household of God”), then a temple (“a holy temple in the Lord”). The movement is from stranger to citizen, from citizen to child of the house, and from the house to a sacred building.

Structure.

  • v. 19: no longer strangers and aliens, but citizens with the saints and members of God’s household.
  • v. 20: built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus as cornerstone.
  • v. 21: in him the whole structure is joined together and grows into a holy temple in the Lord.
  • v. 22: in him you are also built together into a dwelling place for God.

Key words.

  • paroikoi, aliens, resident strangers: People who lived in a city without the rights of citizens. Gentiles were once in this position in relation to the covenant. Now the status has changed completely: they belong.
  • akrogōniaios, cornerstone: The stone at the corner (or at the head) of a building, on which the lines of the walls depend. Christ is the stone by which all the rest is aligned. Compare Psalm 118:22 and 1 Peter 2:6–7.
  • synoikodomeisthe, you are built together: One verb, with two prefixes: “built” and “together, as a dwelling”. The word suggests that each stone is placed in relation to the others. No one is built into the Church alone.

Verse by verse.

“So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God” (2:19)

The change is from outsiders to insiders. The Gentile Christians of Ephesus had no ancestral claim to the promises. Paul says that, in Christ, they belong. The “saints” are God’s holy people, Israel, to whom the Gentiles are now joined (compare Romans 11:17–18).

“built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone” (2:20)

Christ is the cornerstone; the apostles and prophets are the foundation laid on him. The prophets here may be Old Testament prophets or those of the New Testament Church (see 3:5); both readings are held. The Church takes “apostolic” from this verse, as an essential mark.

“In him the whole structure is joined together and grows into a holy temple in the Lord” (2:21)

A building that grows is a strange thing, and Paul does it on purpose. The Church is built, and it is alive. Each new believer is a stone added to the temple, and the whole is “in the Lord”.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church is God’s building, resting on the foundation of the apostles, with Christ the cornerstone (Lumen Gentium 6, in substance), and the Catechism declares that the Church is apostolic because she is founded on the apostles, in three ways: she was built on their witness, she keeps their teaching through their successors, and she is taught and led by them until Christ returns (CCC 857, in substance). In the Creed of the Syro-Malabar Church we say that the Church is “one, holy, catholic and apostolic”. For the Thomas Christians the word apostolic has a particular sweetness: they believe their faith was planted by an apostle of the Lord himself.

John 20:24–29 · My Lord and my God

Setting.

These verses are the last appearance of the risen Lord in the main part of John, and they lead straight to the Gospel’s first ending (20:30–31). Chapter 21 will add an epilogue. On the evening of Easter the risen Jesus has come to the disciples behind locked doors, shown them his hands and his side, and breathed on them the Holy Spirit and the power to forgive sins (20:19–23). Thomas was not with them.

Thomas appears twice before in John: ready to die with Jesus (11:16), asking the way (14:5), and now here. The name Thomas means “twin” (Aramaic te’oma, Greek didymos), as John says. Who his twin was, the Gospel does not say.

Structure.

  • v. 24: Thomas, called the Twin, one of the twelve, was not with them when Jesus came.
  • v. 25: the others say, “We have seen the Lord”; he demands to see and touch before he will believe.
  • v. 26: a week later, Jesus comes again, though the doors are shut, and says, “Peace be with you”.
  • v. 27: Jesus offers Thomas what he asked: “Put your finger here... Do not doubt but believe”.
  • v. 28: Thomas’s confession: “My Lord and my God!”
  • v. 29: Jesus: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe”.

Key words.

  • didymos, twin: A Greek word, translating the Aramaic. John uses it as a second name for Thomas (11:16; 20:24; 21:2). Tradition has often wondered whom he was twin to; the Gospel is silent.
  • apistos … pistos, unbelieving … believing: Jesus’ words in verse 27 are literally “do not become unbelieving, but believing”. The two adjectives are one word with and without a negative. Jesus does not condemn Thomas but calls him to move from one to the other.
  • ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, my Lord and my God: Thomas’s words in Greek use the definite article for each title, as is done for God himself in the Greek Old Testament (for example Psalm 35:23, “my God and my Lord”). The confession gives Jesus the names of God.

Verse by verse.

“Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” (20:25)

Thomas does not ask for more than the others had been given. In verse 20 Jesus showed the others his hands and side. What he wants is to know that the risen Lord is the same as the crucified one, with the wounds still marked on him.

“Although the doors were shut, Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you.”” (20:26)

The week-later appearance happens on the Sunday, the eighth day. The Church has always seen in this the origin of the gathering for the Eucharist on the Lord’s Day. The same greeting of peace, the same Lord, the same doors shut: but now Thomas is inside.

“Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” (20:27)

Jesus repeats Thomas’s own words back to him. He knew the conditions Thomas had set, though he was not there. The text does not say that Thomas touched. It says that he believed.

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” (20:28)

This is the highest confession of faith in the Gospels. It comes at the end of a book that began, “the Word was God” (1:1). The one who doubted most gives the clearest witness, and that is why Thomas is the patron of faith that grows through struggle.

“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

Here Jesus looks beyond Thomas to every later believer, to us, who believe through the witness of the apostles. This is the last beatitude of the Gospel, spoken for the readers of the book.

How the Church reads it.

The Church treats Thomas with great love. The Catechism says that even before the risen Lord the disciples were slow to believe and uses Thomas as an example (CCC 643–644, in substance), and teaches that in “My Lord and my God” the title “Lord” takes on the sense of adoration (CCC 448, in substance). St Augustine says that Thomas saw and touched the man, and confessed the God whom he neither saw nor touched (in substance, Tractates on John 121). St Gregory the Great says that the doubt of Thomas helped our faith more than the quick belief of the other disciples, because his touching leaves no room for doubt in us (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 26). Pope Benedict XVI, in his general audience of 27 September 2006, spoke of Thomas’s confession as the faith of the whole Church, and mentioned the tradition that he carried the Gospel to India (in substance).

Questions people ask

Did St Thomas really come to India?

The Church holds this as an ancient and honoured tradition. There is no document from the first century that settles the matter, but the Thomas Christians of Kerala have passed down the memory of his arrival, according to tradition at Muziris on the Malabar coast in the year 52, and of the churches he founded, and the memory is very old and very firm. In the early third century the Acts of Thomas, probably written in Syriac, perhaps at Edessa, tells of his mission to India. That book has many legendary elements, but it shows that Christians then believed Thomas went east. Popes have spoken of it with respect, and Pope Benedict XVI mentioned the tradition in 2006. When speaking of the details, the Church uses the phrase “according to tradition”, and this is the honest way to say it.

Is Thomas rightly called “doubting Thomas”?

The name is not fair to him. Thomas was loyal: he was ready to die with Jesus (John 11:16). He asked questions that we all ask (14:5). In John 20 he does not refuse to believe; he wants to meet the risen Lord for himself, as the others had. Jesus does not scold him but gives him what he asked for, and Thomas answers with the plainest declaration of Jesus’ divinity in the Gospels. The Church honours him as a man whose honest questioning led to a deeper faith.

Why does a reading from Genesis about a garden belong to the feast of an apostle?

The garden of Eden is where the story of salvation begins, with the human being at home with God. The apostles are sent to bring people back to that home. At the end of the Bible the tree of life and the river appear again (Revelation 22). So the reading sets the apostle’s work in its widest frame: to open the way back to the garden, through Christ, to people in every land, including the East, which Genesis names first.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syriac word Dukhrana means remembrance, and the Dukhrana of Mar Thoma on 3 July is the principal feast of the apostle in the Syro-Malabar Church. According to tradition, on this day his relics were brought from India to Edessa, in the early third century. Tradition also keeps 21 December as the day of his martyrdom at Mylapore. The Church remembers him as “our father in faith”. According to tradition he founded seven communities on the Malabar coast, at Kodungallur, Palayoor, Kottakkavu (Paravur), Kokkamangalam, Niranam, Nilackal and Kollam. Other Churches that trace their origin to him also keep his memory, and the day is a gift to share. Every Holy Qurbana of the Syro-Malabar Church is also a thanksgiving for the faith that came by the apostle’s hand.

For study and teaching

  • Thomas asked for what he needed in order to believe and was not rejected. What honest question about faith am I afraid to bring to the Lord?
  • “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” Who passed the faith on to me, and how can I pass it on to someone else?
  • Ephesians says we are built together as a dwelling place for God. Which “stones” in my parish or family do I find hard to fit beside, and how might Christ the cornerstone align us?
  • Proverbs says the path of the righteous grows brighter. Where in my life do I see the light increasing, and where am I still in the half-light?
