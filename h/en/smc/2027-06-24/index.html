Word for the day

“All who heard them pondered them and said, “What then will this child become?” For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.” Luke 1:66

Reflection

The Church keeps the birthdays of very few people. Of most saints she celebrates the day of their death, their birth into heaven. But she celebrates the birth of the Lord at Christmas, the birth of his Mother, and, from the earliest centuries, the birth of John the Baptist. St Augustine already remarked on it: the Church celebrates the birth of no prophet, patriarch or apostle, except this one. The reason is in the Gospel itself. The angel had told Zechariah, “many will rejoice at his birth, for he will be great in the sight of the Lord… even before his birth he will be filled with the Holy Spirit” (Luke 1:14–15). John's birth is already part of the Gospel. Six months before Christmas, in the season when the days in the northern lands begin to grow shorter, we keep the birth of the one who said of Christ, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30). The Syriac tradition calls him Yohannan Mamdana, John the Baptiser, and our Church honours him among the greatest of the saints: Jesus himself said, “among those born of women no one has arisen greater than John the Baptist” (Matthew 11:11).

Luke tells the birth simply and joyfully: “Now the time came for Elizabeth to give birth, and she bore a son. Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” An old woman who had borne the shame of childlessness for a lifetime now holds her son, and the whole village of the hill country shares her joy. Mary had stayed with her about three months (Luke 1:56); the two mothers had already rejoiced together when the unborn John leaped at the sound of Mary's greeting. Then comes the naming. On the eighth day the relatives want to call the child Zechariah after his father, as custom expected. Elizabeth says, “No; he is to be called John.” They object: “None of your relatives has this name.” Zechariah, who had been mute since he doubted the angel's word, “asked for a writing tablet and wrote, ‘His name is John.’ And all of them were amazed.” The name means ‘the Lord has been gracious’. The child does not belong to the family's plans or traditions; he belongs to God's plan. And the moment Zechariah obeys, “Immediately his mouth was opened… and he began to speak, praising God.” Doubt had silenced him; obedience gives him back his voice. Fear and wonder spread through the hill country, and everyone asks the question that every parent asks over a cradle: “What then will this child become?” Luke answers: “For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.”

The reading from Jeremiah shows that John's vocation began before his birth: “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations.” These words, spoken to Jeremiah, fit John perfectly, and they speak to every human life. Each child in the womb is already known and loved by God; this is why the Church defends human life from conception. Jeremiah protests, “Truly I do not know how to speak, for I am only a boy.” God answers by touching his mouth: “Now I have put my words in your mouth.” John will be “the voice of one crying out in the wilderness” (John 1:23). And God gives the young prophet strength for the struggle ahead: “I for my part have made you today a fortified city, an iron pillar, and a bronze wall… They will fight against you; but they shall not prevail against you, for I am with you, says the Lord, to deliver you.” John needed that strength when he stood before Herod.

That is why the First Set places beside the feast the story of Nathan and David. King David has taken Uriah's wife and arranged Uriah's death. God sends the prophet Nathan, who tells a story of a rich man with many flocks who takes the one little lamb of a poor man, the lamb that “used to eat of his meager fare, and drink from his cup, and lie in his bosom, and it was like a daughter to him.” David burns with anger: the man deserves to die! “Nathan said to David, ‘You are the man!’” A prophet does not flatter kings. A thousand years later John would tell another king, Herod, “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife” (Mark 6:18), and it would cost him his head. There is another link: Nathan's parable turns on a stolen lamb; John's great word will be to point to the Lamb who is not stolen but given of his own will: “Here is the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29). David repented when he heard the truth; Herod imprisoned the one who told it. The feast asks us which we will be when the voice of conscience says, ‘You are the man.’

Paul's letter to the Galatians places John at the hinge of history. Heirs who are minors are “no better than slaves… they remain under guardians and trustees until the date set by the father.” Then: “But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, in order to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as children.” John is the last of the guardians, the greatest of the prophets standing at the door of the fullness of time: “The law and the prophets were in effect until John came” (Luke 16:16). He does not keep the people for himself; he hands them over to the Bridegroom. Through the Son born of the Virgin Mary, and through the Spirit who cries “Abba! Father!” in our hearts, we are no longer slaves but children and heirs. The weekday readings, which the parish may also hear, fit the feast: Paul speaks of “a remnant, chosen by grace” (Romans 11:5), and in Mark blind Bartimaeus hears the call, “Take heart; get up, he is calling you” (Mark 10:49), throws off his cloak and follows Jesus on the way. That is what John lived for: to prepare a people, open their eyes, and send them after Jesus. In this Season of the Apostles, John reminds every apostle, bishop, priest, catechist and parent: point to Christ, then step aside.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church celebrates a birthday. Of most saints we celebrate the day of their death, but of John the Baptist we celebrate his birth, because his birth was already part of the Good News. The angel had said, “many will rejoice at his birth.”

Elizabeth, old and once barren, gives birth to a son, and her neighbours and relatives rejoice with her. On the eighth day the family want to call him Zechariah. But Elizabeth says, “No; he is to be called John,” and Zechariah writes, “His name is John” (Luke 1:63). This child belongs to God's plan. And when Zechariah obeys, his mouth is opened and he praises God.

God said to Jeremiah, “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you” (Jeremiah 1:5). That is true of John, and it is true of every child. Every life in the womb is known and loved by God. Let us protect and welcome every child.

The first reading shows the prophet Nathan telling King David, “You are the man!” (2 Samuel 12:7). A prophet tells the truth even to kings. John did the same with Herod, and it cost him his life. When our conscience says, ‘You are the man’, let us not argue; let us repent as David did.

Saint Paul says that when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, so that we might become children of God (Galatians 4:4–5). John stands at that door. He is the last of the prophets and points to Jesus: “Here is the Lamb of God.”

John's great word is, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30). Parents, teachers, priests, every one of us: let us point to Jesus and step aside, so that he may grow in the hearts of others. What then will this child become? With the hand of the Lord upon us, we too can become voices that prepare his way. Amen.

For children

Children, today is John the Baptist's birthday! His old father Zechariah could not speak, but when he wrote, “His name is John,” he could talk again and he praised God.

God knew John before he was born, and God knew you before you were born too. Do you know what your name means? Ask your parents today, and thank God for calling you by name!

Prayer

Lord God, before John was formed in the womb you knew him, before he was born you consecrated him, and you filled him with the Holy Spirit to prepare a people for your Son. On this day of his birth we rejoice with Elizabeth and Zechariah and all their neighbours, for you have shown your great mercy. Open our mouths, as you opened the mouth of Zechariah, to bless you; give us the courage of John to speak the truth to the great and the small; and teach us to point to the Lamb of God and to decrease, so that he may increase in us. Protect every child in the womb, bless every name given in baptism, and make us your children and heirs through the Spirit who cries “Abba, Father” in our hearts. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church celebrates the birth of John the Baptist, the forerunner of Christ. The four readings form a portrait of the prophet he would become. Nathan stands before King David and says, “You are the man!”: the courage that John would show before Herod (Mark 6:18). Jeremiah hears that God knew him before he was formed in the womb and consecrated him before he was born: the call that John received while still in Elizabeth’s womb (Luke 1:15, 41). Paul says that when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son: John stands at that threshold, the last of the prophets and the first to point to the Lamb. And in Luke, the neighbours ask, “What then will this child become?”

The key words are name, womb and time. God names John before his parents do; God forms and calls his prophets from the womb; and God chooses the time. John’s whole life will say what his name means: the Lord has shown favour.

2 Samuel 12:1–10 · You are the man!

Setting.

Chapter 11 tells how King David, at the height of his power, took Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite, and when she conceived, arranged for Uriah to be killed in battle against the Ammonites. The chapter ends: “the thing that David had done displeased the Lord” (11:27). Now the Lord sends the prophet Nathan.

Nathan had earlier brought David the promise of an everlasting dynasty (7:1–17). The same prophet who announced God’s blessing now announces God’s judgment. In Israel the king was not above God’s law, and the prophet was the voice that reminded him.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: Nathan’s parable: the rich man, the poor man and his one ewe lamb.
  • vv. 5–6: David’s anger: the man deserves to die; he must restore fourfold.
  • v. 7a: “You are the man!”
  • vv. 7b–9: the Lord recalls his gifts to David and names his crimes.
  • v. 10: the sentence: the sword shall never depart from your house.

Key words.

  • kibśâ, ewe lamb: A female lamb. Nathan says it was “like a daughter” to the poor man: the lamb stands for Uriah’s wife, cherished by her husband and taken by the king. The story leads David to judge the case before he knows it is his own.
  • ’attâ hā’îš, you are the man: Two words in Hebrew. The parable’s trap closes. David has pronounced sentence on himself.
  • bāzâ, to despise: The Lord says David “despised” his word (v. 9) and “despised” the Lord himself (v. 10). Sin against a neighbour is contempt for God.

Verse by verse.

“but the poor man had nothing but one little ewe lamb, which he had bought.” (12:3)

Nathan’s details are tender on purpose: the lamb drank from the poor man’s cup and lay in his bosom “like a daughter”. The story wakes David’s pity before it wakes his conscience.

“he shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity.” (12:6)

David applies the law of Exodus 22:1, which required fourfold restitution for a stolen sheep. Zacchaeus will promise the same (Luke 19:8).

“Nathan said to David, “You are the man!” (12:7)

A prophet’s courage in four words. Nathan risks his life, as John the Baptist would when he told Herod, “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife” (Mark 6:18).

“Why have you despised the word of the Lord, to do what is evil in his sight?” (12:9)

Adultery and murder are named plainly. God’s word does not excuse the powerful.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses Nathan’s parable to teach about the tenth commandment: envy and greed can lead to the worst crimes, and Nathan’s story moved David to repentance (CCC 2538). Immediately after this reading David confesses, “I have sinned against the Lord” (12:13), and the Church’s tradition links Psalm 51, the great psalm of repentance, with this moment. St Ambrose, writing to the Emperor Theodosius after the massacre at Thessalonica in 390, urged him to repent as David had done when Nathan rebuked him; the emperor did public penance (in substance, Letter 51). The prophet’s duty to speak the truth to power continued in John the Baptist, and continues in the Church.

Jeremiah 1:4–10, 16–19 · Before I formed you in the womb

Setting.

Jeremiah was called in the thirteenth year of King Josiah (1:2), about 627 BC, and prophesied until after the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC. He came from a priestly family of Anathoth, a village a few kilometres north of Jerusalem (1:1). His ministry was one of the hardest in the Old Testament: he was mocked, beaten, imprisoned and thrown into a cistern.

This reading gives his call (vv. 4–10) and the Lord’s promise of strength for the conflicts ahead (vv. 16–19). The verses between, two visions of an almond branch and a boiling pot (vv. 11–15), are omitted.

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5: the Lord’s word: known, consecrated and appointed before birth.
  • vv. 6–8: Jeremiah’s objection, “I am only a boy”, and the Lord’s answer: go, speak, do not fear.
  • vv. 9–10: the Lord touches his mouth and gives him authority over nations.
  • vv. 16–19: judgment on idolatry; Jeremiah must stand firm, made a fortified city, iron pillar and bronze wall.

Key words.

  • yāda‘, to know: More than information: to know someone personally, to choose and care for them. God’s knowledge of Jeremiah came before his body existed.
  • hiqdîsh, to consecrate: To set apart as holy for God. Jeremiah was dedicated to his mission before he could choose it.
  • na‘ar, boy: A young man, not yet old enough to speak with authority among the elders. Jeremiah pleads his youth; God answers with his presence.

Verse by verse.

““Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations.”” (1:5)

Three verbs, all before birth. The verb “formed” is the potter’s word used of the forming of Adam (Genesis 2:7).

“Then I said, “Ah, Lord God! Truly I do not know how to speak, for I am only a boy.”” (1:6)

Like Moses (Exodus 4:10), Jeremiah feels unequal to the task. God does not deny his weakness; he promises to be with him.

“Now I have put my words in your mouth.” (1:9)

The prophet speaks not his own words but God’s. This is why John the Baptist could call himself only “the voice” (John 1:23).

“to pluck up and to pull down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant.” (1:10)

Four verbs of tearing down, two of building up. Judgment is never God’s last word; it clears the ground for planting.

“They will fight against you; but they shall not prevail against you, for I am with you, says the Lord, to deliver you.” (1:19)

The promise is not that there will be no fight, but that God will be with him in it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Jeremiah 1:5 in teaching that human life must be respected and protected from the moment of conception: God knows and loves each person from the womb (CCC 2270). The liturgy applies these words to John the Baptist, who was filled with the Holy Spirit even before his birth (Luke 1:15). The Catechism says that John was filled with the Holy Spirit from his mother’s womb, and that in him the Holy Spirit completes his speaking through the prophets (CCC 717, 719). Many Fathers held that John was sanctified before birth when he leapt for joy at Mary’s greeting (Luke 1:41, 44); this is why the Church keeps his birthday as a feast, as she does for Christ and for the Virgin Mary.

Galatians 4:1–7 · When the fullness of time had come

Setting.

The Galatians, Gentile converts in central Asia Minor, were being urged by some teachers to accept circumcision and the observance of the Mosaic law in order to be full members of God’s people. Paul insists that they have already received everything through faith in Christ and the gift of the Spirit (3:1–5).

In 3:23–29 Paul compared the law to a paidagōgos, the household slave who supervised a child until he came of age. Here he develops the picture: an heir who is a minor is no better off than a slave, until the day his father sets.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the picture: an heir who is a minor lives under guardians until the date set by his father.
  • v. 3: so we were enslaved to the elemental spirits of the world.
  • vv. 4–5: when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem and adopt.
  • vv. 6–7: God sent the Spirit of his Son, crying “Abba! Father!”; so you are a child and an heir.

Key words.

  • plērōma tou chronou, the fullness of time: Time pictured as a vessel filled up to the brim. The coming of Christ is not an accident of history but the moment God appointed.
  • stoicheia tou kosmou, the elemental spirits of the world: The word means the basic elements of something, like letters of the alphabet or the elements of nature. Scholars debate whether Paul means the elementary principles of religion or the spiritual powers people thought ruled the world. Either way, it is a slavery from which Christ delivers us.
  • huiothesia, adoption: A legal term in the Roman world for taking someone as a son with full rights of inheritance. Through the Son we become children of God by grace.

Verse by verse.

“But when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law” (4:4)

This is probably the earliest mention of Jesus’ mother in the New Testament, though she is not named. The Son was truly human, born as we are, and truly a Jew, living under the law of Moses.

“And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!”” (4:6)

God “sent his Son” (v. 4) and “sent the Spirit of his Son” (v. 6): the two sendings that make us children of the Father. Abba is the Aramaic word Jesus himself used in prayer (Mark 14:36).

“So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if a child then also an heir, through God.” (4:7)

Paul switches to “you” singular. Each believer is addressed personally: you are a child of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it speaks of Mary: God sent forth his Son, but to prepare a body for him he wanted the willing co-operation of a creature (CCC 488). It quotes verse 6 to show that the Holy Spirit, sent into our hearts, makes us cry “Abba! Father!” and so teaches us to pray as children (CCC 683, 742). John the Baptist stands at the edge of the fullness of time: the last prophet under the law, who sees the Son and points to him.

Luke 1:57–66 · His name is John

Setting.

Luke tells the births of John and Jesus side by side: two annunciations (1:5–25 and 1:26–38), the meeting of the two mothers (1:39–56), and two births (1:57–80 and 2:1–21). The pattern shows both the likeness and the difference: John is great, but Jesus is greater.

Zechariah, a priest, had been struck mute when he doubted the angel’s promise of a son in his old age (1:18–20). Elizabeth had been barren. The angel had said that the child was to be named John and that many would rejoice at his birth (1:13–14). Now that word is fulfilled.

Structure.

  • vv. 57–58: Elizabeth gives birth; neighbours and relatives rejoice at the Lord’s mercy.
  • vv. 59–61: on the eighth day, at the circumcision, they want to name him Zechariah; Elizabeth says, “No; he is to be called John.”
  • vv. 62–63: Zechariah writes on a tablet: “His name is John.”
  • v. 64: his mouth is opened, and he praises God.
  • vv. 65–66: fear and wonder through the hill country: “What then will this child become?”

Key words.

  • Yôḥānān, John: The Hebrew name means “the Lord has been gracious” or “the Lord has shown favour”. The name itself announces the time of grace that is beginning.
  • eleos, mercy: The neighbours hear that the Lord “had shown his great mercy” to Elizabeth. Mercy is a key word of Luke’s first chapter, sung by Mary (1:50, 54) and by Zechariah (1:72, 78).
  • pinakidion, writing tablet: A small wooden tablet, often coated with wax, on which one wrote with a stylus. Zechariah cannot speak, so he writes.

Verse by verse.

“Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown his great mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.” (1:58)

The angel’s promise is fulfilled: “many will rejoice at his birth” (1:14). The Church still rejoices on this day.

“But his mother said, “No; he is to be called John.”” (1:60)

Elizabeth already knows the name. Luke does not say how; perhaps Zechariah had written it for her, or perhaps the Spirit who filled her at the Visitation (1:41) had made it known.

“He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, “His name is John.” And all of them were amazed.” (1:63)

Zechariah does not write ‘His name shall be John’, but “His name is John”. The name was given by God; he only obeys. His obedience opens his mouth.

“Immediately his mouth was opened and his tongue freed, and he began to speak, praising God.” (1:64)

His first words after months of silence are praise. The song he sings, the Benedictus, follows in 1:68–79.

“All who heard them pondered them and said, “What then will this child become?” For, indeed, the hand of the Lord was with him.” (1:66)

“The hand of the Lord” in the Old Testament is God’s power at work in his chosen ones. The question is answered in 1:76: “you, child, will be called the prophet of the Most High.”

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls John the Lord’s immediate precursor, sent to prepare his way: “prophet of the Most High”, he surpasses all the prophets, of whom he is the last (CCC 523). St Augustine notes that the Church celebrates the birthday of only two persons in this way, John and Christ (in substance, Sermons on the Nativity of John). He also saw a sign in the seasons: John’s birth is kept as the days begin to grow shorter, and Christ’s as they begin to grow longer, fulfilling John’s word, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30).

Questions people ask

Why does the Church celebrate John’s birth, when for other saints she keeps the day of their death?

For most saints the Church keeps the day of their death, their birth into heaven. John is an exception, with Christ and the Virgin Mary, because his birth itself was holy: he was filled with the Holy Spirit even before birth (Luke 1:15) and leapt for joy in the presence of the Lord (1:41, 44). The Church also remembers his martyrdom on another day.

Why is the feast on 24 June?

The angel told Mary that Elizabeth was in her sixth month (Luke 1:36). The Church therefore keeps John’s birth about six months before Christmas. The date comes from this reckoning, not from any record of the exact day.

Was John the Baptist Jesus’ cousin?

Luke calls Elizabeth Mary’s “relative” (1:36, Greek syngenis), a word for a kinswoman of any degree. So John and Jesus were related, but the Gospel does not say exactly how. ‘Cousin’ is a common way of putting it, but the text says only that they were kin.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the East Syriac tradition of our Church, John the Baptist has a special place in the season of Denha, when the Church celebrates the Lord’s Baptism in the Jordan and the revelation of the Holy Trinity; the first Friday of Denha is kept in his memory. Today, in the Season of the Apostles, his birth reminds us that every mission begins with God’s call, often long before we know it.

Syro-Malabar families give their children the names of saints at baptism, and many of our people bear the name of John. Like Elizabeth and Zechariah, Christian parents receive each child as God’s gift, knowing that the child’s life belongs first to him. The question of the neighbours is the question we ask at every baptism: “What then will this child become?”

For study and teaching

  • Nathan risked his life to tell the king the truth. Is there a truth I need to hear about myself, or one I should lovingly speak to another?
  • God said to Jeremiah, “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you.” How does this change the way I see myself, and every unborn child?
  • Paul says that because we are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying “Abba! Father!” How can I pray more as a child of God this week?
  • Zechariah’s mouth was opened when he obeyed. Is there an act of obedience to God that I have been putting off?
