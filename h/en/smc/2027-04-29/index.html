Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, anyone who hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life, and does not come under judgment, but has passed from death to life.” John 5:24

Reflection

The Letter to the Hebrews takes up a psalm that the Church still prays at the opening of her daily prayer, Psalm 95, and hears in it the voice of the Holy Spirit speaking now: “Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says, ‘Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, as on the day of testing in the wilderness.’” The generation that came out of Egypt had seen the sea divided, eaten manna and drunk from the rock, and still their hearts grew hard. Seeing great works of God does not by itself make a heart soft. The writer then speaks to his own community with deep concern: “Take care, brothers and sisters, that none of you may have an evil, unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God. But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called ‘today,’ so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin.” Two things stand out. First, the word ‘today’. God never speaks to us about tomorrow; his invitation is always for this day. Second, we are responsible for one another. A hard heart rarely forms in a moment; it hardens slowly, by small refusals, and the remedy is daily encouragement within the Christian family. A word from a friend, a parent or a priest at the right time can keep a heart from setting like cement.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks with the authority of the Son to whom the Father has given life: “Very truly, I tell you, anyone who hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life, and does not come under judgment, but has passed from death to life.” The tense matters: has eternal life, has passed. Eternal life begins now, in the one who believes. Then Jesus speaks of two moments. The first is already here: “the hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live.” Those who are dead in sin hear his voice and rise by faith. The second is still to come: “for the hour is coming when all who are in their graves will hear his voice and will come out”, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of condemnation. The Church professes this in every Creed: the resurrection of the body. In our parishes, families go to the cemetery after the Holy Qurbana on many feasts and anniversaries, light candles and pray at the graves of their dead. In the season of the Resurrection those graves look different. They are not the end of a story but a waiting room. The One who called Lazarus out of the tomb will one day speak to every grave, and the bodies laid there in faith will hear his voice.

Today the Church remembers St Catherine of Siena, and the readings given for her fit the whole day. Born in Siena in 1347, one of the youngest of the many children of a wool dyer, she gave herself to Christ as a girl and joined the Dominican laywomen known as the Mantellate. She had little schooling and learned to read late, yet she became one of the great teachers of the Church: her book of the Dialogue and nearly four hundred letters were dictated to her companions. Her first reading proclaims that “God is light and in him there is no darkness at all”, and that “If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness.” Catherine knew her own sinfulness and spent herself bringing others to confession and reconciliation. Her Gospel is Jesus' prayer: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants.” This unlettered young woman wrote with burning frankness to princes, cardinals and the Pope himself. She urged Pope Gregory XI to return from Avignon to Rome, and he did so in 1377; she worked for peace between warring cities and for the unity of the Church when the great schism began. Worn out, she died in Rome on 29 April 1380, aged thirty-three. St Paul VI declared her a doctor of the Church in 1970, and St John Paul II named her a patron of Europe in 1999. Catherine lived the ‘today’ of Hebrews: she never put off what love asked of her. Her life tells every Christian, especially women and lay people, that holiness and the word of truth are not reserved for the learned.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews repeats a word from the Psalm: Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts (Hebrews 3:7–8). God does not speak to us about tomorrow. He speaks today.

The people in the wilderness saw great miracles and still hardened their hearts. So the writer says: exhort one another every day, as long as it is called today, so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin (Hebrews 3:13). We are responsible for one another's faith.

Jesus says: anyone who hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life, and has passed from death to life (John 5:24). And he promises that the hour is coming when all who are in their graves will hear his voice and come out (John 5:28–29). When we pray at the graves of our dead, we pray in this hope.

Today we remember St Catherine of Siena. She had little schooling, yet God made her a teacher of the Church. She wrote to the Pope and begged him to return to Rome, and worked for peace and unity. She died at thirty-three, on this day in 1380. She shows us what Jesus said: the Father reveals his secrets to the little ones (Matthew 11:25).

Let us not wait for tomorrow. Today, let us hear his voice, encourage one another, and live as people who have already passed from death to life. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, St Catherine of Siena could not read when she was small, but she loved Jesus with all her heart. Later she wrote letters to the Pope! Jesus says that God shows his secrets to little children. You can know Jesus and love him too, even now!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of the Father, you have life in yourself and you give it to all who hear your word. Today, as we hear your voice, do not let our hearts be hardened. Raise us now from the death of sin, and on the last day call us out of our graves to the resurrection of life. Through the prayers of St Catherine of Siena, give us her light, her humility to confess our sins, her courage to speak the truth in love, and her fire for the unity of your Church. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Hebrews hears the voice of the Holy Spirit in Psalm 95: “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” John’s Gospel gives the voice that must be heard: the voice of the Son of God, which raises the spiritually dead now and will call all the dead from their graves at the last day. Today is also the memory of St Catherine of Siena (1347–1380), virgin and doctor of the Church; her own readings are 1 John 1:5–2:2 and Matthew 11:25–30. Few saints heard the Lord’s voice “today” with such readiness.

Hebrews 3:7–13 · Today, if you hear his voice

Setting.

Hebrews has just compared Moses, faithful as a servant in God’s house, with Christ, faithful as a Son over it (3:1–6). Now it quotes Psalm 95:7–11, a psalm Israel sang in worship, and applies it to Christians who are tempted to grow weary and drift away. The wilderness generation saw God’s works and still did not trust him; the readers are on their own journey to God’s rest.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–11: the Holy Spirit speaks through the psalm: today, do not harden your hearts as in the wilderness.
  • v. 12: the warning: an evil, unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God.
  • v. 13: the remedy: exhort one another every day, while it is called today.

Key words.

  • sēmeron, today: The key word. The psalm’s “today” did not end in David’s time; it is renewed every time the word is proclaimed. God’s call is always for today, never to be put off until tomorrow.
  • parapikrasmos / peirasmos, rebellion / testing: In the Hebrew psalm these are place names, Meribah and Massah (Exodus 17:7). The Greek translates the names into their meaning, and Hebrews follows the Greek.
  • sklērynō, harden: To make stiff or dry. A hardened heart is not one that cannot hear, but one that has heard and refused so often that it no longer responds.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says, “Today, if you hear his voice,” (3:7)

Hebrews does not say “as David said” or “as the psalm said”, but “as the Holy Spirit says”, in the present tense. Scripture is God speaking now.

“where your ancestors put me to the test, though they had seen my works” (3:9)

Seeing great works does not by itself soften a heart. The wilderness generation had seen the sea divided and eaten manna.

“Take care, brothers and sisters, that none of you may have an evil, unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God.” (3:12)

The danger is not first a wrong idea but a turning away. Unbelief here is a matter of the heart and the will.

“But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called “today,”” (3:13)

The cure for hardness is not solitary effort but the community. Christians are responsible for one another’s faith.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith is a personal act, but not an isolated one: we believe within the Church, supported by the faith of others (CCC 166). Psalm 95 opens the daily prayer of many Churches. Its “today” is the voice of the Spirit to every generation, calling us to listen while there is time.

John 5:24–29 · From death to life

Setting.

Jesus has healed a man on the Sabbath at the pool of Bethesda (5:1–9), and some of the authorities in Jerusalem object, since he calls God his own Father (5:18). Where John says “the Jews” in this chapter, he means these authorities; Jesus and his disciples were themselves Jews. Jesus answers with a long discourse on the Son, who does what the Father does: gives life and judges.

Structure.

  • v. 24: whoever hears and believes has eternal life now.
  • vv. 25–27: the hour is now here: the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God; the Son has life in himself and authority to judge.
  • vv. 28–29: the hour is coming: all in the graves will hear his voice and come out.

Key words.

  • metabebēken, has passed: A perfect tense: the passage from death to life has already happened for the one who believes. Eternal life begins now, in faith and Baptism.
  • hōra, hour: Both “now here” (v. 25) and “coming” (v. 28). John holds together the life given now and the resurrection still to come.
  • huios anthrōpou, Son of Man: The figure of Daniel 7:13–14, who receives dominion from God. Jesus judges because he is the Son of Man: the judge of humanity is one who shared our humanity.

Verse by verse.

“anyone who hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life, and does not come under judgment, but has passed from death to life.” (5:24)

Faith in Christ is not only a ticket for the future; it is life already begun.

“the hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live.” (5:25)

Here “the dead” are those dead in sin. The Lord’s word raises them now; Lazarus (11:43) will be a visible sign of it.

“Do not be astonished at this; for the hour is coming when all who are in their graves will hear his voice” (5:28)

Now the dead in the tombs. The verse echoes Daniel 12:2: “Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake.”

“those who have done good, to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil, to the resurrection of condemnation.” (5:29)

This does not set works against faith. The good that leads to life is the fruit of grace received in faith (v. 24); our deeds matter because God’s grace truly works in us.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine distinguishes two resurrections in this passage: the first now, when souls dead in sin hear the voice of the Son of God and rise by faith; the second at the end, when bodies come out of the tombs (in substance, City of God 20.6). The Catechism quotes vv. 28–29 when it teaches that the resurrection of all the dead, the just and the unjust, will precede the Last Judgment (CCC 1038).

Questions people ask

If believers “do not come under judgment”, why does v. 29 speak of judgment by deeds?

Both are true. The one who believes has already received life and is not condemned. But faith is living: it bears fruit in love, and a person can also turn away (Hebrews 3:12). At the end, Christ will reveal what each life has become. The Church teaches that our good works are themselves gifts of grace, and we hope for salvation by trusting in God’s mercy (CCC 1038–1041, 2008).

For study and teaching

  • Whom could I encourage in faith this week, as Hebrews asks, “every day”?
