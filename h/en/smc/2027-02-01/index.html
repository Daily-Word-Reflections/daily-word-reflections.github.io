Word for the day

“But the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one.” 2 Thessalonians 3:3

Reflection

Paul ends his letter to the Thessalonians by asking for their prayers: “pray for us, so that the word of the Lord may spread rapidly and be glorified everywhere, just as it is among you, and that we may be rescued from wicked and evil people; for not all have faith.” He is honest about the opposition he meets. But he does not rest his hope on how many believe. He rests it on someone else: “But the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one.” Our faith rises and falls; the Lord's faithfulness does not. So Paul can end with a blessing that is also a direction for the whole of life: “May the Lord direct your hearts to the love of God and to the steadfastness of Christ.”

In the Gospel two daughters meet the faithful Lord. A leader of the synagogue kneels before Jesus: “My daughter has just died; but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live.” On the way a woman who has suffered from hemorrhages for twelve years comes up behind him. Under the law her illness made her unclean and kept her apart from worship and from people. She does not dare to ask; she only reaches out: “If I only touch his cloak, I will be made well.” Jesus turns and looks at her. He does not scold her for touching him; he calls her by a family name: “Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.” Then at the leader's house, among the flute players and the noisy mourners, he says, “Go away; for the girl is not dead but sleeping.” They laugh at him. He goes in, takes her by the hand, and the girl gets up. In these last days of Denha Christ is revealed as Lord over sickness and death. A touch of faith is enough, because the one who is touched is faithful.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul asks the Thessalonians to pray that the word of the Lord may spread, because not all have faith. Then he adds: but the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one (2 Thessalonians 3:3).

In the Gospel a woman who has been ill for twelve years comes up behind Jesus. She thinks: if I only touch his cloak, I will be made well (Matthew 9:21). Jesus turns, looks at her and says: take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.

Then he goes to the house of the synagogue leader, whose daughter has died. The mourners laugh when he says she is sleeping. But he takes her by the hand, and the girl gets up.

Our faith may be as small as a touch. It is enough, because the one we touch is faithful. Let us reach out to him today, and let him direct our hearts to the love of God. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings turn on trust. Paul knows that “not all have faith”, but he rests his hope on a stronger fact: “the Lord is faithful”. In the Gospel a father whose daughter has just died and a woman ill for twelve years come to Jesus with nothing left but that same trust, and neither is sent away. In the season of Denha, when Christ is shown to the world, today’s question is how far we will rely on him.

2 Thessalonians 3:1–5 · The Lord is faithful

Setting.

The Christians of Thessalonica, a port city of Macedonia, had been unsettled by talk that the day of the Lord had already come (2:2). The letter comes to us under the name of Paul, and many hold it to be his; some scholars think that a disciple wrote it in his name. After the teaching on the end in chapter 2, and before the warning against idleness in 3:6–15, stands this short passage of prayer and confidence.

The great missionary does not speak here as someone who needs nothing. He asks a small and troubled community to pray for him.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Paul asks for prayer: that the word may spread, and that the missionaries may be rescued.
  • vv. 3–4: God’s faithfulness, and Paul’s confidence in the community because of it.
  • v. 5: a short prayer for their hearts.

Key words.

  • trechē, may spread rapidly: Literally “may run”. The word of God is pictured as a runner, an image that recalls Psalm 147:15: “his word runs swiftly”. The missionaries may be held back, but the word itself keeps moving.
  • pistis / pistos, faith / faithful: The Greek sets two related words side by side: not all have pistis, “but the Lord is pistos”. Our faith may fail. His faithfulness is what holds us.
  • hypomonē, steadfastness: Endurance, literally remaining under a load. The Greek “steadfastness of Christ” can mean the endurance Christ himself showed, or the endurance he gives. The Malayalam POC takes it as what Christ gives. The two belong together.

Verse by verse.

“pray for us, so that the word of the Lord may spread rapidly and be glorified everywhere” (3:1)

Paul asks first for the word and only then for himself (v. 2). The word is glorified when people receive it and live it.

“But the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one.” (3:3)

The Greek for “the evil one” can also be read as “evil”, as in the last petition of the Lord’s Prayer (Matthew 6:13). The Catechism explains that petition as referring to a person, Satan (CCC 2851).

“May the Lord direct your hearts to the love of God and to the steadfastness of Christ.” (3:5)

To “direct” is to make a road straight. Paul prays for two things only: love and endurance. A Christian who has these can bear not knowing the date of the Lord’s coming.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes how the first communities lived this exchange of prayer: Paul gives them a share in his ministry of preaching, and he intercedes for them in turn (CCC 2636). Those who are sent need the prayer of those who stay. When a parish prays for its priests and missionaries, it is doing what the Thessalonians were asked to do.

Matthew 9:18–26 · Your faith has made you well

Setting.

Matthew 8–9 gathers a series of mighty works after the Sermon on the Mount, showing that the Teacher also acts with authority. Here two stories are woven together, as they are in Mark 5 and Luke 8. Matthew tells them in very few words. He does not give the leader’s name, and in his account the girl has already died when her father comes.

Two details belong to the world of that time. A woman with a continuing flow of blood was ritually unclean under Leviticus 15:25–27, a state which touched her contact with others and with worship; it was not a judgment on her as a sinner. And the flute players belong to the mourning customs of the day: a later Jewish saying (Mishnah, Ketubot 4:4) expects even the poorest man to provide flutes and a mourner at his wife’s burial.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19: the father’s request, and Jesus gets up and goes.
  • vv. 20–22: on the way, the woman and the fringe of the cloak.
  • vv. 23–24: the mourners, and the word “sleeping”.
  • vv. 25–26: he takes her by the hand; the report spreads.

Key words.

  • kraspedon, fringe: The word can mean the edge of a garment or the tassels that the Law asked Israelites to wear as a reminder of the commandments (Numbers 15:38–40). Jesus appears here dressed as a faithful Jew.
  • sōzō, to make well, to save: The same Greek verb means both to heal and to save, and it comes three times in vv. 21–22. Her body is healed, and something larger has happened as well.
  • ēgerthē, got up: The ordinary verb for rising from bed, and also the verb Matthew uses of Jesus’ own Resurrection (28:6). The girl’s rising points beyond itself.

Verse by verse.

“for she said to herself, “If I only touch his cloak, I will be made well.”” (9:21)

Her faith is simple and even a little awkward. She wants to stay unseen. Jesus accepts such faith, but he does not leave her hidden.

“Jesus turned, and seeing her he said, “Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.”” (9:22)

He calls her “daughter”, in a story that began with another parent’s daughter. A woman who had suffered for twelve years is told she belongs to a family.

““Go away; for the girl is not dead but sleeping.” And they laughed at him.” (9:24)

Jesus is not denying that she died (v. 18). For him death is a sleep from which he can wake anyone. Compare Daniel 12:2 and what he says of Lazarus in John 11:11.

“he went in and took her by the hand, and the girl got up.” (9:25)

He says nothing in Matthew’s account. Touching a corpse made a person unclean (Numbers 19:11), but here the touch runs the other way: life passes from him to her.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the sick still try to touch Jesus, and that “in the sacraments Christ continues to ‘touch’ us in order to heal us” (CCC 1504). It also teaches that Jesus heard the prayer of faith already in his ministry, through signs that anticipate his death and Resurrection (CCC 2616). St Augustine, preaching on this woman as the other Gospels tell of her, contrasts the crowd that presses around Jesus with the one person who truly touches him: the crowd presses, faith touches (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was the girl really dead, if Jesus says she is sleeping?

Her father says she has just died (v. 18), and the mourners are already there. Matthew tells this as a raising from death. “Sleeping” is the biblical way of speaking of death in the light of God’s power. Early Christians would soon call their burial grounds “cemeteries”, from a Greek word for a place of sleep.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “the Lord is faithful”. When my own faith is weak, what helps me to rest on that?
  • The woman wanted to touch and remain hidden, and Jesus turned to speak to her. What would I say to him if he turned to me today?
