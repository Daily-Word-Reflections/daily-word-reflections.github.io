Word for the day

“Simon Peter answered him, “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.”” John 6:68

Reflection

Paul's letter to Corinth takes an unusual turn. Some in the community are questioning his authority, and he answers with a burst of questions: “Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Have I not seen Jesus our Lord? Are you not my work in the Lord?” His credentials are not a diploma but an encounter and a community: he has seen the Risen Lord, and the Corinthians themselves are “the seal of my apostleship in the Lord.” In the Season of the Apostles this matters to us. An apostle is one who has been sent by the Risen Christ, and the proof of the sending is the life of the Church that grows from it. Our own Church in Kerala is the seal of the apostleship of Mar Thoma, and every parish, every family that prays, every child who is baptized, is still that seal. Paul then defends a right: those who preach the Gospel may live by it. A soldier does not pay his own expenses, the one who plants a vineyard eats its fruit, the shepherd drinks the milk of the flock; and the Law says, “You shall not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain.” The Church has always taught that the faithful must help provide for the needs of the Church and of her ministers (CCC 2043).

Then comes the surprise. Having proved his right, Paul refuses to use it: “Nevertheless, we have not made use of this right, but we endure anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ.” He worked with his own hands as a tentmaker (Acts 18:3) so that no one could say he preached for money. Here is a principle for every Christian and every minister: what we are entitled to is not always what serves the Gospel. A priest may have a right to comfort, a parish committee to recognition, a parent to an apology, a worker to a holiday, and all may be just. But love sometimes lays a right aside so that Christ may be seen more clearly. The priests and religious who serve in our mission dioceses in North India, often in poverty and far from home, live this word of Paul every day. So do the parents who give up their own plans for a child with special needs. The question Paul puts to us is not ‘What am I owed?’ but ‘What might stand in the way of the Gospel, and can I remove it?’

The Gospel brings the long discourse on the Bread of Life to its crisis. Jesus has said, “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood have eternal life” (John 6:54), and many disciples find it too hard. “Because of this many of his disciples turned back and no longer went about with him.” Jesus does not run after them to soften his words; he does not explain the Eucharist away as a mere symbol. Instead he turns to the Twelve: “Do you also wish to go away?” And Simon Peter answers for them all: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God.” Peter does not say that he understands everything. He says that there is no one else to go to. That is often the faith of the Church in hard times: not a clever answer to every question, but a loyal heart that knows where life is found. The Catechism places this very scene at the heart of its teaching on the Eucharist: the Eucharist and the Cross are stumbling blocks, and the Lord's question echoes through the ages, inviting us to discover that only he has the words of eternal life (CCC 1336). The Gospel ends with a shadow: “Did I not choose you, the twelve? Yet one of you is a devil.” Even among the chosen, betrayal is possible. Two days before the feast of the Body and Blood of Christ, let us renew our faith in the Real Presence, and pray for those who have walked away, that they may hear again the question of Jesus and come back.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's reading Paul asks, “Am I not an apostle? Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?” He has met the Risen Lord, and the Church at Corinth is the proof of his mission.

Paul shows that those who preach the Gospel have a right to be supported, as a soldier, a farmer or a shepherd lives by his work. The Church still asks us to support her ministers and her mission.

But then Paul says that he gave up this right: “we endure anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ.” He worked with his own hands so that no one could say he preached for money.

In the Gospel Jesus has spoken about eating his Body and drinking his Blood. Many disciples find this too hard and leave. Jesus does not change his words. He asks the Twelve, “Do you also wish to go away?”

Peter answers, “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” Peter does not understand everything, but he knows that life is found only in Jesus.

On Thursday we celebrate the feast of the Body and Blood of Christ. Let us renew our faith that Jesus is truly present in the Holy Qurbana, and let us say with Peter: Lord, to whom can we go? Amen.

For children

When something is hard, stay close to Jesus like Peter. On Thursday we celebrate Jesus in the Holy Qurbana. Say thank you to him!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Holy One of God, you have the words of eternal life. When your teaching is hard and many turn away, keep us with you, and give us the loyal faith of Peter, who knew there was no one else to go to. Teach us, like your apostle Paul, to lay aside our rights rather than put an obstacle in the way of your Gospel, and to support generously those who proclaim it. Bless the missionaries of our Church who labour far from home. As we prepare for the feast of your Body and Blood, deepen our faith in your true presence, and bring back to your table all who have walked away. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about belonging to Christ when it costs something. Paul has every right to be supported by the Corinthians, yet he gives up that right so that nothing may hinder the Gospel. In John, many disciples leave Jesus after his teaching on the bread of life, and Peter, speaking for the Twelve, stays: “Lord, to whom can we go?” In the Season of the Apostles the Church learns from both: an apostle holds his rights loosely and holds on to Christ.

1 Corinthians 9:1–12 · Am I not an apostle?

Setting.

Paul has just told the Corinthians to give up eating food offered to idols if it would make a weaker brother fall (8:13). Now he shows that he lives by the same rule. Some at Corinth questioned whether he was truly an apostle, perhaps because he worked with his hands as a tentmaker (Acts 18:3) and refused their money. He answers that he has all the rights of an apostle, and has chosen not to use them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Paul’s credentials: he has seen the Lord, and the Corinthians themselves are the seal of his apostleship.
  • vv. 3–6: the rights of an apostle: food and drink, a companion, freedom from manual work.
  • vv. 7–10: proofs from daily life (soldier, vinedresser, shepherd) and from the law of Moses.
  • vv. 11–12: the conclusion: he has the right, but he has not used it, for the sake of the Gospel.

Key words.

  • apostolos, apostle: One who is sent with the authority of the sender. For Paul an apostle must have seen the Risen Lord (15:8) and been sent by him. That is why his first proof is: “Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?”
  • sphragis, seal: The mark pressed into wax to show that a document is genuine. The faith of the Corinthians is the Lord’s own stamp on Paul’s work.
  • exousia, right, authority: The word runs through the whole chapter. Paul does not deny his rights; he shows that love may lay them down. The same freedom marks his Master, who “emptied himself” (Philippians 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?” (9:1)

Paul saw the Risen Lord on the road to Damascus (Acts 9:3–6). His freedom is not freedom from service but freedom for it.

“Do we not have the right to be accompanied by a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas?” (9:5)

The Greek says literally “a sister, a wife”. Many read it of the apostles’ wives, and Peter was married (Mark 1:30). Some Fathers, among them St Jerome, read it of devout women who served the apostles, as women served Jesus (Luke 8:3). On “the brothers of the Lord”, see the question below.

“You shall not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain.” (9:9)

Paul quotes Deuteronomy 25:4. The law that cared for the working ox teaches, all the more, care for the one who labours for the Gospel.

“Nevertheless, we have not made use of this right, but we endure anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ.” (9:12)

This is the heart of the passage. The Gospel must never seem to be for sale.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always taught both halves of this reading: the faithful should support those who serve them in the Gospel (see Luke 10:7; Code of Canon Law, canon 222), and those who serve should not seek gain. The Second Vatican Council asks priests to use goods only for their right purposes and to embrace a voluntary poverty that makes them more like Christ (Presbyterorum Ordinis 17). St John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter, praises Paul for going beyond what was commanded, taking no wages where he could have taken them (in substance, Homilies on 1 Corinthians).

John 6:64–71 · To whom can we go?

Setting.

The long discourse in the synagogue at Capernaum is over (6:59). Jesus has said that he is the bread of life and that his flesh must be eaten and his blood drunk. Many who followed him find it too hard (6:60). These verses tell the result: a great falling away, and a small, firm confession of faith from the Twelve. John’s Gospel has no scene at Caesarea Philippi; this is its counterpart, Peter’s confession.

Structure.

  • vv. 64–65: Jesus knows who do not believe; no one comes unless the Father grants it.
  • v. 66: many disciples turn back.
  • v. 67: the question to the Twelve: “Do you also wish to go away?”
  • vv. 68–69: Peter’s answer: the words of eternal life; the Holy One of God.
  • vv. 70–71: yet one of the chosen Twelve will betray him.

Key words.

  • ho hagios tou theou, the Holy One of God: A rare title, used elsewhere by an unclean spirit who knows who Jesus is (Mark 1:24). Peter confesses that Jesus belongs wholly to God and comes from him.
  • pepisteukamen kai egnōkamen, we have come to believe and know: Both verbs are in the Greek perfect tense: a faith begun in the past that still stands. In John, believing and knowing go together; faith is a way of knowing.
  • diabolos, devil: Literally “slanderer, one who sets apart”. Jesus does not say Judas was created evil. He was chosen and loved, and he let the adversary enter his heart (13:2, 27).

Verse by verse.

“Because of this many of his disciples turned back and no longer went about with him.” (6:66)

It is worth noticing that Jesus does not run after them to soften his words. He does not trade the truth of the Eucharist for numbers.

“So Jesus asked the twelve, “Do you also wish to go away?”” (6:67)

The question respects their freedom. Love that forces is not love.

“Simon Peter answered him, “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” (6:68)

Peter does not say he has understood everything. He says there is no one else. Trust comes before full understanding.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls this scene when it speaks of the Eucharist: the first announcement of the Eucharist divided the disciples, just as the announcement of the Passion scandalized them, and the Lord’s question “Will you also go away?” echoes through the ages as an invitation to discover that only he has the words of eternal life, and that to receive the gift of his Eucharist in faith is to receive the Lord himself (CCC 1336). St Augustine, preaching on this passage, admires Peter’s answer: he speaks for the Twelve, and he puts believing before knowing; we believe so that we may know (in substance, Tractates on John 27).

Questions people ask

Paul speaks of “the brothers of the Lord”. Did Jesus have brothers?

The Church has always believed that Mary remained a virgin. In Hebrew and Aramaic, and in the Bible’s Greek, “brother” is used for close relatives such as cousins (in the Hebrew of Genesis 13:8 Abram calls his nephew Lot his brother; NRSV kindred). James and Joseph, called brothers of Jesus (Matthew 13:55), are sons of another Mary (Matthew 27:56). The Catechism explains that these are close relations of Jesus, according to an Old Testament way of speaking (CCC 500).

For study and teaching

  • Paul gave up a right so that the Gospel would not be hindered. Is there a right or comfort I could lay down for the sake of someone’s faith?
  • When Christ’s teaching is hard for me, what helps me to say with Peter, “Lord, to whom can we go?”
