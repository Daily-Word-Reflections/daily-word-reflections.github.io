Word for the day

“It is the spirit that gives life; the flesh is useless. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.” John 6:63

Reflection

The Second Letter of Peter opens with one of the boldest sentences in the New Testament: “His divine power has given us everything needed for life and godliness, through the knowledge of him who called us by his own glory and goodness. Thus he has given us, through these things, his precious and very great promises, so that through them you may escape from the corruption that is in the world because of lust, and may become participants of the divine nature.” Participants of the divine nature: the Christian vocation is not only to be forgiven, nor only to be good, but to share in the very life of God. The Eastern Churches, our own among them, have always loved this verse. The Fathers summed it up in a famous phrase (in substance): the Son of God became what we are so that we might become what he is, not by nature but by grace. We do not cease to be creatures; we are taken up into the communion of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, as iron placed in the fire glows with the fire's heat while remaining iron. This happens first in Baptism, grows through the sacraments, above all the Holy Qurbana, and will be complete in glory.

Because God has given so much, Peter says, our response must be wholehearted: “For this very reason, you must make every effort to support your faith with goodness, and goodness with knowledge, and knowledge with self-control, and self-control with endurance, and endurance with godliness, and godliness with mutual affection, and mutual affection with love.” It is like a ladder, or like a chain in which each link holds the next. It begins with faith, the gift we received at the font, and it ends with love, the greatest gift. Between them stand the ordinary virtues of a Christian life: goodness in conduct, knowledge of the faith, self-control of tongue and appetite, endurance in trouble, godliness in prayer, the warm affection of brothers and sisters, and finally love that reaches even enemies. Grace and effort are not rivals here. God gives everything needed, and we must “make every effort.” Where these virtues are growing, Peter says, they “keep you from being ineffective and unfruitful.” And the one who lacks them “is short-sighted and blind, and is forgetful of the cleansing of past sins.” To forget our Baptism is to forget who we are.

The Gospel brings us to the end of the discourse on the bread of life. Jesus has said, “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood have eternal life” (John 6:54), and many of his disciples are shaken: “This teaching is difficult; who can accept it?” Jesus does not soften his words. He asks, “Does this offend you? Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?” and adds, “It is the spirit that gives life; the flesh is useless. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.” He is not taking back what he said about his flesh as true food. He is answering a crude, merely human way of hearing it, as if he meant pieces of meat. The flesh he gives is the flesh of the Son of Man who will ascend to the Father, filled with the life-giving Spirit. On its own, human understanding profits nothing here; only the Spirit opens the mystery. That is why, in the Anaphora of Addai and Mari, the priest prays that the Holy Spirit may come and rest upon the offering. Many disciples turned back that day (John 6:66), but Peter answered for the Twelve: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life” (John 6:68). Here the two readings meet. We become participants of the divine nature above all by receiving, in the Spirit, the Body and Blood of the Son of God, and each Communion is meant to lift us one rung higher on Peter's ladder, from faith toward love.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Peter tells us something astonishing: God has called us to “become participants of the divine nature” (2 Peter 1:4). Not just to be forgiven, not just to be good people, but to share the very life of God. That is our dignity as baptised Christians.

God has given us everything we need. But Peter says we must also “make every effort.” He gives us a ladder: faith, then goodness, knowledge, self-control, endurance, godliness, mutual affection, and at the top, love (2 Peter 1:5–7). We cannot skip the rungs in the middle.

In the Gospel, many disciples find Jesus' teaching about eating his flesh and drinking his blood too hard: “This teaching is difficult; who can accept it?” Jesus does not change his words. He says: “It is the spirit that gives life… The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life” (John 6:63). Only the Holy Spirit can open our hearts to this mystery.

Many turned away that day. But Peter said: “Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life.” When something in our faith is hard, let us not walk away. Let us stay with Jesus and ask the Spirit for light.

Every time we receive the Holy Qurbana, we receive the Body and Blood of the Son of God, and we share more deeply in God's own life. So let us climb Peter's ladder, rung by rung, from faith to love. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Peter gives us a ladder. The first step is faith. Then come being good, learning about God, controlling ourselves, being patient, praying, loving our family, and at the very top, loving everyone! This week, try to climb one step higher. And remember: in Holy Communion Jesus gives himself to help you climb.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of a life that only God can give. Second Peter says that God’s divine power has given us everything needed for life and godliness, so that we may become participants of the divine nature, and calls us to make every effort to let faith grow into love. In John, many disciples find Jesus’ teaching on the bread of life too hard, and Jesus answers: “It is the spirit that gives life.” God gives the gift, and his Spirit makes it fruitful in us; our part is to receive it and let it grow.

2 Peter 1:3–9 · Participants of the divine nature

Setting.

Second Peter presents itself as the testament of the apostle Peter, written near the end of his life (1:14). The Church receives it as part of the New Testament. Its authorship was questioned already in the early centuries, and many scholars today think it was written by a disciple in Peter’s name, handing on his teaching to a later generation; others defend Peter’s authorship. Its message is the same either way: hold fast to the faith received from the apostles.

The letter warns against false teachers who mocked the hope of Christ’s coming and lived carelessly (chapters 2–3). Today’s verses come first, as the positive foundation: God has given everything, so Christians must grow in a life that shows it.

Structure.

  • v. 3: God’s divine power has given us everything needed for life and godliness.
  • v. 4: through his promises we escape corruption and share the divine nature.
  • vv. 5–7: a ladder of virtues: faith, goodness, knowledge, self-control, endurance, godliness, mutual affection, love.
  • vv. 8–9: these keep us fruitful; without them we are short-sighted and forget the cleansing of our sins.

Key words.

  • theias koinōnoi physeōs, participants of the divine nature: One of the boldest phrases in the New Testament. It does not mean that we stop being creatures, but that by grace we truly share God’s own life, as children share their father’s life. The Fathers of the East and West built their teaching on our divinisation on this verse.
  • epichorēgeō, to support, supply: In Greek cities a chorēgos paid the costs of a chorus for a festival, generously. The verb came to mean to supply richly. God has supplied everything; the Christian, in turn, is to “supply” virtue upon virtue.
  • aretē, goodness, virtue: Excellence. The same word is used of God’s own “glory and goodness” in verse 3 and of the Christian’s goodness in verse 5. The virtue we grow in is a reflection of his.

Verse by verse.

“His divine power has given us everything needed for life and godliness” (1:3)

The letter begins with gift, not with demand. Everything that follows is a response to what God has already given.

“so that through them you may escape from the corruption that is in the world because of lust, and may become participants of the divine nature.” (1:4)

Two movements: escape from corruption and sharing in God’s life. Salvation is not only being forgiven but being drawn into God’s own life.

“you must make every effort to support your faith with goodness” (1:5)

Grace does not make us passive. Because God has given everything, we make every effort. Faith comes first, and love comes last in the list (v. 7), as its crown.

“For anyone who lacks these things is short-sighted and blind, and is forgetful of the cleansing of past sins.” (1:9)

A Christian who stops growing has forgotten his baptism. Remembering how much we have been forgiven makes us eager to grow.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it explains why the Word became flesh: to make us “partakers of the divine nature”; it adds St Athanasius’ famous words, that the Son of God became man so that we might become God (CCC 460). It teaches that Baptism makes the newly baptized a partaker of the divine nature (CCC 1265), and that grace is a participation in the life of God (CCC 1997). The virtues listed here are not our own achievement apart from God: they grow by grace, with our cooperation.

John 6:60–63 · The words I have spoken are spirit and life

Setting.

Jesus has just finished his discourse in the synagogue at Capernaum, in which he said that he is the bread of life and that his flesh is true food and his blood true drink (6:55). Now the scene changes: not the crowd but many of his own disciples find the teaching too hard.

Jesus does not take back what he said. Instead he points them higher: to his ascension, and to the Spirit who gives life. Only in the Spirit can his words be received.

Structure.

  • v. 60: many disciples: this teaching is difficult; who can accept it?
  • v. 61: Jesus knows they are complaining: does this offend you?
  • v. 62: what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?
  • v. 63: the spirit gives life; the flesh is useless; my words are spirit and life.

Key words.

  • sklēros, difficult: Literally hard, rough, harsh. The disciples do not say the teaching is unclear; they say it is hard to accept. The problem is not understanding but believing.
  • gongyzō, to complain: The same family of words is used of Israel grumbling in the wilderness (Exodus 16:7–8 in the Greek) and the same verb of the crowd earlier in this chapter (6:41). The disciples repeat the old murmuring over the manna.
  • zōopoioun, gives life: The Spirit is the giver of life, as the Creed says. The flesh of Jesus gives life because it is filled with the Spirit; human nature on its own cannot.

Verse by verse.

“This teaching is difficult; who can accept it?” (6:60)

The Greek can also mean “who can listen to him?” Some teachings of Christ are hard not because they are unclear but because they ask for faith.

“Does this offend you?” (6:61)

Jesus names their stumbling openly. He does not soften the teaching to keep followers.

“Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?” (6:62)

If they stumble at his words about his flesh, what will they say when that same flesh ascends to the Father? The bread of life is the body of the One who came down from heaven and will return there.

“It is the spirit that gives life; the flesh is useless. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.” (6:63)

Jesus is not denying what he just said about eating his flesh. “The flesh” here means human nature left to itself, our merely human way of understanding. His words are received only by the Spirit.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine explained that the disciples understood Jesus’ words in a fleshly way, as if he meant flesh cut up like meat in a market; but he gives his flesh in a way that is spiritual and life-giving (in substance, Tractates on John 27). The Catechism quotes St Thomas Aquinas: that the true Body and Blood of Christ are present in this sacrament cannot be apprehended by the senses, but only by faith (CCC 1381). In every Eucharistic prayer the Church calls upon the Holy Spirit, because it is the Spirit who gives life.

Questions people ask

Jesus says, “the flesh is useless.” Does this mean the Eucharist is only a symbol?

No. A few verses earlier he says, “my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink” (6:55). In verse 63 he does not say “my flesh is useless” but “the flesh”, meaning human nature without the Spirit and a merely human way of judging. The Church has always read these verses together: Christ’s flesh, filled with the Spirit and risen, truly gives life, and it is received in faith.

For study and teaching

  • Second Peter says God has given us “everything needed for life and godliness”. Which virtue in the list do I most need to “supply” this week?
  • When a teaching of Christ or his Church is hard for me, do I complain, walk away, or ask the Spirit to help me receive it?
