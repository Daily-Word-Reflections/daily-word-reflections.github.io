Readings

  • Epistle James 2:14–17 · Faith without works is dead

Word for the day

“Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and do not do what I tell you?” Luke 6:46

Reflection

James is the most practical writer in the New Testament, and today he asks a question that cannot be dodged: “What good is it, my brothers and sisters, if you say you have faith but do not have works? Can faith save you?” He answers with a scene that everyone can picture. “If a brother or sister is naked and lacks daily food, and one of you says to them, ‘Go in peace; keep warm and eat your fill,’ and yet you do not supply their bodily needs, what is the good of that?” The words in themselves are kind; they are even a blessing. But they cost nothing, and they leave the hungry still hungry. “So faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead.” James is not contradicting Paul, who teaches that we are saved by grace through faith and not by our own achievements. Paul himself speaks of “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6). The Church has always held the two together: faith is God's unmerited gift, and living faith shows itself in love, as a living tree shows itself in fruit. A faith that never feeds anyone, never forgives anyone, never visits anyone, is like a body without breath. There is a sharp irony in the words “Go in peace”. At the end of every Holy Qurbana the Church sends us out with a blessing of peace. That dismissal is not a polite goodbye; it is a mission. We have received the Body of the Lord who fed the hungry, and we are sent to do the same. The Risen Christ showed his disciples his wounds and then sent them out. Easter faith that stays inside the church walls has not yet understood Easter.

In the Gospel Jesus asks the same question in his own words: “Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and do not do what I tell you?” To call Jesus Lord is the heart of Christian faith; the first Christians were baptised into that confession. But the title must be matched by obedience, or it becomes empty flattery. Then Jesus tells a story that every builder understands. “I will show you what someone is like who comes to me, hears my words, and acts on them. That one is like a man building a house, who dug deeply and laid the foundation on rock; when a flood arose, the river burst against that house but could not shake it, because it had been well built.” Notice the three verbs: comes, hears, acts. And notice the hard work of digging deep, which no one sees once the house is finished. The other man also heard the words, but he “built a house on the ground without a foundation. When the river burst against it, immediately it fell, and great was the ruin of that house.” Both houses may have looked the same in the dry season. Only the flood showed the difference. Kerala has seen in recent years what floods and landslides can do to houses; the parable is not abstract for us. Every life will meet its flood: sickness, betrayal, failure, the death of someone we love, our own death. The question is what lies under the walls. The rock is Christ himself, the stone the builders rejected, which God raised up at Easter to be the cornerstone (Psalm 118:22). We dig down to that rock by hearing his word and doing it, day after day, in ordinary obedience. On this Saturday the Church looks with love to the Blessed Virgin Mary, the one who heard the word of God and did it most perfectly. When a woman in the crowd blessed the womb that bore Jesus, he answered, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” (Luke 11:28). Mary is blessed on both counts: she bore the Word in her body because she first received him in faith and obedience. Her “Here am I, the servant of the Lord” is a house built on rock.

Full homily

Dear friends, St James asks: what good is it if you say you have faith but do not have works? He imagines a brother or sister without clothes or food, and someone says: go in peace; keep warm and eat your fill. Kind words, but the hungry person is still hungry. So faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead (James 2:17).

This does not mean we earn heaven by our deeds. Faith is God's gift. But living faith works through love, as a living tree bears fruit. At the end of every Qurbana we are sent in peace. That is not a goodbye; it is a mission to feed and serve.

In the Gospel Jesus asks: why do you call me Lord, Lord, and do not do what I tell you? Then he tells of two builders. One dug deeply and laid the foundation on rock. The other built on the ground without a foundation. When the flood came, the first house stood, and the second fell with a great crash.

In the dry season the two houses looked the same. Only the flood showed the difference. Every life will meet floods. What matters is what lies underneath. The rock is Christ, the stone the builders rejected, whom God raised at Easter.

Today is Saturday, and we remember the Blessed Virgin Mary, who heard the word of God and did it. Let us ask her to help us dig deep: to hear Jesus and to act. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told of a house built on rock. When the flood came, it did not fall! When you listen to Jesus and obey him, you are building on rock.

Don't just say ‘I will help’. Help! Today, do one helpful thing at home without being asked.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen cornerstone, we call you Lord. Let our lives say it too. Save us from a faith that speaks kind words and leaves the hungry hungry. Give us the patience to dig deep, to hear your word and act on it, so that when the floods come, our house may stand on you. With the Blessed Virgin Mary, who heard the word of God and kept it, let us say each day: be it done to me according to your word. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

James asks what good it is to say “I have faith” and to send away a hungry brother or sister with kind words: faith without works is dead. In Luke, Jesus ends his sermon by asking why we call him “Lord, Lord” and do not do what he says, and he compares the one who hears and acts to a man who dug deep and built on rock. Both readings call for a faith that reaches the hands. Such works are not our own achievement; they are the fruit of grace in those who believe.

James 2:14–17 · Faith without works is dead

Setting.

The Letter of James is a practical letter, close in spirit to the Sermon on the Mount. It has already warned against favouring the rich in the assembly (2:1–7). Now it turns to a faith that speaks but does nothing.

James and Paul use the same words in different ways. Paul denies that we are justified by “works of the law” (Romans 3:28), meaning the law of Moses as a way to earn standing before God. James is speaking of the works of love that living faith produces. The Church holds both together.

Structure.

  • v. 14: two questions: what good is it? can that faith save?
  • vv. 15–16: an example: the hungry brother or sister and the empty blessing.
  • v. 17: the conclusion: faith by itself, without works, is dead.

Key words.

  • pistis, faith: In v. 14 the Greek has the article, “the faith” or “that faith”: the faith just described, which has no works. The POC brings this out: “this faith” (ഈ വിശ്വാസത്തിന്). James is not doubting the power of true faith.
  • erga, works: Not rituals, but deeds of mercy: clothing the naked, feeding the hungry (compare Matthew 25:35–36).
  • nekra, dead: Like a body without breath (2:26). Such faith may look whole, but it has no life in it.

Verse by verse.

“What good is it, my brothers and sisters, if you say you have faith but do not have works? Can faith save you?” (2:14)

The stress falls on “say”. James is speaking of a faith that is only talk.

“Go in peace; keep warm and eat your fill,” (2:16)

A blessing that costs nothing and gives nothing. The words are pious; the hunger remains.

“So faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead.” (2:17)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith without works is dead: when it is deprived of hope and love, faith does not fully unite the believer to Christ and does not make him a living member of his Body (CCC 1815, citing James 2:26). The Council of Trent taught that we are justified by grace, which we cannot earn, and that the justified, by keeping God's commandments and doing good works, grow in that justice by the same grace (Session 6). St Augustine put it simply: good works follow justification; they do not come before it (On Faith and Works, in substance).

Luke 6:46–49 · The house on the rock

Setting.

These verses close the Sermon on the Plain (6:20–49), Luke's shorter counterpart of Matthew's Sermon on the Mount. The sermon has taught love of enemies, mercy, not judging, and good fruit from a good heart. Now Jesus asks what we will do with it.

Luke's picture differs a little from Matthew's (7:24–27): one man digs deep to rock, and the danger is a river in flood bursting against the house.

Structure.

  • v. 46: the question: why call me “Lord” and not do what I say?
  • v. 47: the one who comes, hears and acts.
  • v. 48: the house dug deep, set on rock, unshaken by the flood.
  • v. 49: the house without a foundation, and its great ruin.

Key words.

  • Kyrie, Kyrie, Lord, Lord: The title Christians gave the risen Jesus, and the cry of the Church's prayer. The words are right; Jesus asks that the life match them.
  • ebathynen, he dug deep: Literally “he went deep”. Building on rock costs digging. The foundation is hidden, but everything depends on it.
  • themelios, foundation: Paul uses the same word: “no one can lay any foundation other than the one that has been laid; that foundation is Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 3:11).

Verse by verse.

“Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and do not do what I tell you?” (6:46)
“That one is like a man building a house, who dug deeply and laid the foundation on rock;” (6:48)

Two men may build houses that look alike. The difference is below the ground, and it shows only in the flood.

“the river burst against that house but could not shake it, because it had been well built.” (6:48)

The flood comes to both houses. Faith does not spare us the flood; it keeps us standing in it.

“When the river burst against it, immediately it fell, and great was the ruin of that house.” (6:49)

The sermon ends on a warning, not on a threat: Jesus is telling us the truth while there is time to dig.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of prayer, recalls that the prayer of faith does not consist only in saying “Lord, Lord”, but in disposing the heart to do the will of the Father (CCC 2611). Pope Benedict XVI wrote that those who build their lives on the word of God build on rock that does not give way (Verbum Domini, in substance). Every Holy Qurbana sends us out to do what we have heard.

Questions people ask

Do James and Paul contradict each other?

No. Paul teaches that we cannot earn God's favour; justification is the gift of grace received in faith (Ephesians 2:8–9). James teaches that true faith is alive and acts in love. Paul himself speaks of “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6). The works of the justified are themselves God's gift and also truly ours, and by grace they please God.

For study and teaching

  • James describes a blessing that costs nothing. Whose need have I answered only with words, and what one practical thing can I do this week?
  • Where in my life am I building without digging deep, and what would it mean to put my foundation on the Lord's words?
