Word for the day

“Then Jesus answered her, “Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” And her daughter was healed instantly.” Matthew 15:28

Reflection

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison, not knowing whether he will live or die. He does not ask them to get him released. He asks for something harder: “Only, live your life in a manner worthy of the gospel of Christ.” The Greek word Paul uses means to live as a citizen; Philippi was a Roman colony, proud of its citizenship, and Paul tells them that they belong to a greater city, and must live by its laws. A Christian's daily conduct is the visible face of the Gospel. Paul hopes to hear “that you are standing firm in one spirit, striving side by side with one mind for the faith of the gospel, and are in no way intimidated by your opponents.” Then he says something no one could invent: “For he has graciously granted you the privilege not only of believing in Christ, but of suffering for him as well.” Suffering for Christ is called a gift of grace, a privilege, like faith itself. The apostles in Jerusalem had understood this when, after being flogged, “they rejoiced that they were considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name” (Acts 5:41). Easter does not remove the Cross from the Christian life; it changes its meaning. Those who suffer with the Risen Lord share his victory.

In the Gospel Jesus goes north into the district of Tyre and Sidon, outside the land of Israel. A woman comes out to meet him, and Matthew calls her a Canaanite, using the oldest name for the peoples who lived in the land before Israel: she is as much an outsider as anyone could be. Yet her first words are a confession of faith: “Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David; my daughter is tormented by a demon.” What follows is one of the hardest scenes in the Gospels. “But he did not answer her at all.” The disciples want her sent away. Jesus says he was sent “only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” She will not go. “But she came and knelt before him, saying, ‘Lord, help me.’” Three words, one of the shortest prayers in the Bible and perhaps the most prayed. Jesus answers with a proverb that sounds harsh: “It is not fair to take the children's food and throw it to the dogs.” And she, with a mother's courage and quick wit, turns his own words into her plea: “Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table.”

“Then Jesus answered her, ‘Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.’ And her daughter was healed instantly.” Only twice in Matthew's Gospel does Jesus single out someone's faith for such praise, and both times the person is a Gentile: the centurion at Capernaum and this Canaanite mother. The silence and the hard words were not rejection; they drew out of her a faith that grew stronger with every refusal. The Fathers saw in her the whole Gentile world, waiting at the edge of Israel's table for the bread of life, and receiving not crumbs but the whole loaf. After the Resurrection the Lord will send his apostles to “make disciples of all nations” (Matthew 28:19); the Gospel came to India with Saint Thomas, and the Church of the Saint Thomas Christians is one of the fruits of that mission. Every Holy Qurbana is that table: the children's bread is now given to all who come in faith. And every mother who has prayed for years for a sick or wandering child can stand beside this woman. God's silence is not his absence. Keep kneeling; keep saying, Lord, help me.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul writes from prison, not knowing if he will live. He does not ask the Philippians to get him out. He asks: live your life in a manner worthy of the gospel of Christ.

Our daily behaviour is the visible face of the Gospel. And Paul says something surprising: God has granted you the privilege not only of believing in Christ, but of suffering for him. After Easter the Cross remains, but it has a new meaning. Those who suffer with the Risen Lord share his victory.

In the Gospel a Canaanite woman, a complete outsider, cries out: Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David; my daughter is tormented by a demon. Jesus does not answer. The disciples want her sent away.

But she kneels and says: Lord, help me. When Jesus speaks of the children's bread, she answers: Yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table. She will not give up.

Jesus says: Woman, great is your faith! And her daughter is healed. His silence drew out a greater faith. And now, at the Holy Qurbana, the children's bread is given to all who come in faith, from every nation.

If you have been praying a long time for a child or for someone you love, stand beside this mother. God's silence is not his absence. Keep kneeling. Keep saying: Lord, help me. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, a mother came to Jesus because her little girl was sick. Jesus did not answer at first, but she kept asking: ‘Lord, help me!’ And Jesus healed her daughter.

When you pray and nothing seems to happen, don't stop! Keep saying: ‘Jesus, help me.’ He always hears you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of David, risen Saviour of all nations, you heard the cry of the Canaanite mother and praised her great faith. Have mercy on us, and help us. Give us her humility and her perseverance, so that we do not give up when you seem silent. Make us live in a manner worthy of your Gospel, standing firm together and unafraid, and count it a grace to suffer for your name. Feed us at your table with the bread of your children, and send us to all who wait for it. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about faith that holds on under pressure. Paul, writing from prison, tells the Philippians that it is a gift of grace not only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for him. In the Gospel a Gentile mother will not stop asking, even when Jesus seems to refuse her, and he praises her: “Woman, great is your faith!” The risen Lord does not promise an easy road, but he gives a faith that does not let go. The words to watch are worthy, granted and crumbs.

Philippians 1:27–30 · Not only believing, but suffering for him

Setting.

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison (1:13), not knowing whether he will live or die (1:20–24). Philippi was a Roman colony in Macedonia, proud of its citizenship. It was the first city in Europe where Paul preached, and there he and Silas were beaten and thrown into prison (Acts 16:19–24). The believers had seen his struggle with their own eyes. Now they face opposition of their own.

Structure.

  • v. 27: live as citizens worthy of the Gospel, standing firm and striving together.
  • v. 28: do not be frightened by opponents; God is at work.
  • vv. 29–30: suffering for Christ is a gift, and they share Paul’s own struggle.

Key words.

  • politeuesthe, live your life: Literally “live as citizens”. In a city proud of its Roman rights, Paul reminds them of another citizenship: “our citizenship is in heaven” (3:20).
  • synathlountes, striving side by side: A word from the games: athletes struggling together as a team. The Christian life is not a solo race.
  • echaristhē, has graciously granted: From the same root as charis, grace. Suffering for Christ is counted among the gifts of grace, not as a punishment.

Verse by verse.

“Only, live your life in a manner worthy of the gospel of Christ” (1:27)

Paul does not first say “believe the right things”, but “live worthily”. The Gospel is meant to be seen in the way the community lives.

“and are in no way intimidated by your opponents.” (1:28)

The Greek word was used of a horse that shies in fright. Courage here is not a natural temperament but a fruit of trust that “this is God’s doing”.

“For he has graciously granted you the privilege not only of believing in Christ, but of suffering for him as well” (1:29)

Faith itself is a gift; so is the strength to suffer with Christ. Paul does not seek suffering for its own sake. He sees that a disciple shares the road of the Master.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours the martyrs as those who bore the highest witness to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473). Paul’s words also console all who suffer in many lands for the name of Christ today. The Church prays for them, and for those who wrong them, as Jesus taught (Matthew 5:44).

Matthew 15:21–28 · Woman, great is your faith

Setting.

Jesus goes into the region of Tyre and Sidon, Gentile cities on the coast north of Galilee. Mark calls the woman “a Gentile, of Syrophoenician origin” (Mark 7:26). Matthew calls her “a Canaanite”, an old name that recalls the peoples who lived in the land before Israel. In Matthew’s Gospel Jesus has already sent the Twelve only to “the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:5–6). After the Resurrection he will send the apostles to all nations (28:19). This woman stands between those two moments.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–22: the woman cries out for her daughter.
  • v. 23: Jesus is silent; the disciples want her sent away.
  • v. 24: “I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.”
  • vv. 25–27: she kneels, asks again, and answers his saying about the dogs.
  • v. 28: Jesus praises her faith, and her daughter is healed.

Key words.

  • eleēson me, have mercy on me: The cry of the needy in the Gospels, and the cry the Church has never stopped praying: Kyrie eleison, Lord, have mercy.
  • kynaria, dogs: A diminutive: the little dogs of the house, under the table. The saying is still hard, and the woman does not deny it. She takes it up and finds room in it for herself.
  • megalē sou hē pistis, great is your faith: Only two people in Matthew are praised for a faith so great, and both are Gentiles: this woman and the centurion (8:10).

Verse by verse.

“Have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David; my daughter is tormented by a demon.” (15:22)

A Gentile woman calls Jesus by a title of Israel’s Messiah. She also makes her daughter’s pain her own: “have mercy on me”.

“But she came and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, help me.”” (15:25)

The shortest prayer in the passage, and perhaps the most complete. Silence and refusal do not stop her; they bring her closer.

“Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters’ table.” (15:27)

She does not argue against Jesus; she agrees with him, and then trusts that his table is so rich that even the crumbs are enough.

“Woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you wish.” (15:28)

How the Church reads it.

St John Chrysostom says that Jesus delayed his answer not to hurt the woman but to bring her faith into the light, so that all could see what was hidden in her (Homilies on Matthew, in substance). The Church has always loved her humble persistence as a model of prayer. The Catechism teaches that Jesus hears the prayer of faith, and among its examples is the Canaanite woman who asks for her daughter (CCC 2616). Her words about the crumbs can teach us how to come to the Holy Qurbana: we come not because we deserve the table, but because the Lord is generous.

Questions people ask

Was Jesus being harsh to the woman?

His words sound hard, and Matthew does not soften them. But the story moves from silence to praise. Jesus draws out a faith that the disciples, who wanted her sent away, did not see. Her daughter is healed, and the woman is held up for all generations as a model. The story also shows the order of God’s plan: first to Israel, then through Israel to the nations.

For study and teaching

  • Paul calls suffering for Christ a gift of grace. Is there a hardship in my life that I could offer to the Lord in this light?
  • The woman kept asking when the answer seemed to be no. For whom do I need to keep praying with her persistence?
