Word for the day

“For the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name. His mercy is for those who fear him from generation to generation.” Luke 1:49–50

Reflection

On 8 December 1854 Pope Pius IX defined as a dogma revealed by God that “the most Blessed Virgin Mary, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin” (Ineffabilis Deus). Mary was redeemed too, but in a more sublime way: preserved rather than cleansed. The Eastern tradition has long honoured her as “all-holy” (Panagia) and “all-pure”.

The readings prepare a dwelling for the Word. Wisdom says: “The Creator of all things… chose the place for my tent. He said, ‘Make your dwelling in Jacob.’” Isaiah promises the sign of Immanuel, born of the young woman. Hebrews proclaims that “in these last days God has spoken to us by a Son,” who became like us in every respect. A pure dwelling was prepared for the Son of God: the Immaculate Virgin.

At the very beginning, after the Fall, God promised enmity between the serpent and the woman (Genesis 3:15). The Church sees in Mary the woman in whom the serpent never had a share. And in the Magnificat Mary herself gives the reason for her greatness: not her merits, but God's mercy. “He has looked with favour on the lowliness of his servant… the Mighty One has done great things for me.”

For us the feast is a feast of hope: what God did in Mary at the beginning, he wants to accomplish in us at the end, presenting us “holy and blameless before him in love” (Ephesians 1:4).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, today we celebrate the Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Many people confuse this feast with the virginal conception of Jesus. Today's feast is about Mary herself: from the first moment of her life in her mother's womb, God preserved her from original sin.

Why? Because God was preparing a home for his Son. The book of Sirach describes Wisdom looking for a place to pitch its tent. Isaiah promised a sign: the young woman will bear a son, Immanuel. When the Son of God came, he needed a mother, and God prepared her with a singular grace.

The Church teaches that Mary was preserved from sin in view of the merits of Jesus Christ. So Mary too was saved by her Son, but in a wonderful way: not lifted out of the mud, but kept from falling into it. That is why the angel called her “full of grace”.

And Mary herself tells us the secret of her greatness in the Magnificat: “He has looked with favour on the lowliness of his servant… the Mighty One has done great things for me.” Not pride, but gratitude. Everything is God's mercy.

What God did in Mary at the beginning, he wants to do in us at the end: to make us holy and blameless before him in love. So in this season, let us prepare a clean heart for Jesus by a good confession. Holy Mary, conceived without sin, pray for us who have recourse to you. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, the early Christians did not have the dogma of the Immaculate Conception in its later words, but they had a deep intuition about Mary, drawn from the Scriptures. Let us look at two images that the Fathers loved: Mary as the new Eve, and Mary as the Ark of the Covenant.

In the garden, Eve listened to the serpent and believed his lie. Through her disobedience, sin and death entered the world. But God promised that the offspring of a woman would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). In Nazareth another woman listened, not to a serpent but to an angel, and said: here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word (Luke 1:38). St Irenaeus wrote that the knot of Eve's disobedience was untied by Mary's obedience. Where Eve said no, Mary said yes.

The second image is the Ark of the Covenant. God commanded Moses to make it of acacia wood, covered with pure gold inside and out (Exodus 25:10–11). Inside were the tablets of the law, a jar of manna and Aaron's rod (Hebrews 9:4). When the Ark entered a house, it brought blessing; David danced before it. Mary carried in her womb the Word of God, the true Bread from heaven and the eternal High Priest. When she entered Elizabeth's house, the child leaped for joy (Luke 1:41). If the Ark had to be pure gold, how much more the living Ark of the Son of God.

This is what the Church proclaims today: that from the first moment of her conception Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin, by a singular grace of God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ. She too was saved by her Son, but in a more exalted way: preserved rather than cleansed.

And what God did perfectly for Mary, He wants to begin in us. In Baptism we were washed; in confession we are cleansed again; in the Holy Qurbana we receive Christ as Mary did. In these days before Christmas, let us prepare a clean dwelling for Him. Make a good confession before Christmas, and ask Mary Immaculate to teach you to say yes.

Dear brothers and sisters, many Catholics think that today's feast is about the conception of Jesus in Mary's womb. It is not. That we celebrate on 25 March, the Annunciation. Today we celebrate the conception of Mary herself in the womb of her mother, whom tradition names Anne.

On 8 December 1854, Pope Pius IX solemnly defined that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God, and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ the Saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin (CCC 491). Every word matters. Singular grace: it was God's gift, not Mary's achievement. In view of the merits of Jesus Christ: Mary too was saved by her Son.

Where is this in the Bible? The angel greets Mary as full of grace (Luke 1:28). The Greek word means one who has been, and remains, filled with God's favour. The Church understood that the woman chosen to be the Mother of God was prepared by grace from the beginning. The Catechism says that Mary was redeemed from the moment of her conception, and that by God's grace she remained free of every personal sin her whole life long (CCC 492–493). The Eastern Fathers call her Panagia, the All-Holy.

Four years after the definition, in 1858, Our Lady appeared to a poor girl, Bernadette, at Lourdes. When Bernadette asked her name, she answered: I am the Immaculate Conception. Bernadette did not understand the words, but the parish priest did. Heaven confirmed what the Church had taught.

Why does this matter? Because it shows what God's grace can do. Mary is not a goddess; she is a creature, entirely filled with grace. She shows what the Church is called to become: holy and without blemish (Ephesians 5:27). So let us honour her this Advent with the Rosary, and ask her to help us prepare for her Son at this Holy Qurbana and at Christmas.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today, in the second week of the season of Annunciation, the Church honours the grace God gave to Mary at the first instant of her existence: she was preserved from every stain of original sin, by the merits of the Son she would one day bear. No reading states this doctrine in so many words. Together they prepare the ground for it. In Sirach, Wisdom looks for a resting place and God chooses her tent. In Isaiah, the Lord himself promises a sign: a woman and a son called Immanuel. In Hebrews, the Son who is the reflection of God's glory takes hold of our human race. In Luke, Mary herself sings and gives all the credit to “God my Savior”. The line runs from a tent, to a mother, to the Son, to her song, and its key words are dwelling, sign and mercy.

Sirach 24:1–14 · Wisdom looks for a resting place

Setting.

Ben Sira, the son of Sirach, taught in Jerusalem about 180 BC. His grandson translated the book into Greek in Egypt some decades later, and tells us so in his prologue. Parts of the Hebrew have been recovered, but this chapter is known to us mainly through the Greek. Chapter 24 stands near the centre of the book. Wisdom, spoken of as a woman, praises herself before the heavenly court.

The speech has a clear aim. Greek culture was attractive to Jews of that time, and Ben Sira answers that the highest wisdom is not far away. A few verses after our reading he identifies her with the Law given through Moses (24:23). The one who ruled the whole world was given her home in Israel.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: introduction: Wisdom speaks among her people and before the hosts of the Most High.
  • vv. 3–7: her origin and her search: from God's mouth, over all the earth, looking for a place to rest.
  • vv. 8–12: God's command: she pitches her tent in Jacob and is established in Zion.
  • vv. 13–14: she grows like the great trees of the land, from Lebanon and Hermon to En-gedi and Jericho.

Key words.

  • sophia, wisdom: Here wisdom is more than cleverness. She is the plan and the skill by which God made and guides the world, spoken of as a person. The New Testament will call Christ “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24).
  • skēnē, tent: The word for Israel's tent of meeting in the Greek Old Testament. John uses the related verb when he says the Word “lived among us”, literally pitched his tent (John 1:14).
  • anapausis, resting place: She who rules every nation is still looking for somewhere to settle (v. 7). God's plan is told as a search for a home.

Verse by verse.

“Among all these I sought a resting place; in whose territory should I abide?” (24:7)

The great lady of the universe asks a homeless person's question. It prepares the surprise of the next verse.

“Then the Creator of all things gave me a command, and my Creator chose the place for my tent.” (24:8)

Wisdom does not choose her own home; God chooses it for her. On this feast the Church hears a likeness: before the Word came, God prepared the place where he would dwell.

“I took root in an honored people, in the portion of the Lord, his heritage.” (24:12)

The image moves from a tent to a tree. What came down from above now grows in the soil of one people. The Son of God too will have his roots in Israel, through a daughter of Israel.

How the Church reads it.

The first and literal sense of the chapter concerns Wisdom and the Law. Christians came to see its fullness in Christ, the Word who pitched his tent among us. The Church also applies such passages to Mary, not because Mary is divine Wisdom, but because she is the place where Wisdom came to live. The Catechism says that the Church's Tradition has often read the most beautiful texts on wisdom in relation to Mary, who is acclaimed in the liturgy as the “Seat of Wisdom” (CCC 721).

Isaiah 7:10–16 · The Lord himself will give you a sign

Setting.

The year is about 734 BC. Rezin king of Aram and Pekah king of Israel have marched against Jerusalem to force King Ahaz of Judah into their alliance against Assyria (7:1–6; 2 Kings 16:5–9). Their threat is to remove Ahaz and put another man on David's throne. Isaiah has already told the king to stand firm: “If you do not stand firm in faith, you shall not stand at all” (7:9). Ahaz has privately decided to buy help from Assyria instead.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: God offers Ahaz any sign he wants, as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven.
  • v. 12: the king refuses, with pious words.
  • v. 13: Isaiah's rebuke to “the house of David”.
  • v. 14: the sign: a woman, a son, a name.
  • vv. 15–16: before the child can tell good from evil, the two enemy kingdoms will be deserted.

Key words.

  • ’ôt, sign: A visible token that God is at work. Ahaz refuses the sign, and God gives it anyway, to the whole house of David.
  • ‘almâ, young woman: A young woman of marriageable age. The word does not itself specify virginity. The Greek Old Testament translated it parthenos, “virgin”, and Matthew 1:23 quotes that Greek. The Malayalam POC here has “young woman”.
  • ‘immānû ’ēl, God is with us: A name and a promise. God has not abandoned David's house, even when its king will not trust him.

Verse by verse.

“But Ahaz said, I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test.” (7:12)

It sounds devout (compare Deuteronomy 6:16), but it hides a decision already made. God himself had told him to ask.

“Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel.” (7:14)

Scholars discuss who the woman was in Ahaz's own day; many think of a royal wife, whose son would continue David's line. The prophecy says more than that first horizon could hold.

“For before the child knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land before whose two kings you are in dread will be deserted.” (7:16)

The sign has a short-term point: the threat will soon pass. The curds and honey of v. 15 are probably the food of a land laid waste (see 7:21–22).

How the Church reads it.

Matthew reads this verse as fulfilled when Mary conceives by the Holy Spirit and bears Jesus, God with us (Matthew 1:22–23). The Catechism sees here the fulfilment of the divine promise given through Isaiah (CCC 497). The sign refused by a king of David's line is received by a humble woman of David's people. The verse speaks directly of the Son's coming, not of Mary's own beginning, but the feast reads the two together: the mother of Immanuel was prepared by God for this mission (CCC 490).

Hebrews 1:1–3; 2:16–18 · He spoke by a Son who became our brother

Setting.

Hebrews is a sermon to tired Christians (“a word of exhortation”, 13:22). Its author is not named. It opens with one long, carefully built Greek sentence (1:1–4), among the most polished in the New Testament. Today's reading joins that opening to the close of chapter 2, so that the Son of glory and the brother who suffers stand side by side.

Structure.

  • 1:1–2a: two times of speaking: long ago by the prophets, now by a Son.
  • 1:2b–3: who the Son is: heir, agent of creation, reflection of glory; and what he did: purification, and his seat at God's right hand.
  • 2:16–18: why he became one of us: to be a merciful and faithful high priest.

Key words.

  • apaugasma, reflection, radiance: Light shining out from its source. The Son is not a second light apart from the Father. The Council of Nicaea says “Light from Light”.
  • charaktēr, exact imprint: The mark a seal leaves in wax or a die on a coin. To see the Son is to see what the Father is.
  • epilambanetai, he takes hold of: The verb in 2:16 means to take hold of. The NRSV gives “come to help”; the POC gives “took to himself”. St John Chrysostom took it to mean that the Son took on our nature, not that of angels (in substance). Both readings fit what follows.

Verse by verse.

“but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son,” (1:2)

In Greek there is no “the” here: “by a Son”, one who is Son by nature. The prophets spoke God's word; the Son is that Word.

“He is the reflection of God’s glory and the exact imprint of God’s very being,” (1:3)

The two images say what the Church would later state at Nicaea: the Son is one in being with the Father.

“Therefore he had to become like his brothers and sisters in every respect,” (2:17)

The Son of glory wants a human body, a human family and a human mother. This is why Mary matters: the flesh of the eternal Son was taken from her.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism uses these verses to teach that in his Son, God has said everything (CCC 65). It uses Hebrews 1:3 for the Son's oneness with the Father (CCC 241). The Council of Chalcedon teaches that he is like us in all things except sin. The feast adds a further thought: the one who would be like us without sin received his flesh from a mother whom God had kept from sin by his grace (CCC 490–492).

Luke 1:46–55 · God my Savior

Setting.

Mary has travelled to Elizabeth's house and has been called blessed. Luke gives her answer as a song, which the Latin tradition calls the Magnificat, from its first word. It is close to the song of Hannah, who also received a child from God (1 Samuel 2:1–10), and it is woven from the Psalms and the Prophets. Pope Benedict XVI wrote that the Magnificat is entirely woven from threads of Holy Scripture (Deus Caritas Est 41).

Structure.

  • vv. 46–49: what God has done for Mary.
  • v. 50: his mercy for all who fear him, in every generation.
  • vv. 51–53: what God does in the world: he overturns the proud and lifts up the lowly.
  • vv. 54–55: his promise to Israel, from Abraham onward.

Key words.

  • megalynei, magnifies: To declare great. Mary does not add to God's greatness; she makes it known. The song begins with God and stays with him.
  • sōtēr, Savior: The key word for today. Mary calls God her own Savior. The Immaculate Conception does not take her out of the circle of the redeemed. She is its first and most complete fruit.
  • tapeinōsis, lowliness: Low condition, not the virtue of humility as such. The Greek Old Testament uses it for Hannah's affliction (1 Samuel 1:11). God looks on Mary's littleness.

Verse by verse.

“and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior,” (1:47)

The Church does not say that Mary had no need of a Saviour. She needed him as all do, and she was saved by being kept from sin, not by being cleansed after it (CCC 491–492).

“for he has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant.” (1:48)

This is the whole theology of the feast in one line: the initiative is God's. Mary's greatness starts in the way God looked at her.

“for the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name.” (1:49)

“The Mighty One” echoes Old Testament language for the God who saves (compare Zephaniah 3:17). The “great things” are not Mary's achievements. They are God's work in her.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls the Magnificat the song both of the Mother of God and of the Church (CCC 2619). Pope Pius IX defined the Immaculate Conception in Ineffabilis Deus (8 December 1854): by a singular grace, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ the Saviour, Mary was preserved from all stain of original sin from the first instant of her conception (CCC 491). St Ambrose, preaching on this passage, wishes that the soul of Mary might be in each of us to magnify the Lord, and her spirit in each of us to rejoice in God (in substance). The grace given to her at the start is the grace promised to all of us at the end.

Questions people ask

Is the Immaculate Conception the same as the virgin birth?

No. They are often confused. The Immaculate Conception concerns Mary's own beginning in her mother's womb: she came into being through her parents in the natural way, and from that first moment she was kept from original sin. The virginal conception concerns Jesus, conceived in Mary by the Holy Spirit (Luke 1:35; CCC 496–497).

If Mary was without sin, did she need Jesus as Saviour?

Yes, and she says so herself (Luke 1:47). St Thomas Aquinas hesitated over the doctrine for this very reason, wanting to guard Christ as the Saviour of all. Blessed John Duns Scotus helped the Church see the answer: to be preserved from a fall is a more perfect rescue than to be lifted up after it. So the Catechism says she was “redeemed in a more exalted fashion” in view of her Son's merits (CCC 492; Lumen Gentium 53).

Where is this doctrine in the Bible?

No verse states it in so many words. The Church reads it in the enmity between the woman and the serpent (Genesis 3:15), in the angel's greeting “favored one” (Luke 1:28), and in the role of the mother of the Holy One. The Fathers praised Mary's holiness, especially in the East (CCC 493). The Church's understanding grew over the centuries, and the doctrine was solemnly defined in 1854.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syriac tradition has sung Mary's purity since its earliest poets. St Ephrem the Syrian (4th century) says to the Lord that he and his Mother alone are entirely beautiful, for there is no blemish in him and no stain in his Mother (in substance, Nisibene Hymns 27). He did not use later Western terms, but the love and the wonder are the same. Among St Thomas Christians the Carmelites of Mary Immaculate, the congregation of St Kuriakose Elias Chavara, carry this title in their very name. In the season of Annunciation, we keep this feast while waiting for the birth of her Son. At the Holy Qurbana we too receive him, and ask to be made a fitting dwelling.
