Word for the day

“And the king will answer them, ‘Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.’” Matthew 25:40

Reflection

Today the Syro-Malabar Church keeps the feast of one of her own daughters, St Mariam Thresia, the saint of the families of Kerala. She was born on 26 April 1876 at Puthenchira, in the present district of Thrissur, into the Chiramel Mankidiyan family, an old Christian family that had fallen on hard times. She lost her mother while still a girl. From childhood she was drawn to prayer and penance, and she longed to give herself entirely to the Lord. But the path God opened for her was not the one she first imagined. She dreamed of a hidden life of prayer; God led her instead into the homes of her neighbours, to the sick, the dying, the poor and the troubled families of her village. She died on 8 June 1926 at Kuzhikattussery, was beatified by St John Paul II on 9 April 2000, and was canonised by Pope Francis on 13 October 2019. The readings chosen for her feast trace the shape of her life.

The first reading is the call of Abraham: “Now the Lord said to Abram, ‘Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you.’” Abram did not receive a map; he received a promise. “So Abram went, as the Lord had told him.” Mariam Thresia knew that kind of obedience. She did not leave her country, but she left the security of the ordinary road for a young woman of her time and place, and she stepped into a land God showed her one step at a time. With three companions she began to go from house to house, nursing the sick, washing the bodies of those who died alone, praying with families in trouble, bringing quarrelling relatives to reconciliation, and teaching children their prayers. Such a life was new and surprising in the villages around Puthenchira, and not everyone understood it. Like Abraham, she went out not knowing where she was going (Hebrews 11:8), and like Abraham she became a blessing: “in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” The words fit her well: through her, countless families have been blessed.

Sirach gives the second key: “My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing… For gold is tested in the fire, and those found acceptable, in the furnace of humiliation.” Her life was full of testing. She passed through long seasons of spiritual darkness and through fierce trials that she and her spiritual director, Fr Joseph Vithayathil, understood as attacks of the evil one. Her bishop, Mar John Menachery, Vicar Apostolic of Trichur, examined her experiences carefully and with prudence, as the Church must, and for a time she was misunderstood and doubted. She accepted all this with humility and obedience. Those who knew her also spoke of a deep union with the sufferings of Christ, which she asked the Lord to keep hidden. The Epistle puts words to that hidden centre of her life: “I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me. And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me.” Her many works were not the activity of a restless social worker. They flowed from a life hidden with Christ.

Then comes the Gospel of the last judgment, which reads like a description of her days: “I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was naked and you gave me clothing, I was sick and you took care of me, I was in prison and you visited me.” She saw Christ in the poorest and the most neglected, and she served him there. On 14 May 1914, with the approval of Mar John Menachery, the community that had gathered around her was erected as the Congregation of the Holy Family (CHF), with Kuzhikattussery as its first home. Its charism is the one she lived: to serve families, especially families in difficulty, so that every home may become a Nazareth. Today her sisters serve in Kerala, in other parts of India and abroad, in homes, schools, hospitals, hostels and among the elderly and the sick.

The weekday Gospel of the Fourth Tuesday, the story of Martha and Mary (Luke 10:38–42), fits her feast as well. In Mariam Thresia the two sisters were joined. She sat at the Lord's feet for long hours of prayer, and she rose from prayer to serve in homes like Martha's. “Mary has chosen the better part”: she chose the Lord first, and that is why her service did not become anxious and distracted. In this Season of the Apostles she reminds us that the apostolate begins at home. The Holy Spirit who sent the Twelve to the nations sent this woman of Puthenchira to the family next door. As schools reopen and families are tested by money worries, illness, migration, addiction and quarrels, her feast is an invitation to pray for our homes and to visit the families around us who are carrying burdens alone.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate St Mariam Thresia, a daughter of our own Church, born in 1876 at Puthenchira near Thrissur. She longed for a hidden life of prayer, but God sent her into the homes of her neighbours. She died on 8 June 1926, and in 2019 Pope Francis declared her a saint.

The first reading tells us how God called Abraham: “Go from your country… to the land that I will show you” (Genesis 12:1). Abraham did not know the way; he only trusted. Mariam Thresia also stepped onto a new road. With her companions she went from house to house to care for the sick, to pray with the dying, to make peace in quarrelling families and to teach children their prayers.

Sirach says, “when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing” (Sirach 2:1). She was tested by spiritual darkness, by attacks of the evil one, and by being misunderstood. She remained humble and obedient to her bishop and her spiritual director. Gold is tested in the fire, and she came out as gold.

The secret of her life is in Paul's words: “it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me” (Galatians 2:20). Her service was not mere activity. It came from long hours at the feet of the Lord, like Mary of Bethany, and so she could serve like Martha without losing her peace.

In the Gospel Jesus says: “just as you did it to one of the least of these… you did it to me” (Matthew 25:40). She saw Jesus in the poorest families, and in 1914 she founded the Congregation of the Holy Family to serve families, so that every home might become a Nazareth.

Today let us ask her to bless our homes. Let us pray together as families, make peace where there is a quarrel, and visit one family that is carrying a burden alone. St Mariam Thresia, pray for us and for all the families of our land. Amen.

For children

Children, St Mariam Thresia grew up in Kerala, just like many of you. She loved Jesus so much that she went to help sick people and poor families in her village. She said that when we help someone in need, we are helping Jesus himself.

This week, help someone at home without being asked: your mother in the kitchen, your grandfather with his medicines, or a younger child with homework. You will be doing it for Jesus!

Prayer

Lord God, you called St Mariam Thresia to leave the ordinary road and to serve your Son in the poor, the sick and the families of her people, and you made her strong in testing and hidden with Christ in prayer. Through her intercession bless our homes, so that each may become a Nazareth where Jesus, Mary and Joseph are welcome. Heal families wounded by illness, poverty, addiction and quarrels; comfort the dying who are alone; and give us eyes to see Christ in the least of our brothers and sisters and hands ready to serve him there. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of St Mariam Thresia the four readings trace the road of a life given wholly to God. Abram hears “Go from your country” and goes, not knowing the way. Sirach warns the one who comes to serve the Lord to be ready for testing, for gold is tried in fire. Paul says the secret of such a life: “it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me.” And in Matthew the King reveals where that life is lived: in the hungry, the stranger, the sick and the prisoner, “you did it to me.” Call, testing, union with Christ, and service of the least: that is the shape of every holy life, and it is the shape of the life of the saint of Puthenchira.

Genesis 12:1–4 · Go to the land that I will show you

Setting.

After the story of Babel, where people tried to make a name for themselves (11:4), Genesis narrows to one family. Terah had set out from Ur of the Chaldeans with his son Abram, Sarai his wife and his grandson Lot, and settled at Haran in the north (11:31). There the Lord speaks to Abram. This is the turning point of Genesis: from the scattering of the nations the story moves to the man through whom all the nations will be blessed.

Sarai is barren (11:30), and Abram is seventy-five years old. Everything in the promise of a great nation is humanly impossible. That is the point: the future will be God’s gift.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the command: go from country, kindred and father’s house, to a land God will show.
  • vv. 2–3: the promises: a great nation, blessing, a great name, a blessing to others, and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.
  • v. 4: the response: Abram went, as the Lord had told him.

Key words.

  • lek-lekā, go, go yourself: A doubled Hebrew form, literally “go for yourself”. The same rare form returns when God tells Abraham to go to the land of Moriah with Isaac (22:2). Abraham’s life with God begins and reaches its height with the same call to set out.
  • bĕrākâ, blessing: The word and its verb occur five times in verses 2–3. After the curses of Genesis 3–11, blessing begins again, and it is meant to spread: “you will be a blessing.”
  • mišpĕḥōt hā’ădāmâ, the families of the earth: Not only Israel. From the first call, God’s plan for Abram has all humanity in view.

Verse by verse.

“Now the Lord said to Abram, “Go from your country and your kindred and your father’s house to the land that I will show you.” (12:1)

The three circles grow narrower and dearer: land, clan, family home. God does not name the land. Abram must go first and be shown later.

“I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you, and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing.” (12:2)

At Babel people wanted to make a name for themselves (11:4). Here God gives the name. Greatness is received, not seized.

“and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.” (12:3)

Paul reads this as the Gospel announced beforehand: the nations would be blessed through faith, in Abraham’s offspring, Christ (Galatians 3:8, 16).

“So Abram went, as the Lord had told him; and Lot went with him.” (12:4)

No words from Abram, only obedience. His answer is his journey.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Abraham the model of the obedience of faith: “By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to set out for a place that he was to receive as an inheritance” (Hebrews 11:8; CCC 144–145). It teaches that with Abraham God began to gather scattered humanity, and that the people descended from him would be the root onto which the Gentiles would be grafted (CCC 59–60). The Roman Canon names him “our father in faith”, and the Church honours him as father of all who believe (CCC 146).

Sirach 2:1–6 · Gold is tested in the fire

Setting.

The Book of Sirach was written in Hebrew in Jerusalem about 180 BC by a teacher named Jesus son of Sirach, and translated into Greek in Egypt by his grandson after 132 BC, as the grandson tells in his prologue. It is one of the books the Catholic Church receives as Scripture (CCC 120) and was used so much in teaching new Christians that it was long called Ecclesiasticus, “the church book”.

Chapter 1 praised wisdom and the fear of the Lord. Chapter 2 turns to the cost: anyone who sets out to serve God must expect trials, and must cling to him in them.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the warning: if you come to serve the Lord, prepare for testing.
  • vv. 2–3: set your heart right, be steadfast, cling to him.
  • v. 4: accept what comes, and be patient in humiliation.
  • v. 5: the image: gold in the fire, the acceptable in the furnace of humiliation.
  • v. 6: trust, walk straight, hope in him.

Key words.

  • teknon, my child: The usual address of a wise teacher to his pupil (as in Proverbs). Testing is spoken of within a relationship of love, as a father speaks to a son.
  • peirasmos, testing: The same Greek word as in the Lord’s Prayer, “do not bring us to the time of trial” (Matthew 6:13). Here it means the trials that prove and purify faith.
  • tapeinōsis, humiliation: Being brought low: loss, shame, failure. Sirach says this low place can be a furnace where the soul is refined.

Verse by verse.

“My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing.” (2:1)

An honest beginning. God’s service is not a guarantee of an easy life; it is a guarantee of his company.

“Cling to him and do not depart, so that your last days may be prosperous.” (2:3)

“Cling” is the word of a bond that holds, like the bond of husband and wife (Genesis 2:24 in Greek uses a related verb).

“For gold is tested in the fire, and those found acceptable, in the furnace of humiliation.” (2:5)

The same image appears in Wisdom 3:6 and 1 Peter 1:7. Fire does not destroy gold; it makes it pure.

“Trust in him, and he will help you; make your ways straight, and hope in him.” (2:6)

Three verbs, trust, walk straight, hope: the whole programme of the tested believer.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Sirach as a teacher of the moral life and of perseverance. The Catechism teaches that hope keeps us from discouragement and sustains us in times of abandonment (CCC 1818). The saints often spoke of trials as God’s refining fire. The image of gold in the furnace is taken up in the New Testament, where faith tested by fire is more precious than gold (1 Peter 1:7).

Galatians 2:19–21 · Christ lives in me

Setting.

Paul has just told how he opposed Peter at Antioch when Peter withdrew from eating with Gentile believers (2:11–14). He goes on to state the heart of his Gospel: we are made righteous not by works of the Law but through faith in Jesus Christ (2:16). These three verses are his personal testimony, among the most intimate words he ever wrote.

Structure.

  • v. 19a: through the Law I died to the Law, to live for God.
  • vv. 19b–20a: crucified with Christ; no longer I, but Christ living in me.
  • v. 20b: my life now is faith in the Son of God who loved me and gave himself for me.
  • v. 21: I do not set aside the grace of God; if righteousness came through the Law, Christ died for nothing.

Key words.

  • synestaurōmai, I have been crucified with: A Greek perfect tense: something done once that still stands. In baptism the believer is joined to the death of Christ (Romans 6:3–6), and lives from that moment on.
  • zē de en emoi Christos, Christ lives in me: Not mere imitation of Christ from outside, but his life within. The “I” is not destroyed; it is healed and filled.
  • charis, grace: God’s gift, given without our deserving. To seek righteousness by our own efforts alone would be to treat the Cross as unnecessary.

Verse by verse.

“For through the law I died to the law, so that I might live to God.” (2:19)

The Law itself, by pointing to Christ and by showing sin, led Paul to die to it as a way of being made righteous. He does not despise the Law; he says it has done its work.

“it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me.” (2:20)

The great sentence of Christian mysticism. The saints have read it as the goal of every life of prayer: that Christ may live his life again in us.

“I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me.” (2:20)

The Greek can also be read “by the faith of the Son of God”, that is, his faithfulness; the NRSV notes this. Either way the life rests on him. And Paul says “me”: the love of the Cross is personal.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these words to teach that Jesus knew and loved each one of us during his life, his agony and his Passion, and gave himself up for each one: “The Son of God… loved me and gave himself for me” (CCC 478). It also teaches that justification is the work of God’s grace, received in faith and baptism, and that it does not exclude but brings forth our cooperation in love (CCC 1987–1993). St John Chrysostom, commenting on this verse, notes that Paul makes the love of Christ for all the world his own, as if Christ had died for him alone (in substance, Commentary on Galatians).

Matthew 25:31–40 · You did it to me

Setting.

This is the last of Jesus’ teachings in Matthew before the Passion (26:1–2). After the parables of the bridesmaids and the talents, Jesus describes the judgment itself. The Son of Man comes as King and Shepherd, and all the nations are gathered before him.

In the land of Israel sheep and goats often grazed together in one flock and were separated at evening. The scene is homely and solemn at once. Today’s reading gives the blessed side of the judgment (25:31–40); the Gospel continues with those on the left (25:41–46).

Structure.

  • vv. 31–33: the coming of the Son of Man in glory; all nations gathered; sheep and goats separated.
  • vv. 34–36: the King’s welcome: inherit the kingdom, for I was hungry, thirsty, a stranger, naked, sick, in prison.
  • vv. 37–39: the surprise of the righteous: when did we see you?
  • v. 40: the answer: just as you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me.

Key words.

  • ho huios tou anthrōpou, the Son of Man: Jesus’ favourite title for himself, drawn from Daniel 7:13–14, where “one like a human being” (literally “like a son of man”) receives everlasting dominion over all peoples.
  • elachistoi, the least: The smallest, the lowest. Jesus calls them “members of my family”, literally “my brothers”. He takes the place of the person no one notices.
  • klēronomēsate, inherit: An inheritance is given because one is a child, not earned as wages. The kingdom “prepared for you from the foundation of the world” is the Father’s gift to his children.

Verse by verse.

“When the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on the throne of his glory.” (25:31)

The one who will judge is the one who was judged and crucified. The Judge is the Saviour.

“for I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me,” (25:35)

Six needs, and none is unusual. Holiness here is made of ordinary acts of mercy.

“Then the righteous will answer him, ‘Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food” (25:37)

The righteous did not know they were serving Christ. Their love was simple and unself-conscious.

“just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” (25:40)

Christ identifies himself with the poor. Love of neighbour is not something next to love of Christ; in the least, it is love of Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus identifies himself with the poor of every kind and makes active love toward them the condition for entering his kingdom (CCC 544), and that the Eucharist commits us to the poor: to receive in truth the Body and Blood of Christ given up for us, we must recognise Christ in the poorest, his brethren (CCC 1397). It also reminds us that we will be judged on our love (CCC 1022, quoting St John of the Cross). St Teresa of Calcutta summed up her life’s work with this verse, counting its words on her fingers: “you did it to me.”

Questions people ask

Does Matthew 25 mean that only good works matter, and faith is not important?

No. The righteous are called “blessed by my Father”: their life comes from God’s grace. Faith in Christ and love for the least belong together, as root and fruit. By grace our works of love have real worth before God (CCC 2008–2011), and faith that does not act in love is not living faith (Galatians 5:6). Matthew 25 shows what living faith looks like.

Why is Sirach in the Catholic Bible but not in some other Bibles?

Sirach belongs to the books found in the Greek Old Testament used by the early Church. The Church accepted it as inspired from early times; the Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), and later the Councils of Florence and Trent, list it among the sacred books (CCC 120). The Reformers placed it among the Apocrypha. Catholics and Orthodox read it as the word of God.

Who was St Mariam Thresia?

Mariam Thresia Chiramel Mankidiyan was born in 1876 at Puthenchira, in the present Thrissur district of Kerala. She gave her life to prayer, penance and the service of families, the sick and the dying, visiting homes and nursing those whom others avoided. In 1914 she founded the Congregation of the Holy Family. She died on 8 June 1926, was beatified by Pope St John Paul II in 2000 and canonised by Pope Francis on 13 October 2019.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St Mariam Thresia is a daughter of our own Syro-Malabar Church. Like Abram she left the security she knew to follow a call that others did not understand; like the gold in Sirach she passed through long trials, both inner darkness and misunderstanding; and like Paul she longed for Christ to live in her. Her life is the Gospel of today lived out in Kerala villages: she went to the homes of the poor and the sick, cared for the dying, and saw the family as the place where Christ is served.

The Congregation of the Holy Family, which she founded, continues this work. On her feast, families can pray together for those in their parish who are sick, lonely or in need, and choose one act of the six named by the King to do this week, as a family.

For study and teaching

  • Abram went without knowing where. What is God asking me to leave or begin, even before I see the whole road?
  • Sirach says gold is tested in fire. Looking back, how has a trial purified my faith?
  • Paul says, “it is Christ who lives in me.” In which part of my day do I most need to let Christ live and act instead of my own self?
  • St Mariam Thresia served Christ in families and the sick. Which of the six works in Matthew 25 can my family do together this week?
