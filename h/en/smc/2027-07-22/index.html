Word for the day

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”; and she told them that he had said these things to her.” John 20:18

Reflection

Of all the women who followed Jesus, one is named in every Gospel: Mary of Magdala, a town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Luke tells us who she was before the Lord met her: “Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out”, who with Joanna, Susanna and many others “provided for them out of their resources” (Luke 8:2–3). A woman who had been delivered from a great darkness spent the rest of her life giving back what she had received. We know her only through what the Gospels say: her healing, her service, her courage. She is among the women who watched “from a distance” (Mark 15:40), and John puts her “near the cross of Jesus”, standing with his mother (John 19:25). She saw where the body was laid (Mark 15:47). The Gospels do not say that she was the sinful woman of Luke 7 or Mary of Bethany; the Church today keeps these women distinct, and the older identification made in the Western Church is remembered only as a tradition, which the Eastern Churches never adopted. What the Gospels give is enough: she is the disciple who stayed when others fled.

Her whole story is an answer to the question of today's Epistle: “Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?” Paul's answer is a cry of victory: “No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us. For I am convinced that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.” Mary Magdalene did not read these lines, but she lived them. Not the soldiers, not the darkness, not even the closed tomb could separate her from the love she had received. Many Christians have heard in her search the voice of the bride in the Song of Songs: “I will seek him whom my soul loves” (Song of Songs 3:2).

John tells the Easter morning with a care that makes us see it: “Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb and saw that the stone had been removed.” She runs to Simon Peter and the beloved disciple; they run to the tomb, see the linen wrappings, and the beloved disciple believes, and then “the disciples returned to their homes.” “But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb.” The angels ask, “Woman, why are you weeping?” and then the Lord himself, whom she does not recognise, asks the same question and adds, “Whom are you looking for?” She takes him for the gardener: “Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.” She will carry the body of the Lord herself if she must. Then comes the shortest and greatest speech in the Gospel: “Jesus said to her, ‘Mary!’ She turned and said to him in Hebrew, ‘Rabbouni!’ (which means Teacher).” The Good Shepherd calls his own sheep by name (John 10:3), and she knows his voice. He is not recognised by his face but by the way he says her name.

Then the risen Lord gives her a mission: “Do not hold on to me, because I have not yet ascended to the Father. But go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.’” She must not cling to the old way of being near him; a new nearness is coming, through the Ascension and the Spirit. And for the first time he calls the disciples “my brothers” and shares his Father with them. “Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, ‘I have seen the Lord’.” Luke records how her message was received: the words seemed to the apostles “an idle tale” (Luke 24:11). Yet the Church's whole witness begins with her voice. This is why Saint Thomas Aquinas called her the apostle of the apostles, and why in 2016 the Church raised her memorial to a feast. In the Season of Kaitha, which honours the apostles, the Church places beside the Twelve the woman whom the Lord himself sent to tell the Twelve. The Eastern Churches honour her among the myrrh-bearing women who came to the tomb with their ointments.

The weekday readings, too, speak of prayer and mission. James teaches the prayer of the community: “Are any among you suffering? They should pray. Are any cheerful? They should sing songs of praise.” He mentions the anointing of the sick with oil, confession to one another, and the prayer of the righteous, which “is powerful and effective”: “Elijah was a human being like us, and he prayed fervently”. The prophet whom the Syro-Malankara Church remembered on Tuesday is held up as a man like us, so that we may pray as he did. The Gospel is the sending of the Twelve: “As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’ ... You received without payment; give without payment.” “As you enter the house, greet it. If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it.” Mary Magdalene received without payment, from the Lord who drove out the seven demons, and she gave without payment, all she had. She entered the room of the frightened disciples with the first greeting of Easter on her lips. Today we ask for the same love that does not leave, the same ears that know his voice, and the same courage to announce, “I have seen the Lord.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers Saint Mary Magdalene. The Lord healed her of great darkness, and she followed him to the end. She stood near the cross with Mary his mother, and on Easter morning, while it was still dark, she came to the tomb (John 20:1).

She found the tomb empty and she wept. And then someone said her name: “Mary!” (John 20:16). That is all it took. She knew his voice. The Good Shepherd knows each sheep by name, and he knows you by your name.

Then Jesus gave her work: “go to my brothers and say to them...” (John 20:17). “Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, ‘I have seen the Lord’” (John 20:18). The apostles at first thought it an idle tale, but this woman was the first witness of the Resurrection. That is why the Church calls her the apostle of the apostles.

Saint Paul asks, “Who will separate us from the love of Christ?” (Romans 8:35). Not hardship, not death, not the darkness. Mary did not let go of her love at the cross, and she was the first to see the risen Lord.

This week let us stay near the Lord when it is dark, listen for him calling our name, and tell at least one person what he has done for us. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know the friend of Jesus who cried at the empty tomb? It was Mary Magdalene. She missed Jesus so much! Then Jesus called her name, “Mary!”, and she knew it was him. She ran to tell everyone: “I have seen the Lord!” Jesus knows your name too. Tell someone this week about how good Jesus is!

Prayer

Risen Lord Jesus Christ, you called Mary Magdalene by her name in the garden and sent her to your brothers. Call us by name, and make us hear your voice. Give us her love that stayed at the cross and her courage that sought you in the dark. Let nothing in creation separate us from your love, and make us witnesses who can say, “I have seen the Lord.” Hear us through the prayers of Saint Mary Magdalene, apostle of the apostles, for you live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the readings for St Mary Magdalene come first. Paul asks who can separate us from the love of Christ, and answers that nothing in all creation can. John shows that love at work: Mary Magdalene stays at the tomb when the others go home, hears her name spoken by the risen Lord, and is sent to tell the disciples. The weekday readings of the Third Thursday are also appointed: James 5:13–20, on the prayer of faith for the sick and the anointing of the elders, and Matthew 10:5–15, the sending of the Twelve. Mary’s own sending is a fitting companion to both.

Romans 8:35–39 · More than conquerors

Setting.

This is the end of Romans 8, the great chapter on life in the Spirit. Paul has just said that God did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all (8:32), so that no accusation can stand against God’s chosen (8:33–34). Now he asks whether any power can undo what God has done, and lists the worst things he and the Roman Christians can imagine.

Structure.

  • v. 35: the question, with seven trials: hardship, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, peril, sword.
  • v. 36: a quotation from Psalm 44:22: the faithful suffering for God’s sake.
  • v. 37: the answer: in all these things we are more than conquerors.
  • vv. 38–39: a sweeping list of powers, and the conviction that none can separate us from God’s love.

Key words.

  • hypernikaō, to be more than a conqueror: A strong compound found only here in the New Testament. The believer does not merely survive: the victory is already won by Christ, and the believer shares in it.
  • agapē, love: The word is a gift that begins in God. Paul’s sentence is not about our love for God, which may waver, but about “the love of Christ” and “the love of God in Christ Jesus”, which does not.
  • pepeismai, I am convinced: Perfect tense: Paul has been persuaded and stays persuaded. It is the settled conviction of one who has already suffered much (2 Corinthians 11:23–28).

Verse by verse.

“Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?” (8:35)

Paul does not ignore suffering. He lists it plainly, as one who has known it. The Christian life is not promised to be painless, but it is promised to be never apart from Christ.

“No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.” (8:37)

“In all these things”, not after them: the victory is within the trial. “Through him who loved us”: the strength is Christ’s.

“neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers” (8:38)

Pairs of opposites cover everything: death and life, present and future. “Angels” and “rulers” may mean the spiritual powers that people feared in the ancient world.

“will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.” (8:39)

The chapter ends where it began, in Christ Jesus (8:1). The love of God is not a feeling that comes and goes but is rooted in Christ himself.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads these verses at the feasts of martyrs and saints, because they show where the saints found their strength. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, says that Paul feared nothing so much as being cut off from the love of Christ (in substance, Homilies on Romans). The Catechism reminds us that we are able to turn away from God by mortal sin (CCC 1861), and that we may hope for the grace of final perseverance (CCC 2016). So the verse is a promise of God’s faithfulness and also a call to hold on to him.

John 20:1–18 · Mary, and the garden

Setting.

The scene follows John’s account of the burial in a garden near Golgotha (19:41–42). Jesus was laid in a new tomb, and the Sabbath has passed. Now, on “the first day of the week”, an expression that recalls the first day of creation, a new beginning is about to happen.

Mary Magdalene comes from Magdala, a town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Luke says that seven demons had gone out of her (Luke 8:2) and that she, with other women, supported Jesus out of their means. She is named at the tomb in all four Gospels. She is not the sinful woman of Luke 7, and she is not Mary of Bethany; the Gospels do not say so, and the Church does not teach it.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Mary finds the stone moved and runs to tell Peter and the other disciple.
  • vv. 3–10: the two disciples race to the tomb, see the cloths, and the beloved disciple “saw and believed”.
  • vv. 11–13: Mary stays, weeping, and sees two angels.
  • vv. 14–17: Jesus speaks, she knows him when he says her name, and he sends her.
  • v. 18: Mary announces: “I have seen the Lord.”

Key words.

  • Rabbouni, my Teacher: An Aramaic word, a more personal form of “rabbi”. John calls it “Hebrew”, which for him means the Aramaic spoken by the Jews of that time. Mary uses the word of love and respect she used before the Passion.
  • mē mou haptou, do not hold on to me: The Greek present imperative means “stop clinging”, rather than “do not touch”. Jesus is not rejecting her. The old way of being with him is ending: he is about to ascend, and a new, deeper presence will begin.
  • kēpouros, gardener: Mary takes him for the gardener. The garden, the new day and the woman with the Lord echo the garden of Genesis; many readers see Jesus as the new Adam in a restored garden.
  • angellousa, announcing: In John 20:18 Mary comes “announcing” (the verb gives us the word “angel” and shares a root with “gospel”). She is the first to proclaim the Resurrection.

Verse by verse.

“Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the tomb and saw that the stone had been removed from the tomb.” (20:1)

“While it was still dark”: the darkness is real, but she comes anyway. Love goes to the tomb when there seems to be nothing left to hope for.

“But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb.” (20:11)

The two disciples have gone home (20:10). Mary stays. Her tears are not a lack of faith but a sign of how much she loved.

“Jesus said to her, “Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you looking for?”” (20:15)

Jesus’ first words are two questions. They echo his first words in this Gospel: “What are you looking for?” (1:38). Mary does not yet recognise him.

“Jesus said to her, “Mary!” She turned and said to him in Hebrew, “Rabbouni!” (which means Teacher).” (20:16)

The turning point. She knows him by his voice, as the Good Shepherd’s sheep do (John 10:3–4). One word, her name, is enough.

“But go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.’” (20:17)

Jesus calls his disciples “my brothers” for the first time. Through his Resurrection they share his relation to the Father.

“Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”” (20:18)

The first witness to the Resurrection is a woman, sent by Jesus himself. Her announcement is the beginning of the apostolic preaching.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Mary Magdalene and the holy women were the first to encounter the Risen One, and so women were the first messengers of the Resurrection to the apostles themselves (CCC 641). Early writers, among them Hippolytus of Rome (in substance, Commentary on the Song of Songs), described the women as apostles to the apostles, and St Thomas Aquinas later called Mary Magdalene “the apostle of the apostles”. In 2016 the Roman Church raised her memorial to a feast, with the same rank as the feasts of the apostles. The Church honours her as a model of persistent love, of those who remain when others leave, and of those sent to proclaim the Lord.

Questions people ask

Is Mary Magdalene the sinful woman who anointed Jesus’ feet in Luke 7?

No. The Gospels never identify them. Luke 8:2 introduces Mary Magdalene as a woman from whom seven demons had gone out, and says nothing of any sin of the kind later legend imagined. The idea that she was the woman of Luke 7 grew in the West from the sixth century, and the Roman liturgy today no longer treats her so. She is also distinct from Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus.

For study and teaching

  • Mary stayed at the tomb when the others went home. When has staying faithful, even without answers, opened something new for me?
  • Jesus called her by name, and she knew him. Where do I hear the Lord calling me by name, in prayer, in Scripture, in the Holy Qurbana, in a person in need?
