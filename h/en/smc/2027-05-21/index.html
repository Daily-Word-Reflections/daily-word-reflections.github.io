Word for the day

“But Peter said, “I have no silver or gold, but what I have I give you; in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, stand up and walk.”” Acts 3:6

Reflection

On the first Friday after Pentecost the Churches of the Syriac tradition keep a feast with a shining name: the Friday of Gold. It looks back to the first sign worked by the apostles after the Holy Spirit came upon them. Peter and John go up to the Temple “at the hour of prayer, at three o’clock in the afternoon”, the hour at which, only weeks before, Jesus had died on the Cross. At the gate called Beautiful lies a man “lame from birth”, carried there daily to beg. He asks for alms. “Peter looked intently at him, as did John, and said, ‘Look at us.’” Then comes the sentence that gives the day its name, as it is commonly explained: “I have no silver or gold, but what I have I give you; in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, stand up and walk.” The Church of the apostles is poor in gold and rich in the Name. Peter takes the man by the right hand, raises him, and “immediately his feet and ankles were made strong.” The man enters the Temple “walking and leaping and praising God.” The man lame from birth, more than forty years old (Acts 4:22), who had lain outside the gate, now goes in. Pentecost is not only a wind and tongues of fire; it is a hand stretched out to a beggar at the door.

Isaiah had seen this day from afar. To exiles worn out by grief he cried: “Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees. Say to those who are of a fearful heart, ‘Be strong, do not fear! Here is your God.’” And then: “the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then the lame shall leap like a deer, and the tongue of the speechless sing for joy.” The prophet sees a desert blooming, waters breaking out in the wilderness, and a road through it called “the Holy Way”, on which “the ransomed of the Lord shall return, and come to Zion with singing.” In Acts 3 that prophecy walks into the Temple on healed feet. The lame man leaping at the Beautiful Gate is a living sign that the age of the Messiah has begun, and that the Holy Way now runs through the Church of the apostles. Every parish that welcomes the weak, the disabled, the forgotten, and helps them go in to the house of God is walking that road.

Peter then speaks to the crowd in Solomon’s Portico. He refuses all credit: “why do you stare at us, as though by our own power or piety we had made him walk?” The healing is the work of “the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, the God of our ancestors”, who “has glorified his servant Jesus.” Peter speaks as a son of Israel to his own people, and his words about the Passion are hard. But notice how he continues: “And now, friends, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” He does not condemn; he calls to repentance and blessing: “Repent therefore, and turn to God so that your sins may be wiped out, so that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord.” And he ends with the promise to Abraham: “in your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed”, adding that God sent his servant “first to you, to bless you.” The Church has always read these words without blaming the Jewish people as a whole for the death of Christ (CCC 597–598; Nostra Aetate 4). It is our sins, the sins of all, that brought the Lord to the Cross, and it is to all of us that the times of refreshing are offered.

Why does the calendar join to this feast Paul's hymn to love? Because the healing at the gate was an act of love before it was an act of power. Paul has just listed the gifts of the Spirit, and now he says, “strive for the greater gifts. And I will show you a still more excellent way.” Without love, tongues are “a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal”; prophecy, knowledge and faith that moves mountains are nothing; even giving away all possessions gains nothing. “Love is patient; love is kind; love is not envious or boastful or arrogant or rude… It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Love never ends.” Peter had no gold, but he had this. He looked at the beggar, which the passers-by never did; he said, “Look at us”; he took him by the hand. The gold of this Friday is not the gold Peter lacked. It is love, which outlasts prophecies and tongues and knowledge: “And now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; and the greatest of these is love.”

The Gospel shows us where the apostles learned this. At the gate of the town of Nain, Jesus meets a funeral: “He was his mother’s only son, and she was a widow.” Before any request is made, “When the Lord saw her, he had compassion for her and said to her, ‘Do not weep.’” He touches the bier, the bearers stand still, and he says, “Young man, I say to you, rise!” and “Jesus gave him to his mother.” When John the Baptist's disciples come to ask, “Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?”, Jesus answers with Isaiah's own words: “the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news brought to them.” The gate of Nain and the Beautiful Gate belong together: what Jesus did at one gate, his apostles do at the other in his Name. In the Season of the Apostles the Church learns that she has no treasure of her own to give the world, only Christ, and that he is enough. The gold of our Church is not in her buildings or her accounts, but in the Name of Jesus spoken with love to someone lying at the door.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today our Church keeps the Friday of Gold, the first Friday after Pentecost. It remembers the first miracle the apostles worked after the Holy Spirit came.

Peter and John go to the Temple at three in the afternoon to pray. At the Beautiful Gate a man lame from birth asks them for money. Peter looks at him and says, “Look at us.” Then: “I have no silver or gold, but what I have I give you; in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, stand up and walk.” He takes his hand, and the man walks, leaps and praises God.

Isaiah had promised this: “then the lame shall leap like a deer.” The age of the Messiah has come, and the Church continues his work in his Name.

Peter tells the crowd that it was not his power but the God of Abraham who healed the man. He calls them “friends” and says, “I know that you acted in ignorance.” He invites them to repent and receive blessing. The Church blames no people for the Cross; we all need that call to repentance.

Paul tells us the secret behind the miracle: love. Without love, all gifts are nothing. “Love is patient; love is kind… Love never ends.” Peter had no gold, but he had love. And Jesus, at the gate of Nain, had compassion on a widow and gave her son back to her.

So today, do not say, I have nothing to give. You have the name of Jesus, a hand to hold out and a heart to love. That is the gold of the Church. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, today is the Friday of Gold, when our Church remembers the first miracle of the apostles after Pentecost. Luke begins simply: one day Peter and John were going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, at three o'clock in the afternoon (Acts 3:1). The Holy Spirit had come upon them, yet they kept their hours of prayer.

The Psalm says: evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he will hear my voice (Psalm 55:17). The Spirit does not make prayer unnecessary. He makes it alive. If we want the power of Pentecost, let us also keep a fixed hour of prayer each day.

At the gate called Beautiful sat a man lame from birth. People carried him there every day to beg (Acts 3:2). He lived at the gate of beauty but had never entered. In the old law, those who were lame could not serve at the altar (Leviticus 21:18). He had been left outside all his life. How many people sit at the edges of our parishes: the poor, the disabled, the lonely, those who feel unworthy?

Peter took him by the right hand and raised him up, and immediately his feet and ankles were made strong. Jumping up, he stood and began to walk, and he entered the temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God (Acts 3:7–8). David once danced before the ark with all his might. This man, who had never walked, now danced into God's house.

That is the gold of the Church: not money, but the name of Jesus that brings people from the gate into the house of God. Let us keep our hour of prayer, notice those who sit at our doors, and bring them in with us to the Holy Qurbana, praising God. Amen.

Take-home resolution: This week I will bring my questions to God and remember three things he has done for me.

Dear friends, in today's Gospel even John the Baptist has a question. He is in Herod's prison. He hears of what Jesus is doing, and he sends two of his disciples to ask: are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another? (Luke 7:19). The greatest of the prophets wonders.

There is no shame in an honest question brought to God. The Psalms are full of them: how long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? (Psalm 13:1). God is not offended when we bring our questions to him. He is grieved only when we walk away with them.

Jesus does not argue. He says: go and tell John what you have seen and heard: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news brought to them (Luke 7:22). These are the signs Isaiah promised for the anointed one who would bring good news to the oppressed (Isaiah 61:1). The answer to doubt is to look again at what God is doing.

And he adds: blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me (Luke 7:23). John expected a Messiah of fire and judgment. Jesus came with mercy. Isaiah had said that the Lord would become a stone one strikes against. Paul, in today's Epistle, says that now we see in a mirror, dimly, but love bears all things and hopes all things (1 Corinthians 13:7, 12). Love trusts where it cannot yet see.

On this Friday of Gold, when the apostles began to work the same signs in Jesus' name, let us bring our questions to him. Let us write down three things he has done for us. And when he does not act as we expected, let us trust him still, and receive him at the Holy Qurbana. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Friday of Gold, the first Friday after Pentecost, remembers the first sign the apostles worked after the Spirit came: the healing of the man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate. The four readings form one picture. Isaiah promises that when God comes the lame shall leap like a deer. In Acts it happens: Peter, with no silver or gold, raises the man in the name of Jesus, and he enters the Temple walking and leaping. Paul then shows the gold the Church truly holds, the love that never ends. In Luke, Jesus gives a widow’s son back to her at Nain and answers John the Baptist with the very signs Isaiah had promised. The words to watch are lame, name and love.

Isaiah 35:3–10 · The lame shall leap like a deer

Setting.

Isaiah 34 and 35 form a pair: judgment on Edom, then the blossoming of the desert and the return of the redeemed to Zion. Many scholars think chapter 35 is close in style and date to chapters 40–55, the prophecies of consolation spoken to the exiles in Babylon. The road home through the desert is a new exodus.

The verses just before this reading picture the wilderness bursting into flower and seeing “the glory of the Lord, the majesty of our God” (35:2). Today’s verses turn to the weak and fearful people who will walk that road.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–4: strengthen the weak; say to the fearful: here is your God.
  • vv. 5–6a: the blind see, the deaf hear, the lame leap, the speechless sing.
  • vv. 6b–7: water breaks out in the desert.
  • vv. 8–9: the Holy Way, safe from beasts, for the redeemed.
  • v. 10: the ransomed return to Zion with everlasting joy.

Key words.

  • pissēaḥ, lame: The lame man was among those who could feel shut out from the full life of the people. Isaiah promises that when God comes he will not just walk but leap. Acts 3:8 shows the healed man “walking and leaping”.
  • derek haqqōdeš, the Holy Way: A road that belongs to God. Those who walk it are made holy by the one who leads them. The first Christians called their life “the Way” (Acts 9:2).
  • gĕ’ûlîm / pĕdûyê YHWH, the redeemed / the ransomed of the Lord: Two words for release. The first comes from gō’ēl, the next of kin who buys back a relative from slavery. God acts as Israel’s next of kin.

Verse by verse.

“Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees.” (35:3)

Hebrews takes up this verse to encourage tired Christians: “lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees” (Hebrews 12:12). Encouraging the weak is the work of every believer.

“Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped;” (35:5)

Jesus’ answer to John the Baptist in today’s Gospel echoes this verse. The signs of God’s coming are signs of healing.

“then the lame shall leap like a deer, and the tongue of the speechless sing for joy.” (35:6)

Healing ends in praise. The man at the Beautiful Gate does not go home quietly; he goes into the Temple praising God.

“A highway shall be there, and it shall be called the Holy Way;” (35:8)

The way home is God’s own road, and even “fools” shall not go astray on it. Salvation does not depend on our cleverness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs Jesus worked fulfil the prophecies and show that he is the Messiah, and that they deliver some people from earthly evils as a sign of a deeper liberation from sin (CCC 547–549). The Fathers read this chapter as a picture of the Church: the desert of the nations made fruitful by the waters of Baptism, and the weak made strong by the Spirit.

Acts 3:1–26 · What I have I give you

Setting.

After Pentecost the believers in Jerusalem kept going to the Temple daily (2:46). Peter and John go up at three in the afternoon, the hour of prayer when the evening sacrifice was offered. A man more than forty years old (4:22), lame from birth, is carried each day to a gate called Beautiful. Its exact place is uncertain; many think it was the great bronze gate on the east side of the inner courts.

The healing leads to Peter’s second speech in Acts, given in Solomon’s Portico, the colonnade on the east side of the Temple courts where Jesus had once walked (John 10:23). Peter speaks as a Jew to his own people, calling them “friends” and heirs of the covenant.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–10: the healing at the Beautiful Gate.
  • vv. 11–16: Peter explains: not our power, but the name of Jesus, whom God glorified and raised.
  • vv. 17–21: “you acted in ignorance”; repent, that times of refreshing may come.
  • vv. 22–26: Moses and all the prophets foretold these days; the blessing promised to Abraham comes first to you.

Key words.

  • onoma, name: The word sounds through the chapter (vv. 6, 16) and the next. In the Bible the name is the person present and acting. Peter does not heal by his own power but by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth.
  • pais, servant: God “has glorified his servant Jesus” (v. 13) echoes the Greek of Isaiah 52:13, where the Servant “shall be exalted and lifted up”. The Servant who suffered is the one God has glorified.
  • archēgos tēs zōēs, Author of life: Archēgos means founder, pioneer, the one who leads the way. Peter sets the contrast sharply: a murderer was released, and the Author of life was killed; but God raised him.

Verse by verse.

“Peter looked intently at him, as did John, and said, “Look at us.”” (3:4)

Before the healing comes a look. The man was used to people passing by; now two people stop and meet his eyes.

“But Peter said, “I have no silver or gold, but what I have I give you; in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, stand up and walk.”” (3:6)

These words give the feast its name, as it is commonly explained. The apostles are poor, but they hold the greatest treasure: the name of Jesus and the power of his Spirit.

“Jumping up, he stood and began to walk, and he entered the temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God.” (3:8)

Isaiah 35:6 fulfilled. A man who had sat for years at the gate now goes in. Healing restores him to the worship of his people.

““And now, friends, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers.” (3:17)

After hard words, Peter offers an excuse and an open door. The Catechism cites this verse to teach that responsibility for the death of Jesus cannot be charged to the Jewish people as such (CCC 597).

“You are the descendants of the prophets and of the covenant that God gave to your ancestors,” (3:25)

Peter ends with blessing, not condemnation. The promise to Abraham, that all families of the earth would be blessed, now goes out from Jerusalem to the nations.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the name of Jesus is at the heart of Christian prayer and that there is no other name by which we must be saved (CCC 432, 2666; Acts 4:12). A well-known story, perhaps legendary, tells that St Thomas Aquinas was shown the treasures of the papal court and was told, “The Church can no longer say, ‘I have no silver or gold.’” He answered, “Nor can she say, ‘Stand up and walk.’” The story has lasted because it says something true: the riches of the Church are the name of Jesus, the sacraments and charity.

1 Corinthians 12:31–13:13 · A still more excellent way

Setting.

The Corinthians prized spectacular gifts, above all speaking in tongues. In chapter 12 Paul has said that all gifts come from the one Spirit for the common good, and that the Church is one body with many members. Before he speaks of tongues and prophecy in chapter 14, he sets love at the centre. Chapter 13 is not a wedding poem first of all; it is a correction for a proud and divided community.

Structure.

  • 12:31: strive for the greater gifts; a still more excellent way.
  • 13:1–3: without love, tongues, prophecy, faith and even sacrifice are nothing.
  • 13:4–7: what love does and does not do.
  • 13:8–12: love never ends; our knowledge is partial, like a dim mirror.
  • 13:13: faith, hope and love abide; the greatest is love.

Key words.

  • agapē, love: The word the New Testament chose for the love God has for us in Christ, and that the Spirit pours into our hearts (Romans 5:5). In 13:4–7 Paul describes it with verbs: love is something that acts.
  • esoptron / en ainigmati, mirror / dimly: Literally “in a riddle”. Ancient mirrors were of polished metal and gave a dim image; Corinth was known for its bronze work. God spoke to Moses “face to face… not in riddles” (Numbers 12:8); that clarity is promised to us at the end.
  • pistis, elpis, agapē, faith, hope, love: The three that Christian tradition calls the theological virtues, because they come from God and lead to God (CCC 1812–1813).

Verse by verse.

“If I speak in the tongues of mortals and of angels, but do not have love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal.” (13:1)

Paul begins with the gift the Corinthians valued most. Without love even the speech of angels is only noise.

“and if I hand over my body so that I may boast, but do not have love, I gain nothing.” (13:3)

Textual note: the manuscripts differ by one letter in Greek. Some read “so that I may boast” (followed by the NRSV), others “to be burned” (followed by our Malayalam translation). Either way, even the greatest sacrifice without love gains nothing.

“It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.” (13:7)

Not a naive love that ignores evil, for love “rejoices in the truth” (v. 6). It is a love that does not give up.

“For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then we will see face to face.” (13:12)

The promise of the vision of God. Our present knowledge, even of faith, is true but partial.

“And now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; and the greatest of these is love.” (13:13)

Faith will give way to sight and hope to possession; love alone remains, because God is love (1 John 4:8).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this chapter to teach that charity is the greatest of the virtues and gives life to all the others (CCC 1826–1827), and it cites 13:12 for the vision of God face to face that faith already tastes in advance (CCC 163). St Thérèse of Lisieux tells how, searching for her place in the Church, she read 1 Corinthians 12 and 13 and understood that love is the heart of the Church: “in the heart of the Church, my mother, I shall be love.” On the Friday of Gold this is the gold the Church can always give.

Luke 7:11–23 · Young man, I say to you, rise!

Setting.

Nain was a small town in Galilee, south-east of Nazareth, on the slope of the hill of Moreh. Only Luke tells this miracle. Close by lay Shunem, where the prophet Elisha raised the son of a woman who had welcomed him (2 Kings 4). The scene also recalls Elijah, who raised the only son of a widow at Zarephath (1 Kings 17:17–24).

A widow who has lost her only son has lost not only her child but her support and her future. Jesus acts without being asked. Then John the Baptist, now in prison (Luke 3:20), sends two disciples with a question, and Jesus answers with what they have seen.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: two crowds meet at the gate: one with Jesus, one with the dead.
  • vv. 13–15: compassion; “Do not weep”; he touches the bier; the young man rises.
  • vv. 16–17: fear and praise: a great prophet; God has visited his people.
  • vv. 18–20: John’s question: “Are you the one who is to come?”
  • vv. 21–23: the signs of Isaiah, and a blessing on those not offended.

Key words.

  • splanchnizomai, have compassion: A strong word: to be moved in the depths of one’s being. In Luke it is used of Jesus here, of the Good Samaritan (10:33) and of the father of the prodigal son (15:20).
  • episkeptomai, look favorably on, visit: The same verb as in Zechariah’s song: God “has looked favorably on his people and redeemed them” (1:68). At Nain, God’s visit has a face.
  • skandalizō, take offense: Literally to trip over a stumbling block. John expected a Messiah of fire and judgment (3:16–17); Jesus brings healing and mercy first. Blessed are those who do not stumble over the way he comes.

Verse by verse.

“When the Lord saw her, he had compassion for her and said to her, “Do not weep.”” (7:13)

Here Luke the narrator calls Jesus “the Lord”, the title of God, at a moment of pure compassion. His first word is to the mother, not to the dead son.

“Then he came forward and touched the bier, and the bearers stood still.” (7:14)

Contact with the dead made a person ritually unclean (Numbers 19:11). Jesus is not stopped by that fear; life flows from him into death.

“The dead man sat up and began to speak, and Jesus gave him to his mother.” (7:15)

The last words repeat almost exactly the Greek of 1 Kings 17:23, where Elijah gave the boy to his mother. A greater than Elijah is here.

“the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor have good news brought to them.” (7:22)

Jesus answers John with the words of Isaiah (35:5–6; 61:1). The list ends not with the dead raised but with the poor hearing good news.

“And blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.” (7:23)

A beatitude for John and for us: blessed are those who accept God’s way of saving, even when it is not the way we expected.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christ’s compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a resplendent sign that “God has visited his people” (CCC 1503), quoting this Gospel. St Ambrose saw in the widow of Nain an image of Mother Church, who weeps for her children dead in sin and rejoices when Christ gives them back to her alive (in substance, Commentary on Luke). The Church still prays for the dead at every Holy Qurbana, trusting the Lord who said, “Young man, I say to you, rise!”

Questions people ask

Why is this day called the Friday of Gold?

The name is commonly explained from Peter’s words, “I have no silver or gold, but what I have I give you” (Acts 3:6). The Churches of the Syriac tradition keep the first Friday after Pentecost as a feast of the first apostolic miracle. Its exact history is not certain, but its meaning is plain: the gift the apostles give is better than gold, the healing power of the name of Jesus.

In Acts 3 Peter tells the crowd “you killed the Author of life”. Does this blame the Jewish people?

No. Peter, a Jew, speaks to his fellow Jews in Jerusalem about what happened there weeks before, and at once adds, “I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers” (3:17). The Church teaches that the death of Christ cannot be charged against all Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today, and that all sinners were the authors of his Passion (CCC 597–598; Nostra Aetate 4). Peter’s speech ends with blessing for his people (3:26).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syro-Malabar calendar the Friday of Gold falls in the first week of the Season of the Apostles (Shleeha). The calendar notes that if only two readings are chosen it is proper to read from the Acts of the Apostles, so that the day keeps its memory of the Beautiful Gate. Throughout this season the Holy Qurbana is offered with the Anaphora of the Apostles Mar Addai and Mar Mari.

The day also has a lesson for our parishes and institutions. A Church rich in buildings must still be able to say, with Peter, “what I have I give you”: the name of Jesus, the sacraments, and hands that raise up the weak, the disabled and the elderly so that they too may enter the house of God praising him.

For study and teaching

  • Peter and John stopped and looked at the man at the gate. Who sits at the “gate” of my life whom I usually pass by?
  • Read 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 and put your own name in place of “love”. Where do I fall short, and what do I ask of the Spirit?
  • Jesus acted at Nain before anyone asked him. Whose sorrow can I notice today without waiting to be asked?
  • John the Baptist was puzzled by the way Jesus worked. When have I been disappointed by how God acted, and what did I learn?
