Word for the day

“But he said, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!”” Luke 11:28

Reflection

On the second Friday of Kaitha the Church looks at a mountain. Carmel rises from the Mediterranean coast of the Holy Land, a green ridge in a dry country, and its name means something like garden or orchard. On this mountain the prophet Elijah, after a drought of three years, prayed for rain. The Book of Kings sets the scene: “Elijah went up to the top of Carmel; there he bowed himself down upon the earth and put his face between his knees.” He sends his servant to look toward the sea. “There is nothing.” “Go again seven times.” Seven times the prophet prays and seven times the servant looks, until at the seventh time he says, “Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea.” That was enough. “In a little while the heavens grew black with clouds and wind; there was a heavy rain.” James tells us why this matters to us: “Elijah was a human being like us, and he prayed fervently” (James 5:17). Prayer that persists, that goes up again when the sky is empty, is not a magic trick; it is a human being's faith holding on until God's promise arrives. The monsoon rains of Kerala have taught us to understand a drought and the sound of rain on the roof; Elijah teaches us to wait for it with our face to the ground.

Isaiah sings of the same land restored. “The wilderness and the dry land shall be glad, the desert shall rejoice and blossom; like the crocus it shall blossom abundantly, and rejoice with joy and singing.” Then comes the name: “The glory of Lebanon shall be given to it, the majesty of Carmel and Sharon.” The prophet chooses Carmel as the picture of the whole world made green again by God. And he turns to the weary: “Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees. Say to those who are of a fearful heart, ‘Be strong, do not fear! Here is your God… He will come and save you.’” Christian tradition, and above all the Carmelite tradition, has read the little cloud that rose from the sea as a sign of Mary: small, humble, from the lowly, and bringing in her womb the rain of grace, the Saviour who makes the dry earth bloom. It is a loving reading that the Church allows and cherishes, not a doctrine.

Our Lady of Mount Carmel is the title under which Mary is honoured by the Order of Carmelites. In the twelfth century some pilgrims and Crusaders settled as hermits on Mount Carmel, near the spring that tradition calls Elijah's, and built there a chapel in honour of Mary; they were called the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel. When they were forced to leave the Holy Land they spread across Europe, and Carmelite saints such as Teresa of Avila and John of the Cross made them known. The feast is kept on 16 July. An old tradition says that on that day in 1251 Our Lady gave the scapular to Simon Stock, an English Carmelite, as a sign of her protection; the historical details are uncertain, but the devotion is dear to the Church. The brown scapular is a small garment of two pieces of cloth worn over the shoulders, and the Church asks us to understand it not as a charm but as a sign of belonging to Mary: to live as she lived, listening to the word and doing it. Kerala has a rich Carmelite heritage. Saint Kuriakose Elias Chavara founded the Carmelites of Mary Immaculate (CMI) at Mannanam in 1831, the first indigenous religious congregation of men in India, and with Father Leopold he began the Congregation of the Mother of Carmel (CMC) for women, to which Saint Euphrasia belonged. Many families in our parishes wear the scapular, and the Syro-Malabar Church keeps this day as an Important Marian Feast.

The Gospel shows us the Mary of Carmel. Jesus has just said that an empty house, swept and put in order, may be taken over by worse spirits: it is not enough to be cleaned; the house must be filled. And a woman in the crowd cries out, “Blessed is the womb that bore you and the breasts that nursed you!” We might expect Jesus to agree at once; instead he lifts her words higher: “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” He is not turning away from his mother, for Mary is first among those who hear the word and obey it. “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). “Blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfilment of what was spoken to her by the Lord” (Luke 1:45). “Mary treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart” (Luke 2:19). Saint Augustine said that Mary was more blessed in receiving the faith of Christ than in conceiving the flesh of Christ. The house of Mary was swept and put in order, and it was filled with the Word; so she is the pattern of every disciple who wants to be blessed.

Paul adds the note of consolation. “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction with the consolation with which we ourselves are consoled by God.” Consolation received is consolation to be passed on. Mary stood at the Cross, and the Church has learned to call her Comforter of the Afflicted. Anyone who has sat beside a grieving mother in Kerala, with the rice-water and the silence, knows that the best comfort often comes from those who have wept themselves. “For just as the sufferings of Christ are abundant for us, so also our consolation is abundant through Christ.”

The second commemoration of the day is Mar Mari, and our own Church cannot pass him by. According to the tradition of the East Syriac Church, Mari was a disciple of Mar Addai, Thaddaeus, one of the seventy-two disciples of the Lord. Eusebius, the historian of the early Church, tells that after the Ascension the apostle Thomas sent Thaddaeus to Edessa; and Addai's disciple Mari carried the Gospel further east, into Mesopotamia and the regions along the Tigris, in the area of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, where the Church of the East began to grow. Mar Addai and Mar Mari are honoured as the founders of that Church, and the great Anaphora, the prayer of consecration, that bears their names is one of the three that our Syro-Malabar Church uses at the Holy Qurbana, among the oldest eucharistic prayers still in use. That means on most Sundays we pray with words that have come down from the first apostolic missions of the East. The details of the lives of Mar Addai and Mar Mari come to us through tradition and cannot all be confirmed historically; but the Church's gratitude is real.

The readings given for Mar Mari are like his life. Isaiah gives the promise: “See, I made him a witness to the peoples… See, you shall call nations that you do not know, and nations that do not know you shall run to you.” And God's word is like Carmel's rain: “For as the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and do not return there until they have watered the earth, making it bring forth and sprout, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater, so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty.” The Acts of the Apostles shows the community that was born: “the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul”, and Joseph, called Barnabas, “son of encouragement”, who sold his field and laid the money at the apostles' feet; encouragement and consolation are words of the same family, so the Church's gift of comfort in Paul and her gift of encouragement in Barnabas are one. Paul's reading to the Corinthians is the creed of every missionary: “I handed on to you as of first importance what I in turn had received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he was raised on the third day.” Mari, too, handed on what he had received. Finally John's Gospel gives the Risen Christ in the upper room: “Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” Then Thomas: “My Lord and my God!” The same Thomas whose faith reached the shores of Kerala, and whose sending of Addai, according to tradition, gave the East its first teachers. Carmel and Edessa, the cloud and the word, the Mother and the missionary: today the Church gives thanks that the rain of the Gospel has watered the whole world.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church looks at a mountain: Mount Carmel. After three years of drought, the prophet Elijah went to the top of Carmel, bowed to the ground and prayed for rain. His servant looked toward the sea seven times. Six times there was nothing. At the seventh: “Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea” (1 Kings 18:44).

That little cloud is a picture of hope for everyone who has prayed and waited. And the Carmelite tradition sees in it a picture of Mary: small, humble, and bringing in her womb the Rain that makes the earth bloom, Jesus our Saviour. Today is the feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and in Kerala so many of us belong to her family: the Carmelites of Mary Immaculate, the Sisters of the Congregation of the Mother of Carmel, and thousands of families who wear her scapular.

But what does she teach us? A woman in the crowd praises Jesus' mother, and Jesus answers, “Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” (Luke 11:28). He is not putting his mother down. He is telling us why she is blessed: she heard the word of God and did it. “Let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). The scapular is not a charm. It is a sign that we want to live as she lived.

Paul adds that God is “the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction” (2 Corinthians 1:3–4). When we have been comforted, we are to comfort.

And today the Church also thanks God for Mar Mari, disciple of Mar Addai, who carried the Gospel to the East. The Qurbana we celebrate on most Sundays uses their prayer. This week let us pray again a prayer we had given up, let us keep one line of the Gospel, and let us comfort one person. Our Lady of Mount Carmel, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, a servant looked at the sea seven times, and at last he saw a tiny cloud, and then the big rain came! Our Lady of Mount Carmel teaches us not to stop praying and to listen to Jesus. And Mar Mari and Mar Addai traveled far to tell people about Jesus. We can tell our friends about him too!

Prayer

Holy Mother of Carmel, Mary, who heard the word of God and kept it, pray for us. Lord God, you sent your prophet Elijah to the mountain and gave rain when the sky was empty; give us the faith that prays seven times and looks for the little cloud. Fill the house of our hearts with your word, so that no emptiness may return to us worse than before. Console the afflicted through us, as you have consoled us. We thank you for Mar Addai and Mar Mari and for all who carried the Gospel to the East; keep alive in us the apostolic faith we celebrate at your altar. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church honours Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the four readings gather round the mountain of Elijah. On Carmel, Elijah prays seven times with his face to the ground until a small cloud appears and the drought ends. Isaiah promises that the wilderness will bloom with the splendour of Carmel and Sharon, and that the weak will be strengthened. Paul blesses the God of all consolation, who comforts us so that we may comfort others. In Luke, a woman cries out in praise of the mother who bore Jesus, and he answers that those who hear the word of God and keep it are blessed. The Church sees Mary in all of these: the woman of prayer, the garden God makes bloom, the mother who consoles, and the first to hear the word and keep it. (The calendar also names Mar Mari, disciple of Mar Addai, with Isaiah 55:4–11, Acts 4:32–37, 1 Corinthians 15:1–11 and John 20:19–29; this exegesis follows the readings for Our Lady of Mount Carmel.)

1 Kings 18:41–46 · A little cloud rising from the sea

Setting.

The books of Kings tell of the prophet Elijah in the ninth century before Christ, in the reign of King Ahab and Queen Jezebel, who promoted the worship of Baal. After years of drought, Elijah called the people to Mount Carmel and, in the contest of 18:20–40, the fire of the Lord fell on his offering and the people cried, “The Lord indeed is God.” The verses today follow: the drought ends.

Carmel is a ridge by the Mediterranean in the north of Israel, near the modern city of Haifa. Its name means something like “garden-land” or orchard. Elijah goes up to its top to pray, while King Ahab goes to eat and drink.

Structure.

  • v. 41: Elijah tells Ahab of the sound of rain.
  • vv. 42–44: Elijah’s prayer on Carmel; the servant looks seven times; the little cloud.
  • vv. 45–46: the storm; Ahab rides to Jezreel; the hand of the Lord on Elijah, who runs ahead.

Key words.

  • karmel, garden-land, orchard: A Hebrew word for a fertile place. Isaiah often names Carmel as the sign of beauty and fruitfulness (Isaiah 35:2); Amos pictures the Lord’s judgement as the top of Carmel drying up (Amos 1:2). The mountain stands for what God makes fruitful, and for what withers when his blessing is withdrawn.
  • ʿāb qĕṭannâ, a small cloud: Literally “a cloud, small”, no bigger than the palm of a hand. After three years of drought, a hand-sized cloud is all Elijah needs to hear the rain. Faith can see what is promised before it is large.
  • yad-YHWH, the hand of the Lord: A phrase for the Lord’s power at work in a prophet. The same hand that sent the rain gives the prophet strength to outrun Ahab’s chariot.

Verse by verse.

“Elijah went up to the top of Carmel; there he bowed himself down upon the earth and put his face between his knees.” (18:42)

The prophet’s posture is the posture of one who prays with his whole body: bowed to the earth, hidden in himself, waiting. He has just seen fire fall from heaven, and now he asks, humbly, for rain.

“Go again seven times.” (18:43)

Seven times, because the answer does not come at once. Persevering prayer is a theme of Elijah’s story; James writes that Elijah “prayed fervently” and that the heavens gave rain (James 5:17–18).

“Look, a little cloud no bigger than a person’s hand is rising out of the sea.” (18:44)

The sign is small, yet the whole answer is in it. A later tradition of the Carmelite Order has seen in this small cloud a sign of Mary, from whom the Saviour would come; it is a devotional reading, not the literal meaning of the verse.

“But the hand of the Lord was on Elijah” (18:46)

God’s answer in prayer is also God’s strength for the one who prays.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Elijah the “father of the prophets”, the one who prays for the fire to fall and whose prayer on Carmel is a prayer of faith that the Lord is God (CCC 2582–2584). The Church also reads Scripture in its spiritual senses (CCC 115–117), and in that tradition the Carmelite family has seen the small cloud as a figure of Mary. Whatever one makes of that image, the plain lesson stands: God hears the prayer of those who persevere.

Isaiah 35:1–4 · The splendour of Carmel

Setting.

Isaiah 35 closes the first part of the book with a song of joy. It follows a stern chapter on judgement (34) and looks ahead to the return of God’s people: the desert blooms, the blind see, the lame leap, and a “Holy Way” leads the redeemed to Zion (35:5–10). The verses today are the opening of that promise.

Lebanon, Carmel and Sharon were the fertile, beautiful places of the land: Lebanon for its cedars, Carmel for its orchards and woods, Sharon for the rich coastal plain. The prophet says that even the wilderness will receive their glory.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the wilderness rejoices and blossoms.
  • v. 2: it receives the glory of Lebanon and the majesty of Carmel and Sharon; they see the glory of the Lord.
  • v. 3: strengthen weak hands and feeble knees.
  • v. 4: the message to the fearful: do not fear, your God comes to save.

Key words.

  • kābôd, glory: From a root meaning weight or heaviness: God’s glory is the weight of his presence. The prophet says that the desert will not only be made green; it will see the Lord’s glory.
  • nimharê lēb, fearful of heart (literally, hasty of heart): The Hebrew describes people who are anxious or in a flutter of fear. God’s word to them is not a scolding but “Be strong, do not fear!”

Verse by verse.

“The wilderness and the dry land shall be glad, the desert shall rejoice and blossom” (35:1)

The prophet imagines the place of death turning into a garden. It is a promise to a people in exile, and it is how the Church speaks of grace: where there was nothing, God makes life.

“the majesty of Carmel and Sharon. They shall see the glory of the Lord, the majesty of our God.” (35:2)

The beauty of the land is a gift to the wilderness, and the greatest sight of all is God’s own glory.

“Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees.” (35:3)

The promise comes with a task. Those who have been comforted are to strengthen others.

“Be strong, do not fear! Here is your God.” (35:4)

The command to fear not is rooted in a fact: God is coming. In the Church’s hearing, the one who comes is Jesus, born of Mary.

How the Church reads it.

In the Gospels Jesus points to the fulfilment of this promise: the blind see, the lame walk (Matthew 11:5). On this feast the Carmelite family has loved the line about the “majesty of Carmel” as a hymn of Mary, the flower of Carmel, in whom God’s promise to bring a garden out of the desert is seen. The Church also learns from it a mission: to strengthen weak hands.

2 Corinthians 1:3–7 · The God of all consolation

Setting.

After a short greeting, Paul begins this letter with a blessing, as Jewish prayer does, thanking God for his comfort. He wrote during a troubled time in his relationship with the Corinthians, and he has suffered much (1:8–11 tells of a mortal danger in Asia).

The key word here, parakaleō and its noun paraklēsis, means to console, to encourage, to call alongside. It is related to the title “Paraclete”, which Jesus gives to the Holy Spirit in John’s Gospel. The comfort that Paul receives from God is meant to be passed on.

Structure.

  • v. 3: blessing: the Father of mercies and God of all consolation.
  • v. 4: he consoles us so that we can console others.
  • v. 5: as Christ’s sufferings overflow into us, so does his consolation.
  • vv. 6–7: our affliction and consolation are for you; our hope for you is unshaken.

Key words.

  • paraklēsis, consolation, encouragement: More than soft comfort: it means strength given to stand. The “God of all consolation” does not remove all trouble, but stands beside us and gives courage.
  • koinōnoi, sharers: Paul says that the Corinthians share both in the sufferings and in the consolation. In the Church, no one suffers or is comforted alone.

Verse by verse.

“the Father of mercies and the God of all consolation, who consoles us in all our affliction” (1:3–4)

The Father is the source. Everything that follows in the passage flows from his mercy.

“so that we may be able to console those who are in any affliction with the consolation with which we ourselves are consoled by God.” (1:4)

We are comforted in order to comfort. Someone who has been through sorrow and found God there has something that cannot be learned from a book.

“For just as the sufferings of Christ are abundant for us, so also our consolation is abundant through Christ.” (1:5)

Christ’s own sufferings overflow into the lives of his people, and so does his consolation. The two cannot be separated.

“Our hope for you is unshaken” (1:7)

Paul’s hope is firm because it does not rest on how the Corinthians are doing at this moment, but on God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that by his Passion Christ has given a new meaning to suffering, which can now unite us to his redemptive Passion (CCC 1521). The Church sees Mary as the first to be consoled by her Son in this way, and as a mother who consoles: the Litany of Loreto calls her “Comforter of the afflicted”. Because she stood by the Cross, she is the one who stands by all who suffer.

Luke 11:24–28 · Blessed are those who hear the word of God and keep it

Setting.

This passage comes at the end of a section about Jesus’ casting out a demon and the accusation that he does it by Beelzebul (11:14–23). Jesus warns that a person who has been delivered, if the house is left “swept and put in order” but empty, may fall back into a worse state (11:24–26). Neutral cleanliness is not enough; the house must be filled with God.

Then comes a short scene found only in Luke. A woman in the crowd cries out a blessing on Jesus’ mother. It is a very Jewish way of praising a son: to bless the one who bore him. Jesus’ answer does not reject her words but deepens them.

Structure.

  • vv. 24–26: the unclean spirit returns to the empty house.
  • v. 27: a woman’s praise: blessed is the womb that bore you.
  • v. 28: Jesus: blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and keep it.

Key words.

  • makaria, blessed, happy: The same word as in the Beatitudes and in Elizabeth’s greeting to Mary, “Blessed is she who believed” (Luke 1:45). Luke uses it carefully: Mary is called blessed because of her faith.
  • menoun, rather, indeed: This Greek word can mean “rather” (as in the NRSV) or “yes, but even more”. It does not deny the woman’s words; it moves from the womb to the word, from the body to the faith that makes someone a mother of the Lord in the fullest sense.
  • phylassontes, keeping, guarding: To hear and to keep, not to hear only. Luke says that Mary “treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart” (2:19; see also 2:51). She is the perfect example of the one Jesus describes.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed is the womb that bore you and the breasts that nursed you!” (11:27)

The woman is not wrong. Mary herself says in the Magnificat that “all generations will call me blessed” (Luke 1:48). The Church keeps this blessing in the Hail Mary.

“Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!” (11:28)

Jesus points out what makes Mary great: she heard the word and kept it, and became the mother of the Word through faith before she conceived him in her womb. And he opens that blessing to everyone who hears and obeys.

“the last state of that person is worse than the first” (11:26)

A sober warning: an emptied life, even a cleansed one, needs the word of God to fill it, or the old troubles return.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that Jesus, in extolling a kingdom beyond the bonds of flesh and blood, declared blessed those who hear and keep the word of God, “as she was faithfully doing” (Lumen Gentium 58, citing Luke 2:19, 51). St Augustine says (in substance) that Mary was more blessed in receiving faith in Christ than in conceiving Christ’s flesh. The Catechism teaches that Mary most perfectly embodies the obedience of faith (CCC 148–149), and calls her the perfect “orans”, the one praying, a figure of the Church (CCC 2679).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus’ answer to the woman mean that Mary is not so special?

No. Jesus does not say that his mother is not blessed; he explains why she is. She is blessed above all because she heard the word of God and kept it, and the Church holds this up as the way open to every Christian. Elizabeth called her blessed because she believed (Luke 1:45), and Mary herself said that all generations would call her blessed (Luke 1:48). The Church honours her because of what God did in her, and she leads us to her Son, not away from him.

What is the Brown Scapular, and does wearing it save us?

The Brown Scapular is a small garment of the Carmelite Order which many lay people wear as a sign of devotion to Mary and a wish to live under her protection. It has been loved for centuries, and tradition links it with St Simon Stock in the thirteenth century, though the details of that story cannot be proved. The Church does not teach that a piece of cloth saves anyone. It is a sign of belonging to Mary and of a desire to live as she did, hearing the word of God and keeping it. Salvation comes from Christ, by grace, received in faith and a life of love.

Is the little cloud in 1 Kings really Mary?

The literal meaning is a small cloud bringing rain. The Church teaches that Scripture also has deeper senses (CCC 115–117), and from early times Christians have found figures of Mary in the Old Testament, as the Carmelite tradition has with this cloud. It is a devotional image that helps prayer, not something the text itself says.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar Church keeps the feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel with special joy because the Carmelite family is deep in the history of the Church in Kerala. The Carmelites of Mary Immaculate (CMI), the first indigenous religious congregation of men in India, were founded in 1831 at Mannanam by Kuriakose Elias Chavara and his companions; he was canonised in 2014. Blessed Mother Eliswa founded the first Carmelite congregation for women in India in 1866 (the Teresian Carmelites), and she was beatified in 2025. Many parishes and families in Kerala take the Scapular and the Carmelite devotion. Today’s calendar also remembers Mar Mari, who with Mar Addai is held by tradition to have brought the Gospel to Mesopotamia and the East; the ancient Anaphora of Addai and Mari, one of the three anaphoras of the Holy Qurbana, bears their names.

For study and teaching

  • Elijah prays on Carmel seven times, and the answer is only a small cloud. What prayer of mine needs more patience, and what small sign of God’s answer might I be overlooking?
  • Paul says we are consoled so that we may console others. Whom can I comfort this week with the comfort I have received from God?
  • Jesus names those who “hear the word of God and obey it” as blessed. What one verse of Scripture will I hear and keep this week, as Mary did?
  • What does it mean to me to belong to Mary, and how does she lead me to her Son?
