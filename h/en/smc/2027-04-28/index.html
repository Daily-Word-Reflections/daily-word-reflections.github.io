Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit.” John 3:5

Reflection

Nicodemus comes to Jesus by night. He is a Pharisee, “a leader of the Jews”, a teacher of Israel, a man of learning and position, and he comes with respect: “Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher who has come from God; for no one can do these signs that you do apart from the presence of God.” Jesus does not answer the compliment. He goes straight to the deepest need: “Very truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God without being born from above.” The Greek word means both ‘again’ and ‘from above’, and Nicodemus hears only the first meaning: “How can anyone be born after having grown old? Can one enter a second time into the mother's womb and be born?” His question is honest, even a little humorous. He is perhaps no longer young, and he knows he cannot start his life again. Jesus answers that this is exactly what God offers: not a second birth from a human mother, but a new birth from God. “Very truly, I tell you, no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit. What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the Spirit is spirit.” The Church has always heard in these words the sacrament of Baptism. In the East Syriac tradition the baptismal font is spoken of as a womb, from which the Church our mother gives birth to children of God.

Jesus adds an image that only makes sense in the open air: “The wind blows where it chooses, and you hear the sound of it, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.” In Aramaic, as in Greek, one word means both wind and spirit: ruha. Anyone who has stood on a Kerala beach before the monsoon knows the wind that cannot be seen but bends every coconut palm. The Spirit given in Baptism works like that, unseen but not unfelt. Nicodemus leaves the scene still puzzled, but John does not forget him. Later he speaks up for Jesus before the council (John 7:50–51), and after the Crucifixion he comes, no longer by night, carrying a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes to bury the body of the Lord (John 19:39). The man who asked how one who had grown old could be born again found his answer at the foot of the Cross and beside the tomb that was soon to be empty. New birth is not always sudden; it can be a slow walk from night into day. In these weeks many of our parishes celebrate the First Holy Communion of children, who were born of water and Spirit as infants and now come to the altar for the first time. It is a good moment for every adult in the church to ask whether the life begun in their own Baptism is still growing.

Paul tells the Colossians what that new life looks like in practice. Since you have been raised with Christ, he says, there are things that must die: “Put to death, therefore, whatever in you is earthly: fornication, impurity, passion, evil desire, and greed (which is idolatry).” Then the sins of the mouth: “But now you must get rid of all such things: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and abusive language from your mouth. Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have stripped off the old self with its practices and have clothed yourselves with the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator.” The image is of a garment. In the ancient Church the newly baptised came up from the water and were dressed in white; in our own baptismal rite the child is clothed in a white garment as a sign of the new self. Paul's last line sets the boundaries of that new family very wide: “In that renewal there is no longer Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and free; but Christ is all and in all!” Every division that human beings use to rank one another, nation, culture, class or caste, has no place in the family born from the font. Whoever kneels at the same altar and receives the same Body has the same Father. To put on the new self is to see every baptised person as a brother or sister clothed in the same Christ.

Full homily

Dear friends, Nicodemus, a respected teacher, comes to Jesus by night. Jesus tells him: no one can see the kingdom of God without being born from above (John 3:3). Nicodemus asks: how can an old man be born again?

Jesus answers: no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit (John 3:5). This is what happened to us in Baptism. The font of the church is like a mother's womb, and from it we were born children of God.

The Spirit, Jesus says, is like the wind: you hear it, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes (John 3:8). Nicodemus did not understand at once. But later he defended Jesus, and after the Cross he came openly to bury the Lord. Our new birth also grows slowly, from night into day.

Paul tells us what the new life looks like: put to death what is earthly; get rid of anger, malice, slander and lies; take off the old self and put on the new (Colossians 3:5–10). And in this new family “there is no longer Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and free; but Christ is all and in all!” (Colossians 3:11).

This week, let us thank God for our Baptism, take off one old habit, and treat every baptised person as a brother or sister in Christ. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, when you were baptised, you were born again as a child of God! The priest poured water and the Holy Spirit came to live in you. The Spirit is like the wind: you cannot see it, but you can see the trees move. When you feel the wind today, say: thank you, Jesus, for my Baptism!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you received Nicodemus by night and told him that he must be born from above. We thank you for the new birth we received from water and the Spirit in the font of the Church. Put to death in us whatever is earthly, take away anger, slander and lies from our mouths, and clothe us again and again with the new self, renewed in the image of our Creator. Make every baptised person a brother and sister to us, for you are all and in all. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of a new birth and a new life. Colossians tells the baptized to put off the old self like a garment and put on the new, renewed in the image of the Creator, where Christ is all and in all. In John, Nicodemus comes by night and hears that no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit. The Easter season is the season of Baptism, and today’s readings explain what happened at the font.

Colossians 3:5–11 · Put on the new self

Setting.

The letter is written in the name of Paul to the Church at Colossae in Asia Minor. Many scholars think it was written by a disciple of Paul after his lifetime; others hold that Paul wrote it, perhaps through a co-worker. The Church reads it as part of the apostolic witness. Just before this reading the letter says, “you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God” (3:3). Today’s verses draw the consequences: because you have been raised with Christ, live accordingly.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: put to death what is earthly: a first list of vices.
  • vv. 8–9a: get rid of sins of anger and the tongue: a second list.
  • vv. 9b–10: the old self stripped off, the new self put on.
  • v. 11: in the new creation the old divisions fall away: Christ is all and in all.

Key words.

  • nekrōsate, put to death: A strong word. Baptism has already joined us to Christ’s death (2:12); now that death must be carried into daily life.
  • pleonexia, greed: Literally “wanting more”. The letter calls it idolatry, because whatever we crave above God takes the place of God.
  • apekdysamenoi / endysamenoi, stripped off / clothed: The language of changing clothes. Early Christian Baptism made it visible: the candidate took off old clothes, went down into the water, and was clothed in a white garment.

Verse by verse.

“Put to death, therefore, whatever in you is earthly:” (3:5)

“Earthly” does not mean the body or the material world, which God made good. It means the old life turned away from God.

“On account of these the wrath of God is coming on those who are disobedient.” (3:6)

Textual note: the words “on those who are disobedient” are missing in some of the oldest manuscripts and may have come in from Ephesians 5:6. The POC follows the shorter text.

“and have clothed yourselves with the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator.” (3:10)

An echo of Genesis 1:27. In Christ, the true image of God (1:15), the image in which we were made is restored.

“In that renewal there is no longer Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave and free; but Christ is all and in all!” (3:11)

A “barbarian” was anyone who did not speak Greek; the Scythians, from north of the Black Sea, were the byword for the uncivilized. None of these labels decides a person’s worth in Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Baptism makes the neophyte “a new creature”, an adopted son of God, a member of Christ (CCC 1265). The white garment given at Baptism, still part of our rite, shows that the baptized person has “put on Christ” (Galatians 3:27; CCC 1243). The conversion begun at the font continues through the whole Christian life (CCC 1428).

John 3:1–8 · Born of water and Spirit

Setting.

Nicodemus is a Pharisee and “a leader of the Jews”, a member of the ruling council. He comes by night, perhaps for safety, perhaps for quiet study; in John, night is also the darkness from which a person comes toward the light (3:19–21). He appears twice more: defending Jesus’ right to a hearing (7:50–51) and bringing spices for his burial (19:39). He is a man who slowly comes into the light.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Nicodemus comes by night: “we know that you are a teacher who has come from God.”
  • v. 3: Jesus: to see the kingdom, one must be born from above.
  • v. 4: Nicodemus misunderstands.
  • vv. 5–8: born of water and Spirit; flesh and spirit; the wind.

Key words.

  • anōthen, from above, again: The Greek means both. Jesus means “from above”, from God; Nicodemus hears “again”. Both are true: it is a second birth, and it comes from God. The POC renders it “again”.
  • hydatos kai pneumatos, of water and Spirit: The Church has always heard Baptism in these words: the water is the sign, the Spirit gives the life.
  • pneuma, wind, spirit: The same Greek word means both. Jesus plays on it in v. 8: you hear the wind and cannot see it; so it is with the Spirit and those born of him.

Verse by verse.

“He came to Jesus by night” (3:2)

He comes as a teacher to a teacher. He will learn that this is not a matter of better teaching but of new birth.

“Very truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God without being born from above.” (3:3)

Jesus does not answer Nicodemus’s compliment; he answers his need.

“Very truly, I tell you, no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit.” (3:5)

The Church’s classic text on the necessity of Baptism. God has bound salvation to Baptism, but he himself is not bound by his sacraments (CCC 1257).

“So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.” (3:8)

The life of the Spirit cannot be controlled or predicted, but its effects can be seen, as the trees bend under the wind.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites this Gospel when it explains that Baptism is called “the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit”, because it brings about the birth of water and the Spirit without which no one can enter the kingdom of God (CCC 1215). The Fathers delighted in the image of the font as the womb of Mother Church, where children are born of water and the Spirit.

For study and teaching

  • Colossians names greed as idolatry. What do I want “more” of, so that it competes with God in my heart?
  • Nicodemus came by night and slowly came into the light. Where am I still coming to Jesus “by night”, privately and with caution?
