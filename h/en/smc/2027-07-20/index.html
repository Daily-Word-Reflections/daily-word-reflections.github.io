Word for the day

“And a harvest of righteousness is sown in peace for those who make peace.” James 3:18

Reflection

Who is wise? James asks the question as a teacher in a village school might ask it, and he gives an answer that surprises us: “Show by your good life that your works are done with gentleness born of wisdom.” For James wisdom is not cleverness, learning or a sharp tongue; it is a way of living that can be seen. He then sets two kinds of wisdom side by side. One is “earthly, unspiritual, devilish”, and it grows where there is “bitter envy and selfish ambition”. Its fruit is just as plain: “where there is envy and selfish ambition, there will also be disorder and wickedness of every kind.” Anyone who has sat on a parish committee, a family meeting or a village council knows how true this is. The other wisdom comes “from above”: “first pure, then peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits, without a trace of partiality or hypocrisy.” It is a portrait of Christ himself, and it begins with purity of heart and ends in good fruit.

The last verse carries the image of the Season of Kaitha: “And a harvest of righteousness is sown in peace for those who make peace.” A farmer sows in the right season and in good soil; the seed of righteousness is sown in peace, and the harvest is gathered by those who make peace. It echoes the promise of Isaiah, “The effect of righteousness will be peace, and the result of righteousness, quietness and trust forever” (Isaiah 32:17), and the Beatitude of the Lord, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God” (Matthew 5:9). The Fathers saw that envy is the root of much strife. Saint Basil (in substance) called it grief at the good of a neighbour, and Saint Augustine (in substance) called it hatred of another's happiness. The envious person is never at rest, because there is always someone who is doing better. The remedy is not to be less ambitious for good, but to rejoice with those who rejoice, and to ask God for the wisdom from above (James 1:5).

The Gospel shows envy at work in a small town. Jesus has come to Nazareth, “where he had been brought up”, and in the synagogue he reads from Isaiah: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor.” At first all speak well of him and are amazed. Then comes the question that hides a doubt: “Is not this Joseph's son?” They cannot see beyond the carpenter's family they have known since he was a boy. Jesus answers with the proverb, “no prophet is accepted in the prophet's hometown”, and with two stories from their own Scriptures. In the great famine of Elijah's day, God sent the prophet to a widow of Zarephath in Sidon, a foreigner (1 Kings 17:9). In the time of Elisha, the one leper who was cleansed was Naaman the Syrian (2 Kings 5:14). God's mercy goes beyond every border, and the people of Nazareth, who wanted the miracles for themselves, cannot bear it. “All in the synagogue were filled with rage”: they drive him out of the town and lead him to the brow of the hill. This is the first of the rejections that end at the Cross, and yet, “he passed through the midst of them and went on his way”. His hour had not yet come.

For Kerala the lesson is close to home. We too can fail to see God in what is familiar: the parish priest we have known since he was a deacon, the sister who taught us, the neighbour's son who has become a teacher or a doctor, the grandmother whose prayer holds the house together. Familiarity can close our eyes. And we too can be angry when God is generous to someone we did not expect to be blessed. Today we ask for the wisdom from above: to see Christ in the ordinary, to welcome the blessing of others without envy, and to be, in our own homes and parishes, among those who make peace and gather a harvest of righteousness.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint James asks today: who among you is wise? He answers: the one who shows by a good life that his works are done with gentleness (James 3:13). Wisdom is not what we know; it is how we live.

James names two wisdoms. The wisdom of the world is full of envy and selfish ambition, and wherever it rules there is disorder. The wisdom from above is pure, peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy. Which one do we practise at home, in the parish and in our village?

In the Gospel Jesus returns to Nazareth. At first people are amazed, but then they say: “Is not this Joseph's son?” They cannot see God's work in someone they know so well. Then, when Jesus says that God cared for a widow in Sidon and for Naaman the Syrian, they are filled with rage. They envy the mercy shown to others.

That can happen to us too. We can overlook the grace that comes through the people closest to us, and we can be unhappy when God blesses someone else.

Let us ask for the wisdom from above. “A harvest of righteousness is sown in peace for those who make peace” (James 3:18). This week let us be peacemakers: let us rejoice with others and thank God for the people at home. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus came back to his own town, but the people there said: he is only Joseph's son! They did not see who he really was. Let us look carefully at the people at home, because God does wonderful things through them. And when a friend gets a prize, let us clap for them and be happy. That is God's wisdom!

Prayer

God of wisdom, you give your wisdom from above to those who ask in faith. Make us pure in heart, peaceable, gentle, willing to yield and full of mercy. Heal in us the envy that grieves at another's good, and teach us to rejoice with those you bless. Give us eyes to see you in the people we know best, so that we may not drive you from our towns as Nazareth did. Make us peacemakers, and let the seed of righteousness we sow in peace bear a rich harvest in your Church. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

James asks who is truly wise, and answers that wisdom is seen not in clever talk but in a gentle, peaceable, merciful life. In Luke’s Nazareth scene, a congregation that has just admired Jesus turns on him when he reminds them that God’s mercy has always reached beyond the borders they expected. The first reading asks us to examine what is in our hearts; the Gospel shows what happens when the heart is closed to a grace that is larger than its own plans.

James 3:13–18 · The wisdom from above

Setting.

The letter has just warned teachers about the power of the tongue (3:1–12). Now James asks what real wisdom looks like. In the Jewish tradition a wise person is not simply clever but one who fears the Lord and lives well (Proverbs 1:7; Sirach 1). Two kinds of “wisdom” are set side by side, and the test is the fruit each produces.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the question and the test: a good life, with gentle works.
  • vv. 14–16: false wisdom: envy and selfish ambition, which breed disorder.
  • vv. 17–18: true wisdom, “from above”: seven marks, and a harvest of peace.

Key words.

  • prautēs, gentleness, meekness: The quality of strength under control, like a trained horse. Jesus describes himself as “gentle” (Matthew 11:29). Wisdom does not shout.
  • zēlos kai eritheia, envy and selfish ambition: The second word was originally used of those who canvass for office for their own gain. Wisdom that serves only its own advantage is “earthly, unspiritual, devilish”.
  • eirēnē, peace: In the Bible it is not only the absence of conflict but wholeness, right relations with God and neighbour. Those who “make peace” sow it as a farmer sows seed.

Verse by verse.

“Show by your good life that your works are done with gentleness born of wisdom.” (3:13)

Wisdom is proved in daily conduct. A person may know much and still lack it.

“For where there is envy and selfish ambition, there will also be disorder and wickedness of every kind.” (3:16)

James looks at the life of a community. Envy and the wish to be first tear a parish, a family or a community apart.

“But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits, without a trace of partiality or hypocrisy.” (3:17)

A portrait of a wise heart. “First pure”: wisdom begins with a clean heart, and peace follows. The Greek for “willing to yield” means open to persuasion, not stubborn.

“And a harvest of righteousness is sown in peace for those who make peace.” (3:18)

The saying echoes the Beatitude, “Blessed are the peacemakers” (Matthew 5:9). Peace is a seed that is sown quietly and bears fruit in time.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this portrait the working of the Holy Spirit, whose gifts include wisdom and understanding (CCC 1831; Isaiah 11:2). The Catechism, speaking of the Beatitude of peacemakers, teaches that earthly peace is the image and fruit of the peace of Christ (CCC 2305). Such peace requires the removal of envy and the other roots of discord (compare CCC 2317).

Luke 4:25–30 · No prophet at home

Setting.

Jesus has come to Nazareth, where he grew up, and in the synagogue read from Isaiah 61: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me…” (4:16–21). At first “all spoke well of him” (4:22). Then he says that no prophet is accepted in his hometown (4:24), and gives two examples from Israel’s own Scriptures. These verses are the second half of that conversation.

The anger that follows is the anger of one village at one moment. Luke does not blame a people. Jesus is a Jew teaching in a synagogue, and many Jews believe in him throughout Luke and Acts. The story shows that the Gospel is first heard in Israel’s house and is meant for all.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–26: Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17).
  • v. 27: Elisha and Naaman the Syrian (2 Kings 5).
  • vv. 28–29: the rage and the attempt to throw him off the hill.
  • v. 30: he passes through the midst and goes on.

Key words.

  • thymos, rage, anger: The congregation “were filled with rage”. The mood changes within minutes from wonder to violence, which shows how quickly a hardened heart can turn.
  • ekballō, to throw out: The word is used elsewhere for driving out demons. Luke may be hinting at a certain irony: the people drive out the one who is able to drive out all evil.
  • dielthōn dia mesou, passing through the midst: He is not caught. Many readers see here a foreshadowing: at the Passion he will go to Jerusalem willingly, and no one will take his life from him against his will (John 10:18).

Verse by verse.

“there were many widows in Israel in the time of Elijah” (4:25)

Jesus does not say these widows were unworthy. He says God’s choice was not limited by the borders people drew. The famine lasted “three years and six months”, the same period named in James 5:17.

“yet Elijah was sent to none of them except to a widow at Zarephath in Sidon.” (4:26)

Zarephath lay outside Israel, near Sidon. The prophet was sent to a foreign widow, and her jar of flour and jug of oil did not run out (1 Kings 17:8–16).

“There were also many lepers in Israel in the time of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian.” (4:27)

Naaman was a commander of an enemy army, yet he was healed when he obeyed the word of the prophet (2 Kings 5:14).

“But he passed through the midst of them and went on his way.” (4:30)

The scene ends without explanation. The Gospel will not be stopped by one village’s refusal.

How the Church reads it.

Both prophets, Elijah and Elisha, were sent to people beyond Israel; the Church reads this as a sign that God’s saving plan reaches all nations, a plan that Luke will follow in Acts. The Catechism teaches that Elijah is “the father of the prophets” and that his prayer is a model for the people of God (CCC 2582). Jesus is the greater prophet, and like the prophets he meets resistance (Luke 13:33–34); the Church has always taught that he accepted the Passion willingly (CCC 609).

Questions people ask

Why did the people of Nazareth become so angry?

Luke does not say in a single sentence, but the reason can be seen in the two examples. Jesus implies that God’s favour is not a right that belongs to one town, and that outsiders, a widow of Sidon and a Syrian commander, responded to God better than many in Israel. This was hard to hear. The passage is a challenge to every community, including the Church, never to treat God’s gifts as its own property.

For study and teaching

  • James lists the marks of wisdom from above. Which of them do I most need to grow in this week: purity, peace, gentleness, willingness to yield, mercy?
  • The people of Nazareth were offended that God’s mercy reaches outsiders. Who are the “outsiders” whose good I find hard to welcome?
