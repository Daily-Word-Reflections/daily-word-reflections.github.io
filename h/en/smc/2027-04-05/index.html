Word for the day

“Then their eyes were opened, and they recognized him; and he vanished from their sight.” Luke 24:31

Reflection

The Easter octave is over, and the Church goes back to ordinary Mondays: to work, to school, to the market and the bus stand. The readings of this second week of the Resurrection are chosen for exactly that return. They ask what Easter looks like in a parish with quarrels, in a family with tensions, on a road walked by two tired disciples. Paul begins with a parish that had split into fan clubs. News has reached him through Chloe's household: “there are quarrels among you, my brothers and sisters. What I mean is that each of you says, ‘I belong to Paul,’ or ‘I belong to Apollos,’ or ‘I belong to Cephas,’ or ‘I belong to Christ.’” Each group had chosen its favourite preacher, and even the people who said ‘I belong to Christ’ had turned the Lord into a party badge. Paul answers with three short questions that cut through everything: “Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” There is only one Lord who died for us and only one name into which we were baptised. Paul even thanks God that he baptised very few of them, so that no one could claim to belong to him. His task was something else: “For Christ did not send me to baptize but to proclaim the gospel, and not with eloquent wisdom, so that the cross of Christ might not be emptied of its power.” A Church that argues about its preachers has forgotten its Crucified and Risen Lord. Unity is not a matter of everyone liking the same priest or the same style; it is the fruit of looking together at the Cross and the empty tomb.

The Gospel takes us back to the evening of Easter Sunday, on the road to Emmaus. Two disciples have walked seven miles with a stranger who explained the Scriptures to them. Now they reach their village, and “he walked ahead as if he were going on.” The Risen Lord never forces his company on anyone. He waits to be invited. “But they urged him strongly, saying, ‘Stay with us, because it is almost evening and the day is now nearly over.’ So he went in to stay with them.” That prayer, Stay with us, has been on Christian lips ever since; it is the prayer of every evening and of every life that feels the light going. Then comes the moment the whole story was moving towards: “When he was at the table with them, he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them. Then their eyes were opened, and they recognized him; and he vanished from their sight.” The four verbs are the verbs of the Last Supper, and of every Holy Qurbana: he took, blessed, broke and gave. They knew him not by his face but by what he did with bread. And as soon as they knew him, he was no longer visible in the old way, because from now on he would be present to them in the breaking of the bread.

What happens next is the shape of the Christian life. “They said to each other, ‘Were not our hearts burning within us while he was talking to us on the road, while he was opening the scriptures to us?’ That same hour they got up and returned to Jerusalem.” It was night, and it was seven miles, but the burning heart cannot wait till morning. They find the community gathered, and before they can tell their news they are told: “The Lord has risen indeed, and he has appeared to Simon!” Then they share their own story: “how he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.” Notice the movement: away from Jerusalem in sadness, back to Jerusalem in joy; from two scattered disciples to the gathered Church around Peter. The Risen Lord heals divisions not by argument but by gathering his people at one table. Paul's quarrelling Corinthians and the two lonely disciples need the same cure. In the Syro-Malabar Holy Qurbana, after the anaphora, the priest breaks the Holy Bread and signs the Precious Blood with it, uniting them again: the Body broken and the Blood poured out are joined, because the One who died is alive. Every parish that gathers at that altar is being made one, quarrels and all.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Easter octave is over and we are back to ordinary Mondays. Today's readings show what Easter looks like in ordinary life: in a parish with quarrels, and on a road walked by two tired disciples.

Paul hears that the Corinthians are divided: I belong to Paul, I belong to Apollos, I belong to Cephas. He asks: Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? There is only one Lord who died for us, and only one name in which we were baptised.

We have our own versions of this: my priest, my group, my side of the family. Paul says: look at the Cross. When we look together at the Crucified and Risen Lord, our little parties lose their power.

In the Gospel two disciples walk to Emmaus with a stranger. At the village he seems to go on, and they beg him: Stay with us, because it is almost evening. The Lord waits to be invited.

At table he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them, and their eyes were opened. They knew him in the breaking of the bread, as we know him at every Holy Qurbana. And that same hour they ran back to Jerusalem to the gathered community.

This week, let us invite him every evening: Lord, stay with us. And let us bring one quarrel to his table, so that he may make us one. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, two friends of Jesus did not recognise him on the road. But when he broke the bread at supper, they knew him! At Holy Qurbana, when the priest breaks the Holy Bread, Jesus is there for us too.

Tonight, before you sleep, say a little prayer: ‘Jesus, stay with us!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you walked with your disciples on the road and made yourself known to them in the breaking of the bread. Stay with us, for it is almost evening. Open the Scriptures to us, set our hearts on fire, and heal the divisions in our parishes and families, so that we may be one in you, who were crucified for us and live for ever with the Father and the Holy Spirit. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about recognizing the one Lord. Paul pleads with the Corinthians not to split into parties around their preachers, because Christ is not divided and it was not Paul who was crucified for them. At Emmaus two disciples recognize the risen Jesus in the breaking of the bread and run back to the community in Jerusalem. The Lord is known at the table, and he gathers those who were scattered.

1 Corinthians 1:10–17 · Has Christ been divided?

Setting.

Paul founded the Church at Corinth, a busy port city of Greece, around AD 50–51, and wrote this letter a few years later from Ephesus (16:8). He has heard from “Chloe's people”, probably members of her household, that the community is breaking into groups, each attached to a favourite teacher. This is the first problem he takes up, and it shapes the whole letter.

Structure.

  • v. 10: the appeal for unity.
  • vv. 11–12: the report and the slogans.
  • vv. 13–16: three questions, and Paul's thanks that he baptized so few.
  • v. 17: his mission: to preach the Cross.

Key words.

  • schismata, divisions: Literally “tears”, as in a torn garment. It is the root of our word schism. Paul is not speaking yet of separate Churches but of factions inside one community.
  • katērtismenoi, united: The verb means to mend or restore. Mark uses it of James and John mending their nets (Mark 1:19). Unity is something to be repaired with patience, like a net, so that it can hold.
  • Kēphas, Cephas: The Aramaic name of Peter, meaning “rock”. Paul uses it often. Apollos was an eloquent Jewish Christian from Alexandria who taught at Corinth after Paul (Acts 18:24–28).

Verse by verse.

“Now I appeal to you, brothers and sisters, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you be in agreement and that there be no divisions among you,” (1:10)
“Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” (1:13)

Each question expects the answer no. The Church belongs to the one who died for her, not to the one who preached to her.

“For Christ did not send me to baptize but to proclaim the gospel, and not with eloquent wisdom, so that the cross of Christ might not be emptied of its power.” (1:17)

Paul does not make light of Baptism; he has just said that they were baptized into Christ's name. He means that his own task was to preach, and that the power is in the Cross, not in the preacher's skill.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council begins its decree on ecumenism by saying that Christ founded one Church, and that division openly contradicts his will, is a scandal to the world and harms the preaching of the Gospel (Unitatis Redintegratio 1). Paul's questions still speak to every parish and diocese: whenever we gather around a person rather than around Christ, the Body is torn.

Luke 24:28–35 · Known in the breaking of the bread

Setting.

On the day of the Resurrection two disciples, one named Cleopas, are walking from Jerusalem to a village called Emmaus, sad and confused. A stranger joins them and explains the Scriptures about the Messiah (24:13–27). As evening falls they reach the village. This reading tells what happened at the table and afterwards.

Structure.

  • vv. 28–29: he seems to go on; they urge him to stay.
  • vv. 30–31: at table he takes, blesses, breaks and gives; their eyes are opened; he vanishes.
  • v. 32: their hearts were burning.
  • vv. 33–35: they return to Jerusalem; the Lord has appeared to Simon; they tell their story.

Key words.

  • meinon, stay: The same verb that John uses for abiding. The disciples' prayer, “Stay with us”, has become one of the Church's best-loved prayers at evening.
  • klasis tou artou, the breaking of the bread: The phrase the first Christians used for the Eucharist (Acts 2:42). Luke's four verbs, took, blessed, broke and gave, are the verbs of the feeding of the crowd (9:16) and of the Last Supper (22:19).

Verse by verse.

“But they urged him strongly, saying, “Stay with us, because it is almost evening and the day is now nearly over.” So he went in to stay with them.” (24:29)

He does not force his company on them. He waits to be invited.

“When he was at the table with them, he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them.” (24:30)

The guest becomes the host.

“Then they told what had happened on the road, and how he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.” (24:35)

The story moves in the same order as the Holy Qurbana: first the Scriptures opened, then the bread broken, then the mission.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists “the breaking of bread” among the names of the Eucharist, the name by which the first Christians called their gatherings, and it points to Emmaus (CCC 1329). It describes the Eucharistic celebration as following the same movement as the paschal meal of the risen Jesus with his disciples: he walked with them, explained the Scriptures, and broke the bread (CCC 1347).

Questions people ask

Does Paul make light of Baptism when he thanks God that he baptized few?

No. He is thankful only because no one can now claim to belong to Paul's party. In the same letter he teaches that “in the one Spirit we were all baptized into one body” (12:13). Baptism unites us to Christ, not to the minister who baptizes.

For study and teaching

  • Around whom or what do I sometimes gather, instead of around Christ? What helps me mend the net of unity in my parish?
  • The disciples recognized Jesus only at the table. How can I prepare my heart so that I recognize him at the Holy Qurbana?
