Word for the day

“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Reflection

On the last day of July the Church remembers a soldier who became a saint. Ignatius was born in 1491 in the castle of Loyola in the Basque country of Spain, the youngest of a large family, and was brought up to be a knight, with dreams of fame and glory. In 1521, at the siege of Pamplona, a cannonball broke his leg. During the long months of recovery he asked for novels of chivalry, but the house had only a Life of Christ and a book on the lives of the saints. He read them, and he noticed that when he thought about the saints, he was left joyful and peaceful, whereas his dreams of worldly glory left him dry. That was the beginning of a conversion. He laid down his sword before the altar of Our Lady at Montserrat, lived in prayer in a cave at Manresa, and wrote the notes which became the Spiritual Exercises. As a student in Paris he gathered companions, among them Francis Xavier and Peter Faber, and in 1540 the Pope approved their Society of Jesus. He died in Rome on 31 July 1556 and was canonised in 1622. His motto has gone round the world: Ad maiorem Dei gloriam, “for the greater glory of God.”

That motto comes from today's reading from Paul: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God. Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God, just as I try to please everyone in everything I do, not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved. Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.” Everything: eating and drinking, work and rest, prayer and the market. Ignatius taught that God can be found in all things and that every action can be offered to him; he built a whole spirituality out of the morning offering of the day. One of his companions, Francis Xavier, came to India in 1542, working first at Goa and then among the fishing communities of the coast of southern India and Travancore; Jesuit schools and colleges have since served many families in Kerala. The Holy Qurbana in our Church shows the same principle in another form: the bread and the wine, ordinary things of the table, are lifted up so that God may be glorified in them.

The Gospel is one of the strongest of Luke. Large crowds travel with Jesus, and he turns and says what no recruiting officer would say. “Whoever comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and even life itself, cannot be my disciple.” In the language of Jesus, “hate” means to put in second place: Matthew has it plainly, “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me.” The family is not despised; but Christ must come first. Then two small parables: a man who wants to build a tower sits down first and counts the cost, and a king going out to war considers whether he has ten thousand men to meet twenty thousand. “So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” Ignatius did precisely that. When he put his sword on the altar of Montserrat, and when he left his family's wealth and a possible career at court, he was not despising the world; he was counting the cost and choosing the better kingdom. His prayer, which is known in many languages as the Suscipe, runs in substance: Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding and my entire will; all that I have and possess you have given me, and to you I give it back; give me only your love and your grace, and I am rich enough.

The weekday readings are quieter and harder. In the Gospel, Jesus and the disciples pass the fig tree in the morning and find it “withered away to its roots.” Peter remembers and says, “Rabbi, look!” Jesus answers, not with an explanation but with a call: “Have faith in God.” And he adds the sentence about the mountain: “if you say to this mountain, ‘Be taken up and thrown into the sea,’ and if you do not doubt in your heart, but believe that what you say will come to pass, it will be done for you. So I tell you, whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours.” The Church has always understood this as a promise that faith and prayer, joined to the will of God, can move what looks immovable. A lame ex-soldier with a handful of friends started a movement that now serves in nearly every country; that is a mountain moved. In Kaitha, the harvest season, we pray in faith for the harvest of the Lord.

Paul's words to the Thessalonians have a darker edge, and they must be read with care. He speaks of “the mystery of lawlessness” that is already at work, of a power of lies, signs and wonders that deceives “those who are perishing, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved.” The apostle does not give a timetable; he gives a warning that deception is real, and that the defence against it is the love of truth. A little further on he gives thanks that God chose the Thessalonians “as the first fruits for salvation through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth.” Ignatius made this into the art of discernment: not every light, not every wonder and not every fervour comes from God, and we must test the spirits and hold to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). A believer in the world of the mobile phone and the news feed, where false things are quickly spread, needs this love of the truth as much as any Thessalonian. Tomorrow we begin August; let us offer to God the whole of the month, and say with Ignatius: for the greater glory of God.

Homily outline

Theme: “Whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.”

On the memorial of Saint Ignatius of Loyola: everything for the glory of God, counting the cost of discipleship, faith in prayer, and the love of truth.

1. Everything for the glory of God

  • Eating, drinking, working, praying.
  • Find God in all things.
  • Give no offense, and seek the good of many.
“The earth is the Lord’s and all that is in it,” Psalm 24:1
“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Apply: Say each morning: “Lord, today everything for your glory.”

2. Count the cost

  • Christ comes first, even before family.
  • The tower and the king.
  • Give up all that keeps you from him.
“Prepare your work outside, get everything ready for you in the field; and after that build your house.” Proverbs 24:27
“So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” Luke 14:33

Apply: Write the cost and the gain of one decision you have postponed for Christ's sake.

3. Have faith, and love the truth

  • Faith and prayer move mountains.
  • Deception is real; the defence is love of the truth.
  • Test everything; hold fast to what is good.
“Then when you call upon me and come and pray to me, I will hear you.” Jeremiah 29:12
“but test everything; hold fast to what is good;” 1 Thessalonians 5:21

Apply: Test one message you receive before you pass it on.

Ignatius shows that a whole life can be given to the glory of God, in ordinary things, with a clear sense of the cost.

Let us offer everything and say: take, Lord, and receive.

Take-home resolution: This week, offer your day each morning, count one cost honestly, and test what you read before you share it.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers Saint Ignatius of Loyola. He was a soldier in Spain, about five hundred years ago, who loved to dream about glory. At the siege of Pamplona a cannonball broke his leg, and he lay in bed for months. He wanted to read stories of knights, but the house had only a book on the life of Christ and a book on the lives of the saints. He read them, and slowly his heart changed. He left his sword at the altar of Our Lady, and he began a new life. He founded the Society of Jesus, and one of his first friends, Francis Xavier, came to India.

His motto comes from the second reading: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31). Do everything: eating, working, studying, resting, praying. Ignatius taught us to find God in all things and to offer the whole day to him. Every morning we can say: Lord, today everything for your glory.

The Gospel is demanding. Jesus says that to follow him we must put him first, even before our family, and give up everything that stands in his way (Luke 14:26, 33). He gives two small parables: a man building a tower first counts the cost, and a king going to war first counts his soldiers (Luke 14:28–32). Ignatius counted the cost. He left the court and the comfort and chose Jesus. His prayer says: “Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding and my entire will.”

The weekday readings give two more lessons. In the Gospel, Jesus says: “Have faith in God” (Mark 11:22). Faith and prayer can move mountains. A lame soldier with a few friends started a movement that now serves in nearly every country. And Saint Paul warns the Thessalonians that deception is real, and that the defence against it is the love of the truth (2 Thessalonians 2:10). We need this love in a world of rumours and false messages. Test everything, and hold fast to what is good.

Tomorrow August begins. Let us offer the month to God, saying with Saint Ignatius: for the greater glory of God. Saint Ignatius, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Ignatius was a soldier whose leg was hurt. In bed he read about Jesus and the saints, and he said, ‘I want to be a soldier for Jesus!’ He started the Jesuits, and his friend Francis Xavier came to India. Ignatius's motto was: for the greater glory of God. Every morning you can say: ‘Jesus, today everything I do is for you.’ Then even brushing your teeth and doing your homework is a prayer!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you called Ignatius from the battlefield to the service of your kingdom, and taught him to find you in all things. Teach us to do everything for your glory: our work and our rest, our eating and our prayer. Give us the courage to count the cost and to put you before everything, and the faith to ask you for great things. Keep us from deception, and give us a love of the truth that tests every spirit and holds to what is good. Bless the Jesuits and all who teach in the schools and colleges of our land. Take, Lord, and receive our liberty, our memory, our understanding and our will; give us only your love and your grace, and we shall be rich enough. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The weekday readings face the mystery of evil and the power of faith. Paul tells the Thessalonians that the lawlessness already at work will be unmasked and destroyed by the Lord Jesus at his coming, and that those who refuse to love the truth are deceived. In Mark, the day after the cursing of the fig tree, Jesus answers Peter’s astonishment with a call: “Have faith in God”, and promises that prayer offered in faith is heard. Today the Church also keeps the memory of St Ignatius of Loyola and gives two readings for him: Paul’s rule “do everything for the glory of God”, and Jesus’ plain word to the crowds that a disciple must be ready to give up everything, after counting the cost like a builder or a king. The common thread is truth and faith: love the truth, trust God, and do all for his glory.

2 Thessalonians 2:5–12 · Those who refused to love the truth

Setting.

The letter answers a community alarmed by a rumour that “the day of the Lord is already here” (2:2). Paul (the Church receives the letter as his, though some scholars think a disciple wrote it in his name) reminds them of what he had taught: before the Lord’s day there must come a great rebellion and the appearance of “the lawless one” (2:3–4). In today’s verses he says that this power of lawlessness is already at work, held back for now by someone or something he does not name, and that when the Lord comes, he will destroy the lawless one “with the breath of his mouth”.

This is one of the most debated passages in the New Testament. Paul’s readers knew who or what “restrains”, but we do not. Early Christian writers suggested the Roman Empire or the order of law, and others suggested other things; St Augustine honestly confessed that he did not know what Paul meant (City of God 20.19). The main teaching is plain: evil has a limit, Christ will overcome it, and the only protection is love of the truth.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: Paul reminds them of his teaching; the mystery of lawlessness is already at work, but is restrained.
  • v. 8: the lawless one will be revealed, and the Lord Jesus will destroy him by the manifestation of his coming.
  • vv. 9–10: his coming is with Satan’s power, false signs and deception for those who are perishing because they refused to love the truth.
  • vv. 11–12: God lets a powerful delusion work on those who have not believed the truth but took pleasure in unrighteousness.

Key words.

  • anomia, lawlessness: Opposition to God’s order. The “lawless one” stands for the power that sets himself against God and wants to be worshipped.
  • to katechon / ho katechōn, what restrains / the one who restrains: A neuter and a masculine form in the same passage. Its meaning is not certain, and the Church has never defined it.
  • agapē tēs alētheias, love of the truth: Truth is not only to be believed but to be loved. Those who refuse the love of the truth are open to deception.

Verse by verse.

“For the mystery of lawlessness is already at work” (2:7)

Evil is already present in history. Christians are not surprised by it, nor are they to be afraid.

“whom the Lord Jesus will destroy with the breath of his mouth, annihilating him by the manifestation of his coming.” (2:8)

The victory is Christ’s. The language comes from Isaiah 11:4: the word of the Lord is enough.

“because they refused to love the truth and so be saved.” (2:10)

Deception is not only the work of a trickster; it grows where people refuse the truth. Love of the truth is a protection.

“For this reason God sends them a powerful delusion, leading them to believe what is false” (2:11)

This is a biblical way of speaking of God’s permission: those who persistently reject the truth are left to the consequences of their choice (compare Romans 1:24–28). God is not the author of evil.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that before Christ’s second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers, in which a religious deception offers people an apparent solution to their problems at the price of apostasy from the truth (CCC 675–677). It teaches also that God is never the cause of evil, but permits it, and in his wisdom brings good from it (CCC 311–314). The Church does not try to say who the “lawless one” is, or when he will come, and she warns against calculating the signs; she calls her children to watch and to love the truth.

Mark 11:20–24 · Have faith in God

Setting.

The reading is the second half of a pair. On the way into Jerusalem, Jesus had cursed a fig tree that had leaves but no fruit (11:12–14), then cleansed the Temple (11:15–19). Now, on the way back the next morning, the disciples see that the tree has withered from the roots. Mark places the Temple action between the two parts of the fig tree story, so the tree is a sign: a religion of leaves without fruit stands under judgment. The sign is directed against the failure of those entrusted with the Temple, not against the Jewish people.

Peter points out the tree. Jesus does not explain the sign but turns the disciples to faith in God and the power of prayer.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: the withered fig tree; Peter remembers and points it out.
  • v. 22: Jesus: “Have faith in God.”
  • v. 23: a believer who says to this mountain, be taken up and thrown into the sea, and does not doubt, will see it done.
  • v. 24: whatever you ask in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours.

Key words.

  • pisteuete theou, have faith in God: Literally “have the faith of God” or “faith in God”. The faith meant is trust in God’s power and faithfulness, not belief in our own power.
  • to oros touto, this mountain: Probably the Mount of Olives or the Temple Mount, where Jesus was standing. Moving a mountain was a Jewish figure for doing what seems impossible.

Verse by verse.

“Have faith in God.” (11:22)

The whole teaching is in these words. Everything depends on God, and so everything is possible to one who trusts him.

“if you say to this mountain, ‘Be taken up and thrown into the sea,’ and if you do not doubt in your heart, but believe that what you say will come to pass, it will be done for you.” (11:23)

A bold picture, not an instruction to try magic tricks. It shows the power of faith to overcome what seems immovable, in line with God’s will.

“whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours.” (11:24)

Prayer is not a way to get what we want but a sharing in God’s purposes. St John explains that we receive what we ask “according to his will” (1 John 5:14).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ words on faith show that nothing is impossible for the one who believes (CCC 2609–2610), and also that prayer of petition must be made with trust in God’s wisdom, since he knows what we need and his answers are for our true good (CCC 2734–2737). The Church teaches that we should ask in the name of Jesus and with the same mind as his, and then we will be heard. Saints who suffered, such as St Ignatius in his illness and conversion, show that the answer sometimes comes in a form we did not expect.

1 Corinthians 10:31–11:1 · Do everything for the glory of God

Setting.

Paul has been discussing whether Christians in Corinth may eat food that was offered to idols (chapters 8–10). His answer: they have freedom, but love for the weak limits it. This passage is his conclusion: do everything for the glory of God, give no offence, and imitate Paul as he imitates Christ. The chapter break falls in the wrong place; 11:1 belongs with the end of chapter 10.

Structure.

  • v. 31: whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all for the glory of God.
  • vv. 32–33: give no offence to Jews, Greeks or the church of God, as Paul seeks the good of many that they may be saved.
  • 11:1: be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.

Key words.

  • doxa theou, glory of God: The weight and radiance of God. To live for it is to make God known in daily life.
  • mimētai, imitators: Paul invites his readers to follow him as he follows Christ. Holiness is learned from living examples.

Verse by verse.

“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” (10:31)

Nothing is too ordinary. Meals, work and rest can all be offered to God.

“not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved.” (10:33)

The measure of freedom is love: not what suits me but what helps others to the Lord.

How the Church reads it.

St Ignatius of Loyola made verse 31 the heart of his spirituality: his motto, Ad majorem Dei gloriam, “for the greater glory of God”, is a way of living this verse. The Catechism teaches that all human activity, however ordinary, can be offered to God and sanctified, so that work becomes a way of holiness (CCC 2427).

Luke 14:25–33 · A disciple must give up everything

Setting.

Jesus is on the road to Jerusalem with large crowds, and he turns to tell them what following him means. The language is sharp on purpose: “hate” father and mother, carry the cross, give up all possessions. “Hate” is a Semitic way of saying “love less, put second”, as Matthew 10:37 shows when it says “whoever loves father or mother more than me”. Two short parables, the builder of the tower and the king going to war, show that following Christ demands careful reflection and total commitment, not a casual enthusiasm.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–27: whoever does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, even life itself, and does not carry the cross, cannot be my disciple.
  • vv. 28–30: the builder who does not count the cost will be ridiculed.
  • vv. 31–32: the king who does not weigh his forces makes peace with an approaching enemy.
  • v. 33: so none of you can be my disciple without giving up all possessions.

Key words.

  • misei, hate: A Hebrew idiom of comparison: to put in a second place. It does not mean contempt for family; the commandment to honour father and mother remains.
  • apotassetai, gives up, says farewell to: The verb means to take leave of something, to let go. The disciple lets go of what could keep him from following.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” (14:27)

The cross is a daily following, not a single heroic act. Jesus walks the way ahead of us.

“For which of you, intending to build a tower, does not first sit down and estimate the cost” (14:28)

Jesus asks for clear-eyed commitment. He does not hide the cost, and he does not ask us to carry it alone.

“So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” (14:33)

Everything is placed in God’s hands: possessions, plans and ties. What is given to God is not lost but received back in a new way.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544), and that family ties, though important, are not absolute (CCC 2232). St Ignatius, a soldier from the Basque country who was wounded at Pamplona in 1521 and began his conversion while recovering, understood this saying in his own life: he gave up his career and wealth and, after pilgrimage and study, founded with his companions the Society of Jesus, approved in 1540. His prayer in the Spiritual Exercises, “Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty”, is a way of living verse 33.

Questions people ask

Who was St Ignatius of Loyola?

Ignatius was born in 1491 in Loyola in the Basque country of Spain and was a soldier. In 1521 a cannonball wounded his leg at Pamplona, and during his long recovery he read the lives of Christ and the saints, which began his conversion. He spent time in prayer at Manresa, where he began to write the Spiritual Exercises, studied in Paris, and with a few companions, among them St Francis Xavier, founded the Society of Jesus, which Pope Paul III approved in 1540. He died in Rome on 31 July 1556. Francis Xavier came to India in 1542 and also worked on the Kerala coast. Ignatius is remembered for his teaching on discerning God’s will and for finding God in all things.

What does Paul mean by “the one who restrains” in 2 Thessalonians?

No one knows for sure. Paul’s first readers knew it, but it is not recorded. Ancient Christian writers suggested the Roman Empire or the order of law; others suggested other things, and Augustine admitted that he did not know. The Church has not defined the meaning. What matters is Paul’s message: evil is limited, Christ will overcome it, and the believer’s task is to love the truth and hold to what Paul taught.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar Church remembers St Ignatius with thanks for the Jesuit missionaries and teachers who have served Kerala through the centuries. The Gospel for him asks the question every family can take to prayer: what is my first loyalty, and what would I give up to follow Christ? The Holy Qurbana offers every day the same answer, in which Christ gives himself for us and we offer ourselves to the Father with him.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that evil is limited and that Christ will destroy it. How does this give me courage when I see wrong in the world?
  • Jesus says, “Have faith in God.” In what situation do I most need to put my whole trust in God?
  • Ignatius lived for the greater glory of God. What ordinary part of my day can I offer to God’s glory this week?
