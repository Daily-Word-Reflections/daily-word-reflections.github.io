Word for the day

“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Reflection

On the last day of July the Church remembers a soldier who became a saint. Ignatius was born in 1491 in the castle of Loyola in the Basque country of Spain, the youngest of a large family, and was brought up to be a knight, with dreams of fame and glory. In 1521, at the siege of Pamplona, a cannonball broke his leg. During the long months of recovery he asked for novels of chivalry, but the house had only a Life of Christ and a book on the lives of the saints. He read them, and he noticed that when he thought about the saints, he was left joyful and peaceful, whereas his dreams of worldly glory left him dry. That was the beginning of a conversion. He laid down his sword before the altar of Our Lady at Montserrat, lived in prayer in a cave at Manresa, and wrote the notes which became the Spiritual Exercises. As a student in Paris he gathered companions, among them Francis Xavier and Peter Faber, and in 1540 the Pope approved their Society of Jesus. He died in Rome on 31 July 1556 and was canonised in 1622. His motto has gone round the world: Ad maiorem Dei gloriam, “for the greater glory of God.”

That motto comes from today's reading from Paul: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God. Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God, just as I try to please everyone in everything I do, not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, so that they may be saved. Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.” Everything: eating and drinking, work and rest, prayer and the market. Ignatius taught that God can be found in all things and that every action can be offered to him; he built a whole spirituality out of the morning offering of the day. One of his companions, Francis Xavier, came to India in 1542, working first at Goa and then among the fishing communities of the coast of southern India and Travancore; Jesuit schools and colleges have since served many families in Kerala. The Holy Qurbana in our Church shows the same principle in another form: the bread and the wine, ordinary things of the table, are lifted up so that God may be glorified in them.

The Gospel is one of the strongest of Luke. Large crowds travel with Jesus, and he turns and says what no recruiting officer would say. “Whoever comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and even life itself, cannot be my disciple.” In the language of Jesus, “hate” means to put in second place: Matthew has it plainly, “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me.” The family is not despised; but Christ must come first. Then two small parables: a man who wants to build a tower sits down first and counts the cost, and a king going out to war considers whether he has ten thousand men to meet twenty thousand. “So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” Ignatius did precisely that. When he put his sword on the altar of Montserrat, and when he left his family's wealth and a possible career at court, he was not despising the world; he was counting the cost and choosing the better kingdom. His prayer, which is known in many languages as the Suscipe, runs in substance: Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding and my entire will; all that I have and possess you have given me, and to you I give it back; give me only your love and your grace, and I am rich enough.

The weekday readings are quieter and harder. In the Gospel, Jesus and the disciples pass the fig tree in the morning and find it “withered away to its roots.” Peter remembers and says, “Rabbi, look!” Jesus answers, not with an explanation but with a call: “Have faith in God.” And he adds the sentence about the mountain: “if you say to this mountain, ‘Be taken up and thrown into the sea,’ and if you do not doubt in your heart, but believe that what you say will come to pass, it will be done for you. So I tell you, whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours.” The Church has always understood this as a promise that faith and prayer, joined to the will of God, can move what looks immovable. A lame ex-soldier with a handful of friends started a movement that now serves in nearly every country; that is a mountain moved. In Kaitha, the harvest season, we pray in faith for the harvest of the Lord.

Paul's words to the Thessalonians have a darker edge, and they must be read with care. He speaks of “the mystery of lawlessness” that is already at work, of a power of lies, signs and wonders that deceives “those who are perishing, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved.” The apostle does not give a timetable; he gives a warning that deception is real, and that the defence against it is the love of truth. A little further on he gives thanks that God chose the Thessalonians “as the first fruits for salvation through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth.” Ignatius made this into the art of discernment: not every light, not every wonder and not every fervour comes from God, and we must test the spirits and hold to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). A believer in the world of the mobile phone and the news feed, where false things are quickly spread, needs this love of the truth as much as any Thessalonian. Tomorrow we begin August; let us offer to God the whole of the month, and say with Ignatius: for the greater glory of God.

Homily outline

Theme: “Whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.”

On the memorial of Saint Ignatius of Loyola: everything for the glory of God, counting the cost of discipleship, faith in prayer, and the love of truth.

1. Everything for the glory of God

  • Eating, drinking, working, praying.
  • Find God in all things.
  • Give no offense, and seek the good of many.
“The earth is the Lord’s and all that is in it,” Psalm 24:1
“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Apply: Say each morning: “Lord, today everything for your glory.”

2. Count the cost

  • Christ comes first, even before family.
  • The tower and the king.
  • Give up all that keeps you from him.
“Prepare your work outside, get everything ready for you in the field; and after that build your house.” Proverbs 24:27
“So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” Luke 14:33

Apply: Write the cost and the gain of one decision you have postponed for Christ's sake.

3. Have faith, and love the truth

  • Faith and prayer move mountains.
  • Deception is real; the defence is love of the truth.
  • Test everything; hold fast to what is good.
“Then when you call upon me and come and pray to me, I will hear you.” Jeremiah 29:12
“but test everything; hold fast to what is good;” 1 Thessalonians 5:21

Apply: Test one message you receive before you pass it on.

Ignatius shows that a whole life can be given to the glory of God, in ordinary things, with a clear sense of the cost.

Let us offer everything and say: take, Lord, and receive.

Take-home resolution: This week, offer your day each morning, count one cost honestly, and test what you read before you share it.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers Saint Ignatius of Loyola. He was a soldier in Spain, about five hundred years ago, who loved to dream about glory. At the siege of Pamplona a cannonball broke his leg, and he lay in bed for months. He wanted to read stories of knights, but the house had only a book on the life of Christ and a book on the lives of the saints. He read them, and slowly his heart changed. He left his sword at the altar of Our Lady, and he began a new life. He founded the Society of Jesus, and one of his first friends, Francis Xavier, came to India.

His motto comes from the second reading: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31). Do everything: eating, working, studying, resting, praying. Ignatius taught us to find God in all things and to offer the whole day to him. Every morning we can say: Lord, today everything for your glory.

The Gospel is demanding. Jesus says that to follow him we must put him first, even before our family, and give up everything that stands in his way (Luke 14:26, 33). He gives two small parables: a man building a tower first counts the cost, and a king going to war first counts his soldiers (Luke 14:28–32). Ignatius counted the cost. He left the court and the comfort and chose Jesus. His prayer says: “Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding and my entire will.”

The weekday readings give two more lessons. In the Gospel, Jesus says: “Have faith in God” (Mark 11:22). Faith and prayer can move mountains. A lame soldier with a few friends started a movement that now serves in nearly every country. And Saint Paul warns the Thessalonians that deception is real, and that the defence against it is the love of the truth (2 Thessalonians 2:10). We need this love in a world of rumours and false messages. Test everything, and hold fast to what is good.

Tomorrow August begins. Let us offer the month to God, saying with Saint Ignatius: for the greater glory of God. Saint Ignatius, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Ignatius was a soldier whose leg was hurt. In bed he read about Jesus and the saints, and he said, ‘I want to be a soldier for Jesus!’ He started the Jesuits, and his friend Francis Xavier came to India. Ignatius's motto was: for the greater glory of God. Every morning you can say: ‘Jesus, today everything I do is for you.’ Then even brushing your teeth and doing your homework is a prayer!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you called Ignatius from the battlefield to the service of your kingdom, and taught him to find you in all things. Teach us to do everything for your glory: our work and our rest, our eating and our prayer. Give us the courage to count the cost and to put you before everything, and the faith to ask you for great things. Keep us from deception, and give us a love of the truth that tests every spirit and holds to what is good. Bless the Jesuits and all who teach in the schools and colleges of our land. Take, Lord, and receive our liberty, our memory, our understanding and our will; give us only your love and your grace, and we shall be rich enough. Amen.
