Word for the day

“And hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” Romans 5:5

Reflection

One week after Pentecost the Church lifts her eyes from all that God has done to who God is. In the Season of the Apostles we have recalled the coming of the Spirit, the first healing in the Name of Jesus, the Church as one Body. Today we adore the source of it all: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The Catechism calls the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity “the central mystery of Christian faith and life”, the mystery of God in himself and the light that shines on every other mystery (CCC 234). We did not invent it, and no philosopher discovered it. God revealed it by acting: the Father sent the Son, and the Father and the Son sent the Spirit. This is why our faith begins and ends in the Name. We were baptized “in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19); we sign ourselves with the Cross in that Name; every Holy Qurbana begins and ends with the glory of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and the Church of the East has always loved to sing it. The Trinity is not a puzzle for scholars. It is the family into which we have been born again.

The readings for the feast begin under the oaks of Mamre. “The Lord appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre, as he sat at the entrance of his tent in the heat of the day. He looked up and saw three men standing near him.” Abraham runs to meet them, bows to the ground, and calls them “My lord”, in the singular. He washes their feet, Sarah bakes cakes from the finest flour, a tender calf is prepared, “and he stood by them under the tree while they ate.” The text does not say that Abraham saw the Trinity; the Old Testament does not yet reveal the three Persons. But the Fathers of the Church, reading this page in the light of Christ, saw in the three visitors who speak as one Lord a hint of the mystery to come, and the Christian East has painted it again and again as the icon of the Hospitality of Abraham. Two lessons stay with us. God comes as a guest, and he is welcomed by hospitality: “some have entertained angels without knowing it” (Hebrews 13:2). And the God who is three Persons in one communion of love is himself, in a sense, eternal welcome: the Father gives everything to the Son, the Son returns everything to the Father, and, as St Augustine taught, the Spirit is the love between them.

Isaiah gives us the voice of the one God who is “the first” and “the last”. “Listen to me, O Jacob, and Israel, whom I called: I am He; I am the first, and I am the last.” His hand laid the foundation of the earth and spread out the heavens. And then a striking line: “And now the Lord GOD has sent me and his spirit.” The Fathers heard in it the Son speaking, sent by the Father with the Spirit. Whether or not the prophet saw so far, the Church reads this oracle today because it shows how God has always revealed himself: as the One who calls, who sends and who teaches. The reading ends with a tender promise: “I am the LORD your God, who teaches you for your own good, who leads you in the way you should go.” The doctrine of the Trinity is not cold. It tells us that the God who made the heavens wants to walk with us on the road.

Paul, writing to Rome, shows the Trinity at work in the life of every believer. “Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand.” Peace with the Father, access through the Son. And then the third Person: “hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” Notice that this happens in the middle of suffering: “we also boast in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope.” The Trinity does not float above our troubles. The Father reconciles us, the Son opens the door, and the Spirit pours love into hearts that are hurting. The Catechism uses this very verse to say that God is love, and love is his first gift, containing all the others (CCC 733).

In the Gospel Jesus, on the night before he died, tells the apostles: “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth.” And he describes the life of God in three short sentences: the Spirit “will glorify me, because he will take what is mine and declare it to you. All that the Father has is mine.” Everything the Father has, he gives to the Son; everything of the Son, the Spirit makes known to us. The Church professes one God in three Persons, really distinct, yet one in nature, each wholly God (CCC 253–255). The Sunday readings, also set for today, show the same Trinity in our salvation: Paul tells the Corinthians, “you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God” (1 Corinthians 6:11); Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, says that “there is no other name under heaven” (Acts 4:12); and the woman at Simon's table weeps at the feet of Jesus and hears, “Your sins are forgiven.” The Trinity is not far away. We are invited into it, and Jesus promises: “Those who love me will keep my word, and my Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them” (John 14:23). The goal of our whole life is to enter the perfect unity of the Blessed Trinity (CCC 260).

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today, the Second Sunday of the Apostles, our Church celebrates the feast of the Most Holy Trinity: one God, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.

We meet this mystery every day. We were baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. We make the sign of the Cross in that Name. Every Holy Qurbana begins and ends with the glory of the Three.

In Genesis the Lord appears to Abraham at Mamre as three visitors. Abraham runs to meet them, washes their feet and serves them a meal. The Fathers of the Church saw in this scene a sign of the Trinity. God comes to us as a guest, and we meet him when we welcome others.

Isaiah hears God say, “I am the first, and I am the last,” and, “I am the LORD your God, who teaches you for your own good, who leads you in the way you should go.” God is not far away; he leads us on our road.

Paul shows the Three at work in us: we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, and “God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit.” Even our sufferings, Paul says, can produce hope.

Jesus says that the Spirit will take what is his and give it to us, and “All that the Father has is mine.” The Father gives all to the Son, the Spirit shares it with us. That is the life of God, and we are invited into it. Let us live today as children of the Father, brothers and sisters of the Son, temples of the Holy Spirit. Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, today we celebrate the Most Holy Trinity. When we were baptised, the priest did not say: in the names. He said: in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19). One Name, three Persons. Our whole life as Christians is lived inside that Name.

God told Moses to bless the people with the words: the LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face to shine upon you; the LORD lift up his countenance upon you, and give you peace. Then he added: so they shall put my name on the Israelites, and I will bless them (Numbers 6:24–27). In Baptism God's Name was placed upon us. When parents sign their children with the cross at night, they put that Name on them again.

Israel's great prayer says: hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD alone (Deuteronomy 6:4). We Christians do not believe in three gods. Paul says: for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things (1 Corinthians 8:6). And the Spirit is the Lord, the giver of life. One God in three Persons, equal in glory, one in being.

Isaiah saw the Lord on his throne, and the seraphs called to one another: holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts (Isaiah 6:3). John heard the same song in heaven (Revelation 4:8). In every Holy Qurbana we join that song. We do not fully understand the Trinity; we adore.

So this week let us bless our children each night with the sign of the cross. Let us pray the Creed slowly, phrase by phrase. And at the Holy Qurbana let us sing the Holy, holy, holy with full hearts, as people who bear the Name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Dear friends, today's feast can seem like a difficult doctrine for theologians. But listen to how Paul speaks of the Trinity in today's Epistle. He is not writing a textbook. He is writing to people who suffer.

He begins: since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 5:1). The Father is not our enemy. Through his Son he has made peace with us. Isaiah said: the effect of righteousness will be peace (Isaiah 32:17). If we have been far from God, the door of confession is open.

Then Paul says something surprising: we also boast in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope (Romans 5:3–4). Job, in his pain, said: when he has tested me, I shall come out like gold (Job 23:10). Suffering in itself is not good. But in God's hands it can shape us, like gold in the fire.

And hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us (Romans 5:5). God promised through Isaiah: those who wait for me shall not be put to shame (Isaiah 49:23). Our hope is not wishful thinking. It rests on the love of the Father, shown in the Son, and poured into us by the Spirit.

So the Trinity is not far from our kitchens and hospital beds. The Father holds us, the Son walks with us, the Spirit comforts us. Let us offer one suffering to God this week with trust, and let us bring hope to someone who is suffering, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

One week after Pentecost the Church turns from what God has done to who God is: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The four readings of the feast do not give a definition; they show God acting. At Mamre, the Lord comes to Abraham as three visitors and is welcomed at a meal. In Isaiah, the God who is “the first and the last” speaks, and a mysterious voice says, “the Lord God has sent me and his spirit.” Paul shows the three at work in our lives: peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, and God’s love poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit. In John, Jesus says that the Spirit takes what is his, and that all the Father has is his. The words to watch are sent, poured and mine. The calendar also gives readings for the Second Sunday of the Apostles (Joel 2, Acts 4, 1 Corinthians 6, Luke 7); these notes follow the readings of the feast.

Genesis 18:1–8 · Three men by the oaks of Mamre

Setting.

Abraham has settled by the oaks of Mamre near Hebron (13:18). God has promised him a son, but he and Sarah are old and still childless. In the heat of the day, when travel stopped, three men appear. The story continues after today’s reading with the promise that Sarah will have a son within a year (18:10), and in the next chapter two of the visitors, now called angels, go on to Sodom (19:1).

The narrator tells us at the start that it is “the Lord” who appears. Abraham sees three men. The text moves between singular and plural, and this has made the scene one of the most pondered pages of the Old Testament.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the Lord appears at Mamre in the heat of the day.
  • v. 2: three men; Abraham runs to meet them and bows down.
  • vv. 3–5: he begs them to stay: water, rest, a little bread.
  • vv. 6–8: the great meal prepared in haste, and Abraham standing by them as they eat.

Key words.

  • ’ădōnāy, my lord: In verse 3 Abraham speaks to one (“my lord… your servant”); in verses 4–5 he speaks to all three. The Jewish scribes who added vowels to the Hebrew text pointed the word as the divine title, Lord, showing that they understood Abraham to be speaking to God.
  • rûṣ / mihēr, run / hasten: Abraham runs (vv. 2, 7) and hastens (vv. 6, 7) in the heat of the day, an old man serving strangers. Hospitality here is not a duty done slowly but love in a hurry.
  • šālōš sĕ’îm, three measures: Sarah kneads three measures of choice flour, far more than three guests could eat. Jesus’ parable of the yeast, read on Wednesday, uses the same measure (Matthew 13:33). God is welcomed with abundance.

Verse by verse.

“The Lord appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre, as he sat at the entrance of his tent in the heat of the day.” (18:1)

God comes at an ordinary hour, to an ordinary tent. The Letter to the Hebrews remembers this scene: “by doing that some have entertained angels without knowing it” (Hebrews 13:2).

“He looked up and saw three men standing near him.” (18:2)

One Lord, three men. The Old Testament does not reveal the Trinity, but the Church has always seen in this meeting a shadow of what would be revealed in Christ.

“Let a little water be brought, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree.” (18:4)

Abraham offers “a little” and gives a feast. The washing of feet will return, in a greater way, when the Lord himself washes the feet of his disciples (John 13:5).

“and he stood by them under the tree while they ate.” (18:8)

The host does not sit; he waits on his guests. The man to whom God made promises now serves God at table.

How the Church reads it.

Many Fathers saw in the three visitors a figure of the Trinity, and the Latin liturgy long sang a line often traced to St Ambrose: “He saw three, and worshipped one” (in substance). Others, such as St Justin Martyr, saw the Word of God with two angels. Both readings come from the same faith: the God who visits Abraham is the God who will make himself known as Father, Son and Spirit. The famous icon of St Andrei Rublev (early fifteenth century) shows the three at table with a cup in the centre. The Catechism sees in the hospitality at Mamre a prelude to the annunciation of the true Son of the promise (CCC 2571).

Isaiah 48:12–17 · I am the first, and I am the last

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the exiles in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ. God has raised up Cyrus, king of Persia, to release them. Chapter 48 closes the first half of these prophecies with a strong call to listen, and just after this reading comes the cry, “Go out from Babylon” (48:20).

In these verses God speaks as Creator and Lord of history. Then, in verse 16, a new voice is heard: someone sent by the Lord God, together with his spirit. Many scholars think it is the prophet himself, or the Servant of whom the next chapter speaks.

Structure.

  • v. 12: “Listen to me”: I am He, the first and the last.
  • v. 13: the Creator of earth and heavens.
  • vv. 14–15: the one whom the Lord loves (Cyrus) will do his will against Babylon.
  • v. 16: “Draw near”; a voice: the Lord God has sent me and his spirit.
  • v. 17: the Redeemer who teaches and leads in the way.

Key words.

  • ’ănî hû’, I am He: A short solemn formula in these chapters (41:4; 43:10). In the Greek Old Testament it is egō eimi, “I am”, which Jesus uses of himself in John’s Gospel (John 8:58).
  • ri’šôn / ’aḥărôn, first / last: God is before all things and remains after all. In the Book of Revelation the Risen Christ takes the same title: “I am the first and the last” (Revelation 1:17; 22:13).
  • rûaḥ, spirit: In verse 16 the Hebrew can be read “the Lord God has sent me and his spirit”, with the spirit sent along with the speaker, or “has sent me, and his spirit has”. The phrase joins the Lord God, the one he sends, and his spirit.

Verse by verse.

“Listen to me, O Jacob, and Israel, whom I called: I am He; I am the first, and I am the last.” (48:12)

God’s self-naming begins with a relationship: “whom I called”. The God of all ages is the God of a people.

“My hand laid the foundation of the earth, and my right hand spread out the heavens;” (48:13)

The Creator of all is not far away. The same hand that spread the heavens will lead the exiles home.

“And now the Lord God has sent me and his spirit.” (48:16)

Many Fathers read this verse in the light of the New Testament: the Father sends the Son, and with him the Spirit. The prophet did not know the doctrine of the Trinity, but his words are open to the fullness God would reveal.

“I am the Lord your God, who teaches you for your own good, who leads you in the way you should go.” (48:17)

God teaches and leads. Jesus will promise a Spirit who “will guide you into all the truth” (John 16:13), today’s Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God’s inmost being as Holy Trinity is a mystery that cannot be reached by reason alone, nor even by the faith of Israel before the Incarnation of the Son and the sending of the Spirit (CCC 237). Yet Israel’s faith in the one God, “I am He; I am the first, and I am the last,” remains the foundation. The Church confesses not three gods but one God in three Persons (CCC 253).

Romans 5:1–5 · God’s love poured into our hearts

Setting.

In chapters 1–4 Paul has shown that all, Jews and Gentiles, need God’s saving justice, and that it is given through faith in Jesus Christ, as it was given to Abraham (chapter 4). Chapter 5 begins a new section: what life is like for those who have been justified. Paul writes to a Church he had not yet visited, in about AD 57.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2a: justified by faith, we have peace and access to grace through Christ.
  • v. 2b: we boast in hope of sharing God’s glory.
  • vv. 3–4: we boast even in sufferings: endurance, character, hope.
  • v. 5: hope does not disappoint, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Spirit. ¶ In five verses God, the Lord Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit are named, each at work in the believer.

Key words.

  • prosagōgē, access: The word for being brought into the presence of a king. Through Christ we are led into the grace in which we now stand.
  • dokimē, character: Literally the quality of something that has been tested and proved, like metal through fire. Endurance produces a tried and true character.
  • ekkechytai, has been poured: The verb is used of the Spirit “poured out” at Pentecost (Acts 2:17, 33). The tense means poured once and remaining: the love of God is not a passing feeling but a gift that stays.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,” (5:1)

Textual note: the manuscripts differ by one letter. Many ancient ones read “let us have peace”; others read “we have peace”, which the NRSV follows. Our Malayalam translation reads it as an invitation. Both are true: peace is a gift, and we are to live in it.

“through whom we have obtained access to this grace in which we stand;” (5:2)

Grace is not a moment but a place where we stand. The access is “through” Christ: he is the door.

“knowing that suffering produces endurance,” (5:3)

Paul does not boast of suffering for itself. He boasts because God can make it bear fruit.

“and hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” (5:5)

“God’s love” here means first God’s love for us, given and felt within. Christian hope rests on that gift, not on our own strength.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Romans 5:5 when it teaches that God is love and that love is his first gift, containing all others, poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit (CCC 733). It also teaches that justification is the most excellent work of God’s love, given by grace through faith and Baptism, and that it truly makes us just from within (CCC 1987–1992, 1994). St Augustine returned to this verse again and again when he taught that grace is not only a law shown to us but love given to us (in substance, On the Spirit and the Letter).

John 16:12–15 · The Spirit of truth will guide you

Setting.

These verses come from Jesus’ farewell discourse at the Last Supper (John 13–17). In it he speaks five times of the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete (14:16–17, 26; 15:26; 16:7–11, 12–15). This is the last of these sayings. The disciples are grieving because Jesus is going away (16:6); he tells them that the Spirit will lead them further into what he has given.

Structure.

  • v. 12: many things you cannot bear now.
  • v. 13: the Spirit of truth will guide you into all the truth; he speaks what he hears.
  • v. 14: he will glorify me, taking what is mine.
  • v. 15: all that the Father has is mine. ¶ The movement is from the Father to the Son to the Spirit to the disciples.

Key words.

  • hodēgeō, guide: To lead on the road, as a guide leads travellers (compare Psalm 25:5). The Spirit does not bring a new Gospel; he leads the Church deeper into the truth that is Jesus himself (14:6).
  • anangellō, declare: The verb is used three times in these verses. The Spirit announces, makes known, what he receives. He does not speak “on his own”.
  • alētheia, truth: In John truth is not only correct ideas; it is the reality of God made known in Jesus, who is “full of grace and truth” (1:14).

Verse by verse.

“I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now.” (16:12)

Jesus respects the slowness of his disciples. Understanding will grow after Easter and Pentecost.

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth;” (16:13)

The Second Vatican Council teaches that through the Holy Spirit the Tradition that comes from the apostles develops in the Church, as there is a growth in understanding of the realities and words handed down (Dei Verbum 8).

“He will glorify me, because he will take what is mine and declare it to you.” (16:14)

The Spirit always points to Jesus. A spirituality that leads away from Christ is not from this Spirit.

“All that the Father has is mine.” (16:15)

One of the clearest words in the Gospels on the unity of the Father and the Son. What the Spirit gives us comes from the Father, through the Son.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers used this verse against those who denied that the Son is equal to the Father: if all that the Father has belongs to the Son, the Son is God with the Father. The Catechism teaches that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son; it explains that the Eastern tradition expresses this by saying he comes from the Father through the Son, and that, rightly understood, the two ways of speaking are complementary (CCC 246–248). St Ephrem compared the Trinity to the sun, its light and its warmth: distinct, yet one (in substance).

Questions people ask

Is the word “Trinity” in the Bible?

The word is not, but what it names is. The New Testament speaks of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit as one God, as in the command to baptize “in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19) and in Paul’s blessing (2 Corinthians 13:13). The Church, guided by the Spirit of truth, found the words to confess this faith at the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) (CCC 249–252).

Did Abraham see the Trinity at Mamre?

The text says that the Lord appeared and that Abraham saw three men. It does not teach the doctrine of the Trinity; that was revealed only in Christ (CCC 237). Some Fathers read the three as a sign of the Trinity, others as the Word of God with two angels. The Church allows both readings, and the icon of Rublev has made the first one beloved in East and West.

If God is three, how can he be one?

The Church does not say that God is one and three in the same respect. God is one in nature, one God, and three in Persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who are really distinct in their relations to one another (CCC 253–255). It is a mystery, not a contradiction: reason cannot discover it, but it can see that it is not absurd.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syro-Malabar calendar the feast of the Most Holy Trinity falls on the Second Sunday of the Apostles, one week after Pentecost, and the calendar gives the Sunday’s own readings as a second set. Our Holy Qurbana is soaked in the praise of the Trinity. In the Anaphora of the Apostles Mar Addai and Mar Mari, which we pray through this season, the great prayer opens by praising the adorable and glorious Name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, who created the world by his grace.

The Season of the Apostles began with the Spirit poured out on the Church. Today we see where that gift comes from: from the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit. Each time we make the sign of the Cross, or sing “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit”, we name the God into whose life we were baptized.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham ran to welcome strangers. Whom can I welcome this week as if I were welcoming God?
  • Isaiah’s God says, “I teach you for your own good.” Where do I need God to teach and lead me now?
  • Paul says suffering can produce hope. Name one present trouble and ask: what might the Spirit be forming in me through it?
  • How can my family pray to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit, more consciously at home?
