Word for the day

“for God is a God not of disorder but of peace.” 1 Corinthians 14:33

Reflection

On the day after Pentecost the Church looks at the woman who was in the upper room when the Spirit came. Luke had named her among the praying disciples: “together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). At the Cross Jesus had given her to the beloved disciple with the words “Woman, here is your son,” and to the disciple, “Here is your mother” (John 19:26–27). The Church has always understood that in that disciple all disciples are given a Mother. On 21 November 1964, at the Second Vatican Council, St Paul VI solemnly proclaimed Mary Mother of the Church, that is, of the whole people of God, faithful and pastors. In 2018 Pope Francis placed this memorial on the Monday after Pentecost, so that the Church, newly born of the Spirit, might begin her journey with her Mother. Our First Set calendar lists the day without proper readings, and so we hear the readings of the first weekday of the Season of the Apostles; they speak, as it turns out, of the Church at prayer and of the humble heart, the two things Mary teaches best. She is not above the Church as a goddess, nor outside it as a spectator; she is its first and most perfect member, and for that very reason its Mother.

Paul writes to an assembly that has received many gifts of the Spirit and is in danger of turning them into noise. Everyone wants to speak at once. “When you come together, each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation. Let all things be done for building up.” Tongues only with interpretation, prophets one by one, the others weighing what is said, the first speaker silent when another receives a revelation: “For you can all prophesy one by one, so that all may learn and all be encouraged.” The reason is theological, not merely practical: “for God is a God not of disorder but of peace.” The Spirit who came at Pentecost with wind and fire is the same Spirit of order and peace. Our Syro-Malabar liturgy, with its fixed prayers, its roles for priest, deacon, choir and people, and its moments of silence, is a school in Paul's teaching: each voice in turn, everything for building up. Mary is the model of such a gift. She speaks little in the Gospels; she treasures and ponders (Luke 2:19). Her few words build up: “Do whatever he tells you” (John 2:5). In a Church tempted by noise and rivalry, the Mother of the Church teaches silence that listens and speech that serves.

The Gospel returns us to the prayer of Jesus: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and the intelligent and have revealed them to infants”. If anyone was such an infant before God, it was Mary, who sang that the Lord “has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant”. Jesus continues: “Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls.” Where did the human heart of Jesus learn its gentleness? From the Father, first of all; and also in the home of Nazareth, from a mother who was gentle and humble. The Mother of the Church does not draw us to herself; she leads us to her Son, who gives rest to the weary. Many in our parishes carry heavy burdens: illness, debt, a broken marriage, children far away, loneliness in old age. On this first day of the Season of the Apostles, the Church, with Mary her Mother, says to them what her Son says: Come to him. Learn from him. His yoke is easy and his burden light, because he carries it with you.

Full homily

Dear friends, yesterday was Pentecost, the birthday of the Church. Today, the first weekday of the Season of the Apostles, the Church honours Mary as Mother of the Church. She was in the upper room with the apostles when the Spirit came (Acts 1:14).

At the Cross Jesus said to the beloved disciple, “Here is your mother” (John 19:27). In that disciple, we were all given a Mother. St Paul VI proclaimed her Mother of the Church in 1964, and Pope Francis set this memorial on the Monday after Pentecost.

Paul tells the Corinthians that when they come together each one has a gift, and “Let all things be done for building up” (1 Corinthians 14:26). Speak in turn, listen to one another, “for God is a God not of disorder but of peace” (14:33). Mary is the model: she pondered in silence, and her few words built up: “Do whatever he tells you.”

Jesus thanks the Father for revealing his secrets to infants, and says: “learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29). Mary was the little one, the humble servant. She learned and lived the gentleness of God, and she leads us to her Son, who gives rest to the weary.

Let us take Mary into our homes as the beloved disciple did. Let our parish meetings and prayer groups be places of peace, where each gift builds up. And let us bring our burdens to Jesus, with our Mother beside us. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus gave his own mother to us! Mary is the Mother of the whole Church. When you are sad or afraid, you can say: “Mother Mary, pray for me,” and she will take you to Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, from the Cross you gave your Mother to the beloved disciple and to all your disciples. Grant that your Church, born of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, may grow in faith and love under the care of Mary, her Mother. Make our assemblies places of peace and not of disorder, where every gift builds up the body. Teach us, with her, to be little before the Father, and give us your gentle and humble heart, that the weary may find rest in you through us. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the day after Pentecost the Church honours Mary as Mother of the Church. Paul asks the Corinthians to let all their gifts serve the building up of the assembly, because God is a God of peace, not of disorder. In Matthew, Jesus invites the weary to learn from him, gentle and humble in heart. The Church born of the Spirit grows when gifts are shared in order and in humility, and Mary, the humble handmaid, is the first to have learned from the heart of her Son.

1 Corinthians 14:26–33 · Let all things be done for building up

Setting.

Paul is near the end of his long answer about spiritual gifts (chapters 12–14). He has said that love is the greatest gift (chapter 13) and that prophecy, which builds up the community, is more useful in the assembly than tongues without interpretation (14:1–5). Now he gives practical rules for the Corinthians’ gatherings for worship, which seem to have been lively and at times chaotic.

Structure.

  • v. 26: each brings something; let all be for building up.
  • vv. 27–28: tongues: two or three, in turn, with an interpreter, or silence.
  • vv. 29–31: prophets: two or three, the others weigh what is said, one by one.
  • vv. 32–33: the spirits of prophets are subject to the prophets; God is a God of peace.

Key words.

  • oikodomē, building up: Literally the building of a house. Paul uses it again and again in this chapter. The test of every gift in worship is whether it builds the house of God.
  • diakrinō, weigh, discern: To sift and judge. Even true prophecy must be tested by the community; no one speaks beyond the Church’s judgment.
  • akatastasia, disorder: Confusion, unrest, even tumult. Its opposite is peace, eirēnē, the order that comes from God.

Verse by verse.

“When you come together, each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation. Let all things be done for building up.” (14:26)

A rare window into early Christian worship: hymns, teaching and prophecy shared by many members. Variety is welcome, but it must serve the whole.

“For you can all prophesy one by one, so that all may learn and all be encouraged.” (14:31)

The Spirit does not compete with himself. Waiting one’s turn is itself a gift of the Spirit.

“for God is a God not of disorder but of peace.” (14:33)

The NRSV places the next words, “As in all the churches of the saints”, with what follows; other translations end verse 33 with them. Either way, the order of worship reflects the God who is worshipped.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the judgment about the genuineness and proper use of charisms belongs to those who preside over the Church, whose special task is not to extinguish the Spirit but to test all things and hold fast to what is good (Lumen Gentium 12). The Church’s liturgy, with its fixed order, is not opposed to the Spirit. It is the house the Spirit has built over centuries, so that the whole people may pray with one voice.

Matthew 11:25–30 · Learn from me

Setting.

Jesus has just thanked his Father for revealing his mysteries to infants (11:25–27). He now turns to the people. Many of them felt weighed down: by poverty, by sickness, and by the many interpretations of the Law laid on them by some teachers (compare 23:4). Jewish teachers spoke of taking up “the yoke of the kingdom of heaven”, meaning faithful obedience to God. Jesus offers his own yoke.

Structure.

  • v. 28: come to me, you who are weary; I will give you rest.
  • v. 29: take my yoke, learn from me, gentle and humble in heart.
  • v. 30: my yoke is easy, my burden light.

Key words.

  • zygos, yoke: The wooden beam that joins two oxen to share a load. A yoke is not taken off; it is shared. Jesus does not promise a life without burden, but a burden carried with him.
  • praus, tapeinos, gentle, humble: Praus is the meek of the Beatitudes (5:5); tapeinos means lowly. In Luke, Mary sings that God “has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant” (Luke 1:48), using the related noun tapeinōsis.
  • chrēstos, easy: More exactly “kind, good, well-fitting”, like a yoke made to fit the neck so that it does not chafe.

Verse by verse.

“Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls.” (11:29)

This is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus describes his own heart. The lesson he gives is not first a doctrine but a way of being: gentleness and humility.

“For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.” (11:30)

The commandments of Christ are not lighter because they ask less, but because love carries them, and he carries them with us.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees Mary as the first and best disciple of the gentle and humble heart of her Son. Pope St Paul VI proclaimed her “Mother of the Church” on 21 November 1964, at the Second Vatican Council, and in 2018 Pope Francis set her memorial on the Monday after Pentecost. The Council teaches that Mary, by her faith, love and obedience, cooperated in the birth of the faithful in the Church, and is for that reason a mother to us in the order of grace (Lumen Gentium 53, 61).

Questions people ask

Why is Mary called Mother of the Church if she is herself a member of the Church?

Both are true. Mary is a member of the Church, its most excellent member, redeemed by her Son. She is also its mother, because she gave birth to the Head and, at the Cross, received the beloved disciple as her son (John 19:26–27). The Council calls her “a preeminent and singular member of the Church” and also honours her “as a most beloved mother” (Lumen Gentium 53). In the upper room she prayed with the apostles for the Spirit (Acts 1:14), as a mother among her children.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar Church, in the first days of the Season of the Apostles, keeps Mary in the midst of the apostles as their mother. In our East Syriac tradition Mary is above all Mother of Christ and Mother of God, honoured in every Holy Qurbana; today’s title adds that the Mother of the Head is also the mother of his body.

For study and teaching

  • Jesus says, “Learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart.” In what relationship do I most need to learn gentleness this week?
