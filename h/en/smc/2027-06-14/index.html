Word for the day

“I have said this to you, so that in me you may have peace. In the world you face persecution. But take courage; I have conquered the world!”” John 16:33

Reflection

The Gospel takes us back to the Upper Room on the night before the Passion. Jesus has spoken at length to his disciples, and now he says, “I have said these things to you in figures of speech. The hour is coming when I will no longer speak to you in figures, but will tell you plainly of the Father.” He promises a new closeness in prayer: “On that day you will ask in my name. I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God.” Jesus does not stand between us and a distant, reluctant Father, persuading him to be kind; he brings us into the Father's own love. Then he sums up his whole mission in one sentence: “I came from the Father and have come into the world; again, I am leaving the world and am going to the Father.” The disciples are delighted: “Yes, now you are speaking plainly… by this we believe that you came from God.” But Jesus knows how fragile their certainty is: “Do you now believe? The hour is coming, indeed it has come, when you will be scattered, each one to his home, and you will leave me alone. Yet I am not alone because the Father is with me.” Within hours they would run from Gethsemane. And yet his last word that night is not a reproach but a promise: “I have said this to you, so that in me you may have peace. In the world you face persecution. But take courage; I have conquered the world!” He announces the victory before the battle, on the eve of the Cross. In this Season of the Apostles we know how the story continued: the men who scattered that night were gathered again by the risen Lord, filled with the Spirit at Pentecost, and sent to the ends of the earth, where they did not run away again.

Paul's letter shows us one of those apostles in the thick of the world's persecution, and he is honest about it: “We do not want you to be unaware, brothers and sisters, of the affliction we experienced in Asia; for we were so utterly, unbearably crushed that we despaired of life itself.” We do not know exactly what happened in the Roman province of Asia, whether it was the riot at Ephesus (Acts 19), an imprisonment or a severe illness. What we do know is that a great apostle reached the point where he could not go on. Holiness does not mean having no breaking point. Paul tells us what he learned there: “Indeed, we felt that we had received the sentence of death so that we would rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead.” The faith of Easter is not only for the end of life; it is for the hospital corridor, the failed examination, the business that collapsed, the marriage that seems dead. The God who raised Jesus from the tomb can raise us from what crushes us. Paul's hope then stretches across time: “He who rescued us from so deadly a peril will continue to rescue us; on him we have set our hope that he will rescue us again.” Past rescue becomes the ground of future trust.

Then Paul adds something that every parish should hear: “as you also join in helping us by your prayers, so that many will give thanks on our behalf for the blessing granted us through the prayers of many.” The apostle who founded Churches asks the Churches to carry him in prayer. Prayer for one another is not a pious extra; it is real help, and when God answers, the thanksgiving too becomes common. So Jesus was not alone, because the Father was with him; and Paul was not alone, because the Church was praying. Neither are we. Our evening family prayer, the intentions of the family unit, the names read out in the parish, the Karozutha of the Holy Qurbana in which the deacon lifts up the sick and the suffering: all these are the prayers of many through which God keeps rescuing his people. Whoever is crushed today should be able to say, with Paul, I am not carrying this alone.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul tells the Corinthians something surprising: “we were so utterly, unbearably crushed that we despaired of life itself” (2 Corinthians 1:8). The great apostle had a breaking point. If you feel today that you cannot go on, you are in good company.

Paul tells us why God allowed it: “so that we would rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead” (2 Corinthians 1:9). When our strength runs out, God's strength begins. The God who raised Jesus from the tomb can raise us from what crushes us.

And Paul asks for help: “join in helping us by your prayers.” Prayer for one another is real help. When we pray for the sick, the anxious, the poor, we carry them. And when God answers, we all give thanks together.

In the Gospel, Jesus tells his disciples that they will run away and leave him alone. “Yet I am not alone because the Father is with me.” Then he says: “In the world you face persecution. But take courage; I have conquered the world!” (John 16:32–33). He said this the night before he died. The victory was certain even before the battle.

So let us not carry our burdens alone. Let us bring them to the Lord who has conquered the world, and let us carry one another in prayer. Then many will give thanks for the blessings granted through the prayers of many. Amen.

For children

Children, sometimes even big people feel that their problems are too heavy. Saint Paul felt like that. But he trusted God, and his friends prayed for him. When you are sad or scared, tell Jesus, and ask your family to pray with you. And when a friend is sad, pray for them! Jesus says: “Take courage; I have conquered the world!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on the night before you suffered you told your disciples that they would scatter and leave you alone, and still you promised them peace. Father, you were with your Son in his loneliness, and you raised him from the dead. When we are crushed beyond our strength, teach us to rely not on ourselves but on you, who raise the dead. Make us a people who carry one another in prayer, and who give thanks together for every rescue. Take courage in us, Lord, for you have conquered the world. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of trouble that does not have the last word. Paul tells the Corinthians how he was crushed in Asia until he despaired of life, and learned to rely not on himself but on God who raises the dead. In John, on the night before his Passion, Jesus warns his disciples that they will be scattered and will face persecution, and then gives them his peace: “take courage; I have conquered the world!” An apostle is not someone spared from trouble, but someone who has found that Christ is stronger.

2 Corinthians 1:8–11 · God who raises the dead

Setting.

Paul wrote this letter from Macedonia (2:13; 7:5), after a painful time in his relations with the Corinthians. He opens it by blessing “the God of all consolation” (1:3), who consoles us in every affliction so that we can console others. Now he tells them what he has just been through.

Asia here is the Roman province in the west of what is now Türkiye, with its capital at Ephesus, where Paul worked for a long time. He does not say what happened. Some connect it with the riot at Ephesus (Acts 19:23–41) or with the “wild animals” he fought there (1 Corinthians 15:32); others think of a grave illness. We do not know. What Paul wants them to know is not the details but what God did.

Structure.

  • v. 8: the affliction in Asia: crushed beyond strength, despairing of life.
  • v. 9: the sentence of death, so that we would rely on God who raises the dead.
  • v. 10: he rescued us, he will rescue us, on him we have set our hope.
  • v. 11: the Corinthians’ prayers help, so that many will give thanks.

Key words.

  • thlipsis, affliction: Literally pressure, crushing, as grapes are pressed. It is the same word Jesus uses in today’s Gospel: “In the world you face persecution” (John 16:33). The apostle’s life is lived under this pressure, and God’s consolation meets him there.
  • apokrima tou thanatou, the sentence of death: The word apokrima means an official answer or verdict. Paul felt as if the verdict of death had already been given. That verdict became a lesson in trust.

Verse by verse.

“we were so utterly, unbearably crushed that we despaired of life itself.” (1:8)

Paul does not pretend to be stronger than he was. He speaks openly of despair. Holiness is not the absence of fear but faith that holds on through it.

“so that we would rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead.” (1:9)

A prayer of Israel, still said daily, praises God “who gives life to the dead”. Paul has learned it in his own body: the God who raised Jesus is the one to lean on when every human hope fails.

“as you also join in helping us by your prayers, so that many will give thanks on our behalf” (1:11)

The great apostle asks for the prayers of a troubled community. Prayer joins Christians together, and the answer to many prayers becomes the thanksgiving of many.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this passage the meaning of Christian suffering: it can teach us to rely on God and unite us to Christ’s death and Resurrection. The Catechism teaches that intercession, asking on behalf of another, is a prayer that conforms us to the prayer of Jesus, and that the first Christian communities lived this fellowship intensely, as Paul gave them a share in his ministry through their prayers (CCC 2634–2636).

John 16:25–33 · I have conquered the world

Setting.

These are the last words of Jesus’ long farewell discourse at the Last Supper (John 13–16), just before his great prayer to the Father (chapter 17). He has spoken of going away, of the coming of the Spirit, and of sorrow turned into joy like a mother’s after childbirth (16:21). Now he sums up: he came from the Father and is going back to the Father.

The disciples think they understand at last. Jesus gently shows them that their faith will soon be tested: within hours they will run away. Yet he speaks these words so that they may have peace.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–28: no longer in figures: the Father himself loves you; I came from the Father and go to the Father.
  • vv. 29–30: the disciples: now you speak plainly; we believe you came from God.
  • vv. 31–32: “Do you now believe?” You will be scattered; yet I am not alone.
  • v. 33: peace in me; persecution in the world; take courage, I have conquered the world.

Key words.

  • paroimia, figure of speech: A saying with a hidden meaning, a riddle or image, like the good shepherd (10:6) or the woman in labour (16:21). The “hour” when Jesus speaks plainly is the hour of his death and Resurrection, and the gift of the Spirit who leads into all truth (16:13).
  • parrēsia, plainly, openly: Speech that is frank and without fear. After Pentecost the apostles speak about Jesus with the same parrēsia, boldness (Acts 4:13, 29, 31).
  • nenikēka, I have conquered: The perfect tense: a victory already won whose effect remains. Jesus says it before the cross, as if the battle were over. The same verb appears again in 1 John 5:4: “this is the victory that conquers the world, our faith.”

Verse by verse.

“for the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God.” (16:27)

Jesus does not need to persuade an unwilling Father. The Father’s love comes first and meets the disciples who love his Son.

“you will be scattered, each one to his home, and you will leave me alone. Yet I am not alone because the Father is with me.” (16:32)

Mark recalls the prophecy “I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered” (Mark 14:27; Zechariah 13:7). Jesus knows they will fail, and he still loves them and promises peace.

“In the world you face persecution. But take courage; I have conquered the world!” (16:33)

Jesus does not promise a life without trouble. He promises himself: peace “in me”, and a victory that is already his.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that from the time of his Passover Jesus teaches us to pray to the Father “in his name”, and that faith in the Son introduces the disciples into the knowledge of the Father (CCC 2614). The Church has always read “I have conquered the world” as the source of the martyrs’ courage. Pope St John Paul II began his ministry in 1978 with the words “Do not be afraid! Open wide the doors for Christ”, a call that comes from the same confidence in the Risen Lord.

Questions people ask

Jesus says, “I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf.” Does Jesus not intercede for us?

He does. Scripture says that Christ always lives to make intercession for us (Hebrews 7:25; Romans 8:34). In this verse Jesus means that the Father does not need to be won over: “the Father himself loves you.” When we pray in Jesus’ name, we are not standing outside, asking him to persuade God; we pray united with the Son, as children loved by the Father.

For study and teaching

  • Paul learned to rely not on himself but on God who raises the dead. When did a difficult time teach me to trust God more?
  • Jesus says, “In the world you face persecution. But take courage.” Whom do I know who needs to hear this today, and how can I bring them Christ’s peace?
