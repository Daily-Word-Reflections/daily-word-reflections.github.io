Word for the day

“Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?… Feed my lambs.” John 21:15

Reflection

The Church is a flock with a Shepherd. Peter, who once denied the Lord, is asked three times, “Do you love me?” and three times receives the charge: “Feed my lambs… tend my sheep.” Authority in the Church is born of love and exists for service.

Years later Peter writes to the elders: “Tend the flock of God that is in your charge… not for sordid gain but eagerly… be examples to the flock.” He had learned shepherding from the Shepherd. Today pray for your bishop and parish priest, and ask how you too shepherd those entrusted to you.

Peter's threefold confession of love becomes a threefold mission.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Church is a flock with a Shepherd. Peter, who once denied the Lord, is asked three times: do you love me? And three times he receives the charge: feed my lambs, tend my sheep (John 21:15–17). Authority in the Church is born of love and exists for service.

Years later Peter himself tells the elders of the Church: tend the flock of God, not under compulsion but willingly, not lording it over those in your charge but being examples to the flock (1 Peter 5:2–3). And when the chief Shepherd appears, they will receive the crown of glory that never fades.

Let us pray today especially for our priests and bishops. And each of us is a shepherd in some way: parents, teachers, catechists. Jesus asks us too: do you love me? Let us answer with our service. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus asked Peter: do you love me? Today you too can say: Jesus, I love you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, give your Church shepherds after your own heart. Teach me to love you first and to serve those you entrust to me. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about shepherds. In the Gospel the risen Jesus asks Peter three times, "Do you love me?", and gives him his flock to feed. In the Epistle, Peter, now an elder himself, passes the same charge on to the elders of the Church. The house of God is cared for by shepherds who love the chief Shepherd.

1 Peter 5:1–4 · The chief shepherd will appear

Setting.

1 Peter is written to Christians scattered in Asia Minor (1:1), who suffer insults and hostility for their faith. The letter presents itself as written by Peter from "Babylon" (5:13), an early Christian name for Rome. Near its end the letter turns to the leaders of the communities.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Peter speaks as a fellow elder and a witness of Christ's sufferings.
  • v. 2: tend the flock of God willingly and eagerly.
  • v. 3: not as lords, but as examples.
  • v. 4: the reward: the unfading crown of glory.

Key words.

  • sympresbyteros, fellow elder: Peter, the first of the apostles, does not command from above. He stands beside the elders as one of them. Presbyteros is the word from which "priest" comes.
  • archipoimēn, chief shepherd: Christ alone is the chief Shepherd. Every pastor in the Church is a shepherd under him and will give account to him.
  • amarantinos, unfading: From the amaranth, a flower that was said never to wither. Athletes won crowns of leaves that faded in days; the crown of glory never fades.

Verse by verse.

“as an elder myself and a witness of the sufferings of Christ” (5:1)

Peter's authority rests on what he saw. He watched the Lord suffer, and he himself had denied him. A shepherd who knows his own weakness can be gentle with the flock.

“Do not lord it over those in your charge, but be examples to the flock.” (5:3)

The echo of the Gospel is clear: "the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them … It will not be so among you" (Matthew 20:25–26). The pastor leads by walking ahead, not by driving from behind.

“And when the chief shepherd appears, you will win the crown of glory that never fades away.” (5:4)

The reward of a pastor is not money or honour now ("not for sordid gain", v. 2), but glory when Christ appears.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that bishops, with the priests as their co-workers, govern the Church as shepherds after the example of the Good Shepherd, who came not to be served but to serve (Lumen Gentium 27–28). St Gregory the Great, in his Pastoral Rule, insists that the one who leads must first be humble and must teach more by his life than by his words (in substance).

John 21:15–19 · Feed my sheep

Setting.

John 21 is an epilogue to the Gospel. By the Sea of Tiberias the risen Jesus gives the disciples a great catch of fish and a breakfast of bread and fish on a charcoal fire (21:9). The only other charcoal fire in the Gospels is in the courtyard of the high priest, where Peter denied Jesus three times (18:18).

Structure.

  • v. 15: first question: "do you love me more than these?" "Feed my lambs."
  • v. 16: second question. "Tend my sheep."
  • v. 17: third question; Peter is hurt. "Feed my sheep."
  • vv. 18–19: Peter's death foretold, and the call: "Follow me."

Key words.

  • agapan / philein, to love: In vv. 15–16 Jesus asks with agapas, and Peter answers with philō; the third time Jesus too uses phileis. Some Fathers and teachers see a lesson in this change; many scholars think John uses the two verbs as synonyms. Either way, the threefold question matches the threefold denial.
  • boskein / poimainein, to feed / to tend: The first means to give pasture, to feed; the second to shepherd, to guide and protect. Peter's office includes both: to nourish the flock with the word and sacraments, and to govern it.

Verse by verse.

“Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?” (21:15)

Jesus uses Peter's old name, as at his first call (1:42). "More than these" may mean more than the other disciples, recalling Peter's boast that he would never fall away (Matthew 26:33).

“Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” (21:17)

Peter no longer trusts his own strength. He appeals to the Lord's knowledge. This is humble love, which the Lord can build on.

“when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will fasten a belt around you and take you where you do not wish to go.” (21:18)

John explains that this pointed to Peter's death (v. 19). St Clement of Rome already speaks of Peter's martyrdom, and later writers such as Tertullian and Eusebius record that he died in Rome under Nero, by crucifixion.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine says that the threefold confession of love answered the threefold denial, so that love might speak as often as fear had spoken (in substance). The Catechism teaches that Jesus entrusted a specific authority to Peter, and that after the Resurrection he confirmed it with the words "Feed my sheep" (CCC 553; see also CCC 881).

For study and teaching

  • Peter's authority comes from a love that has been forgiven (John 21:15–17). How does the memory of my own failures make me more gentle with others?
  • 1 Peter 5:3 asks leaders to be "examples to the flock". Where am I a shepherd (in my family, my parish, my work), and what example do I give?
