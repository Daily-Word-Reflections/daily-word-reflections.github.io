Word for the day

“For it is the God who said, “Let light shine out of darkness,” who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” 2 Corinthians 4:6

Reflection

On the last Thursday of the Season of the Apostles the Church sets two kinds of preaching side by side: a preacher who has nothing to hide, and teachers who have forgotten why they speak. Paul begins with a sentence of quiet courage: “Therefore, since it is by God’s mercy that we are engaged in this ministry, we do not lose heart.” He writes to the Corinthians, who had heard from other teachers that Paul was unimpressive and unreliable. He does not answer with an attack. He answers with his way of working: “We have renounced the shameful things that one hides; we refuse to practice cunning or to falsify God’s word; but by the open statement of the truth we commend ourselves to the conscience of everyone in the sight of God.” A messenger of the Gospel has no secret plan. He does not bend the word to please, to frighten or to gain. He puts the truth in the open and lets every conscience judge it, because God is watching.

Paul is honest also about the sorrow of preaching: “And even if our gospel is veiled, it is veiled to those who are perishing. In their case the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers, to keep them from seeing the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God.” We read this with sorrow, not with scorn. Paul is not speaking of our neighbours of other faiths, whom the Church respects; he speaks of the blindness that can fall on any heart, ours included, when money, comfort or reputation take the place of God. Then comes the sentence that every priest, catechist, parish council member and parent can test their work against: “For we do not proclaim ourselves; we proclaim Jesus Christ as Lord and ourselves as your slaves for Jesus’ sake.” Am I proclaiming Christ, or myself? The reading ends in light: “For it is the God who said, ‘Let light shine out of darkness,’ who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” Paul has the first day of creation in mind (“Then God said, ‘Let there be light’; and there was light”) and his own day outside Damascus. The light of God is not only an idea; it has a face, and we see it in Jesus.

The Gospel takes us into the Temple in the last days of Jesus' ministry, where he speaks sharply to the scribes and Pharisees. We must hear these words as the Church hears them. Jesus is a Jew speaking to his own people, in the manner of the prophets, who also scolded priests and teachers out of love for the covenant. The “woes” are not a verdict on a whole people, and the Second Vatican Council teaches that what happened in his Passion cannot be charged against all Jews of that time or of ours (Nostra Aetate 4). They are a warning to every religious leader and every believer, and so to us. The first woe: “you lock people out of the kingdom of heaven. For you do not go in yourselves, and when others are going in, you stop them.” Religion can become a gatekeeper instead of a door. The second: a zeal that crosses “sea and land to make a single convert”, but without the truth and love that make a person new. A Church that is busy and loud but has not itself been converted can do harm.

Then Jesus turns to oaths. The teachers had made a system of fine distinctions: an oath by the sanctuary binds nothing, but an oath by the gold of the sanctuary binds; an oath by the altar binds nothing, but an oath by the gift on the altar binds. Jesus answers with plain questions: “which is greater, the gold or the sanctuary that has made the gold sacred?” and “which is greater, the gift or the altar that makes the gift sacred?” Then he closes every loophole: “So whoever swears by the altar, swears by it and by everything on it; and whoever swears by the sanctuary, swears by it and by the one who dwells in it; and whoever swears by heaven, swears by the throne of God and by the one who is seated upon it.” God is present in every word spoken before him, and there is no small oath that he does not hear. In a country where people swear by temples, by their children and by God in courts and markets, this is very practical. The Catechism teaches that the holiness of God's name forbids false and careless oaths (CCC 2153–2155). Let our word be so true that no oath is needed: “Let your word be ‘Yes, Yes’ or ‘No, No’.” The apostle who does not falsify God's word and the believer whose Yes is Yes are the people through whom the light of Christ's face reaches others.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's reading Saint Paul says something very brave: “We do not lose heart.” He was tired, criticised and misunderstood. Yet he says: God has been merciful to me, and so I will not give up.

Then he tells us how he works. “We refuse to practice cunning or to falsify God’s word; but by the open statement of the truth we commend ourselves to the conscience of everyone in the sight of God” (2 Corinthians 4:2). A good preacher, and a good parent and a good friend, has nothing to hide. And he adds: “We do not proclaim ourselves; we proclaim Jesus Christ as Lord” (2 Corinthians 4:5).

Why? Because the light is Jesus. “It is the God who said, ‘Let light shine out of darkness,’ who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ” (2 Corinthians 4:6). God does not shine from a distance; he shines from a face, the face of his Son.

In the Gospel Jesus is sad and strong at the same time. Some teachers who should have opened the door of God's kingdom closed it, and played clever games with words and oaths. Jesus tells them: God hears every word. Whoever swears by the altar swears by God, and whoever swears by heaven swears by God's throne (Matthew 23:20–22). We do not need clever games. We need honest hearts.

So let us do one small thing today: keep one promise we have delayed. Let our Yes be Yes and our No be No (Matthew 5:37). Then the light of Christ's face will shine through our words. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Paul says: Jesus is the light! Light helps us to see, and light helps others to find the way. You can carry a little of that light when you tell the truth, even when it is hard.

Jesus does not like clever tricks with promises. When you say Yes, mean Yes! When you say No, mean No! And remember: God hears every word you say.

Prayer

Lord God, you said, “Let light shine out of darkness”, and you have shone in our hearts in the face of Jesus Christ. Keep us from every cunning and every double heart. Make us preachers who proclaim your Son and not ourselves, believers whose Yes is Yes, and a Church that is a door and not a gatekeeper. Teach us to speak every word as in your sight, and let the light of Christ's face shine in our homes and parishes. Through Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what makes a teacher of God’s word trustworthy. Paul describes his own ministry: no cunning, no tampering with the word, no preaching of himself, only the open statement of the truth, because the light that shines in the face of Christ is not his to dim or to own. In Matthew, Jesus speaks to teachers who have made access to God harder rather than easier, and whose cleverness over oaths has lost sight of the God in whose name they are sworn. In the last days of the season of the Apostles, the Church hears what the sending of the apostles was for: that others might see the light, and that no one be kept from it.

2 Corinthians 4:1–6 · The light shines in the face of Christ

Setting.

Second Corinthians is the most personal of Paul’s letters. He writes after a time of sharp tension with the Corinthian community, in which some had questioned his authority and his sincerity. In 2:14 to 3:18 he has defended his ministry as a ministry of the new covenant, of the Spirit and not of the letter, and has compared it with the veil that lay over Moses’ face (3:12–18). Chapter 4 begins with a quiet conclusion: because this ministry is a mercy from God, he does not lose heart.

The verses are also a kind of examination of conscience for anyone who preaches. Paul lists what he has put aside (hidden shame, cunning, the falsifying of God’s word) and what he does instead (the open statement of the truth). Then comes one of the most luminous sentences in his letters: the God who said at creation “Let light shine out of darkness” has shone in our hearts.

Structure.

  • v. 1: because the ministry is God’s mercy, we do not lose heart.
  • v. 2: what we have renounced (hidden shame, cunning, falsifying God’s word) and what we do: state the truth openly.
  • vv. 3–4: if the gospel is veiled, it is so to those who are perishing, whose minds the “god of this world” has blinded.
  • v. 5: we do not proclaim ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord, and ourselves as your servants for his sake.
  • v. 6: the God of the first creation has shone in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of his glory in the face of Christ.

Key words.

  • diakonia, ministry, service: The ordinary word for waiting at table and for any service done for others. Paul uses it for his whole apostolic work: he is not a ruler of the Corinthians but a servant given to them by God’s mercy.
  • eikōn, image: The Greek word behind our “icon”. Christ is the image of God (v. 4): in him the invisible God can be seen. The same thought stands behind Colossians 1:15 and Hebrews 1:3.
  • phōtismos, light, illumination: A rare word in the New Testament, found in verses 4 and 6. It means the shining out of light, not only light that is there. It is God who makes it shine, in creation and in the heart.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since it is by God’s mercy that we are engaged in this ministry, we do not lose heart.” (4:1)

Paul has just spoken of the glory of the new covenant (3:7–18). The ministry is a gift, so he does not rest on his own strength. The same phrase, “we do not lose heart”, returns in 4:16, after he has listed his sufferings.

“we refuse to practice cunning or to falsify God’s word” (4:2)

The Greek verb for “falsify” was used of traders who watered down wine or diluted goods. A preacher who adapts the word of God to please his hearers does something like this.

“the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers” (4:4)

This is a title for Satan, the great adversary, who has real but limited power (compare John 12:31). Paul is not teaching two gods: the Catholic faith holds one God, creator of all, and the Tempter is a creature. Blindness here is a darkening that the Gospel can lift, not a sentence on any person, and Paul himself was once blind in this way.

“we proclaim Jesus Christ as Lord and ourselves as your slaves for Jesus’ sake” (4:5)

“Lord” is the earliest Christian confession (1 Corinthians 12:3; Philippians 2:11). Paul uses the word “slaves” of himself as a minister of the Church, which turns the proud claims of the Corinthian teachers upside down.

“in the face of Jesus Christ” (4:6)

Moses’ face shone with a fading glory that he covered with a veil (3:13). Now the glory of God is seen, without a veil, in a human face: that of Jesus Christ. The vision on the road to Damascus, which Paul remembers here, lies behind the verse.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this passage the pattern of all preaching. The Second Vatican Council begins its constitution on the Church with the same image: Christ is the light of the nations, and the Council wishes to brighten all people with the light of Christ shining on the face of the Church (Lumen Gentium 1). The Church is not the source of the light, but the face in which it shows. The Catechism teaches that the Church does not speak of herself but hands on what she has received from the Lord (CCC 76–78 on the handing on of the faith, in substance), which is exactly what Paul means by “we do not proclaim ourselves”.

Matthew 23:13–22 · Woe to you who lock people out

Setting.

Matthew 23 is the last of the five great discourses of Jesus, delivered in the Temple in the final days before his Passion. It is addressed “to the crowds and to his disciples” (23:1) and begins with a statement that is easy to forget: the scribes and Pharisees sit on Moses’ seat, so do what they teach, but do not follow what they do (23:2–3). The seven woes that follow (counted without the disputed verse 14) are the speech of a prophet inside his own people, like those of Amos or Jeremiah. They are not a verdict on Pharisees as such, still less on the Jewish people.

The Church has to hear these words with care. In the Gospel they warn every religious leader, the apostles included, that teaching can become a barrier. Many Pharisees of the time were sincere and devout, and the early Church includes Pharisees who came to faith (Acts 15:5; Philippians 3:5). The Second Vatican Council teaches that what happened in the Passion cannot be blamed on all Jews then living, nor on Jews today (Nostra Aetate 4).

Structure.

  • v. 13: first woe: you shut the kingdom of heaven in people’s faces, neither entering yourselves nor letting others in.
  • v. 15: second woe: zeal to make a convert, who then becomes a worse “child of hell” than the teachers.
  • vv. 16–22: third woe: the teaching on oaths. Swearing by the sanctuary or altar was treated as not binding, but by the gold or the gift as binding. Jesus answers that every such oath reaches God.
  • ¶ Verse 14 does not appear in the text of the NRSV, because it is missing in the best manuscripts; many copies added it from Mark 12:40 and Luke 20:47, with the words about devouring widows’ houses. The verse numbering therefore jumps from 13 to 15.

Key words.

  • hypokritēs, hypocrite, actor: In Greek the word first meant an actor on stage, one who plays a part. In the Gospel it describes a person whose religious role covers over a heart that has not been changed.
  • ouai, woe: A prophetic cry that is also a lament. The same cry opens several of the prophets’ oracles, for example Isaiah 5:8–24. It expresses grief over those who go wrong and a warning to turn back.
  • omnyō, to swear: An oath calls God as witness to the truth of what is said. Jesus does not abolish oaths here; he rejects the idea that some formulae bind and others do not.

Verse by verse.

“you lock people out of the kingdom of heaven. For you do not go in yourselves, and when others are going in, you stop them.” (23:13)

The key image is in Luke 11:52: the teachers have taken away the key of knowledge. A good teacher opens a door; a bad one stands in the doorway. Jesus does not say the teachers had no learning but that they used it as a barrier.

“you cross sea and land to make a single convert, and you make the new convert twice as much a child of hell as yourselves” (23:15)

The first century had many Jewish teachers who welcomed Gentile converts. Jesus is not blaming the mission itself but the harm done when a convert is taught zeal without mercy, and copies the faults of the teacher. The word “hell” is Gehenna (see Matthew 18:9).

“Whoever swears by the sanctuary is bound by nothing, but whoever swears by the gold of the sanctuary is bound by the oath.” (23:16)

The teachers had worked out a system for which oaths were binding and which were not. The result was that people could swear and escape. Jesus sees that such a system trains the conscience to look for loopholes.

“whoever swears by the altar, swears by it and by everything on it” (23:20)

Jesus’ answer is plain: the altar sanctifies the gift, and the sanctuary is the dwelling of God, so every oath ends in God. In the Sermon on the Mount he asks that the disciple’s “Yes” simply be “Yes” (5:37).

“whoever swears by heaven, swears by the throne of God and by the one who is seated upon it.” (23:22)

The last step in the argument takes the oath to its end. No formula is empty, because God is present to everything that is said in his name.

How the Church reads it.

The Church applies these woes first to herself. The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ words on oaths show that every oath involves a reference to God, whose presence and truth are to be honoured in all speech (CCC 2150–2155, in substance). St John Chrysostom, preaching on Matthew 23, reads the woes as a call to those entrusted with teaching, who are above all to be careful that their own life agrees with their words (in substance, Homilies on Matthew 72–73). The Church’s teaching on the Jewish people, from Nostra Aetate 4 onwards, reminds us that the Gospel’s sharp words belong to a family quarrel in Israel and give no ground for contempt.

Questions people ask

Who is “the god of this world” in 2 Corinthians 4:4, and is Paul saying that there are two gods?

No. The Church believes in one God, creator of all things. Paul uses a bold phrase for Satan, the tempter, who has real influence over human minds that turn from God but is only a creature and has no power that God does not permit. The phrase appears in the setting of Paul’s own call: he too once lived in darkness, and the light of Christ opened his eyes. So the verse is a warning about the blindness that sin can cause, and a promise that the Gospel is stronger.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “we do not proclaim ourselves”. In my own speaking about the faith, at home, in the parish or at work, where do I speak more about myself than about Christ?
  • Are there words that I use in a way that lets me escape what I have promised? What would it mean for my “Yes” to be simply “Yes”?
