Readings

  • Epistle 1 Timothy 6:17–21 · The life that really is life

Word for the day

“But Jesus looked at them and said, “For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.”” Matthew 19:26

Reflection

Paul closes his first letter to Timothy with a word for the rich members of the Church at Ephesus. Ephesus was a wealthy port, and the Church there was not made up only of slaves and labourers; there were merchants and householders too. Paul does not tell them that their wealth makes them bad Christians. He tells them where to put their hope: “As for those who in the present age are rich, command them not to be haughty, or to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but rather on God who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment.” The phrase is beautiful: God is the one who richly provides, and he provides “for our enjoyment”. Creation is a gift, not a trap. The danger lies elsewhere: in pride, and in hoping in what cannot last. So Paul gives the rich a programme: “They are to do good, to be rich in good works, generous, and ready to share, thus storing up for themselves the treasure of a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of the life that really is life.” The life that really is life: Paul knows that there is a life that only looks like life, busy and comfortable and empty, and another life that will not end. In this Easter season we know its name. It is the life of the Risen Christ, already planted in us at Baptism.

Then Paul turns to Timothy himself: “Timothy, guard what has been entrusted to you.” The word he uses is a banker's word: a deposit, something placed in safe keeping to be returned whole. What has been deposited with Timothy is the faith of the apostles, the Gospel of the crucified and risen Lord. He is not its owner, at liberty to change it; he is its guardian, bound to hand it on intact. Paul warns him against “the profane chatter and contradictions of what is falsely called knowledge”: there were already teachers who claimed a higher, secret knowledge than the plain faith of the Church. The bishops of the Church, successors of Timothy, still have this task, and every parent and catechist shares it. Our forefathers in Kerala called the faith they received from St Thomas the Way of Thomas, Thomma Margam; for centuries they guarded it with little more than the liturgy, the family prayer and the memory of the elders. A deposit is kept not by burying it but by living it and passing it on.

The Gospel picks up just after a rich young man has walked away from Jesus with a sad face. Jesus watches him go and says to his disciples: “Truly I tell you, it will be hard for a rich person to enter the kingdom of heaven. Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God.” The picture is deliberately impossible: the largest animal the hearers knew, the smallest opening. The disciples are astounded: “Then who can be saved?” And Jesus looks at them, Matthew notes that look, and says the words that hold the whole Gospel: “For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.” No one, rich or poor, saves himself. Salvation is God's work, and God can do what we cannot: he can release a heart tied to its possessions, as he can raise a dead man from a sealed tomb. Easter is the proof. Peter, practical as ever, asks what those who have left everything will receive, and Jesus promises a hundredfold, eternal life, and a share in “the renewal of all things”. That renewal began on Easter morning. The last will be first: the Church has always seen this in the poor who are rich in faith, and in those who have given up home and family to follow Christ as priests, religious and missionaries.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Paul speaks to the rich, and Jesus speaks about camels and needles. Both are speaking about where we put our hope.

Paul does not say that riches are evil. He says: do not be proud, and do not set your hopes “on the uncertainty of riches, but rather on God who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment.” Then he gives a plan: do good, be rich in good works, be generous and ready to share, and so take hold of “the life that really is life.” That life is the life of the Risen Christ.

Paul also tells Timothy: “guard what has been entrusted to you.” The faith is a deposit placed in our hands. We do not own it or change it; we keep it by living it and handing it on whole to our children.

In the Gospel Jesus says it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom. The disciples ask, “Then who can be saved?” And Jesus looks at them and says: “For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.”

No one saves himself. God can release a heart tied to its possessions, just as he raised his Son from the tomb. And he promises a hundredfold to those who leave things for his sake, and a share in the renewal of all things.

So today let us move our hope from what we own to the God who gives. Let us give something away before we buy something for ourselves. For God, all things are possible. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said a camel could go through the hole of a needle more easily than a rich person could get into heaven. That is impossible! But Jesus said: for God, everything is possible.

God can make our hearts kind and generous. Today, share something you like with someone else!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you looked with love on the rich young man and on your astonished disciples, and you told us that for God all things are possible. Release our hearts from hope in uncertain riches. Make us rich in good works, generous and ready to share, so that we may take hold of the life that really is life. Help our bishops, priests, parents and catechists to guard the faith entrusted to them and to hand it on whole. Renew all things, beginning with us. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about wealth and what it can and cannot buy. Paul tells Timothy what to say to the rich: do not be haughty, do not hope in riches, be rich in good works, and so “take hold of the life that really is life”. In Matthew, after the rich young man has gone away sad, Jesus says that it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom, and then adds that for God all things are possible. The key words are hope, deposit and hundredfold.

1 Timothy 6:17–21 · The life that really is life

Setting.

After the solemn doxology of vv. 15–16 the letter returns to practical matters. Ephesus was a rich commercial city, and the church there included people of means. Earlier in the chapter the letter warned those who want to be rich (6:9–10); now it speaks to those who already are.

The last verses go back to the letter's first concern: false teaching (1:3–7). Timothy is to guard what he has received and avoid empty talk.

Structure.

  • vv. 17–19: a charge to the rich: hope in God, do good, share, and so lay up a good foundation.
  • vv. 20–21a: guard the deposit; avoid what is falsely called knowledge.
  • v. 21b: the blessing.

Key words.

  • ontōs zōē, the life that really is life: Riches give a life that looks like life. The letter points to the life that is real, the life of God which begins now and lasts.
  • koinōnikos, ready to share: From the same root as koinōnia, communion. Sharing goods is the outward side of sharing in the one life of Christ.
  • parathēkē, what has been entrusted, the deposit: A legal word for something left in another's safekeeping, to be returned intact. The Catechism uses this text for the deposit of faith entrusted by the apostles to the whole Church (CCC 84).

Verse by verse.

“As for those who in the present age are rich, command them not to be haughty, or to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but rather on God who richly provides us with everything for our enjoyment.” (6:17)

The letter does not condemn the good things of creation; God gives them “for our enjoyment”. The danger lies in pride and in false hope.

“so that they may take hold of the life that really is life.” (6:19)
“Timothy, guard what has been entrusted to you.” (6:20)

The phrase “what is falsely called knowledge” (in Greek, pseudōnymos gnōsis) gave St Irenaeus the title of his great work against the Gnostics, the “Refutation and Overthrow of Knowledge Falsely So Called”.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches the universal destination of goods: what we own is given to us also for others (CCC 2402–2406). Generosity is one way of laying up treasure in heaven, as the Lord said (Matthew 6:20), and it is done by grace, not to buy grace. The command to guard the deposit stands behind the Church's care to hand on the faith whole, the faith which the apostles received from the Lord and which the Holy Spirit keeps alive in her (CCC 84).

Matthew 19:23–30 · For God all things are possible

Setting.

A rich young man has just asked Jesus what good deed he must do to have eternal life. Told to sell his possessions and follow, he went away grieving, “for he had many possessions” (19:22). Jesus now turns to his disciples.

In the thinking of the time, riches were often taken as a sign of God's blessing (compare Deuteronomy 28:1–14). That is why the disciples are so astounded: if the blessed cannot be saved, who can?

Structure.

  • vv. 23–24: the hardness of entering the kingdom for the rich; the camel and the needle.
  • vv. 25–26: “Who can be saved?” “For God all things are possible.”
  • v. 27: Peter's question: what will we have?
  • vv. 28–29: the reward of the Twelve and of all who leave everything.
  • v. 30: the first and the last.

Key words.

  • kamēlos, camel: The largest animal in Palestine and the smallest opening anyone knew: a deliberate picture of the impossible. The popular story of a small city gate called “the needle's eye” has no ancient evidence behind it. A few late manuscripts read kamilos, “rope”, which shows that some readers found the saying too hard.
  • palingenesia, renewal of all things: Literally “new birth”. It is used here of the renewal of the world at the end, and in Titus 3:5 of baptism. Both meanings belong together: the new creation begins in the font.
  • hekatontaplasiona, a hundredfold: An overflowing return. Mark adds that it comes “with persecutions” (Mark 10:30).

Verse by verse.

“it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God.” (19:24)
“For mortals it is impossible, but for God all things are possible.” (19:26)

Jesus does not soften his saying; he points beyond human strength. The same truth was spoken to Abraham (Genesis 18:14) and to the Virgin Mary at the Annunciation (Luke 1:37).

“everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or children or fields, for my name’s sake, will receive a hundredfold, and will inherit eternal life.” (19:29)

“For my name's sake” is the key. Loss for its own sake is not the point; love of the Lord is.

How the Church reads it.

Clement of Alexandria, in his little book Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?, taught that the Lord asks us to banish from the soul the passion for wealth more than wealth itself, and to use what we have for others (in substance). The Catechism speaks of poverty of heart: Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544–2547). Every vocation that leaves home and fields for the Lord, the priest's, the religious sister's or brother's and the missionary's, lives from this promise.

Questions people ask

Is it wrong to be rich?

No. Abraham and Job were rich, and the letter to Timothy speaks of God who gives us everything “for our enjoyment”. The danger is to trust in riches, to be proud because of them, and to keep them from those in need. What is impossible for us, a heart loosened from that grip, God can give.

For study and teaching

  • Where do I set my hope: on what I have, or on God who gives it?
  • What has the Lord asked me to leave for his name's sake, and what “hundredfold” have I already received?
