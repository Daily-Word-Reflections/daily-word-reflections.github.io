Word for the day

“My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” 2 Corinthians 12:9

Reflection

Few saints are loved across so many Churches and nations as St George. In the Syriac tradition he is Mar Geevarghese Sahada, George the Martyr; Sahada means witness, the one who bears testimony with his blood. Kerala is full of his churches and his name: in Christian families of every tradition, boys are called Geevarghese, Varghese, Varkey and George, and great pilgrim churches such as Aruvithura and Edathua draw crowds who come to him with their fears and their vows. What do we know of him? The early Church honoured his tomb at Lydda, the town in Palestine where Peter once healed Aeneas, and pilgrims were visiting it by the sixth century. The tradition remembers him as a soldier in the Roman army who refused to deny Christ in the great persecution under the emperor Diocletian, at the beginning of the fourth century, and who was tortured and beheaded. An ancient list of writings, attributed to Pope Gelasius at the end of the fifth century but probably compiled somewhat later, named him among those saints whose names are rightly honoured but whose deeds are known only to God. The famous story of the dragon came much later, in the Middle Ages, and is a legend, not history; the Church reads it as a picture of the truth, that the martyr's faith overcomes the ancient serpent. The history is short, but it is enough: a man under orders, with everything to lose, who chose Christ above his career and his life.

The readings chosen for the feast gather around that choice. Daniel, a Jewish exile serving the king of the Medes and Persians, keeps praying to his God three times a day although a royal decree has made it a crime. His enemies report him, and the king, who loves Daniel, cannot revoke his own law. Daniel is thrown to the lions and a stone is sealed over the mouth of the den. At dawn the king runs to the den and calls out: “O Daniel, servant of the living God, has your God whom you faithfully serve been able to deliver you from the lions?” And Daniel answers: “My God sent his angel and shut the lions’ mouths so that they would not hurt me.” He comes up unharmed, “because he had trusted in his God.” The scene speaks of Easter: a stone sealed, a long night, a hurried dawn visit, and the servant of God alive. The reading ends grimly with the punishment of the accusers, as the law of that ancient kingdom demanded; Christ has taught us instead to pray for those who persecute us, and the martyrs, St George among them, died forgiving.

The Acts of the Apostles takes us to the first martyrdom among the twelve. “About that time King Herod laid violent hands upon some who belonged to the church. He had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword.” Seeing that this won him favour with some, he arrested Peter too, during the days of Unleavened Bread, intending to bring him out after the Passover. Herod, Agrippa I, was a ruler anxious to please, and Luke shows a politician's calculation; the Church does not read this as blame on the Jewish people, from whom the apostles themselves came. Then comes the verse that has carried the Church through every persecution: “While Peter was kept in prison, the church prayed fervently to God for him.” James gave his life; Peter was released by an angel; both were held in the Church's prayer. In his letter to the Corinthians Paul reveals the secret of such courage. He had been caught up in visions, yet God left him “a thorn… in the flesh” and would not remove it. Three times he begged the Lord, and the answer came: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” So Paul concludes: “whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” Martyrs are not people without fear. They are weak people who have let the power of the Risen Christ dwell in them.

The Gospel states the cost plainly: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me… and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” These are hard words in a culture like ours, where family is everything. Jesus does not ask us to love our families less; he asks to be loved first, so that our love for them may be pure and strong. Then he gives the law of Easter: “Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” George lost his rank, his safety and his life, and found them all again in the Risen Lord; seventeen centuries later we still keep his day. Most of us will not be asked to die for Christ. But we are asked every day to lose a little life for him: to refuse a dishonest deal, to keep Sunday holy when work demands it, to stand by the faith in an office or a college where it is mocked. The Gospel ends with a tender promise: even “a cup of cold water” given to one of Christ's little ones will not lose its reward. On this feast we pray for Cardinal Mar George Alencherry, Major Archbishop emeritus, for all bishops, priests and faithful who bear the name of St George, and for Christians who are persecuted today, that they may be strong in weakness, as he was.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we keep the feast of Mar Geevarghese Sahada, St George the Martyr, whose name is carried by so many of our families and churches.

History tells us little, but enough: George was a soldier who refused to deny Christ in the persecution under Diocletian, and he was put to death, probably at the beginning of the fourth century. His tomb at Lydda was honoured by pilgrims. The dragon story came later; it is a legend that shows the victory of faith over evil.

Daniel too chose God above the king's law. Thrown to the lions, he was found alive at dawn, “because he had trusted in his God.” A sealed stone, a long night, a dawn visit, and the servant of God alive: it sounds like Easter.

In Acts, Herod kills James and imprisons Peter, and “the church prayed fervently to God for him.” The Church has always carried its martyrs and prisoners in prayer. Let us do the same today for Christians who suffer for their faith.

Paul reveals the secret of courage. He begged God to take away his thorn, and heard: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” Martyrs are not people without fear; they are weak people in whom the power of Christ lives.

Jesus says: “those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” Most of us will not be asked to die, but we are asked every day to lose a little life for him. Let us ask St George to make us brave, and pray for all who bear his name. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, St George was a brave soldier who loved Jesus more than anything. He would not give up Jesus, even when he was threatened.

You can be brave like St George: say your prayers, tell the truth, and be kind even when it is hard!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you gave St George the courage to choose you above his rank, his safety and his life. By his prayers, strengthen all who suffer for your name. As you shut the lions' mouths for Daniel and answered the prayer of the Church for Peter, protect your Church today. Let your power be made perfect in our weakness, so that we may love you first, take up our cross and find our life in you. Bless Cardinal Mar George Alencherry and all who bear the name of St George. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church keeps Mar Geevarghese Sahada, St George the Martyr, a soldier who, tradition says, chose Christ above his career and his life in the persecution under Diocletian. The four readings of the feast are four pictures of faithful witness under threat. Daniel keeps praying to his God although a royal decree forbids it, and God shuts the lions' mouths. In Acts, King Herod kills James with the sword and imprisons Peter, and the Church prays. Paul, pressed by a thorn in the flesh, hears the Lord say, “My grace is sufficient for you”. In Matthew, Jesus asks to be loved above all, and promises that whoever loses his life for his sake will find it.

The calendar gives longer forms of these readings (Acts 12:1–23; 2 Corinthians 12:1–14; Matthew 10:37–42 with 16:24–27 and 19:29–30). The weekday readings of the Fourth Saturday are 1 Corinthians 14:20–25 and Mark 6:1–6. In the Latin calendar St George was kept on 23 April. Sahada is Syriac for witness, martyr. The key words are witness, prayer, weakness and worthy.

Daniel 6:12–24 · Daniel in the den of lions

Setting.

The first half of the Book of Daniel tells stories of Jewish exiles who stay faithful at the court of foreign kings. Chapter 6 is set under “Darius the Mede”, a king not known from other ancient sources; many scholars think the book reached its final form in the second century BC, when the Jewish people suffered under the Syrian king Antiochus IV, and that these stories were told to strengthen those who were being forced to give up their faith. Whatever its literary form, the Church reads the book as inspired Scripture.

Envious officials have persuaded the king to sign a decree that for thirty days no one may pray to any god or man except the king. Daniel goes home, opens his windows toward Jerusalem, and prays three times a day “just as he had done previously” (6:10). This chapter, like most of Daniel 2–7, is written in Aramaic.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: the accusation: Daniel prays three times a day.
  • vv. 14–15: the king's distress; the law that cannot be changed.
  • vv. 16–18: Daniel thrown to the lions; the stone and the seal; the king's sleepless night.
  • vv. 19–22: at dawn: “has your God been able to deliver you?” “My God sent his angel.”
  • vv. 23–24: Daniel taken up unharmed; the accusers punished.

Key words.

  • ’ĕsār, interdict: A binding prohibition. Its weakness is that it cannot be changed even when the king who signed it wants to. Human law that refuses God is shown to be a trap for the very ruler who makes it.
  • gubbā’, den, pit: The lions' pit, closed with a stone and sealed. Christian readers have long seen in it a picture of the sealed tomb (Matthew 27:66), from which the servant of God comes out alive at dawn.
  • ’ĕlāhā’ ḥayyā’, the living God: The king's own words (v. 20). Daniel's God is not an image or a decree; he acts. Hebrews remembers those who “shut the mouths of lions” by faith (Hebrews 11:33).

Verse by verse.

“May your God, whom you faithfully serve, deliver you!” (6:16)

The king's helpless prayer is the first sign that Daniel's faithfulness has already won his heart.

“Then, at break of day, the king got up and hurried to the den of lions.” (6:19)

The hour of dawn, and a hurried visit to a sealed pit: Christians cannot hear this without thinking of the women at the tomb on Easter morning.

“My God sent his angel and shut the lions’ mouths so that they would not hurt me,” (6:22)
“no kind of harm was found on him, because he had trusted in his God.” (6:23)

The reason is stated plainly: trust. Not every martyr is delivered from the lions, as St George was not; but every one who trusts is held by God, in life and in death.

“The king gave a command, and those who had accused Daniel were brought and thrown into the den of lions” (6:24)

The ancient story ends with a harsh punishment, which also falls on the accusers' wives and children. The Law itself forbids putting children to death for their parents' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16), and the Gospel asks us to pray for our persecutors (Matthew 5:44). We read this ending as part of an old tale, not as a model.

How the Church reads it.

Daniel among the lions is one of the scenes most often painted in the catacombs of Rome: the first Christians, themselves thrown to beasts, saw in him the promise of God's faithfulness and of the resurrection. St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to die in the arena, wrote to the Romans: “I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, that I may be found pure bread of Christ” (Letter to the Romans 4). The Church reads Daniel as a witness that the faithful must obey God rather than any human power that commands what God forbids (compare Acts 5:29).

Acts 12:1–5 · James is killed; the Church prays for Peter

Setting.

“King Herod” here is Herod Agrippa I, a grandson of Herod the Great, who ruled Judea from AD 41 to 44. Acts tells of his sudden death a little later in the chapter (12:20–23). The killing of James can therefore be dated to about AD 44.

Luke writes that Herod saw “that it pleased the Jews”. He means the circles in Jerusalem whose favour the king was seeking at that time, not the Jewish people as a whole; Peter, James and the whole Jerusalem church were themselves Jews. As the Church refuses to blame the Jewish people as a whole for the Passion of Christ (CCC 597), so she does not blame them for the sufferings of the apostles.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Herod begins to persecute members of the church.
  • v. 2: James, the brother of John, killed with the sword.
  • vv. 3–4: Peter arrested at the festival of Unleavened Bread and guarded by four squads.
  • v. 5: Peter kept in prison; the church prays fervently.

Key words.

  • machaira, sword: James is killed with the sword, most likely beheaded, as tradition says St George would be. Jesus had told James and John, “You will indeed drink my cup” (Matthew 20:23). James is the first of the Twelve whose martyrdom the New Testament records.
  • tetradion, squad of four: Four squads of four soldiers, taking turns through the night. Luke shows how strongly Peter is held, so that his rescue in the verses that follow is plainly God's work.
  • ektenōs, fervently: Stretched out, earnest. Luke uses a form of the same word for the prayer of Jesus in his agony (Luke 22:44, a verse found in many manuscripts).

Verse by verse.

“He had James, the brother of John, killed with the sword.” (12:2)

A single verse for the death of an apostle. Luke does not dwell on it; the witness is given, and the Gospel goes on.

“(This was during the festival of Unleavened Bread.)” (12:3)

Peter is arrested at Passover time, as his Lord was. The disciple's way follows the Master's.

“While Peter was kept in prison, the church prayed fervently to God for him.” (12:5)

This is the Church's answer to persecution. She does not have swords or soldiers; she prays.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always prayed for those in prison for their faith and for those who suffer persecution in many lands. In Acts she prays for Peter, and the Church still prays especially for the successor of Peter. The Catechism calls martyrdom the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473), and it speaks of the Church gathering the records of those who have gone to the end to bear witness to their faith (CCC 2474). We pray also for those who persecute, as Christ and the martyrs did.

2 Corinthians 12:1–10 · Power made perfect in weakness

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 Paul answers rivals in Corinth whom he calls, with irony, “super-apostles” (11:5). They boasted of their visions and their eloquence. Paul reluctantly enters a “fool's speech” (11:16–12:13): if he must boast, he will boast of what shows his weakness, his beatings, shipwrecks and dangers (11:23–29).

Now he speaks of a vision fourteen years before, which would place it in the early 40s, before his great missionary journeys. He speaks of himself in the third person, as “a person in Christ”, as if the experience were too great to claim as his own.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the man caught up to the third heaven, into Paradise.
  • vv. 5–6: Paul will boast only of his weaknesses.
  • v. 7: the thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan.
  • vv. 8–9a: three times he asks; the Lord answers.
  • vv. 9b–10: “when I am weak, then I am strong”.

Key words.

  • paradeisos, Paradise: A Persian word for a walled garden. The Greek Old Testament uses it for the garden of Eden, and Jesus promises it to the thief on the cross (Luke 23:43).
  • skolops tē sarki, a thorn in the flesh: A stake or sharp splinter. Paul does not say what it was. St John Chrysostom understood it of the opponents and persecutions that troubled Paul (in substance); others have thought of an illness, perhaps the one he mentions in Galatians 4:13. The Church has never settled it, and the lesson is the same either way.
  • arkei, is sufficient: Enough. The Lord does not remove the thorn; he promises enough grace to carry it.
  • episkēnōsē, may dwell: Literally “may pitch a tent upon”. The power of Christ comes to rest on Paul's weakness as the glory of God rested on the tent in the wilderness.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, to keep me from being too elated, a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me,” (12:7)

“Was given”: Paul sees God's hand even in what Satan uses to torment him.

“Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me,” (12:8)

Three times, like Jesus in Gethsemane (Mark 14:32–41). It is right to ask for healing. The answer may come in another form than the one we asked for.

“My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.” (12:9)

Paul seldom quotes words the risen Lord spoke to him personally; here he does. The verb “said” is in the Greek perfect tense: a word given once and still standing.

“Therefore I am content with weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and calamities for the sake of Christ; for whenever I am weak, then I am strong.” (12:10)

“For the sake of Christ” is what gives the sufferings their meaning. Weakness in itself is not strength; weakness handed over to Christ becomes the place of his power.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only faith can embrace the mysterious ways of God's almighty power, and that this faith glories in its weaknesses in order to draw to itself Christ's power (CCC 273). The martyrs prove it. Tradition remembers a soldier who would not save his life by denying Christ, and was put to death; and the Church, centuries later, still calls him victorious. In every age the sick, the persecuted and the poor who hold to Christ are a sign of this power made perfect in weakness.

Matthew 10:37–42 · Whoever loses his life for my sake will find it

Setting.

Matthew 10 is the second of the five great discourses in his Gospel: Jesus sends out the Twelve and tells them what to expect. He has just warned them of persecution, of trials before governors and kings, and of division even inside families (10:16–36).

These verses end the discourse. They begin with the cost of discipleship and end with the reward of those who receive the disciples. Luke has a harsher form of the first saying: whoever does not “hate” father and mother (Luke 14:26), a Semitic way of saying “love less”. Matthew gives the meaning: love more than me.

Structure.

  • v. 37: love for Christ above father, mother, son and daughter.
  • v. 38: taking up the cross.
  • v. 39: losing and finding one's life.
  • v. 40: whoever welcomes you welcomes me, and the one who sent me.
  • vv. 41–42: the reward of welcoming a prophet, a righteous person, a little one.

Key words.

  • axios, worthy: Worth as much as. Said three times in vv. 37–38. Nothing earns this worthiness; it is a matter of what we place first.
  • stauros, cross: The disciples knew the cross as the Roman punishment for rebels and slaves. In Matthew the saying comes before Jesus speaks of his own cross; after Easter the Church heard it as a call to share his.
  • psychē, life: Life, self, soul. To keep one's life for oneself is to lose it; to give it for Christ is to find it in him.
  • dechomai, to welcome, receive: To take someone in as a guest. Jewish teaching said that a person's agent is like the one who sends him; Jesus applies this to his messengers.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me;” (10:37)

Only God can rightly ask this. Jesus speaks with the authority of the God who commanded Israel to love him with all the heart (Deuteronomy 6:5).

“and whoever does not take up the cross and follow me is not worthy of me.” (10:38)
“Those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it.” (10:39)

This is one of the sayings the Gospels repeat most often (compare 16:25). The martyr is its clearest proof.

“whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones in the name of a disciple” (10:42)

The smallest act of kindness to a disciple is not forgotten. Not everyone is called to martyrdom, but everyone can welcome and help those who suffer for Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that martyrdom is the supreme witness to the truth of the faith: the martyr bears witness to Christ who died and rose, to whom he is united by charity (CCC 2473). Tertullian wrote that the blood of Christians is seed (Apology 50, in substance). St George is honoured as one who heard this Gospel and did it: he loved Christ more than his own life, and he found it. The saying about the cup of cold water reminds us that the Church honours the martyrs also by caring for those who suffer for their faith today.

Questions people ask

Did St George really kill a dragon?

No. The story of the dragon appears many centuries after his death, in the Middle Ages, and it is a legend, not history. What history gives us is small but solid: a martyr honoured at his tomb at Lydda from the early centuries. The Church reads the dragon as a picture of a truth: the martyr's faith overcomes the ancient serpent (Revelation 12:9–11).

Why was James killed and Peter rescued?

Acts does not explain it, and we should not try to. Both were faithful; both belonged to the Lord. Peter too would later give his life in Rome. God's ways with each of his servants are his own, and the Church honours the one who died and the one who was spared, as Daniel was.

Does Jesus set himself against love of family?

No. He commands us to honour father and mother (Matthew 15:4; 19:19). He asks to be loved first, and love of family is then held within love of him. When a family demands that we deny Christ, as St George's world demanded of him, then Christ must come first.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syriac tradition martyrs are called sahde, witnesses, and George is Mar Geevarghese Sahada. Early in this season, on the Friday after the feast of the Resurrection, the Syro-Malabar Church kept the feast of all the holy martyrs; today she honours one of the best-loved among them. Kerala is full of his churches and his name, and great pilgrim churches such as Aruvithura and Edathua draw crowds who come with their fears and their vows. The readings ask us not only to seek his help but to share his choice: Christ first.

For study and teaching

  • Daniel kept praying as he had done before, decree or no decree. What daily prayer of mine would I keep if it became costly?
  • The Church prayed fervently for Peter. Whom should I pray for who suffers for the faith?
  • Where in my life might the Lord be saying, “My grace is sufficient for you”?
  • What do I love more than Christ, and what would it mean to put him first?
