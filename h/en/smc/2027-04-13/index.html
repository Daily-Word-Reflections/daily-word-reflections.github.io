Word for the day

“Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances; for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus for you.” 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18

Reflection

The First Letter to the Thessalonians is probably the oldest Christian writing we possess, written only about twenty years after the Resurrection to a community that was a few months old. Paul had been forced to leave the city quickly, and he writes like a father who could not finish teaching his children. At the end of the letter he gathers his advice into a string of short commands, almost like a rule of life for a new parish. He begins with those who serve: “But we appeal to you, brothers and sisters, to respect those who labor among you, and have charge of you in the Lord and admonish you; esteem them very highly in love because of their work. Be at peace among yourselves.” A parish lives by the labour of many people: the priest, the sisters, the catechism teachers who come every Sunday morning, the sacristan who opens the church before dawn, the choir, the family units. Paul asks that they be held in love, not because they are perfect but “because of their work.” Then he turns to the care of the weak: “admonish the idlers, encourage the fainthearted, help the weak, be patient with all of them. See that none of you repays evil for evil, but always seek to do good to one another and to all.” Each kind of person needs a different kind of love: a word of correction for one, a word of courage for another, a hand for a third, and patience for everyone.

Then come three short lines that sum up the Easter life: “Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances; for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus for you.” Paul is not asking us to feel cheerful all the time. He wrote these words to a persecuted church. Christian joy rests not on our circumstances but on the fact that Christ is risen, and that fact does not change when the day goes badly. Prayer without ceasing is not endless words but a heart turned toward God all day, returning to him again and again, the way a mother's heart keeps returning to a sick child. Thanksgiving in all circumstances is the language of the Holy Qurbana, whose very name in Greek, Eucharist, means thanksgiving. Then Paul looks toward the gift of Pentecost: “Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise the words of prophets, but test everything; hold fast to what is good; abstain from every form of evil.” The Spirit is like a fire that can be smothered by neglect or by fear. A Christian is open to the Spirit, yet not naive: he tests, he weighs things by the faith of the Church, he holds on to the good and lets the evil go.

In the Gospel Jesus explains the parable of the sower to his disciples. “The sower sows the word.” The seed is always good; the difference lies in the soil. On the path, the word never goes in: “Satan immediately comes and takes away the word that is sown in them.” On rocky ground, the word is welcomed “with joy”, but it has no root, and when trouble comes, “immediately they fall away.” Among thorns, the word grows for a while, but “the cares of the world, and the lure of wealth, and the desire for other things come in and choke the word, and it yields nothing.” Finally the good soil: “they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” The Easter season is a time of harvest and of testing. Many people came to the Easter Qurbana with joy. Will that joy put down roots, or will it wither with the first difficulty? Will the worries of exams, loans and prices, and the pull of money and pleasure, choke what grew at Easter? The parable is not a sorting of people into fixed groups. Each of us has some path, some rock and some thorns in the heart. The Lord gives us the readings of this day as a farmer's tools: patient prayer breaks up the hard path, perseverance in trial lets roots go down, and thanksgiving pulls out the thorns of anxiety.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul writes to a very young church in Thessalonica, and he begins with those who serve it. Respect those who labour among you, he says, and esteem them very highly in love because of their work (1 Thessalonians 5:12–13). Think of your priest, the sisters, the catechism teachers, the sacristan who opens the church before dawn. Let us thank them.

Then Paul gives us the Easter life in three short lines: rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances. He wrote this to people who were suffering. Our joy does not depend on how the day goes. It depends on the fact that Christ is risen, and that never changes.

And he adds: do not quench the Spirit. The Spirit is a fire. Neglect and fear can smother it. As we move toward Pentecost, let us keep that fire burning by prayer.

In the Gospel Jesus explains the sower. The seed is always good; the soil makes the difference. The hard path, where the word never enters. The rocky ground, where joy has no root. The thorns, where worries and money choke the word. And the good soil, which bears fruit a hundredfold.

Every heart has a little of each. This week, let us break up the hard path with prayer, let our Easter joy put down roots, and pull out the thorns of worry by giving thanks in all things. Alleluia! Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul ends the oldest of his letters with a string of short commands for a young community: respect those who labour among you, rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks, do not quench the Spirit. In Mark, Jesus explains the parable of the sower: one word falls on four kinds of hearers. Read together, the two readings show what good soil looks like in a parish: people who receive the word with joy, keep it through trouble, and let it bear fruit in prayer, thanksgiving and patience with one another.

1 Thessalonians 5:12–22 · Rejoice, pray, give thanks

Setting.

Thessalonica was the chief city of Macedonia. Paul founded the church there on his second missionary journey and had to leave the city in haste (Acts 17:1–10). He wrote this letter from Corinth, probably about AD 50, and most scholars hold it to be the oldest writing in the New Testament. The community was only a few months old.

Our passage comes at the very end of the letter, just before the closing blessing. After teaching about the Lord's coming (4:13–5:11), Paul gathers his practical counsel into short lines that are easy to remember.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: respect and love for those who lead and labour in the community; peace among all.
  • vv. 14–15: care for the idle, the fainthearted and the weak; no repaying evil for evil.
  • vv. 16–18: rejoice, pray, give thanks: three commands and one reason, the will of God in Christ.
  • vv. 19–22: the Spirit and prophecy: do not quench, do not despise, test, hold fast, abstain.

Key words.

  • proistamenoi, those who have charge of you: Literally “those who stand before” you. Paul uses the same word for those who lead in Romans 12:8. Within some twenty years of the Resurrection the young Church already had recognised leaders, and Paul asks that they be loved “because of their work”.
  • adialeiptōs, without ceasing: Without a break, kept up steadily. Paul uses the same word of his own constant remembering and thanksgiving for this church (1:3; 2:13). He asks of them what he already does for them.
  • eucharisteite, give thanks: The verb from which the word Eucharist comes. Thanksgiving “in all circumstances” is the habit of a people whose central act of worship is itself a thanksgiving.

Verse by verse.

“Be at peace among yourselves.” (5:13)

Peace in a community is the first fruit of respect for those who serve it.

“See that none of you repays evil for evil, but always seek to do good to one another and to all.” (5:15)

“And to all”: kindness does not stop at the church door. Paul echoes the Lord's own teaching (Matthew 5:44; compare Romans 12:17).

“Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances; for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus for you.” (5:16–18)

People often ask what God's will is for them. Here is one clear answer, and it fits every state of life.

“Do not quench the Spirit.” (5:19)

The image is of fire (compare Acts 2:3). The gifts of the Spirit can be put out by neglect or by contempt.

“but test everything; hold fast to what is good;” (5:21)

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council turns to this passage when it speaks of the gifts of the Spirit: those who lead the Church must judge them, not to extinguish the Spirit, but to test all things and hold fast to what is good (Lumen Gentium 12, citing 1 Thessalonians 5:12, 19–21). The Catechism takes “pray constantly” as a description of the Christian life itself (CCC 2742). St Augustine, writing to the widow Proba, explains how this is possible: we pray without ceasing when we go on desiring, in faith, hope and love, the life that God alone can give (in substance).

Mark 4:13–20 · Four kinds of ground

Setting.

Jesus has told the parable of the sower from a boat to the crowd on the shore (4:1–9). When he is alone with the Twelve and those around him, they ask him about the parables (4:10–12). Now he explains this one, and he treats it as the key to the others: the kingdom comes as a word sown, and everything depends on how it is received.

The explanation moves from the seed to the people. The seed on the path becomes “the ones on the path”. Each kind of ground is a kind of hearer.

Structure.

  • v. 13: a question to the disciples: if they miss this parable, how will they understand the rest?
  • v. 14: the sower sows the word.
  • v. 15: the path: Satan takes the word away at once.
  • vv. 16–17: rocky ground: joy without root; trouble makes them fall away.
  • vv. 18–19: thorns: cares, wealth and desires choke the word.
  • v. 20: good soil: they hear, accept and bear fruit.

Key words.

  • ho logos, the word: In Mark the word is the Gospel message itself (compare 2:2, “he was speaking the word to them”). The first Christians called their preaching simply “the word” (Acts 8:4).
  • skandalizontai, they fall away: From skandalon, a trap or a stumbling block. Trouble and persecution become a trap that trips them, because there is no root to hold them.
  • apatē tou ploutou, the lure of wealth: Apatē means deceit. Wealth promises more than it can give, and so it deceives.

Verse by verse.

“The sower sows the word.” (4:14)

Jesus does not stop to describe the sower. In the parable the seed falls everywhere, even where a careful farmer would not throw it. God is generous with his word.

“But they have no root, and endure only for a while;” (4:17)

Joy is good, but joy without roots does not last. Roots grow out of sight: in prayer, in the sacraments, in the slow habits of faith.

“but the cares of the world, and the lure of wealth, and the desire for other things come in and choke the word, and it yields nothing.” (4:19)

Thorns do not kill quickly. They grow up beside the good plant until it has no light.

“they hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirty and sixty and a hundredfold.” (4:20)

Three verbs: hear, accept, bear fruit. The harvest is far beyond what anyone expected from so much seed that seemed wasted.

How the Church reads it.

St Gregory the Great, preaching on this parable, said that people would hardly believe him if he called riches thorns; yet they are thorns, because they tear the mind with their cares and do not let it rest (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the word of God is the strength of faith and the food of the soul for the Church's children (Dei Verbum 21). In every Holy Qurbana the word is sown before the Bread of life is given.

Questions people ask

Is the parable saying that some people are simply bad soil?

No. The four kinds of ground can be found in one heart, and at different times in one life. The path can be broken up and the thorns pulled out. The parable is a warning and an invitation, not a sentence passed on anyone.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks the community to love those who labour among them. Whom in my parish do I need to thank, or to pray for, this week?
  • Which of the four kinds of ground best describes my heart this Easter season, and what one thorn do I need to pull out?
