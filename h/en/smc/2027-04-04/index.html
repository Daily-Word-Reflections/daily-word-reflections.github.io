Word for the day

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!” Jesus said to him, “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.”” John 20:28–29

Reflection

Every Christian in Kerala knows this Sunday by its name: Puthu Njayar, New Sunday. It is the eighth day of Easter, and the Church calls it new because everything has become new: “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (2 Corinthians 5:17). In the ancient Church the newly baptised, who had worn white all through Easter week, laid aside their white garments on this day and took their place among the faithful; so the Syro-Malabar calendar also calls it the Sunday of the Neophytes. But for the St Thomas Christians it is above all the Sunday of their father in faith. The Gospel of the day is the Gospel of Mar Thoma, and the Church that by tradition he founded on the Malabar coast hears it as a family story. From early morning, and on the days around it, pilgrims climb the rocky hill of Malayattoor, where the tradition of the place holds that the apostle prayed, many of them carrying wooden crosses and calling on him as they climb. The climb is a confession in the body: we come to the hill of Thomas to say with him what he said in the upper room.

The Gospel begins on the evening of Easter day. “When it was evening on that day, the first day of the week, and the doors of the house where the disciples had met were locked for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them and said, ‘Peace be with you.’” John's phrase ‘the Jews’ here means certain religious authorities of that time; the disciples behind the locked doors were themselves Jews, and the Church warns against reading such words as blame on the Jewish people as a whole (Catechism 597; Nostra Aetate 4). Locked doors do not keep out the Risen Lord. His first word is peace, and his first gesture is to show his wounds: “After he said this, he showed them his hands and his side. Then the disciples rejoiced when they saw the Lord.” He has risen in the same body that was crucified; the wounds are not erased but glorified, signs of a love stronger than death. Then he sends them: “As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” And he breathes on them, as God breathed into Adam in the beginning (Genesis 2:7): “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.” On the very evening of the Resurrection the Lord gives his Church the power to forgive sins in his name. The Council of Trent saw in these words the institution of the Sacrament of Penance. The first gift of the Risen Christ is mercy: peace, wounds, the Spirit, forgiveness. That is why many keep this day as the Sunday of Divine Mercy: the mercy of God flows from the wounds of the Risen One.

Thomas was not there. When the others say, “We have seen the Lord,” he answers with the most honest sentence in the Gospel: “Unless I see the mark of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in the mark of the nails and my hand in his side, I will not believe.” This is the same Thomas who had said, when Jesus went up to Bethany, “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (John 11:16), and who had asked at the Last Supper, “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” (John 14:5). He is brave, practical, and unwilling to pretend. “A week later his disciples were again in the house, and Thomas was with them.” Note that he stays with the community; his doubt does not take him away from the Church, and it is in the Church, on the eighth day, that the Lord finds him. Jesus speaks to him in his own words: “Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” And Thomas makes the greatest confession of faith in all the Gospels: “My Lord and my God!” He saw a man; he confessed God. Then Jesus looks beyond Thomas to us: “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” In many Kerala churches, when the priest lifts up the consecrated Host and the Chalice, people whisper Thomas's words: My Lord and my God. We do not see the wounds, but we believe that the Risen Lord himself is present, truly and substantially, Body and Blood, soul and divinity. John tells us why he wrote: “so that you may come to believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and that through believing you may have life in his name” (John 20:31).

The other readings show what the faith of Thomas builds. Isaiah sees nations streaming to God: “See, you shall call nations that you do not know, and nations that do not know you shall run to you.” A Church in India, far from Jerusalem, is one of those nations that ran. Isaiah also promises that God's word does not fail: “so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose.” The word that came to the Malabar coast has not returned empty in nearly two thousand years. In Acts we see the fruit of the Resurrection in a community: “Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common. With great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all. There was not a needy person among them.” Barnabas, “son of encouragement”, sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet. Kerala Christian homes once kept the custom of the pidiyari, a handful of rice set aside at every cooking for the Church and the poor: small, daily, and together enough to feed a parish. And Paul sings of the Christ whom Thomas touched: “He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation.” “He is the head of the body, the church; he is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead.” Through him God was pleased “to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, by making peace through the blood of his cross.” The wounds Thomas touched are the wounds through which the whole universe is reconciled to God.

Homily outline

Theme: “Thomas answered him, ‘My Lord and my God!’ Jesus said to him, ‘Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.’”

New Sunday: the peace of the Risen Lord, the Spirit given for the forgiveness of sins, the confession of Mar Thoma, and the community of one heart and soul.

1. Peace be with you

  • Locked doors.
  • Wounds shown.
  • Sent as the Father sent him.
“For you shall go out in joy, and be led back in peace; the mountains and the hills before you shall burst into song, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands.” Isaiah 55:12
“Jesus said to them again, “Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, so I send you.”” John 20:21

Apply: Name the fear that locks the door of your heart, and let the Risen Lord come through it with his peace.

2. Receive the Holy Spirit

  • The breath of new creation.
  • The power to forgive.
  • Mercy from the wounds.
“let the wicked forsake their way, and the unrighteous their thoughts; let them return to the LORD, that he may have mercy on them, and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.” Isaiah 55:7
“When he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them, “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.”” John 20:22–23

Apply: Go to the Sacrament of Reconciliation during this Easter season.

3. One heart and soul

  • Testimony with great power.
  • No one in need.
  • The son of encouragement.
“How very good and pleasant it is when kindred live together in unity!” Psalm 133:1
“Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one claimed private ownership of any possessions, but everything they owned was held in common.” Acts 4:32

Apply: Keep a modern pidiyari: set aside something small each day this season for the poor.

4. My Lord and my God

  • Thomas stays with the community.
  • The wounds offered.
  • Blessed without seeing.
“See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands; your walls are continually before me.” Isaiah 49:16
“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!” Jesus said to him, “Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.”” John 20:28–29

Apply: At every elevation this season, say in your heart the words of Mar Thoma.

On the eighth day the Risen Lord comes through our locked doors, shows us his wounds, breathes his Spirit and forgives our sins.

With Mar Thoma our father in faith we confess him, My Lord and my God, and we live as one heart and soul.

Take-home resolution: This Easter season, go to confession, whisper ‘My Lord and my God’ at every elevation, and keep a daily pidiyari for the poor.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today is Puthu Njayar, New Sunday. Christ is risen, and everything is new. In the ancient Church the newly baptised laid aside their white garments today and took their place among the faithful. And for us, children of Mar Thoma, it is the Sunday of our father in faith. Thousands are climbing the hill of Malayattoor today to say with him: My Lord and my God.

On Easter evening the disciples were behind locked doors, afraid. Jesus came through the doors and said: “Peace be with you.” Then he showed them his hands and his side. He is risen in the same body that was nailed to the Cross. The wounds are still there, now shining with glory: the marks of a love that death could not defeat.

Then he breathed on them, as God breathed into Adam, and said: “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them.” On the night of the Resurrection the Lord gave his Church the power to forgive sins. The first gift of Easter is mercy. If you have stayed away from confession for a long time, this Easter season is your time to come home.

Thomas was not there. He said: “Unless I see the mark of the nails… I will not believe.” But notice what he did: he stayed with the community. A week later, on Sunday, he was with them, and Jesus came for him. The Lord did not scold him; he offered him his wounds. And Thomas said the greatest words in the Gospel: “My Lord and my God!” If you have doubts, do not walk away from the Church. Bring them here, on the eighth day. This is where the Lord finds Thomas.

Jesus then speaks to us: “Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” We do not see the wounds. But when the priest lifts up the Host and the Chalice, our grandmothers taught us to whisper Thomas's words. It is not a pious habit only. It is the faith of the Church: the Risen Lord is truly here, Body and Blood.

And faith in the Risen Lord builds a community: “of one heart and soul… There was not a needy person among them.” Our grandparents kept a handful of rice for the poor at every cooking. Let us find our own pidiyari this season: something small, every day, for those in need. And let us go out as Isaiah promised: “For you shall go out in joy, and be led back in peace.” My Lord and my God! Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Thomas said: ‘I will not believe unless I touch Jesus' wounds.’ Then Jesus came and said: ‘Touch them, Thomas, and believe!’

Thomas said: ‘My Lord and my God!’ Mar Thoma is the apostle who, our tradition says, came to Kerala.

When the priest lifts up the Host at Qurbana, you can whisper like Thomas: ‘My Lord and my God!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you came through locked doors and stood among your frightened disciples, saying, Peace be with you. You showed them your hands and your side, you breathed on them the Holy Spirit, and you gave your Church the power to forgive sins. You did not abandon Thomas in his doubt, but came back for him on the eighth day and let him touch your wounds. With Mar Thoma, our father in faith, we confess you: My Lord and my God! Make us blessed among those who have not seen and yet believe. Make our parishes one heart and soul, with no one in need among us, and send us to the nations as the Father sent you. Through the prayers of your holy Mother, who believed without seeing, keep the Church of Mar Thoma faithful in every land. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

New Sunday closes the first week of the Resurrection, and for the Syro-Malabar Church it is the feast of the faith of Mar Thoma. A week after Easter the risen Lord comes again through locked doors and leads Thomas to the confession “My Lord and my God!”. Isaiah promises a word that will not return empty and nations that will run to God's people. Acts shows the first fruit of the Resurrection: a community of one heart and soul, where the apostles bear witness with great power and no one is in need. Colossians sings who the risen one is: the image of the invisible God, the firstborn from the dead, in whom all things hold together. The readings move from God's word, through the community it creates, to the Lord himself confessed as God. The key words are witness, peace and believe.

Isaiah 55:4–13 · My word shall not return to me empty

Setting.

This is the last chapter of the part of Isaiah addressed to the exiles in Babylon. It opens with an invitation to a banquet: “Ho, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters” (55:1). God promises “an everlasting covenant, my steadfast, sure love for David” (55:3). The promise once made to David's house is now opened to the whole people.

St Paul, preaching at Antioch in Pisidia, quotes v. 3 as fulfilled in the Resurrection: God raised Jesus from the dead, “no more to return to corruption”, and so gave “the holy promises made to David” (Acts 13:34). That is one reason this chapter is read in the season of the Resurrection.

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5: David a witness to the peoples; the nations will run to you.
  • vv. 6–7: seek the Lord; let the wicked return; he will pardon.
  • vv. 8–9: God's thoughts and ways are higher.
  • vv. 10–11: rain and snow; the word that accomplishes its purpose.
  • vv. 12–13: going out in joy; creation sings; the myrtle in place of the brier.

Key words.

  • ‘ēd, witness: David was a witness to the nations of what God had done for Israel. The New Testament gives the same task to the apostles: “you will be my witnesses … to the ends of the earth” (Acts 1:8).
  • šûb, return: The great word of conversion in the prophets. The wicked are to “return to the Lord” (v. 7), and God's word does not “return” to him empty (v. 11). Our return to God is the fruit of his word coming to us.
  • dābār, word: In Hebrew a word is also a deed. God's word is not a sound that fades: it goes out, does its work and comes back fulfilled.

Verse by verse.

“See, I made him a witness to the peoples, a leader and commander for the peoples.” (55:4)
“Seek the Lord while he may be found, call upon him while he is near;” (55:6)

Urgency and tenderness together. God is near now; the time to seek him is now.

“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, nor are your ways my ways, says the Lord.” (55:8)

In context this is first about mercy: God pardons more abundantly than we would (v. 7). His ways are higher above all in forgiving.

“so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose,” (55:11)

Christians hear in this the Word who came from the Father and returned to him with his work accomplished: “I came from the Father and have come into the world; again, I am leaving the world and am going to the Father” (John 16:28).

“Instead of the thorn shall come up the cypress; instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle;” (55:13)

Thorns and thistles were the sign of the curse in Genesis 3:18. Here the curse is reversed, and the earth itself shares in the joy of the redeemed.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that such is the force and power of the word of God that it is the support and energy of the Church, the strength of faith for her children and the food of the soul (Dei Verbum 21, in substance). Today's Gospel shows that power at work: Thomas comes to faith because the other disciples told him, “We have seen the Lord,” and because the Lord himself spoke to him. Today, says the Gospel, others will believe through the word without seeing (John 20:29, 31).

Acts 4:32–37 · One heart and soul

Setting.

This is the second of three summaries in which Luke describes the life of the Jerusalem community (2:42–47; 4:32–35; 5:12–16). It comes just after the apostles have been released by the council and the community has prayed so that the place shook (4:23–31). The common life is the fruit of the Resurrection and of the Spirit.

The sharing was not imposed by law. When Ananias and Sapphira lie about their gift, Peter reminds them that the land was theirs and the money was at their disposal (5:4). Their sin was the lie, not keeping their property.

Structure.

  • v. 32: one heart and soul; possessions held in common.
  • v. 33: the apostles' powerful witness to the Resurrection.
  • vv. 34–35: no one in need; goods distributed according to need.
  • vv. 36–37: the example of Barnabas.

Key words.

  • kardia kai psychē mia, one heart and soul: A common expression for deep friendship among the Greeks, and a Hebrew way of speaking of the whole person. Luke shows that the risen Lord has made of many believers one person.
  • koina, in common: The same word as in 2:44. Luke may also be echoing God's promise to Israel: “There will, however, be no one in need among you” (Deuteronomy 15:4). The Resurrection community lives what the Law hoped for.
  • Barnabas, son of encouragement: Luke gives the name the meaning “son of encouragement”. Barnabas will later stand beside the newly converted Saul when others were afraid of him (9:27) and will go with him on mission.

Verse by verse.

“Now the whole group of those who believed were of one heart and soul,” (4:32)
“With great power the apostles gave their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all.” (4:33)

The common life is set around the witness to the Resurrection. The sharing is not a programme on its own; it flows from faith in the risen Lord.

“There was not a needy person among them,” (4:34)
“He sold a field that belonged to him, then brought the money, and laid it at the apostles’ feet.” (4:37)

To lay a gift at someone's feet was a sign of handing it over completely. The gift goes through the apostles, so it is shared by the whole Church and not given as a private favour.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, speaking of the communion of saints, recalls that the first believers “had everything in common” and that what the true Christian has is to be regarded as a good possessed in common with everyone else (CCC 952, in substance). St Augustine opened his Rule for those living together in community with this verse: to live in harmony, with one mind and one heart on the way to God (in substance). Religious life in every age has looked to this passage, and every parish is called to its spirit of sharing.

Colossians 1:12–20 · The firstborn from the dead

Setting.

After the greeting and a prayer of thanksgiving, the letter sings a hymn to Christ (vv. 15–20). Many scholars think it was an early Christian hymn that the author took up. It has two strophes: Christ and creation (vv. 15–17), and Christ and the new creation, the Church and reconciliation (vv. 18–20). The word “firstborn” stands in each.

The hymn answers those at Colossae who feared other powers. Whatever “thrones or dominions or rulers or powers” there may be, all were created through Christ and for him (v. 16).

Structure.

  • vv. 12–14: thanks to the Father, who has moved us from darkness into the kingdom of his Son.
  • vv. 15–17: Christ, the image of God, firstborn of all creation; all things created in him and held together in him.
  • v. 18: head of the body, the Church; firstborn from the dead.
  • vv. 19–20: all the fullness dwells in him; peace through the blood of his Cross.

Key words.

  • eikōn, image: The invisible God becomes visible in Christ. Humanity was made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); Christ is the image. “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (John 14:9).
  • prōtotokos, firstborn: In Scripture the firstborn has first place and the inheritance. The word does not mean that Christ is the first creature. St Athanasius answered the Arians that the Son is called firstborn not because he was made, but because he came down to his creatures and became the first to rise from the dead (in substance). The Creed says, “begotten, not made”.
  • apokatallaxai, to reconcile: To change enemies into friends. The reconciliation reaches “all things, whether on earth or in heaven”, and it is made at the price of “the blood of his cross”.

Verse by verse.

“He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son,” (1:13)

The language of a new Exodus. The baptized have changed kingdoms.

“He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation;” (1:15)
“He himself is before all things, and in him all things hold together.” (1:17)

Christ is not only the beginning of creation but also what holds it together now. Nothing falls apart that is held in him.

“He is the head of the body, the church; he is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead,” (1:18)

The Resurrection makes him the first of a new creation. On New Sunday the Church hears that the one Thomas touched is the head of the body to which we belong.

“and through him God was pleased to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, by making peace through the blood of his cross.” (1:20)

The wounds Thomas is invited to touch are the price of this peace.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this hymn to teach that God created everything by the eternal Word, his beloved Son: in him all things were created, and in him all things hold together (CCC 291). It quotes v. 18 when it speaks of Christ as head of the Church, his body (CCC 792). The faith of Nicaea, that the Son is “begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father”, is the Church's reading of this hymn: the one who is firstborn is the eternal Son, through whom all things were made.

John 20:19–29 · My Lord and my God

Setting.

It is the evening of the first day of the week. Mary Magdalene has told the disciples, “I have seen the Lord” (20:18), yet they are behind locked doors. John says they were afraid “of the Jews”. In John's Gospel this phrase often means the authorities in Jerusalem who had opposed Jesus (compare 7:13; 9:22). The disciples themselves were Jews, and so was Jesus. It is no accusation against the Jewish people.

The passage has two scenes, a week apart. Thomas is absent from the first, refuses to believe the testimony of the others, and is present at the second. The Gospel's first ending follows at once: these things are written “so that you may come to believe” (20:31).

Structure.

  • vv. 19–20: Jesus comes; “Peace be with you”; he shows his hands and side; joy.
  • vv. 21–23: the sending, the breath of the Spirit, the power to forgive and retain sins.
  • vv. 24–25: Thomas absent; “Unless I see …”.
  • vv. 26–27: a week later; Jesus invites Thomas to touch.
  • vv. 28–29: Thomas's confession; the blessing of those who believe without seeing.

Key words.

  • eirēnē hymin, peace be with you: The ordinary Hebrew greeting, šālôm, but now spoken by the one who promised, “my peace I give to you” (14:27). He says it three times in this passage. To men who had abandoned him, his first word is peace, not reproach.
  • enephysēsen, he breathed on: The Greek Old Testament uses this verb in Genesis 2:7, when God breathed into the first man the breath of life. The risen Lord breathes the Holy Spirit, and a new creation begins.
  • Didymos, the Twin: The Greek translation of his Aramaic name, Thomas, which means “twin”. The same Thomas had said, when Jesus went toward Jerusalem, “Let us also go, that we may die with him” (11:16).
  • ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, my Lord and my God: The highest confession of faith in the Gospels. John's Gospel began, “the Word was God” (1:1), and near its end a disciple says it to Jesus himself.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you.”” (20:19)
“As the Father has sent me, so I send you.” (20:21)

The mission of the Church is the mission of the Son, continued.

“If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained.” (20:23)

The first gift of the risen Lord through the Spirit is the power to forgive. The Church sees here the institution of the sacrament of reconciliation (CCC 1485).

“Put your finger here and see my hands. Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe.” (20:27)

Jesus offers Thomas exactly what he had demanded. The risen body still bears its wounds. John does not say that Thomas touched them; the invitation was enough.

“Thomas answered him, “My Lord and my God!”” (20:28)
“Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have come to believe.” (20:29)

This is the last beatitude in the Gospels, and it is spoken for us.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that at the appearance of the risen Jesus the title “Lord” becomes adoration: “My Lord and my God!” (CCC 448). It teaches that on Easter evening the Lord gave the apostles his own power to forgive sins (CCC 976; 1485), and the Council of Trent taught that these words refer to the power of forgiving and retaining sins in the sacrament of Penance. St Gregory the Great said in a homily that the unbelief of Thomas did more for our faith than the faith of the disciples who believed: his doubt was healed, and our doubt is healed with his (in substance).

Questions people ask

Was Thomas wrong to doubt?

He was slow to believe the witness of his brothers, and Jesus gently rebukes that: “Do not doubt but believe.” But his doubt was not a rejection of Jesus. He stayed with the community, he was there the next Sunday, and when the Lord came, he made the greatest confession in the Gospels. His story shows that honest struggle, held within the Church, can end in deep faith.

Did the first Christians abolish private property (Acts 4:32)?

No. Their sharing was willing, as Peter's words in 5:4 show. The Church has always taught both the right to private property and that goods are meant for all, so that property must serve the common good and the poor (CCC 2402–2406).

What does it mean that Christ is the “firstborn of all creation”?

It means that he has first place over all creation, not that he was the first thing God made. The same hymn says that all things were created in him and through him, so he cannot himself be one of the things created. The Church confesses him as the eternal Son, “begotten, not made”.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

For the Syro-Malabar Church, New Sunday (Puthu Njayar) is a feast of family. We are the Mar Thoma Christians, and today's Gospel tells the faith of our father. According to our tradition, Mar Thoma Shliha came to Kerala in AD 52, founded communities, and was martyred at Mylapore. His confession, “My Lord and my God”, is on the lips of many of our faithful at the elevation of the Holy Qurbana. On this day many go on pilgrimage to the hill shrine of Malayattoor, linked by our tradition to the apostle's prayer. The name “New Sunday” also recalls the newness of the Resurrection: the first Sunday after the feast, when the Church begins again to live the new life given at Easter.

For study and teaching

  • Thomas came to faith in the community, on the next Sunday. What helps me to stay with the Church when my faith is struggling?
  • Jesus' first word to those who abandoned him was “Peace.” To whom do I need to bring that first word?
  • Acts says there was no one in need among them. Who is in need in my parish, and what can I share?
  • When I say or hear “My Lord and my God” at the Holy Qurbana, what do I mean by it?
