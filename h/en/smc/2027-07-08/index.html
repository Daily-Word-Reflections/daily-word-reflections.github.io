Readings

Word for the day

“Jesus said to them, “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.”” John 8:58

Reflection

The Corinthian church was rich in gifts, and the gift they prized most was speaking in tongues, ecstatic prayer in sounds no one could understand. Paul does not despise it; he thanks God that he speaks in tongues more than all of them. But in chapter 14 he asks a pastor's question: what does the gift do for the whole assembly? “One who speaks in a tongue should pray for the power to interpret.” Then he gives one of the clearest sentences in the New Testament on how a Christian prays: “I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also; I will sing praise with the spirit, but I will sing praise with the mind also.” Heart and head are not rivals. Prayer that is only feeling, without understanding, leaves the mind “unproductive”, and prayer that is only thought, without the Spirit, is dry. The Church prays with both.

The second half of the reading takes us into the Church's worship. If you give thanks only in the spirit, “how can anyone in the position of an outsider say the ‘Amen’ to your thanksgiving, since the outsider does not know what you are saying?” The Greek word for thanksgiving here is the word from which we have Eucharist, and the Amen is the people's answer. In the Qurbana the people do not watch; they answer, and the Amen is their signature on the prayer, as if each one said: this is mine as well. For that Amen to be true it must be understood, which is why our Church celebrates the Qurbana in the mother tongue of the people, and why Paul would rather speak “five words with my mind, in order to instruct others also, than ten thousand words in a tongue”. The measure of the gift is how it builds up the Church. This is a good word at the opening of Kaitha, the season of the Church's growth.

In the Gospel the argument in the Temple court has become sharp. Jesus has said, “whoever keeps my word will never see death,” and his hearers answer with an insult: “Are we not right in saying that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?” They are Jews speaking to a Jew, in a family quarrel about who he is, as the prophets had quarrelled with their own people. John's phrase “the Jews” names these hostile voices in Jerusalem; it cannot be turned against the Jewish people, and Christians have often sinned by doing so. The Church condemns every hatred and persecution of the Jews (Nostra Aetate 4). Jesus answers calmly: “I do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and he is the judge.” He does not defend himself; he honours the Father.

Then the argument turns to Abraham. “Your ancestor Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day; he saw it and was glad.” The Jewish tradition spoke of Abraham seeing the days to come, and the Church has read in Isaac's birth and the sacrifice on Moriah a sign of the One to come. His hearers object: “You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?” And Jesus says the sentence on which the whole Gospel of John rests: “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.” He does not say “I was”; he says “I am”. It is the name God gave to Moses at the burning bush, “I am who I am”, now spoken by a man standing in the Temple. They pick up stones, for they understood him. Jesus hides himself and goes out. For us the “I am” is not a danger but a promise: the One who is before Abraham, before the world, is with us in every Qurbana, and whoever keeps his word will not see death for ever.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul tells the Corinthians how to pray: “I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also” (1 Corinthians 14:15). Prayer needs both the heart and the head. When we only feel, we may not know what we are saying. When we only think, our prayer may become dry.

He also speaks about the Amen. When the priest gives thanks to God at the altar, the people answer Amen. That small word means: yes, this is true, and this is my prayer too. But for the people to say Amen, they must understand. Our Qurbana is celebrated in our own language so that every Amen may be real.

Paul says that in church he would rather speak five words that teach others than ten thousand that no one can follow (1 Corinthians 14:19). The measure of everything in the Church is whether it builds up. This is a good thought at the start of Kaitha, the season of growth.

In the Gospel Jesus is in the Temple, and the argument is hard. Some people insult him. Jesus does not insult them. He says: “I honor my Father.” Then he says something wonderful: “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58). He uses the name that God told Moses at the burning bush. He is God's Son, and he is God.

They picked up stones. But we pick up our Amen. Let us say it this week with understanding, at the Qurbana and in every prayer, and keep his word. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what Amen means? It means: yes, this is true, and I agree! When the priest prays at Holy Qurbana, we all say Amen. Say it with your heart, not only with your mouth! And Jesus told us a great secret: he was there even before Abraham, long, long ago. Jesus is God's Son, and he is always with us.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are before Abraham and before all ages, and you are with us now. Teach us to pray with the Spirit and with the mind, so that our Amen may be true. Let every word of thanksgiving at the altar be understood and answered by the whole assembly, and let every gift you give us build up your Church. Help us to keep your word, and give us the joy of Abraham, who saw your day and was glad. Keep us from every hatred of our neighbours, and make us your witnesses in a world that does not know you. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what it means to speak and to be heard. In First Corinthians Paul will not let the gift of tongues stand alone in the assembly: prayer must engage the mind, and the whole community must be able to say its “Amen”. In John, the debate in the Temple over who Jesus is reaches its height: he speaks of Abraham’s joy at his day, and answers the question “Who do you claim to be?” with the divine name: “before Abraham was, I am.” One reading protects the people’s understanding of the prayer of the Church; the other shows who is addressed in that prayer.

1 Corinthians 14:13–19 · I will pray with the spirit and with the mind also

Setting.

Chapters 12–14 of First Corinthians treat the gifts of the Spirit in the Corinthian assembly. Paul has placed love above all gifts (chapter 13). In chapter 14 he compares prophecy, which is speaking that others understand, with “tongues”, ecstatic prayer in a language no one in the church could follow unless someone interpreted it.

Paul does not despise tongues; he says he speaks in them more than all the Corinthians (14:18). His question is how the gift serves the community: its purpose is “building up” (14:12, 26).

Structure.

  • v. 13: whoever speaks in a tongue should pray to be able to interpret.
  • vv. 14–15: prayer in a tongue leaves the mind unproductive; Paul will pray and sing with the spirit and with the mind.
  • vv. 16–17: if the blessing is not understood, the outsider cannot say “Amen”.
  • vv. 18–19: Paul’s own gift, and his rule: five words with the mind in church rather than ten thousand in a tongue.

Key words.

  • glōssa, tongue: Here it means a prayer-language, a charism given by the Spirit, not a known foreign language. Paul asks that it be interpreted in the assembly.
  • nous, mind: The reasoning, understanding part of a person. Paul insists that Christian prayer engages the mind as well as the heart and the Spirit.
  • amēn, amen: A Hebrew word meaning “so be it, truly”, by which the community makes a prayer its own. To say it, one must understand what has been prayed.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, one who speaks in a tongue should pray for the power to interpret.” (14:13)

Even a spiritual gift is meant to reach others. Interpretation turns private ecstasy into common prayer.

“I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also;” (14:15)

Paul does not choose between heart and mind. The Spirit works through both.

“how can anyone in the position of an outsider say the “Amen” to your thanksgiving, since the outsider does not know what you are saying?” (14:16)

The “Amen” belongs to the whole people. It is the assembly’s answer, spoken in understanding. This shows how early the Church’s worship was shared: the leader gives thanks, and the people answer.

“nevertheless, in church I would rather speak five words with my mind, in order to instruct others also, than ten thousand words in a tongue.” (14:19)

Five against ten thousand is a deliberate exaggeration. The measure of the assembly’s prayer is whether others are built up.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the faithful should take part in the liturgy fully, consciously and actively, and that the rites should be within their grasp (Sacrosanctum Concilium 14, 21). The Catechism speaks of the “Amen” by which the people make the prayer their own, in the Creed and in the liturgy (CCC 1061–1065). Paul’s principle, that what is done in church must serve the building up of all, is behind the Church’s care that the prayers of the liturgy be heard and understood by the people.

John 8:48–59 · Before Abraham was, I am

Setting.

The dispute in John 8 takes place in the Temple during the Feast of Tabernacles (7:2, 37). Jesus has said “I am the light of the world” (8:12) and has spoken of his Father, of freedom, and of Abraham. The conversation grows sharper; the opponents are the leaders and listeners who will not accept his claim. John’s phrase “the Jews” here means these particular opponents; Jesus himself, Mary, and all the first disciples were Jews, and nothing in the passage can be used against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4).

To call Jesus “a Samaritan” was an insult. Samaritans were despised by many Jews of that time, and the word means something like a heretic or outsider.

Structure.

  • vv. 48–50: the insult “Samaritan and demon”; Jesus answers that he honours the Father, and the Father seeks his glory.
  • v. 51: the promise: whoever keeps his word will never see death.
  • vv. 52–53: the objection: Abraham and the prophets died; who do you claim to be?
  • vv. 54–56: the Father glorifies him; Abraham rejoiced to see his day.
  • vv. 57–59: “Before Abraham was, I am”; they take up stones; Jesus hides and leaves the Temple.

Key words.

  • tērein ton logon, to keep the word: To keep is to guard and to obey, in the heart and in life. In John, keeping Jesus’ word is the sign of love (14:23).
  • egō eimi, I am: Not “I was” and not simply “I exist”. The phrase echoes God’s name given to Moses (Exodus 3:14) and God’s own words in Isaiah (43:10, 13), and in John it is used again and again by Jesus. Here the sense is of timeless being, in contrast to Abraham, who “came to be”.
  • ēran lithous, they picked up stones: The penalty for blasphemy was stoning (Leviticus 24:16). The listeners understood that Jesus was claiming to be equal to God.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus answered, “I do not have a demon; but I honor my Father, and you dishonor me.” (8:49)

Jesus does not return insult. He points to the one thing his whole life is about: the honour of his Father.

“Very truly, I tell you, whoever keeps my word will never see death.” (8:51)

The word of Jesus gives life that bodily death cannot end. The listeners understand “death” only as the death of the body; Jesus speaks of life with God.

“Your ancestor Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day; he saw it and was glad.” (8:56)

Abraham is not a rival to Jesus but a witness to him. The promise made to him is fulfilled in Christ, and his faith looked ahead to it.

“Jesus said to them, “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.”” (8:58)

The verb changes: Abraham “was” (came to be); Jesus simply is. Jesus claims a being that has no beginning.

“So they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple.” (8:59)

The Light leaves the Temple; the same will be said at 12:36. The revelation is real, and so is the refusal of some who heard it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists this saying among the places where Jesus claims divine identity: “Before Abraham was, I am” (CCC 590). It teaches that the name “I AM” revealed to Moses was taken up by Jesus, so that in the New Testament the title “Lord” is given to him (CCC 446; see CCC 211). The Church has always read Jesus’ words about Abraham’s joy as showing that the promises to Abraham are fulfilled in Christ, and that Abraham is the father in faith of all believers (CCC 145–146). With respect for the Jewish people, the Church teaches that what was done in the Passion cannot be blamed on all Jews then or now (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

Questions people ask

Why does the Church say Jesus is claiming to be God when he says “I am”?

Because of the way the words are used. In Greek the plain way to say “I am” would not need the word egō; Jesus uses it deliberately, and the Greek of Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10 has the same “I am”. His opponents react as to blasphemy and pick up stones (Leviticus 24:16). John also uses the saying elsewhere, for example “unless you believe that I am he” (8:24). The Church reads it with the whole Gospel, which from its first verse says that the Word was God (John 1:1).

For study and teaching

  • Paul would rather speak five words that others understand than ten thousand that they cannot follow. How well do I understand, and join in, the prayers of the Holy Qurbana?
  • Jesus does not answer insult with insult but speaks of the honour of his Father. When I am wronged, whose honour do I seek?
