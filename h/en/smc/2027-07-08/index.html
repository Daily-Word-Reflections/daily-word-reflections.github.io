Word for the day

“Jesus said to them, “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.”” John 8:58

Reflection

The Corinthian church was rich in gifts, and the gift they prized most was speaking in tongues, ecstatic prayer in sounds no one could understand. Paul does not despise it; he thanks God that he speaks in tongues more than all of them. But in chapter 14 he asks a pastor's question: what does the gift do for the whole assembly? “One who speaks in a tongue should pray for the power to interpret.” Then he gives one of the clearest sentences in the New Testament on how a Christian prays: “I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also; I will sing praise with the spirit, but I will sing praise with the mind also.” Heart and head are not rivals. Prayer that is only feeling, without understanding, leaves the mind “unproductive”, and prayer that is only thought, without the Spirit, is dry. The Church prays with both.

The second half of the reading takes us into the Church's worship. If you give thanks only in the spirit, “how can anyone in the position of an outsider say the ‘Amen’ to your thanksgiving, since the outsider does not know what you are saying?” The Greek word for thanksgiving here is the word from which we have Eucharist, and the Amen is the people's answer. In the Qurbana the people do not watch; they answer, and the Amen is their signature on the prayer, as if each one said: this is mine as well. For that Amen to be true it must be understood, which is why our Church celebrates the Qurbana in the mother tongue of the people, and why Paul would rather speak “five words with my mind, in order to instruct others also, than ten thousand words in a tongue”. The measure of the gift is how it builds up the Church. This is a good word at the opening of Kaitha, the season of the Church's growth.

In the Gospel the argument in the Temple court has become sharp. Jesus has said, “whoever keeps my word will never see death,” and his hearers answer with an insult: “Are we not right in saying that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?” They are Jews speaking to a Jew, in a family quarrel about who he is, as the prophets had quarrelled with their own people. John's phrase “the Jews” names these hostile voices in Jerusalem; it cannot be turned against the Jewish people, and Christians have often sinned by doing so. The Church condemns every hatred and persecution of the Jews (Nostra Aetate 4). Jesus answers calmly: “I do not seek my own glory; there is one who seeks it and he is the judge.” He does not defend himself; he honours the Father.

Then the argument turns to Abraham. “Your ancestor Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day; he saw it and was glad.” The Jewish tradition spoke of Abraham seeing the days to come, and the Church has read in Isaac's birth and the sacrifice on Moriah a sign of the One to come. His hearers object: “You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?” And Jesus says the sentence on which the whole Gospel of John rests: “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.” He does not say “I was”; he says “I am”. It is the name God gave to Moses at the burning bush, “I am who I am”, now spoken by a man standing in the Temple. They pick up stones, for they understood him. Jesus hides himself and goes out. For us the “I am” is not a danger but a promise: the One who is before Abraham, before the world, is with us in every Qurbana, and whoever keeps his word will not see death for ever.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul tells the Corinthians how to pray: “I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also” (1 Corinthians 14:15). Prayer needs both the heart and the head. When we only feel, we may not know what we are saying. When we only think, our prayer may become dry.

He also speaks about the Amen. When the priest gives thanks to God at the altar, the people answer Amen. That small word means: yes, this is true, and this is my prayer too. But for the people to say Amen, they must understand. Our Qurbana is celebrated in our own language so that every Amen may be real.

Paul says that in church he would rather speak five words that teach others than ten thousand that no one can follow (1 Corinthians 14:19). The measure of everything in the Church is whether it builds up. This is a good thought at the start of Kaitha, the season of growth.

In the Gospel Jesus is in the Temple, and the argument is hard. Some people insult him. Jesus does not insult them. He says: “I honor my Father.” Then he says something wonderful: “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58). He uses the name that God told Moses at the burning bush. He is God's Son, and he is God.

They picked up stones. But we pick up our Amen. Let us say it this week with understanding, at the Qurbana and in every prayer, and keep his word. Amen.

For children

Children, do you know what Amen means? It means: yes, this is true, and I agree! When the priest prays at Holy Qurbana, we all say Amen. Say it with your heart, not only with your mouth! And Jesus told us a great secret: he was there even before Abraham, long, long ago. Jesus is God's Son, and he is always with us.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are before Abraham and before all ages, and you are with us now. Teach us to pray with the Spirit and with the mind, so that our Amen may be true. Let every word of thanksgiving at the altar be understood and answered by the whole assembly, and let every gift you give us build up your Church. Help us to keep your word, and give us the joy of Abraham, who saw your day and was glad. Keep us from every hatred of our neighbours, and make us your witnesses in a world that does not know you. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.
