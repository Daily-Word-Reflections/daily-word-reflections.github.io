Word for the day

“Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven;” Luke 6:37

Reflection

Peter's second letter closes with a word about time. Christians were asking why the Lord delayed his coming. Peter answers: “regard the patience of our Lord as salvation.” Every day God waits is a day of mercy, given so that more may repent. He warns that some twist the hard things in Paul's letters “to their own destruction”, and he ends with a simple programme: “grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.” In the Gospel Jesus asks us to treat others as God treats us: “Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven; give, and it will be given to you. A good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over, will be put into your lap.” Then comes the picture that makes us smile and then makes us blush: “Why do you see the speck in your neighbor's eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?” The patience God shows us is the measure of the patience we owe to others. Only the one who has first removed his own log can help a brother with his speck.

Today the Church honours St John de Britto, a Portuguese Jesuit martyred in Tamil Nadu. Born in Lisbon in 1647 and brought up at the royal court, he left everything to serve in the Madurai mission. There he lived as an Indian ascetic, a pandaraswami, in saffron dress, eating simple vegetarian food, speaking Tamil, so that the people knew him as Arul Anandar. He lived Paul's words in the saint's readings: “I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some.” He was arrested, tortured and expelled, went back to Portugal, refused the comfortable life offered to him there, and returned to the Marava country. When a local prince, Thadiya Thevar, was baptized and kept only one wife as the Gospel requires, the family of a dismissed wife complained to the ruler, and the missionary was condemned. He was beheaded at Oriyur on 4 February 1693, and Pope Pius XII canonized him in 1947. He had heard the Lord's warning: “If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me before it hated you.” He judged no one; he gave himself, good measure, running over.

Full homily

Dear friends, Peter tells us: regard the patience of our Lord as salvation (2 Peter 3:15). Every day that God waits is a day of mercy, given so that we may grow in the grace and knowledge of Christ.

Jesus asks us to show others the same patience: do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven (Luke 6:37). The measure we give is the measure we get back.

Then he gives us a picture: someone with a log in his own eye trying to take a speck out of his neighbour's eye. First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly.

St John de Britto left the royal court of Portugal to live like a poor Tamil ascetic. He became all things to all people, and in 1693 he gave his life at Oriyur. May we, too, judge less, love more, and give ourselves in good measure. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a funny story about a man with a big log in his eye who wanted to take a tiny speck from his friend's eye! Let us look at our own faults first, and be kind to others.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you are patient with us, not wanting any to perish. Free us from judging and condemning, teach us to forgive and to give in good measure, take the log out of our own eyes, and through the prayers of St John de Britto make us all things to all people, so that we may win some for you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask us to look at time and at other people as God does. Second Peter answers those who complain that the Lord is slow: his delay is mercy, “the patience of our Lord as salvation”. In the Gospel Jesus asks us to give others the same patience: do not judge, forgive, give, and look first at the log in your own eye. The Church also remembers St John de Britto, who gave his life on Indian soil.

2 Peter 3:14–18 · Regard the patience of our Lord as salvation

Setting.

These are the last lines of the letter. It bears the name of Simon Peter (1:1). Many scholars think that it is among the latest writings of the New Testament, composed by someone in Peter’s tradition, who speaks in his name; others hold to more direct links with the apostle. The Church receives it as Scripture.

The letter answers scoffers who were asking, “Where is the promise of his coming?” (3:4). Its reply is that God’s time is not ours, and that he is patient, “not wanting any to perish, but all to come to repentance” (3:9). Our reading draws the conclusion for daily life.

Structure.

  • v. 14: while you wait, strive to be found at peace.
  • v. 15a: the delay is salvation.
  • vv. 15b–16: Paul says the same, and some twist his letters.
  • v. 17: a warning.
  • v. 18: grow; and a doxology to Christ.

Key words.

  • makrothymia, patience: Literally “long-temper”, the opposite of being quick to anger. The Greek Old Testament uses related words for God’s patience with his people (Exodus 34:6). Every day that the end does not come is a day of mercy.
  • dysnoēta, hard to understand: Used only here in the New Testament. A writer of the New Testament admits that Paul can be difficult. This should comfort every reader who has struggled with Romans or Galatians.
  • graphai, scriptures: “As they do the other scriptures”: this is an early witness that Paul’s letters were being placed beside the sacred writings. It is one step on the road by which the Church came to discern the canon (CCC 120).

Verse by verse.

“and regard the patience of our Lord as salvation.” (3:15)

We would like the wrong put right at once. The letter reads the delay another way: the time we complain about is also the time in which we ourselves have room to repent.

“There are some things in them hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other scriptures.” (3:16)

Difficult is not the same as closed. The warning is against bending the text to suit oneself. This is why the Church reads Scripture within the living Tradition (Dei Verbum 10, 12).

“But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.” (3:18)

The letter ends with a command that keeps the reader moving. The opposite of being carried away (v. 17) is growing. Grace and knowledge go together.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this letter’s words about God’s patience when it teaches that God predestines no one to hell, and that the Church implores the mercy of God, who does not want “any to perish, but all to come to repentance” (CCC 1037, citing 2 Peter 3:9). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the task of authentically interpreting the word of God has been entrusted to the living teaching office of the Church, which serves the word and does not stand above it (Dei Verbum 10; CCC 85–86).

Luke 6:37–42 · Do not judge, and you will not be judged

Setting.

This is part of Luke’s Sermon on the Plain (6:17–49), which has much in common with Matthew’s Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has just taught love of enemies, ending with the words, “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (6:36). Today’s reading shows what that mercy looks like when we deal with those around us.

Structure.

  • vv. 37–38: four commands and the image of the measure.
  • vv. 39–40: the blind guide; a disciple and his teacher.
  • vv. 41–42: the speck and the log.

Key words.

  • apolyete, forgive: The usual meaning of this verb is to release or let go. To forgive is to stop holding someone in our debt.
  • kolpos, lap: The fold of a long garment above the belt, used like a large pocket. The picture is of a grain seller who presses down and shakes the measure so that it holds as much as possible, and then lets it spill over. “Will be put” is literally “they will give”, a Semitic way of speaking that here most likely means God.
  • karphos / dokos, speck / log: A chip of wood or a bit of straw, and a beam that holds up a roof. A carpenter from Nazareth would have known both. The exaggeration is deliberate and a little comic.

Verse by verse.

“Do not judge, and you will not be judged; do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven;” (6:37)

Two prohibitions, then two positive commands: “Forgive” and, in v. 38, “give”. The passive verbs quietly name God as the one who judges and forgives.

“for the measure you give will be the measure you get back.” (6:38)

We set the scale ourselves. God’s generosity far outruns ours, as the overflowing lap shows, but a closed hand cannot receive.

“Why do you see the speck in your neighbor’s eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?” (6:41)

The fault of another is easy to see because it is the size of a speck, and my own is hard to see because it is too close to me.

“You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor’s eye.” (6:42)

Jesus does not say the speck should stay. He says “first”. A person who has faced his own sin can help a brother gently. Hypokritēs was the Greek word for a stage actor.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism warns against rash judgment, which assumes a neighbor’s moral fault without sufficient foundation (CCC 2477), and quotes St Ignatius of Loyola: every good Christian should be more ready to give a favorable interpretation to another’s statement than to condemn it (CCC 2478). St Augustine sees in the speck and the log the difference between anger, which can pass, and hatred, which has grown old in the heart (in substance).

Questions people ask

If I must not judge, may I never say that something is wrong?

Jesus is not forbidding us to tell right from wrong; he expects the speck to be removed. The Catechism gives the distinction: we can judge that an act is in itself gravely wrong, but we must entrust the judgment of persons to the justice and mercy of God (CCC 1861). We can name a sin without passing sentence on a soul.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St John de Britto (1647–1693), born in Lisbon and brought up at the royal court, gave up that life to serve as a Jesuit in the Madurai mission. He lived as a simple Indian ascetic, a pandaraswami, spoke Tamil, and was known to the people as Arul Anandar. Expelled once and invited to stay in Portugal, he came back. He was beheaded at Oriyur in the Marava country on 4 February 1693, and Pope Pius XII canonized him in 1947. The Madurai mission lies not far from our own land. His life gives flesh to today’s Gospel: he gave a good measure, pressed down and running over. Those who suffer for the faith in many lands today are in our prayer, with all who have wronged them.

For study and teaching

  • When has God’s “delay” in my life turned out to be mercy, for me or for someone else?
  • Whom do I judge most quickly? What is the log in my own eye that I would rather not look at?
