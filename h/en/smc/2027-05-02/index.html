Word for the day

“that they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” John 17:21

Reflection

The Sixth Sunday of Resurrection stands at a threshold. On Thursday the Church will celebrate the Ascension of the Lord, and the readings of this Sunday already have the sound of a farewell and a sending. The first reading is a cry of joy from the prophet who comforted Israel in exile: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, who brings good news, who announces salvation, who says to Zion, ‘Your God reigns.’” The watchmen on the ruined walls of Jerusalem see the Lord coming home, and the ruins themselves are told to sing: “Break forth together into singing, you ruins of Jerusalem; for the Lord has comforted his people, he has redeemed Jerusalem.” Then a promise that reaches far beyond one city: “The Lord has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations; and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God.” Paul hears in this text the vocation of every preacher of the Gospel: “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!” (Romans 10:15). The feet of the apostles were dusty and tired; one pair of them, the Church in India believes, walked the coast of Malabar. On this Sunday we give thanks for every messenger who brought the good news to us, and we remember that our own feet are meant for the same road.

The Acts of the Apostles shows how far that road would go. Peter is praying on a rooftop at Joppa around noon. He is hungry, and in a trance he sees “the heaven opened and something like a large sheet coming down, being lowered to the ground by its four corners”, full of animals that the Law of Moses forbade him to eat. A voice says, “Get up, Peter; kill and eat.” Peter answers as a faithful Jew who has kept the commandments all his life: “By no means, Lord; for I have never eaten anything that is profane or unclean.” And the voice replies, “What God has made clean, you must not call profane.” Three times this happens. Peter does not yet understand, but at that very moment messengers from a Roman centurion named Cornelius are at the gate. The vision is not mainly about food; it is about people. Soon Peter will stand in a Gentile house and say, “I truly understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him” (Acts 10:34–35). Notice how gently God leads his apostle. Peter's hesitation came from love of God's Law, not from hatred; God opens his heart step by step. The Church has never ceased to honour the people of Israel, from whom Peter, Mary and Jesus himself came; “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). What the vision opens is the door of that same family to all the nations.

Paul writes to the Ephesians, most of them Gentiles by birth, and asks them to remember what they once were: “without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world.” Then comes the great turning: “But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ. For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” The image comes from the Temple in Jerusalem, where a barrier kept Gentile visitors from the inner courts. In the Cross, Christ creates “in himself one new humanity in place of the two”, reconciling both to God in one body. And Paul draws the conclusion that every baptised Indian Christian can read as his own: “So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.” Our society still knows many dividing walls: of caste, of language, of wealth, of region and politics. Sadly, they have sometimes stood even inside our churches. The Ephesian letter says plainly that in the household of God those walls have already been broken down by the blood of Christ. It is our task not to rebuild them.

The Gospel lets us listen to Jesus praying on the night before he died. After praying for his disciples, he looks beyond them to us: “I ask not only on behalf of these, but also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” The unity Jesus asks for is not a mere agreement or a good organisation; it is a share in the communion of the Father and the Son. And it is missionary: “so that the world may believe.” Divided Christians make the Gospel hard to believe. We feel this pain in Kerala, where the descendants of the Christians of St Thomas now belong to several Churches. The Catholic Church prays and works for the full unity Christ willed, with respect and love for our brothers and sisters of the other Churches (Unitatis Redintegratio 1). The prayer ends with a desire that turns our minds to the Ascension: “Father, I desire that those also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory, which you have given me because you loved me before the foundation of the world.” Where he goes on Thursday, he wants us to follow. Until then, the sign that we belong to him is the love that makes us one: “so that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.”

Full homily

Dear friends, this is the last Sunday before the Ascension. On Thursday we will celebrate the Lord going up to heaven. Today's readings are like a farewell and a sending.

Isaiah sings: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace” (Isaiah 52:7). The apostles were such messengers; Mar Thoma brought the good news to our land. Now our feet are called to carry it.

In Acts, Peter sees a vision and hears: “What God has made clean, you must not call profane” (Acts 10:15). God was teaching him that the Gospel is for every nation. Soon Peter entered the house of a Roman officer, Cornelius, and baptised his family.

Paul tells the Ephesians that Christ “is our peace” and “has broken down the dividing wall” (Ephesians 2:14). In Christ there are no strangers. We too have walls: caste, money, language, old quarrels. Christ has broken them down. Let us not build them again.

In the Gospel Jesus prays for us: “that they may all be one... so that the world may believe that you have sent me” (John 17:21). When Christians are divided, people find it hard to believe. When we love one another, the world sees Jesus.

So this week, let us pray for unity, make peace with one person, and prepare to celebrate the Ascension on Thursday. The Lord wants us to be with him where he is. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus prayed for you! He asked his Father that all his friends would be one big family.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, before your Passion you prayed to the Father that all who believe in you may be one. By your blood you have broken down the walls that divide us and made us members of one household. Make our feet beautiful with the good news of peace; open our hearts as you opened the heart of Peter; heal the divisions among Christians and in our society; and draw us after you to the glory where you are, until the world believes that the Father sent you. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s readings are about walls coming down and a people made one. Isaiah sees a messenger running over the mountains with good news for the ruins of Jerusalem: “Your God reigns.” In Acts, Peter on a rooftop in Joppa is told in a vision, “What God has made clean, you must not call profane,” and the way opens for the first Gentiles to be baptized. Ephesians says that Christ is our peace, who has broken down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile and made them one household of God. In John, on the night before he dies, Jesus prays for all who will believe through the apostles’ word: “that they may all be one.” The words to watch are peace, near and one. The Risen Lord does not gather a crowd of individuals; he builds one body, and he asks his Father that its unity may be a sign to the world.

Isaiah 52:7–12 · How beautiful are the feet of the messenger

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ. Many scholars think they come from a prophet of the exile who stands in the tradition of Isaiah of Jerusalem. The great promise of these chapters is a new exodus: God himself will lead his people home.

Jerusalem lies in ruins, and watchmen stand on what is left of its walls. Today’s poem imagines the moment when a runner appears on the hills with the news that the exile is over. It comes just before the fourth Servant song (52:13–53:12), and the Church has always read the two together.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the messenger on the mountains: peace, good news, salvation, “Your God reigns.”
  • v. 8: the sentinels sing: they see the Lord returning to Zion.
  • vv. 9–10: the ruins are told to sing; the Lord bares his holy arm before the nations.
  • vv. 11–12: the command to go out from Babylon, purified, carrying the vessels of the Lord; not in haste, for the Lord goes before and behind.

Key words.

  • mĕbaśśēr, messenger of good news: One who brings glad tidings. The Greek translation uses the verb euangelizomai, from which comes the word “gospel”. St Paul quotes this verse for the preachers of Christ (Romans 10:15).
  • mālak ’ĕlōhāyik, your God reigns: The heart of the message: not a new king of Judah, but God himself as king. Jesus begins his preaching with the same news: “The kingdom of God has come near” (Mark 1:15).
  • šālôm, peace: Not only the end of war but wholeness, all things set right. The messenger announces peace before he announces salvation; Ephesians today will say that Christ himself is that peace.

Verse by verse.

“How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, who brings good news,” (52:7)

The feet are dusty from the road, yet they are called beautiful because of the message they carry. The Church sees here every preacher of the Gospel, beginning with the apostles.

“for in plain sight they see the return of the Lord to Zion.” (52:8)

Literally “eye to eye”. The watchmen do not hear a rumour; they see. The apostles will later say, “we have seen with our eyes” (1 John 1:1).

“The Lord has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations;” (52:10)

A warrior rolls back his sleeve to act. Isaiah 53:1 asks, “to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?”, and the answer there is the suffering Servant.

“For you shall not go out in haste,” (52:12)

The first exodus was in haste (Exodus 12:11). This one will be in peace, with God as guard before and behind, like the pillar of cloud.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul quotes v. 7 when he asks how anyone will believe unless preachers are sent (Romans 10:15), and the Church has always applied it to the apostles and to all who bring the Gospel. The Catechism explains that the word “Gospel” means good news, the news that God has sent his Son (CCC 422). Read beside today’s Gospel, the messenger’s cry “Your God reigns” is fulfilled when the Risen Lord sends his disciples into the world with the news of his victory.

Acts 10:9–16 · What God has made clean

Setting.

Acts 10–11 is the longest story in Acts, and Luke tells the main event twice: here and in Peter’s report at Jerusalem (11:4–17). It concerns Cornelius, a Roman centurion at Caesarea, a Gentile who feared God, prayed and gave alms. An angel has told him to send for Peter, and his messengers are on the road.

Peter is staying at Joppa, on the coast, in the house of Simon, a tanner (9:43). For Jewish believers, the food laws of Leviticus 11 were part of their faithfulness to the covenant, marking Israel as a people set apart for God. Peter, a faithful Jew, has kept them all his life. The vision is not about lunch alone: it prepares him to enter a Gentile’s house.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: noon, the rooftop, prayer, hunger, a trance.
  • vv. 11–12: the heaven opened; a sheet with every kind of creature.
  • vv. 13–14: “Get up, Peter; kill and eat.” “By no means, Lord.”
  • vv. 15–16: “What God has made clean, you must not call profane.” Three times; the sheet taken up.

Key words.

  • koinos, profane, common: Literally “common”, that is, not set apart for God. Peter uses it for food. He will soon learn to apply the lesson to people: “God has shown me that I should not call anyone profane or unclean” (10:28).
  • ekstasis, trance: A state in which a person is taken out of ordinary awareness to receive a vision. Peter falls into it while praying, as Paul later did in the Temple (22:17).
  • ekatharisen, has made clean: An aorist verb: God has already done it. Peter is not asked to decide what is clean, but to accept what God has decided.

Verse by verse.

“Peter went up on the roof to pray.” (10:9)

Flat roofs were used for rest and prayer. The vision comes to a man at prayer, as Cornelius too received his message while praying (10:30).

“But Peter said, “By no means, Lord; for I have never eaten anything that is profane or unclean.”” (10:14)

Peter calls the voice “Lord” and still refuses. His refusal is not stubbornness but loyalty to what he had always understood as God’s will.

““What God has made clean, you must not call profane.”” (10:15)

The decisive word. Mark’s Gospel already notes that Jesus “declared all foods clean” (Mark 7:19).

“This happened three times,” (10:16)

Three times, as Peter had denied the Lord three times and was three times asked, “Do you love me?” (John 21:15–17). A lesson that cuts so deep must be given more than once.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads this vision as a turning point in the mission: the Gospel crosses the boundary between Jew and Gentile, and the Council of Jerusalem later confirms that Gentile believers need not take on the whole Mosaic law (Acts 15:7–11). The lesson is not that God’s gifts to Israel were bad: the law was holy and given by God (Romans 7:12). It is that in Christ God now gathers all nations into one people without asking them first to become Jews. The Catechism speaks of the Church as the new People of God, made up of Jews and Gentiles, called together in Christ (CCC 781), and the Second Vatican Council teaches that God’s call to the Jewish people is irrevocable (Nostra Aetate 4).

Ephesians 2:11–22 · He is our peace

Setting.

Ephesians is written in the name of Paul, from prison (3:1; 4:1). Many scholars think it was composed by a disciple of Paul who gathered his teaching into a great letter for several Churches; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. The Church reads it as part of the apostolic witness. Its theme is the Church as the one body of Christ.

The readers are Gentile Christians. In the first half of chapter 2 the letter has told them that they were dead in sin and have been made alive by grace (2:1–10). Now it reminds them what they were in relation to Israel: outsiders to the covenants. Many scholars think the “dividing wall” recalls the barrier in the Jerusalem Temple beyond which Gentiles could not go; an inscription from it, warning foreigners not to pass, has been found.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: remember what you were: far from Christ, outside Israel, without hope.
  • v. 13: but now, brought near by the blood of Christ.
  • vv. 14–16: he is our peace: the wall broken down, one new humanity, one body reconciled through the Cross.
  • vv. 17–18: peace proclaimed to far and near; access in one Spirit to the Father.
  • vv. 19–22: no longer strangers: fellow citizens, household, a temple built on the apostles and prophets with Christ as cornerstone.

Key words.

  • mesotoichon, dividing wall: The wall between two groups. The letter names it at once: “the hostility between us”. Christ has not destroyed Israel; he has destroyed the hostility.
  • prosagōgē, access: The right of entry, used of being brought into the presence of a king. Through Christ both Jew and Gentile have access to the Father in one Spirit: a verse in which Father, Son and Spirit are named together.
  • akrogōniaios, cornerstone: The stone at the corner, or perhaps the keystone, which holds the whole building in line. Christ is the stone the builders rejected (Psalm 118:22).

Verse by verse.

“being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise,” (2:12)

The Gentiles’ poverty is described by what Israel had: citizenship, covenants, hope. The promises belong first to Israel; the Gentiles are brought into them.

“But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” (2:13)

“Far” and “near” are the words of Isaiah 57:19, which v. 17 will quote. The blood of the Cross closes the distance.

“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” (2:14)

Not “he brings peace” but “he is our peace”. Peace is a person.

“He has abolished the law with its commandments and ordinances,” (2:15)

This must be read with care. Paul elsewhere says the law is holy (Romans 7:12) and that faith upholds the law (Romans 3:31). What is ended is the law as a wall that kept the nations apart; Gentiles enter God’s people through Christ, not by first keeping the commandments that marked Israel off.

“So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are citizens with the saints and also members of the household of God,” (2:19)

The Gentiles do not form a second people beside Israel. They are made fellow citizens with the saints, joined to the people God had first chosen.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes this passage where it speaks of the Jewish people: the Church believes that by his Cross Christ, our peace, reconciled Jews and Gentiles, making both one in himself (Nostra Aetate 4). The same document says that the Church draws sustenance from the root of that good olive tree onto which the wild branches of the Gentiles have been grafted (Romans 11:17–24). The Catechism uses this passage for the Church as the temple of God built on the apostles, with Christ as cornerstone (CCC 756). The Church is not built in place of Israel but on Israel’s promises, opened to every nation.

John 17:20–26 · That they may all be one

Setting.

John 17 is the prayer of Jesus at the end of the Last Supper, often called his “priestly prayer”. He has washed the disciples’ feet, given the new commandment, and spoken of his going to the Father. Now he lifts his eyes to heaven and prays: first for his own glorification (17:1–5), then for the disciples (17:6–19), and in today’s verses for all who will believe through their word.

In other words, Jesus prays here for us. Every believer since the apostles is included in “those who will believe in me through their word”. Here Jesus says plainly that he is praying for believers not yet born.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: for all future believers: that they may be one, as Father and Son are one, so that the world may believe.
  • vv. 22–23: the glory given, so that they may be completely one, so that the world may know.
  • v. 24: “I desire that those also … may be with me where I am, to see my glory.”
  • vv. 25–26: the world has not known the Father; Jesus has made his name known, so that the Father’s love may be in them.

Key words.

  • hina ōsin hen, that they may be one: “One” is neuter in Greek: not one person, but one thing, one communion. The model is the unity of the Father and the Son, and the means is being “in us”.
  • doxa, glory: In John, glory is the radiance of God’s love, shown above all on the Cross. Jesus has given this glory to his disciples: the share in his life that makes them one.
  • katabolē kosmou, foundation of the world: Before anything was made, the Father loved the Son. The disciples are drawn into a love older than creation.

Verse by verse.

““I ask not only on behalf of these, but also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word,” (17:20)

Faith comes through the apostles’ word. The Church is apostolic: we believe because they preached.

“that they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” (17:21)

Unity is not only a good for the Church; it is a witness to the world. Division among Christians makes it harder for the world to believe.

“The glory that you have given me I have given them, so that they may be one, as we are one,” (17:22)

The unity of Christians is not achieved by agreement alone. It is a gift of the glory of Christ, received in the Spirit.

“Father, I desire that those also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory,” (17:24)

The prayer looks beyond this life. The final goal of unity is to be with Christ and see his glory.

“so that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.”” (17:26)

The last words of the prayer. Its goal is love: the Father’s love for the Son dwelling in the disciples.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council begins its decree on ecumenism by citing this prayer and teaches that division among Christians openly contradicts the will of Christ and is a scandal to the world (Unitatis Redintegratio 1–2). St John Paul II took the title of his encyclical on ecumenism, Ut Unum Sint (1995), from v. 21. The Catechism teaches that Christ always gives his Church the gift of unity, but the Church must always pray and work to maintain, reinforce and perfect it (CCC 820). St Cyril of Alexandria, commenting on this chapter, teaches that believers are made one body with Christ and with one another through sharing in his holy flesh in the Eucharist (in substance).

Questions people ask

Does Ephesians 2:15 mean that the law of Moses was bad?

No. The law was given by God and is “holy and just and good” (Romans 7:12). The letter means that the law, as a wall separating Jews from Gentiles, no longer decides who belongs to God’s people. In Christ the Gentiles are brought near without first becoming Jews. The Church teaches that God’s gifts and call to the Jewish people are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; CCC 839).

Do Catholics still keep food laws?

The Church does not bind Christians to the food laws of Leviticus, following Acts 10 and the Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15). Christians do keep fasts and abstinence, not because foods are unclean, but as acts of penance and love. All food is a gift of God to be received with thanksgiving (1 Timothy 4:4).

If Jesus prayed for unity, why are Christians still divided?

Jesus’ prayer has not failed: the Church is one in Christ, and the Catholic Church believes this unity subsists in her (CCC 820). But Christians have sinned against unity, on many sides, over many centuries (CCC 817). The Council teaches that there can be no true ecumenism without conversion of heart, and it calls prayer for unity the soul of the whole ecumenical movement (Unitatis Redintegratio 7–8).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Sixth Sunday of Resurrection falls in the last stretch of the season before the Ascension, and the Church’s prayer turns toward the gift of the Spirit who makes the Church one. For the St Thomas Christians of India this Gospel has a special weight. The one community that received the faith from Mar Thoma is now found in several Churches, Catholic and Orthodox and others. The Lord’s prayer “that they may all be one” makes our reunion a matter of prayer, patience and love, not of blame.

In the Holy Qurbana the priest prays for the peace and unity of the Church, and as the anaphora begins the people give one another the peace. That sign is a small answer to today’s readings: those who were far and near give peace to one another before they approach the one altar of the Lord.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah calls the messenger’s dusty feet beautiful. Who brought the Gospel to me, and how can I carry it to someone else this week?
  • Peter needed three times to hear “What God has made clean, you must not call profane.” Whom do I treat as unclean or beneath me?
  • Ephesians says Christ is our peace, who broke down the dividing wall. What wall in my family or parish needs to come down?
  • Jesus prayed for us “that they may all be one.” What can I do, in prayer and in action, for unity among Christians?
