Word for the day

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 14:11

Reflection

After yesterday's Dukhrana of Mar Thoma, our Church turns today to all twelve. “He called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles”, Luke says, after a night on the mountain “in prayer to God”. Their names are given in the Gospels in four lists, and the first is this: “These are the names of the twelve apostles: first, Simon, also known as Peter, and his brother Andrew; James son of Zebedee, and his brother John; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew the tax collector; James son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus; Simon the Cananaean, and Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” Mark gives the purpose: “And he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message.” First to be with him, then to be sent. The number is deliberate: twelve, like the tribes of Israel, because Jesus was gathering the whole people of God anew. “You who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.” After the betrayal of Judas, the apostles prayed and “the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles”: the Twelve is a college that the Church keeps whole (CCC 880). In the last book of the Bible the holy city has “twelve foundations, and on them are the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb”. Today we keep the feast of all the names, including those about whom the Gospels tell us almost nothing; they too are foundation stones.

Today also opens the Season of Kaitha, the summer of the Church's year. After the apostles have sown, the Church looks at the growth and the harvest. The first reading chooses a very fitting beginning: a prophet builds an altar. In the days of King Ahab, Israel was “limping with two different opinions”, between the Lord and Baal, and the altar of the Lord on Mount Carmel “had been thrown down”. “Then Elijah said to all the people, ‘Come closer to me’; and all the people came closer to him. First he repaired the altar of the Lord that had been thrown down; Elijah took twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob, to whom the word of the Lord came, saying, ‘Israel shall be your name’; with the stones he built an altar in the name of the Lord.” Twelve stones, one altar: the divided people is gathered again, and the Lord's name is the name above the whole. Elijah has the sacrifice soaked with water three times, so that whatever follows can only be the Lord's doing, and his prayer is short and humble: “Answer me, O Lord, answer me, so that this people may know that you, O Lord, are God, and that you have turned their hearts back.” Then “the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the burnt offering, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and even licked up the water that was in the trench.” The people fall on their faces: “The Lord indeed is God; the Lord indeed is God.” The Church has always seen in the twelve stones the twelve apostles, on whom the new altar of the Church is built; and in the fire from heaven the fire of Pentecost, which made the same twelve a witness.

The Acts reading shows what the fire did. “Now many signs and wonders were done among the people through the apostles. And they were all together in Solomon’s Portico.” They stayed in the Temple, as faithful sons of Israel; the Church did not begin by leaving the house of the Lord but by praying in it. The people held them in high esteem, and “more than ever believers were added to the Lord, great numbers of both men and women, so that they even carried out the sick into the streets, and laid them on cots and mats, in order that Peter’s shadow might fall on some of them as he came by. A great number of people would also gather from the towns around Jerusalem, bringing the sick and those tormented by unclean spirits, and they were all cured.” This is harvest. Then the high priest and the party of the Sadducees, “being filled with jealousy”, arrested the apostles and put them in the public prison. We see here one group of leaders, the high priest and his party, and not the Jewish people, who held the apostles in high esteem. “But during the night an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, brought them out, and said, ‘Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life.’” An open prison door and a command to go back to the very place of danger: the Church is sent, again and again, to speak the whole message and not only the parts that are welcome.

Paul, writing to Corinth, shows what can happen to a Church that forgets the one Lord. “God is faithful; by him you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord. Now I appeal to you, brothers and sisters, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you be in agreement and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same purpose.” The Corinthians were each saying, “I belong to Paul,” or “I belong to Apollos,” or “I belong to Cephas,” or “I belong to Christ.” The apostles, great as they were, are never meant to be the names of parties. Paul answers with three questions: “Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” The Twelve are many, and the Lord is one. This is also a prayer for our own day: for the unity of our parishes and our Church, and for the unity of all who are baptised into Christ, with the Orthodox, the Mar Thoma and the Malankara Churches, who also honour the apostles and share much of our heritage. “There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to the one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (Ephesians 4:4–5; CCC 813).

The Gospel is set at a meal. Jesus is at table in the house of a leading Pharisee, and he sees how the guests choose the places of honour. He tells them a parable: “When you are invited by someone to a wedding banquet, do not sit down at the place of honor… But when you are invited, go and sit down at the lowest place, so that when your host comes, he may say to you, ‘Friend, move up higher’; then you will be honored in the presence of all who sit at the table with you.” The principle is the one that should rule the Church: “For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” The apostles themselves had to learn it. At the Last Supper “a dispute also arose among them as to which one of them was to be regarded as the greatest,” and Jesus answered, “the greatest among you must become like the youngest, and the leader like one who serves… I am among you as one who serves.” Then he turns to the host: “When you give a luncheon or a dinner, do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, in case they may invite you in return… But when you give a banquet, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind. And you will be blessed, because they cannot repay you, for you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.” In Kerala this is the parish feast, with its nercha meal: let the first to be served and the best seat be for the one who cannot give anything back. In this season of the harvest the rice-ears that are full bow their heads, and the empty ones stand straight. The Twelve, humbled by their failure and lifted by the Spirit, are the ears that bowed.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the First Sunday of Kaitha, and our Church celebrates the Feast of the Twelve Apostles. Yesterday we remembered Mar Thoma. Today we remember all the twelve friends of Jesus. “He called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles” (Luke 6:13).

In the first reading the prophet Elijah builds an altar. He takes twelve stones, one for each tribe of Israel, and the people are one again before the Lord. Then the fire falls from heaven and the people say, “The Lord indeed is God” (1 Kings 18:39). The Church has always seen in those twelve stones the twelve apostles, on whom the altar of our Church is built, and in the fire the fire of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost.

In the Acts the apostles are very busy and very happy. Many people are healed, and more and more believe. The authorities arrest them, but during the night an angel opens the prison and says, “Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life” (Acts 5:20). The Church is sent to say all of the message, not just the parts that are pleasant to hear.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians that some say, “I belong to Paul”, and others “I belong to Apollos” or “to Cephas”. He asks, “Has Christ been divided?” (1 Corinthians 1:13). The Twelve are many, but Christ is one. Let us pray for unity in our families and parishes, and among all who are baptised in Christ.

In the Gospel Jesus is a guest at a meal and sees people choosing the best seats. He says: sit in the lowest place, “for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted” (Luke 14:11). And invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind, who cannot pay you back. The apostles themselves once argued about who was the greatest, and Jesus taught them to serve. In the Kaitha season the full ears of rice bow down. Let us bow, too: let us take the last seat, serve first the one who cannot repay us, and so bring fruit that will last. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember Jesus' twelve special friends, the apostles. Jesus asked them first to be with him, and then to go and tell everyone about God. They were ordinary people: fishermen, farmers and a tax collector.

Jesus also teaches us today: do not rush for the best seat. Take the last seat and let your friend sit first! And when you have a party, invite someone who is alone. That is how the apostles learned to love, and that is how we can too!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you prayed all night and chose twelve to be with you and to be sent. We thank you for Peter and Andrew, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew, James and Thaddaeus, Simon and Matthias. As you built an altar of twelve stones in the days of Elijah, build your Church on the foundation of the apostles, with yourself as cornerstone. Send the fire of your Spirit on us at the opening of this season; keep us one in mind and purpose; teach us to take the lowest place, to invite the poor, and to bring a rich harvest for your kingdom. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the first Sunday of Kaitha, the summer season, the Church keeps the feast of the Twelve Apostles, and the four readings ask what it means to belong to a people that God has gathered and sent. In the First Book of Kings, Elijah takes twelve stones, one for each tribe of Israel, and builds the Lord’s altar again; when the fire falls, the people cry, “The Lord indeed is God”. In Acts, signs and wonders are done through the apostles, the sick are carried into the streets, the high priest’s party seizes them, and an angel opens the prison and sends them back to the temple to tell “the whole message about this life”. In First Corinthians, Paul begs a divided community to be united, for Christ is not divided. And in Luke, Jesus teaches at a dinner the way of the kingdom: take the lowest place, and invite those who cannot repay.

The twelve stones and the Twelve Apostles are not an accident of numbers. God builds a people around a new foundation, and the people are marked by unity, by humility and by an open table. The feast asks us to look at the Church as she is in this world, a people sent, often divided, always dependent on the Lord, and still the place where his signs are seen.

1 Kings 18:30–39 · Elijah builds an altar of twelve stones

Setting.

Elijah appears in the reign of King Ahab and his wife Jezebel, who have promoted the worship of Baal, the Canaanite storm god, in Israel. There has been a long drought, and the question on Mount Carmel is who gives rain and life: the Lord or Baal. The prophets of Baal have called on their god from morning until evening with no answer (18:26–29). Now Elijah calls the people, “Come closer to me”, and begins by repairing an altar of the Lord that had been thrown down.

Everything about this passage speaks of restoration. The old altar is repaired, the twelve tribes are remembered even though the kingdom is divided into two, and the prayer begins with the God of Abraham, Isaac and Israel. Elijah does not claim power for himself: he asks the Lord to “let it be known this day that you are God in Israel”.

Structure.

  • vv. 30–32: Elijah repairs the altar of the Lord and builds it of twelve stones, with a trench around it.
  • vv. 33–35: the wood, the bull, and the water poured out three times, so that the trench is filled.
  • vv. 36–37: Elijah’s prayer: let it be known that you are God, and that you have turned their hearts back.
  • vv. 38–39: the fire of the Lord falls and consumes everything; the people fall on their faces and confess the Lord.

Key words.

  • ’Ēliyyāhû, Elijah, “my God is the Lord”: The prophet’s name is a confession: ’Ēl-i-Yah-u, “my God is Yahweh”. The whole scene on Carmel is the name acted out, and the people’s cry at the end, “The Lord indeed is God”, uses the same words as his name.
  • ‘ōlâ, burnt offering: The offering that is wholly consumed by fire and rises to God. The Greek word holokauston comes from it. It is a sacrifice of complete giving, in which nothing is kept back.
  • ‘ănēnî, answer me: A double plea in verse 37: “Answer me, O Lord, answer me”. Elijah’s prayer is brief and direct. He asks not for his own honour but for the hearts of the people to be turned back.

Verse by verse.

“Elijah took twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob, to whom the word of the Lord came, saying, “Israel shall be your name”” (18:31)

The kingdom was split at the time (1 Kings 12), and ten tribes belonged to the northern kingdom where Elijah lives, but his altar has twelve stones. In the eyes of God, Israel is still one. The verse echoes Genesis 35:10, where Jacob receives the name Israel.

“Fill four jars with water and pour it on the burnt offering and on the wood.” (18:33)

The water is poured out three times (verses 33–35), and so the trench is full. In a land of drought, water was precious; it is given up to make the miracle impossible to doubt. No one will say that the fire was a spark that happened to catch.

“Answer me, O Lord, answer me, so that this people may know that you, O Lord, are God, and that you have turned their hearts back.” (18:37)

The goal of the miracle is conversion: that the people know the Lord and have their hearts turned back. A sign in the Bible is never for show but for faith.

“The Lord indeed is God; the Lord indeed is God.” (18:39)

The people’s answer is a profession of faith. The story shows them first as hesitating (“How long will you go limping with two different opinions?”, 18:21) and then on their faces, confessing. The prophet’s task is to bring them to this point.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Elijah as the prophet of prayer and of zeal for the Lord. The Catechism, speaking of the prophets who taught Israel to pray, says that Elijah is the “father of the prophets” and teaches that his prayer on Carmel is the prayer of faith, which asks for the heart of the people to be converted (CCC 2581–2584, in substance). The Gospels show him again at the Transfiguration with Moses, beside Jesus (Matthew 17:3), and Jesus applies the promise of Elijah’s return to John the Baptist (Matthew 11:14). Because of the twelve stones, many readers have seen in this passage a sign of the Twelve whom Jesus chose, on whom the Church’s altar is built.

Acts 5:12–20 · Many signs and wonders through the apostles

Setting.

The verses are one of the summaries of the life of the first community in Jerusalem. They come soon after the death of Ananias and Sapphira (5:1–11), who had lied to the Holy Spirit about their gift to the community, and which brought “great fear” on the whole Church. The apostles continue their ministry in the temple, in Solomon’s Portico, a covered walk along the east side of the temple court, where Jesus himself had walked (John 10:23).

Then comes a new open conflict (the apostles had already been arrested once, in 4:1–22): the high priest and the party of the Sadducees, filled with jealousy, arrest the apostles. The text is exact about who acts: it is the high priest and “all who were with him”, and the people continue to hold the apostles in high esteem. The Acts of the Apostles never blames the Jewish people as a whole; the first believers were themselves Jews, and they continued to worship at the temple.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: signs and wonders; the believers gather in Solomon’s Portico; the others hold back, but the people hold them in esteem.
  • vv. 14–16: more and more believers; the sick carried out so that Peter’s shadow might fall on them; crowds from the towns around; all are cured.
  • vv. 17–18: the high priest and the Sadducees, filled with jealousy, arrest the apostles.
  • vv. 19–20: an angel opens the prison at night: “Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life”.

Key words.

  • sēmeia kai terata, signs and wonders: A pair of words from the Greek Old Testament, used of the Exodus (Deuteronomy 34:11). The signs point to something beyond themselves; the wonders astonish. Luke uses them to show that the God of the Exodus is acting again.
  • homothymadon, with one accord: A favourite word of Luke’s: the first community was “of one mind” (1:14; 2:46; 4:24; 5:12). It means more than agreement: it is an inner unity of heart given by the Spirit.
  • zēlos, jealousy, zeal: The word can mean either, and here it means envy: the leaders saw the apostles’ growing influence and could not bear it. The word shows how good things can be hated when we are jealous of them.

Verse by verse.

“Now many signs and wonders were done among the people through the apostles. And they were all together in Solomon’s Portico.” (5:12)

The apostles do not work wonders by their own power; the signs are “done” through them. The community meets in a public place, in the temple area. The Church is not hidden.

“so that they even carried out the sick into the streets, and laid them on cots and mats, in order that Peter’s shadow might fall on some of them as he came by.” (5:15)

Luke reports what the people believed and did; he does not say that the shadow healed, but that all were cured (verse 16). The Church understands that God works through the persons and things that he chooses (compare Acts 19:11–12).

“Then the high priest took action; he and all who were with him (that is, the sect of the Sadducees), being filled with jealousy” (5:17)

Luke names the group: the high priest and the party of the Sadducees. This is one group inside the Jewish people of the time, and many other Jews, such as the Pharisee Gamaliel in the next verses, take a different view (5:34–39).

“Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life.” (5:20)

The angel does not tell them to flee. The released prisoners go back to the same temple, to the same people, to give “the whole message”. The Gospel is not a private matter but a public word. “This life” means the new life that has come through the risen Lord.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has seen in the signs done by the apostles the proof that Christ continues his work in the Church. The Catechism teaches that the apostles’ gifts of healing belong to the life of the Church (CCC 1506–1509, in substance), and that popular devotion, such as reverence for relics, finds a place in this faith (CCC 1674, in substance). The text also shows the pattern of the apostolic Church: they pray and teach together in public, and when they are shut in prison, the Lord sends them out again. The apostles go back into the very place where they were arrested, and the Church sees in this the courage that the Spirit gives.

1 Corinthians 1:9–16 · Be united in the same mind and the same purpose

Setting.

First Corinthians was written by Paul from Ephesus around the year 55, to a lively and gifted community in a port city, which had also begun to split into parties. After the greeting and thanksgiving (1:1–9) he comes to the first and heaviest matter, which is the quarrels in the community. Verses 9 to 16 introduce his appeal.

In the Corinth of Paul’s day it was usual to attach oneself to a famous teacher. The Christians of Corinth had done the same: some followed Paul, who had founded the community; some Apollos, a learned preacher from Alexandria who had come after him (Acts 18:24–28); some Cephas, that is Peter; and some, perhaps with a sense of being superior, said “I belong to Christ”. Paul answers with three rapid questions that lead to the heart of the matter.

Structure.

  • v. 9: God is faithful; he has called you into the fellowship of his Son.
  • v. 10: the appeal: all of you agree, with no divisions, united in mind and purpose.
  • vv. 11–12: the report from Chloe’s people: four parties, Paul, Apollos, Cephas, Christ.
  • v. 13: three questions: Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptized in Paul’s name?
  • vv. 14–16: Paul is glad that he baptised few, so that no one can claim baptism in his name.

Key words.

  • koinōnia, fellowship, sharing: A rich word for sharing in a common life. Here it is a participation in the Son of God himself, and so it is the root of the unity of the Church. The same word is used of the Eucharist in 10:16.
  • schismata, divisions, rifts: From a verb meaning to tear. The word is used in Matthew 9:16 for the tear in a garment. Paul’s concern is that the seamless body of Christ be torn.
  • katērtismenoi, united, joined, mended: A verb used of mending nets (Mark 1:19) and, more widely, of putting right what is broken. It is Paul’s word for what unity needs: a repair and a fitting together.

Verse by verse.

“God is faithful; by him you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord.” (1:9)

Paul begins with God’s faithfulness before he speaks of human failures. The community did not create itself: it was called, and the one who called remains faithful.

“that all of you be in agreement and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same purpose” (1:10)

The appeal is made “by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ”, the name by which they were baptised. Unity does not mean uniformity: the Corinthians have many gifts (chapters 12 to 14). It means one heart and one mind in the faith.

“Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” (1:13)

The three questions each expect the answer No. Christ is one, and he alone was crucified. The name in which we were baptised is the only name that can unite us. Paul puts himself last in the line of those who might claim the Corinthians.

“I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius” (1:14)

Paul does not despise baptism. Verse 17 shows that his emphasis is on preaching, and elsewhere he teaches the greatness of baptism (Romans 6:3–4). His point here is that baptism does not make the baptiser the owner of the baptised.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council begins its decree on ecumenism with the wound of division, which openly contradicts the will of Christ and gives scandal to the world (Unitatis Redintegratio 1, in substance), and the Catechism teaches that the wounds to unity are healed by repentance and prayer, and that the Church must grow in unity until the end (CCC 817–822, in substance). In her prayer the Church asks for what Paul asks: that we be of one mind. As to baptism, the Council teaches that when anyone baptises, it is Christ himself who baptises (Sacrosanctum Concilium 7). Syro-Malabar Christians pray in the Holy Qurbana for the unity of the Church, and the Catholic Church today speaks with respect and love of the other Churches who share baptism with her.

Luke 14:7–14 · Take the lowest place; invite the poor

Setting.

Jesus is at the house of a leading Pharisee on the Sabbath for a meal (14:1). The setting is a banquet in which people are watching one another closely. Jesus has just healed a man with dropsy and asked whether it is lawful to heal on the Sabbath (14:2–6). Now he notices how the guests choose the places of honour, and speaks first to the guests and then to the host.

The two sayings are a lesson in humility and in generosity, but they are also a picture of the kingdom, to which Luke will turn in the next verse: “Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God” (14:15). In the kingdom, the lowly are lifted up, and the table is open to those who cannot pay back. The meal that Jesus describes is the meal he himself will give.

Structure.

  • v. 7: Jesus sees how the guests choose the places of honour and tells a parable.
  • vv. 8–10: do not take the place of honour, lest you be shamed; take the lowest place, and the host will say, “Friend, move up higher”.
  • v. 11: the principle: those who exalt themselves will be humbled; those who humble themselves will be exalted.
  • vv. 12–14: to the host: do not invite those who can repay you; invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind, and you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.

Key words.

  • protoklisiai, places of honour: Literally “first couches”. At a banquet guests lay on couches around a table, and the places nearest the host were the most honoured. The word shows how closely the first place was watched.
  • tapeinoō, to humble, to lower: The verb of Luke’s whole theme. Its noun is Mary’s “lowliness” in the Magnificat (1:48), and the verb is used of Jesus himself in Philippians 2:8. Humility in the Bible is not self-contempt but truth about who we are before God.
  • antapodoma, repayment: A word used for the return of a gift. It appears in verse 12, “you would be repaid”. Jesus asks us to give where no repayment is possible, so that all is God’s gift.

Verse by verse.

“do not sit down at the place of honor, in case someone more distinguished than you has been invited by your host” (14:8)

Jesus first speaks as a practical teacher: it is better not to be shamed. But the practical advice carries a deeper lesson, and the final verse shows that Jesus is speaking of more than manners.

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” (14:11)

This saying appears in several places in Luke (18:14) and in Matthew (23:12). It is the law of the kingdom. God raises those who do not grasp, and the one who is raised in the end is the one whom God raises.

“do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, in case they may invite you in return” (14:12)

Jesus does not forbid hospitality to friends and family; in the language of the East, the saying says “not only”, and warns against a circle of mutual favours which shuts out the poor. The point is the direction of the gift.

“invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind” (14:13)

These are the same people whom the host of the great banquet will invite in the parable that follows (14:21). The poor have a place at the table of the kingdom, and a community that sets a place for them is already living in it.

“you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous” (14:14)

The reward is real but is held in God’s hands. Charity is not a transaction, but it is not lost. The resurrection is the horizon of the kingdom.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in these verses the humility of Christ himself, who “humbled himself” and took the lowest place (Philippians 2:6–8). The Catechism teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer (CCC 2559, in substance), and that works of mercy toward the poor are a witness to the Gospel and one of the main ways in which we practise charity (CCC 2443–2447, in substance). The Lord’s invitation to the poor has shaped the works of mercy of the Church throughout her history, in the Syro-Malabar Church as everywhere.

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus choose twelve apostles?

Twelve is the number of the tribes of Israel. By choosing twelve, Jesus showed that he was gathering and renewing the whole people of God, not starting something unrelated to Israel. The Church is built on the Twelve (Revelation 21:14). After Judas’s death the group was made up again by the choice of Matthias (Acts 1:15–26). Paul and Barnabas are also called apostles in the New Testament (Acts 14:14), though they are not among the Twelve. The Catechism teaches that the Twelve are the foundation of the Church and that the bishops are their successors (CCC 551–553, 880–881, in substance).

Did Peter’s shadow really heal people?

Luke tells us that the people carried out the sick so that Peter’s shadow might fall on them, and that all were cured. The Church does not treat the shadow as a magic charm. In the Catholic understanding, healing is always the work of God, who may use the persons and things that he chooses as signs of his closeness. This is the root of the Church’s respect for holy persons and, in a different way, for relics and sacramentals, always with the understanding that they point to Christ.

Does Jesus say that we should never invite our friends and family to dinner?

No. In the way of speaking used in his time, “do not invite A, but B” often means “not only A, but also B”. Jesus is against a kind of hospitality that is closed, where we give only to those who can give back. He asks us to open the table to those who cannot repay, and so to share what we have as God has shared it with us.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar Church begins the season of Kaitha today. The name comes from the Syriac word for summer, and the Church’s year turns from the season of the Apostles, which ends with the feast of Mar Thoma, to the weeks in which the seed sown in the earlier seasons is expected to grow and bear fruit. On this first Sunday the Church honours all the Twelve together, and we remember that, according to tradition, the faith of the Thomas Christians comes from one of them, who is named among the Twelve in every list in the Gospels (Matthew 10:3; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). The Church of the East is proud to call itself “apostolic”, and the Syro-Malabar Church prays in the Holy Qurbana for the one Church built on the foundation of the apostles.

For study and teaching

  • Elijah repaired the altar before he prayed. What in my life of prayer or in my family’s faith needs repair?
  • Paul asks for unity “in the same mind and the same purpose”. What divides my parish, family or community, and what is one thing I could do to heal it?
  • Jesus asks us to invite those who cannot repay. Whom could I invite to my table, or to my prayer, this month?
  • Luke says the apostles were sent back to the temple to tell “the whole message”. Which part of the Gospel message do I most prefer to leave out when I speak of it?
