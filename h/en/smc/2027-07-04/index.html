First Sunday of Kaitha: Feast of the Twelve Apostles

Readings

  • First Reading 1 Kings 18:30–39 · Elijah builds an altar of twelve stones
  • Second Reading Acts 5:12–20 · Many signs and wonders through the apostles
  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 1:9–16 · Be united in the same mind and the same purpose
  • Gospel Luke 14:7–14 · Take the lowest place; invite the poor

Word for the day

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 14:11

Reflection

After yesterday's Dukhrana of Mar Thoma, our Church turns today to all twelve. “He called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles”, Luke says, after a night on the mountain “in prayer to God”. Their names are given in the Gospels in four lists, and the first is this: “These are the names of the twelve apostles: first, Simon, also known as Peter, and his brother Andrew; James son of Zebedee, and his brother John; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew the tax collector; James son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus; Simon the Cananaean, and Judas Iscariot, the one who betrayed him.” Mark gives the purpose: “And he appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles, to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message.” First to be with him, then to be sent. The number is deliberate: twelve, like the tribes of Israel, because Jesus was gathering the whole people of God anew. “You who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.” After the betrayal of Judas, the apostles prayed and “the lot fell on Matthias; and he was added to the eleven apostles”: the Twelve is a college that the Church keeps whole (CCC 880). In the last book of the Bible the holy city has “twelve foundations, and on them are the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb”. Today we keep the feast of all the names, including those about whom the Gospels tell us almost nothing; they too are foundation stones.

Today also opens the Season of Kaitha, the summer of the Church's year. After the apostles have sown, the Church looks at the growth and the harvest. The first reading chooses a very fitting beginning: a prophet builds an altar. In the days of King Ahab, Israel was “limping with two different opinions”, between the Lord and Baal, and the altar of the Lord on Mount Carmel “had been thrown down”. “Then Elijah said to all the people, ‘Come closer to me’; and all the people came closer to him. First he repaired the altar of the Lord that had been thrown down; Elijah took twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob, to whom the word of the Lord came, saying, ‘Israel shall be your name’; with the stones he built an altar in the name of the Lord.” Twelve stones, one altar: the divided people is gathered again, and the Lord's name is the name above the whole. Elijah has the sacrifice soaked with water three times, so that whatever follows can only be the Lord's doing, and his prayer is short and humble: “Answer me, O Lord, answer me, so that this people may know that you, O Lord, are God, and that you have turned their hearts back.” Then “the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the burnt offering, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and even licked up the water that was in the trench.” The people fall on their faces: “The Lord indeed is God; the Lord indeed is God.” The Church has always seen in the twelve stones the twelve apostles, on whom the new altar of the Church is built; and in the fire from heaven the fire of Pentecost, which made the same twelve a witness.

The Acts reading shows what the fire did. “Now many signs and wonders were done among the people through the apostles. And they were all together in Solomon’s Portico.” They stayed in the Temple, as faithful sons of Israel; the Church did not begin by leaving the house of the Lord but by praying in it. The people held them in high esteem, and “more than ever believers were added to the Lord, great numbers of both men and women, so that they even carried out the sick into the streets, and laid them on cots and mats, in order that Peter’s shadow might fall on some of them as he came by. A great number of people would also gather from the towns around Jerusalem, bringing the sick and those tormented by unclean spirits, and they were all cured.” This is harvest. Then the high priest and the party of the Sadducees, “being filled with jealousy”, arrested the apostles and put them in the public prison. We see here one group of leaders, the high priest and his party, and not the Jewish people, who held the apostles in high esteem. “But during the night an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors, brought them out, and said, ‘Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life.’” An open prison door and a command to go back to the very place of danger: the Church is sent, again and again, to speak the whole message and not only the parts that are welcome.

Paul, writing to Corinth, shows what can happen to a Church that forgets the one Lord. “God is faithful; by him you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord. Now I appeal to you, brothers and sisters, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you be in agreement and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same purpose.” The Corinthians were each saying, “I belong to Paul,” or “I belong to Apollos,” or “I belong to Cephas,” or “I belong to Christ.” The apostles, great as they were, are never meant to be the names of parties. Paul answers with three questions: “Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” The Twelve are many, and the Lord is one. This is also a prayer for our own day: for the unity of our parishes and our Church, and for the unity of all who are baptised into Christ, with the Orthodox, the Mar Thoma and the Malankara Churches, who also honour the apostles and share much of our heritage. “There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to the one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (Ephesians 4:4–5; CCC 813).

The Gospel is set at a meal. Jesus is at table in the house of a leading Pharisee, and he sees how the guests choose the places of honour. He tells them a parable: “When you are invited by someone to a wedding banquet, do not sit down at the place of honor… But when you are invited, go and sit down at the lowest place, so that when your host comes, he may say to you, ‘Friend, move up higher’; then you will be honored in the presence of all who sit at the table with you.” The principle is the one that should rule the Church: “For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” The apostles themselves had to learn it. At the Last Supper “a dispute also arose among them as to which one of them was to be regarded as the greatest,” and Jesus answered, “the greatest among you must become like the youngest, and the leader like one who serves… I am among you as one who serves.” Then he turns to the host: “When you give a luncheon or a dinner, do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, in case they may invite you in return… But when you give a banquet, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind. And you will be blessed, because they cannot repay you, for you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.” In Kerala this is the parish feast, with its nercha meal: let the first to be served and the best seat be for the one who cannot give anything back. In this season of the harvest the rice-ears that are full bow their heads, and the empty ones stand straight. The Twelve, humbled by their failure and lifted by the Spirit, are the ears that bowed.

  • I belong to Paul, to Apollos, to Cephas.
  • One Lord, one faith, one baptism.
  • Unity in the parish and among the Churches.

“Has Christ been divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” 1 Corinthians 1:13

  • Not the places of honour.
  • The apostles who argued and learned.
  • Invite the poor, who cannot repay.

“For all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 14:11

Apply: At the next gathering, take the last seat and serve another first.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the First Sunday of Kaitha, and our Church celebrates the Feast of the Twelve Apostles. Yesterday we remembered Mar Thoma. Today we remember all the twelve friends of Jesus. “He called his disciples and chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles” (Luke 6:13).

In the first reading the prophet Elijah builds an altar. He takes twelve stones, one for each tribe of Israel, and the people are one again before the Lord. Then the fire falls from heaven and the people say, “The Lord indeed is God” (1 Kings 18:39). The Church has always seen in those twelve stones the twelve apostles, on whom the altar of our Church is built, and in the fire the fire of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost.

In the Acts the apostles are very busy and very happy. Many people are healed, and more and more believe. The authorities arrest them, but during the night an angel opens the prison and says, “Go, stand in the temple and tell the people the whole message about this life” (Acts 5:20). The Church is sent to say all of the message, not just the parts that are pleasant to hear.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians that some say, “I belong to Paul”, and others “I belong to Apollos” or “to Cephas”. He asks, “Has Christ been divided?” (1 Corinthians 1:13). The Twelve are many, but Christ is one. Let us pray for unity in our families and parishes, and among all who are baptised in Christ.

In the Gospel Jesus is a guest at a meal and sees people choosing the best seats. He says: sit in the lowest place, “for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted” (Luke 14:11). And invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind, who cannot pay you back. The apostles themselves once argued about who was the greatest, and Jesus taught them to serve. In the Kaitha season the full ears of rice bow down. Let us bow, too: let us take the last seat, serve first the one who cannot repay us, and so bring fruit that will last. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember Jesus' twelve special friends, the apostles. Jesus asked them first to be with him, and then to go and tell everyone about God. They were ordinary people: fishermen, farmers and a tax collector.

Jesus also teaches us today: do not rush for the best seat. Take the last seat and let your friend sit first! And when you have a party, invite someone who is alone. That is how the apostles learned to love, and that is how we can too!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you prayed all night and chose twelve to be with you and to be sent. We thank you for Peter and Andrew, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew, James and Thaddaeus, Simon and Matthias. As you built an altar of twelve stones in the days of Elijah, build your Church on the foundation of the apostles, with yourself as cornerstone. Send the fire of your Spirit on us at the opening of this season; keep us one in mind and purpose; teach us to take the lowest place, to invite the poor, and to bring a rich harvest for your kingdom. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.
