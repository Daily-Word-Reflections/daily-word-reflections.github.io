Word for the day

“leave your gift there before the altar and go; first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.” Matthew 5:24

Reflection

The Great Fast has begun. Yesterday was Pethurtha Sunday; the Syriac word means putting an end, turning back, and the calendar explains that the days of rich food and celebration are over. In many Syro-Malabar homes Pethurtha evening is still a family meal shared with some solemnity, a farewell to meat and fish until Easter, and in older Syrian Christian households the vessels used for meat were scrubbed clean or set aside for the whole season. The St Thomas Christians have long called this season the fifty days fast, counted from Pethurtha to Easter. Today, the first Monday, the Church keeps the penitential service called Vibhoodi. The ashes are traditionally made by burning the tender coconut-leaf palms kept from last year's Oshana, and the priest traces a cross with them on our foreheads, recalling the word spoken to Adam: “you are dust, and to dust you shall return.” The calendar asks for fasting and abstinence from meat today, and recommends abstinence throughout the season.

It is striking that the Church opens the Great Fast with the first page of the Bible. “In the beginning when God created the heavens and the earth, the earth was a formless void and darkness covered the face of the deep.” Into that darkness God speaks: “Let there be light”; and there was light. Again and again the refrain returns: “God saw that it was good.” The fast is not contempt for the body or for the good things of the world; it is God's word speaking again over the void and darkness in us, bringing order and light. Joshua stands where we stand today, at the edge of a river. Moses is dead, the Jordan lies ahead, and God says: “Be strong and courageous; do not be frightened or dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go.” He gives Joshua one method for the crossing: “This book of the law shall not depart out of your mouth; you shall meditate on it day and night.” Paul names what went wrong with humanity: people “exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator.” Fasting puts the creature back in its place, so that the Creator may be first again. And Paul is not ashamed: the gospel “is the power of God for salvation to everyone who has faith.”

In the Gospel Jesus says he has come “not to abolish but to fulfill” the law, and he carries it into the heart. Not only murder, but anger and insult are judged. Then he describes a scene every Christian knows: “So when you are offering your gift at the altar, if you remember that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go; first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.” This is why the Vibhoodi is a penitential service of reconciliation. Ash on the forehead and peace in the home belong together. A fast that gives up fish and meat but keeps a quarrel alive has missed the heart of the matter. Seven weeks lie ahead: dust that knows it is dust, light that God speaks into the dark, courage for the crossing, and a brother or sister waiting for our first step.

Theme: “first be reconciled to your brother or sister, and then come and offer your gift.”

  • Pethurtha: the feasting ends.

  • Now is the acceptable time.

  • Darkness over the deep.
  • God saw that it was good.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” 2 Corinthians 5:17

Apply: Ask God each morning of this week to speak his light into one dark corner of your life.

  • The gift left at the altar.

Apply: Take the first step towards one estranged person before Sunday.

The Great Fast begins with ash and with light: we are dust, and God speaks over us again.

Take-home resolution: Make peace with one person before receiving Holy Qurbana on Sunday.

Full homily

Dear friends, this morning the ash has marked our foreheads. The words remind us of the first sin and its sentence: you are dust, and to dust you shall return (Genesis 3:19). Yesterday was Pethurtha; the feasting is over, and the fifty days fast has begun.

But the Church gives us today the first page of the Bible. God speaks into the darkness: let there be light (Genesis 1:3). Again and again he looks at what he has made and sees that it is good. The fast does not despise God's gifts. It makes room for God to create us anew.

Like Joshua at the Jordan, we stand at the edge of a crossing. God says to us too: be strong and courageous; do not be frightened or dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go (Joshua 1:9). And he gives us his word to carry, to meditate on day and night.

Jesus goes to the heart of the matter: if you remember at the altar that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there and go; first be reconciled (Matthew 5:24). Let the ash on our foreheads lead to peace in our homes. Then our fast will be pleasing to God. Amen.

For children

Children, today the priest put ash on our foreheads in the shape of a cross. It means: I belong to Jesus, and I want to start again. Is there someone you need to say sorry to today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, at the beginning of this holy fast you mark us with ashes and remind us that we are dust. Speak your light into our darkness, give us courage for the crossing of these days, and do not let us bring our gift to your altar while a brother or sister waits for our pardon. Make this Great Fast a time of new creation for us and for our families. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
