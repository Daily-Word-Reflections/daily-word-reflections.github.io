Readings

  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 · When you come together, is it really the Lord's supper?
  • Gospel Matthew 21:28–32 · Do the will of the Father

Word for the day

“For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”” 1 Corinthians 11:23–24

Reflection

On the first Monday of Kaitha the Church begins the season of summer and harvest with an uncomfortable passage, and that is a mercy. Paul writes to the Corinthians about what happened when they came together for the Lord's Supper. “Now in the following instructions I do not commend you, because when you come together it is not for the better but for the worse. For, to begin with, when you come together as a church, I hear that there are divisions among you; and to some extent I believe it.” In the first century the Eucharist was celebrated within a shared meal. At Corinth the rich arrived early and ate and drank their own food, and the poor, who had to work until evening, arrived to find nothing left. “When you come together, it is not really to eat the Lord’s supper. For when the time comes to eat, each of you goes ahead with your own supper, and one goes hungry and another becomes drunk.” Paul is angry, and his anger is love for the poor: “What! Do you not have homes to eat and drink in? Or do you show contempt for the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing? What should I say to you? Should I commend you? In this matter I do not commend you!” Yesterday's Epistle asked, “Has Christ been divided?”; today's shows how a community can divide the very table of unity. The verse in between, “there have to be factions among you, for only so will it become clear who among you are genuine”, does not praise division. It says that when divisions appear, the way people act shows who really lives by Christ.

Paul does not stop at rebuke. The very next verses, which the Church has always kept beside today's passage, give the oldest written account of the Eucharist, set down about the year 55, before any of the Gospels: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, ‘This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ In the same way he took the cup also, after supper, saying, ‘This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.’ For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” This is the Qurbana in its first form. The bread is his body given for us, the cup his blood; the Qurbana is the memorial of his death and the pledge of his coming. And the Church holds, in her faith in the Real Presence, that what is on the altar after the consecration is truly the Body and Blood of Christ (CCC 1374). That is why Paul says elsewhere, “The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ? Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.” To eat the one bread and to despise the hungry brother is a contradiction. “For all who eat and drink without discerning the body, eat and drink judgment against themselves.” To discern the body means to recognise Christ in the bread, and also in the community gathered round it, and above all in its poorest member (CCC 1385, 1397).

The Gospel is a short parable which asks us a question: “What do you think?” Jesus has been teaching in the Temple, where the chief priests and the elders of the people have come to him to ask, “By what authority are you doing these things?” He answers with a story. “A man had two sons; he went to the first and said, ‘Son, go and work in the vineyard today.’ He answered, ‘I will not’; but later he changed his mind and went. The father went to the second and said the same; and he answered, ‘I go, sir’; but he did not go. Which of the two did the will of his father?” They give the plain answer: the one who went, whatever he first said. Jesus then says, “Truly I tell you, the tax collectors and the prostitutes are going into the kingdom of God ahead of you. For John came to you in the way of righteousness and you did not believe him, but the tax collectors and the prostitutes believed him; and even after you saw it, you did not change your minds and believe him.” The parable is told to the leaders who were questioning him, and it is not a verdict on a people. It is a call to every one of us to change our minds: the tax collectors, who had said No to God by their way of life, heard John and turned back; those who said Yes in their words did not follow through in their lives. The word of the Gospel here is the same word the Church uses for repentance: a change of mind and heart.

The parable fits Paul's rebuke exactly. The Corinthians were saying “I go, sir” every Sunday, with the Lord's Supper on their lips, and they were not doing the Father's will, which is love for the poor brother and sister at the same table. “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father in heaven.” The Catechism teaches that we enter the kingdom not by speaking but by doing the Father's will, and that prayer is where we learn it (CCC 2826). Jesus himself is the Son who always says Yes: “I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me”; and Mary, the first disciple, said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” And the first son gives us hope: a No said in anger or fear or pride is not final. “Bear fruit worthy of repentance.” Kaitha is the season of fruit. In these monsoon weeks, when the fields must be sown and tended in the rain, the father's word is very concrete: “go and work in the vineyard today.” Not tomorrow, and not only when it is convenient. Today, this Monday, with our hands, at the table of the Lord and the table of the poor, we say Yes.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the first Monday of Kaitha, the season of summer and harvest. Our readings begin the season with two serious questions. The first is from Saint Paul, and the second is from Jesus.

Paul says to the Corinthians: “When you come together, it is not really to eat the Lord’s supper” (1 Corinthians 11:20). Why? Because each one ate his own food. One person was hungry and another was drunk. They came to the table of the Lord, and they humiliated the poor. “Do you not have homes to eat and drink in?” Paul asks. He is angry because he loves the poor.

Then Paul reminds them of what the Lord did on the night before he died. He took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said, “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.” The cup is the new covenant in his blood. As often as we eat this bread and drink this cup, we proclaim the Lord's death until he comes (1 Corinthians 11:23–26). Because we eat one bread, we are one body. We cannot be one at the altar and strangers at the door.

In the Gospel Jesus tells a short story. A father says to his two sons, “Go and work in the vineyard today.” One says, “I will not,” but later he changes his mind and goes. The other says, “I go, sir,” and does not go. Which one did the will of the father (Matthew 21:28–31)? Jesus does not praise the No; he praises the going. Words are not enough: “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father” (Matthew 7:21).

Today let us ask ourselves: Where have I said Yes to God, and have not done it? Where have I said No, and can I still change my mind? It is never too late to turn back. And let us do something today: make peace with someone before Sunday's Qurbana, and do the one task we have been saying Later to. “Son, go and work in the vineyard today.” Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus tells a story about two sons. The father asked them to work in the vineyard. One said, ‘No!’ but later he went. The other said, ‘Yes!’ but he did not go.

Which boy did what his father wanted? The one who went! Jesus wants us to say Yes with our hands. Today, do one thing you have been putting off, and help someone at home without being asked!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, on the night when you were betrayed you took bread and a cup and gave us your Body and your Blood. Forgive us the divisions at your table and the contempt we show to those who have nothing. Teach us to discern your body in the bread and in the poor, and to share what we have with a glad heart. When we say No, turn our hearts; when we say Yes, make our Yes true. Send us today into the Father's vineyard, to work with you for a harvest of mercy. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.
