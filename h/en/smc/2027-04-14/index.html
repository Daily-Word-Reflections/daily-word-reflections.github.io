Word for the day

“For to me, living is Christ and dying is gain.” Philippians 1:21

Reflection

Paul writes to the Philippians from prison, not knowing whether his trial will end in release or in death. A man in that position might be expected to write about his fear. Instead he writes one of the most serene sentences in the New Testament: “For to me, living is Christ and dying is gain.” Everything turns on the first half. If living were money, then dying would be loss; if living were reputation, success or pleasure, death would take it all away. But if living is Christ, then death cannot take away what matters, because it brings us closer to him. That is why Paul can weigh the two possibilities so calmly. “If I am to live in the flesh, that means fruitful labor for me; and I do not know which I prefer. I am hard pressed between the two: my desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better; but to remain in the flesh is more necessary for you.” This is Easter faith in its plainest form. Because Christ has been raised, death has lost its sting (1 Corinthians 15:55). Paul does not despise this life. He loves the Philippians and wants to stay with them “for your progress and joy in faith.” But he has stopped being afraid of the end. For him, to die is to “be with Christ”, and to live is to serve him in his brothers and sisters. A Christian who has learned this can face illness, old age, danger and even martyrdom with a peace that the world cannot explain. The Church in India has seen this peace in her martyrs and in many ordinary believers who died with the name of Jesus on their lips. When we pray the Office for the departed at a funeral, we are not pretending that death does not hurt. We are saying, with Paul, that for those who belong to Christ it is the door to something far better.

Matthew places the cleansing of a leper immediately after the Sermon on the Mount, as if to say: here is the teacher who spoke with such authority; now see what his authority does. “When Jesus had come down from the mountain, great crowds followed him; and there was a leper who came to him and knelt before him, saying, ‘Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.’” Under the Law of Moses a person with a leprous disease had to live apart: “He shall live alone; his dwelling shall be outside the camp” (Leviticus 13:46). The rule protected the community, but it meant years of loneliness, and the leper should not even have come near. He does not doubt that Jesus can heal him; his only question is whether Jesus wants to. Jesus answers with his hand before his words: “He stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, ‘I do choose. Be made clean!’ Immediately his leprosy was cleansed.” The touch that should have made Jesus unclean makes the leper clean. Holiness here is not something fragile that must be protected from contact; it is a power that flows outward and heals. Then Jesus sends him to the priest, “and offer the gift that Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.” Jesus does not set the Law aside; he fulfils it, and he gives the man back not only his skin but his place in the worshipping community of Israel. The Risen Lord still stretches out his hand. In the sacrament of Reconciliation he touches what we are ashamed to show anyone, and says, I do choose. In the Anointing of the Sick he lays his hand on the suffering. And at every Qurbana, before Communion, we pray in humility because we know we need his touch.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul is in prison. He does not know whether he will live or die. And he writes: for me, living is Christ and dying is gain (Philippians 1:21). If my life is money, death is loss. If my life is Christ, death brings me closer to him.

Paul is torn. He would like to depart and be with Christ, which is far better. But he knows his people still need him, so he is ready to stay and work for their progress and joy in faith. That is Easter faith: no fear of death, and great love for this life as a time of service.

In the Gospel a leper kneels before Jesus. Lepers had to live alone, outside the village. He says: Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean. He does not doubt the power of Jesus; he only asks whether Jesus wants to.

And Jesus stretches out his hand and touches him. I do choose. Be made clean! The touch that should have made Jesus unclean makes the leper clean. Then Jesus sends him to the priest, so that he can return to his people and to worship.

The Risen Lord still stretches out his hand, in Confession, in the Anointing of the Sick, and in Holy Qurbana. Let us bring him what we hide. And let us be his hand for someone whom everyone else avoids. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, a sick man asked Jesus: do you want to make me well? Jesus touched him and said: yes, I want to! Jesus always wants to help us. Tell him what makes you sad.

Is there a child in your class whom nobody plays with? Be like Jesus: go and say hello first!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you are our life, and in you death becomes gain. Deliver us from the fear of death and from every false life that cannot last. Make us love the people you have given us and serve them gladly while we remain here. Stretch out your hand to us as you did to the leper; touch the places we hide, and say to each of us, I do choose. Be made clean. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul writes from prison, weighing life and death, and finds that both belong to Christ: “to me, living is Christ and dying is gain.” In Matthew, a man shut out by his disease kneels before Jesus and says, “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean,” and Jesus touches him. Both readings are about belonging wholly to the Lord: Paul who wants nothing but Christ, and the leper who leaves everything to the Lord's will and is not refused.

Philippians 1:21–26 · Living is Christ

Setting.

Paul is in chains (1:7, 13) and does not know whether he will be released or put to death. Where he was imprisoned is debated: Rome, Ephesus and Caesarea have all been proposed. Philippi was the first church he founded in Europe (Acts 16:11–40), and it remained especially dear to him; this is the warmest of his letters.

Just before our passage he has said that Christ will be exalted in his body, “whether by life or by death” (1:20). Now he thinks aloud about which he would choose.

Structure.

  • v. 21: the principle: life is Christ, death is gain.
  • vv. 22–24: the dilemma: fruitful labour here, or departing to be with Christ.
  • vv. 25–26: the decision: for their sake he expects to remain.

Key words.

  • kerdos, gain: A word from business: profit. Two chapters later Paul uses it again: “whatever gains I had, these I have come to regard as loss because of Christ” (3:7).
  • analysai, to depart: The verb was used for loosing a ship from its moorings or striking a tent to move on. For Paul, death is a setting out. The POC renders it simply “to die”.
  • synechomai, I am hard pressed: To be held tight from both sides. Paul is not torn between something good and something bad, but between two goods.

Verse by verse.

“For to me, living is Christ and dying is gain.” (1:21)

Not “living is serving Christ”, but “living is Christ”. His whole life has one name.

“my desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better;” (1:23)

Paul expects to be with Christ at once after death, not only at the end of time. The Church teaches the same hope (CCC 1011).

“but to remain in the flesh is more necessary for you.” (1:24)

Love decides the question. What Paul wants for himself gives way to what the community needs.

“I know that I will remain and continue with all of you for your progress and joy in faith,” (1:25)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the meaning of Christian death with verse 21: because of Christ, death has a positive meaning (CCC 1010). It adds that the Christian can feel a desire for death like Paul's, “to depart and be with Christ” (CCC 1011). St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom in Rome, wrote in the same spirit that it was better for him to die in Christ Jesus than to reign over the ends of the earth (Letter to the Romans 6, in substance). Paul's choice also teaches that the desire for heaven never excuses us from the work and love owed to others here.

Matthew 8:1–4 · If you choose

Setting.

Jesus comes down from the mountain after the Sermon (chapters 5–7). Matthew now gathers ten mighty works in chapters 8–9, so that the Lord who has taught with authority is seen to act with authority. The first is the cleansing of a leper.

The law of Leviticus 13–14 kept a person with a skin disease outside the community and gave the priest the task of examining and declaring someone clean. The law protected the people; for the sick person, however, it meant living apart.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Jesus comes down; the crowds follow.
  • v. 2: the leper kneels and asks.
  • v. 3: Jesus stretches out his hand, touches, speaks; the man is clean at once.
  • v. 4: silence, and the way back through the priest and the offering Moses commanded.

Key words.

  • lepros, leper: In the Bible the word covers a range of skin diseases, not only what we now call Hansen's disease. What they shared was the separation they brought (Leviticus 13:45–46).
  • thelō, I choose: The leper does not doubt the Lord's power; he leaves the matter to his will. Jesus answers with the same word: “I do choose.”
  • katharisthēti, be made clean: The leper asks to be made clean, not only healed: he asks to be given back to the community and to worship.

Verse by verse.

“and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, if you choose, you can make me clean.”” (8:2)

A model of prayer: faith in his power, trust in his will, and no attempt to bargain.

“He stretched out his hand and touched him,” (8:3)

The touch comes before the word. Jesus could have healed from a distance; he chose to touch a man no one would touch.

““I do choose. Be made clean!” Immediately his leprosy was cleansed.” (8:3)
“go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.” (8:4)

Jesus respects the law of Moses and sends the man back into the life of his people.

How the Church reads it.

Many Fathers, among them St John Chrysostom, notice that when the Lord touched the leper his hand did not become unclean; instead the leper became clean (in substance). The Catechism sees in Christ's compassion for the sick, and in his many healings, the sign that God has visited his people (CCC 1503). The leper's words, “Lord, if you choose”, have become a prayer of the Church for every sickness of body and soul.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus tell the man to say nothing to anyone?

Matthew, following Mark, often shows Jesus avoiding public fame from his miracles. He did not want to be followed only as a wonder worker, and his mission would be fully understood only after the Cross and the Resurrection. Here the man is also sent first to the priest, so that his healing becomes a witness through the proper path of the law.

For study and teaching

  • Paul can say “living is Christ”. What one word would honestly describe what my life is about at present?
  • The leper prayed, “Lord, if you choose.” Can I bring my most pressing need to the Lord this week in those words?
