Word for the day

“But the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you.” John 14:26

Reflection

On the eve of the feast of Peter and Paul the Church remembers a bishop who loved the apostles' teaching more than his own life, and who handed it on so faithfully that he is called today the Doctor of Unity. Irenaeus was born in Asia Minor, probably at Smyrna, in the first half of the second century. As a boy he listened to Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, who had known the apostle John. Many years later Irenaeus wrote to a friend that he could still remember the place where Polycarp sat when he taught, how he went in and out, and how he spoke of his conversations with John and with others who had seen the Lord. That is a chain of only three links between Irenaeus and Jesus: the Lord, the beloved disciple, Polycarp, Irenaeus. He went to Gaul, served as a priest in Lyons, and after the persecution of 177, in which the old bishop Pothinus died in prison, he became bishop of that Church. He wrote five books against the false teachings of his day, usually called Against Heresies, and a shorter work, the Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching. When a dispute about the date of Easter threatened to divide the Church of Rome from the Churches of Asia, he wrote to Pope Victor asking for patience and peace; the historian Eusebius says that he lived up to his name, which means peacemaker. He died around the year 200 and has long been honoured as a martyr, though the details of his death are not known. In 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church, with the title Doctor of Unity.

The readings chosen for him speak of the priest and the teacher. In Exodus the high priest Aaron wears the breastpiece engraved with the names of the twelve tribes: “So Aaron shall bear the names of the sons of Israel in the breastpiece of judgment on his heart when he goes into the holy place, for a continual remembrance before the Lord.” A true shepherd carries his people on his heart into the presence of God. Malachi recalls God's covenant with Levi and describes the priest as God wanted him: “True instruction was in his mouth, and no wrong was found on his lips. He walked with me in integrity and uprightness, and he turned many from iniquity. For the lips of a priest should guard knowledge, and people should seek instruction from his mouth, for he is the messenger of the Lord of hosts.” Irenaeus was such a priest: he guarded knowledge, not secret knowledge for a few, but the public faith of the whole Church.

Paul's words to Timothy could be Irenaeus' own motto: “But as for you, continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it.” The Gnostic teachers of the second century claimed to possess secret knowledge passed down privately, and they despised the body, the created world and the God of the Old Testament. Irenaeus answered that the apostles had hidden nothing: their teaching is public, kept in the Churches they founded, and handed on by the bishops who succeeded them. He listed the bishops of Rome, “the very great, very ancient and universally known Church founded and organised at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul” (in substance), and said that every Church must agree with it because of its preeminent authority. So tomorrow's feast and today's saint belong together. He defended the goodness of creation and of the flesh, which the Son of God took from the Virgin Mary; he saw Mary as the new Eve, whose obedience untied the knot of Eve's disobedience; he taught that Christ sums up and renews the whole of human history in himself. And he gave us the sentence that sums up his joy: the glory of God is the human being fully alive, and the life of the human being is the vision of God. He knew that the Spirit was the guarantee of this teaching, as Jesus promised in today's Gospel: “the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you.” All scripture, Paul adds, “is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness.”

The weekday readings fit the peacemaker well. Paul writes about a member of the Corinthian Church who had caused great pain and been punished by the community. Now Paul asks for mercy: “so now instead you should forgive and console him, so that he may not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. So I urge you to reaffirm your love for him.” Discipline in the Church is never meant to crush, only to heal; and an unforgiving community gives the devil an opening: “we do this so that we may not be outwitted by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his designs.” In the Gospel the scribes and Pharisees ask Jesus for a sign. He gives them only the sign of Jonah, three days in the belly of the sea monster, a sign of his own death and resurrection, and he reminds them that the people of Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching and that the queen of the South came “from the ends of the earth to listen to the wisdom of Solomon, and see, something greater than Solomon is here!” Irenaeus, who carried the Gospel from Asia to the far west of the empire, and we in India, who received it from Mar Thoma at the eastern edge of the known world, know what it means to come from the ends of the earth to hear the One who is greater than Solomon. Let us listen, repent, forgive, and continue in what we have learned.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we remember Saint Irenaeus, a bishop of the second century. As a boy he listened to Polycarp, who had known the apostle John, who had known Jesus. Think of it: only a few links between Irenaeus and the Lord.

False teachers in his time said that they had secret knowledge that ordinary Christians did not have. Irenaeus answered: the apostles hid nothing. Their teaching is public, kept in the Churches they founded and handed on by the bishops who came after them. Paul says the same to Timothy: “continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it” (2 Timothy 3:14).

The readings for his feast describe a true priest. Aaron carried the names of the tribes on his heart before God. Malachi says, “the lips of a priest should guard knowledge” (Malachi 2:7). And Jesus promises the Holy Spirit, who “will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you” (John 14:26).

Irenaeus was also a peacemaker; his name means peaceful. When a quarrel about the date of Easter threatened to divide the Church, he begged for patience. In the weekday reading Paul too asks for mercy: forgive and console the one who did wrong, reaffirm your love for him, so that Satan may not outwit you (2 Corinthians 2:7–11).

Irenaeus wrote: the glory of God is the human being fully alive. Tomorrow we celebrate Peter and Paul, the apostles whose Church in Rome Irenaeus honoured. Today let us thank God for those who handed on the faith to us, let us forgive, and let us continue in what we have learned. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Irenaeus heard about Jesus from an old bishop who had known Saint John, Jesus' friend. The faith came to him like a chain, from hand to hand. Today, say thank you to the person who taught you to pray. And be a peacemaker, like Irenaeus!

Prayer

Lord God, you gave your Church the bishop Saint Irenaeus to guard the faith of the apostles and to make peace among your people. Through his prayers keep us faithful to what we have learned and firmly believed, and grateful to those from whom we learned it. Give our bishops and priests the heart of Aaron, to carry your people before you, and the lips of a true priest, to guard knowledge and to teach. Send us the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, to teach us everything and to remind us of all that your Son has said. Make us quick to forgive and console, so that Satan may not outwit us, and let us live fully, for your glory, until we see you face to face. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The weekday readings speak of mercy and of signs. Paul asks the Corinthians to forgive a member who has hurt the community, so that he is not overwhelmed by sorrow and Satan gains nothing. In Matthew, Jesus refuses a sign on demand and points to the sign of Jonah: his own death and resurrection, and a call to repent. Today the Church also keeps the memory of St Irenaeus of Lyons, bishop, whose name means “peace”, and offers readings for him: the high priest who bears Israel’s names on his heart, the priest whose lips guard knowledge, the apostolic teaching handed on to Timothy, and the Spirit who reminds the Church of all that Jesus said. Irenaeus guarded that teaching against error and worked for peace in the Church.

2 Corinthians 2:5–11 · Forgive and console him

Setting.

Paul had made a painful visit to Corinth, during which someone seems to have wronged or openly defied him, and he then wrote a letter “with many tears” (2:4). The community responded and disciplined the offender. Now Paul says the punishment is enough. Older interpreters, among them several Fathers, thought this was the man of 1 Corinthians 5; many scholars today think rather of someone who opposed Paul during that visit. Either way, the point is the same: discipline must lead to healing.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–6: the offender hurt the whole community; the punishment by the majority is enough.
  • vv. 7–8: now forgive and console him; reaffirm your love.
  • vv. 9–11: Paul forgives with them, before Christ, so that Satan may not take advantage.

Key words.

  • charizomai, to forgive: From charis, grace: to forgive is to give freely what is not deserved, as God has done for us in Christ.
  • parakaleō, to console, encourage: The same root as Paraclete. The community must not only stop punishing; it must comfort and restore.
  • en prosōpō Christou, in the presence of Christ: Literally “in the face of Christ”. The Latin Vulgate rendered it in persona Christi, words that later theology used for the priest who acts in the person of Christ.

Verse by verse.

“so now instead you should forgive and console him, so that he may not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow.” (2:7)

Correction that never ends can crush a person. The goal of discipline is to bring the sinner home.

“So I urge you to reaffirm your love for him.” (2:8)

Forgiveness is not only a decision in the heart; it must be shown in a public act of welcome.

“And we do this so that we may not be outwitted by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his designs.” (2:11)

Satan can use a community’s harshness as easily as its laxity. An unforgiving Church plays into his hands.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that reconciliation with the Church is inseparable from reconciliation with God: whomever the Church receives anew into her communion, God will welcome back into his (CCC 1445). This passage shows that pattern in the first generation: the community corrects, then forgives and restores, and the apostle forgives with it. The Church has always held that the purpose of penance is healing and return, not shame.

Matthew 12:38–42 · Something greater than Jonah

Setting.

Jesus has healed a man who was blind and mute, and some have said he casts out demons by Beelzebul (12:22–24). Now some scribes and Pharisees ask for a sign, some proof from heaven that will settle the question. Jesus has already given many signs; what they ask for is a sign that will force belief, on their terms.

Structure.

  • v. 38: the request: “Teacher, we wish to see a sign from you.”
  • vv. 39–40: no sign but the sign of Jonah: three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.
  • vv. 41–42: the Ninevites and the queen of the South will rise in judgment; something greater is here.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: In the Gospels a sign points beyond itself to God’s action. Jesus refuses signs that are demanded to test him (16:1–4).
  • kētos, sea monster: The word in the Greek Old Testament for the “large fish” of Jonah 1:17. The point is not the kind of animal but God’s rescue from death.
  • pleion, something greater: The Greek is neuter: “something more”. Not only a greater prophet or king is here, but the Kingdom itself, present in Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth.” (12:40)

In Jewish reckoning part of a day could count as a day, so Friday afternoon to Sunday morning fits “three days”. The sign is the Resurrection.

“because they repented at the proclamation of Jonah, and see, something greater than Jonah is here!” (12:41)

Pagan Nineveh repented at the word of a reluctant prophet. The warning is to every hearer who has the Gospel and does not turn.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs worked by Jesus attest that the Father has sent him, but that they are not meant to satisfy curiosity or a desire for magic, and that in spite of his evident miracles some reject him (CCC 548). The Church has always read the sign of Jonah as the Lord’s burial and Resurrection (CCC 994 names the sign of Jonah among the Lord’s announcements of his Resurrection). “This generation” in Matthew is the generation that refuses to believe, in any age; it is not a judgment on the Jewish people, and the first to believe in the Risen Lord were themselves Jews (Nostra Aetate 4).

Exodus 28:28–30 · On his heart before the Lord

Setting.

Exodus 28 describes the vestments of Aaron, the high priest. Over his heart he wore the breastpiece, set with twelve precious stones engraved with the names of the twelve tribes (28:15–21). Inside it were kept the Urim and Thummim, sacred lots used to seek God’s decision.

Structure.

  • v. 28: the breastpiece fastened to the ephod with a blue cord, so that it never comes loose.
  • v. 29: Aaron bears the names of Israel on his heart as a continual remembrance before the Lord.
  • v. 30: the Urim and Thummim on his heart: he bears the judgment of Israel before the Lord continually.

Key words.

  • ḥōšen mišpāṭ, breastpiece of judgment: A square pouch of woven cloth worn on the chest. It is called “of judgment” because through it God’s decision was sought for the people.
  • ’ûrîm wĕtummîm, Urim and Thummim: Sacred lots whose exact form is unknown. The words are often explained as “lights and perfections”. They showed that the priest does not decide by himself but seeks God’s will.

Verse by verse.

“So Aaron shall bear the names of the sons of Israel in the breastpiece of judgment on his heart when he goes into the holy place, for a continual remembrance before the Lord.” (28:29)

The priest enters God’s presence carrying the people on his heart. A shepherd remembers his flock by name when he prays.

“thus Aaron shall bear the judgment of the Israelites on his heart before the Lord continually.” (28:30)

“Continually” is said twice. Intercession is not occasional; it is the shape of a priest’s life.

How the Church reads it.

The Letter to the Hebrews sees all the priesthood of Aaron fulfilled in Christ, the high priest who “always lives to make intercession” for those who approach God through him (Hebrews 7:25). The Catechism teaches that everything the priesthood of the Old Covenant prefigured finds its fulfilment in Christ, the one mediator (CCC 1544). A bishop like Irenaeus shares in that priesthood and carries his people on his heart.

Malachi 2:4–7 · The lips of a priest should guard knowledge

Setting.

Malachi preached after the return from exile, in the fifth century before Christ, when the Temple had been rebuilt but worship had grown careless. The priests offered blemished animals and taught falsely (1:6–14; 2:8). God reminds them of his covenant with Levi, the ancestor of the priestly tribe, as a picture of what a priest should be.

Structure.

  • v. 4: the command is given so that the covenant with Levi may stand.
  • vv. 5–6: a covenant of life and peace; Levi revered God, taught truly and turned many from sin.
  • v. 7: the priest’s lips guard knowledge; he is the messenger of the Lord of hosts.

Key words.

  • tôrâ, instruction: Here not the written Law alone but the teaching the priest gives the people. True instruction was “in his mouth”.
  • mal’ak, messenger: The same word as the prophet’s name, Malachi, “my messenger”. The priest who teaches truly speaks for God.

Verse by verse.

“True instruction was in his mouth, and no wrong was found on his lips. He walked with me in integrity and uprightness, and he turned many from iniquity.” (2:6)

Teaching and life go together. Levi’s words could turn others because his own walk was upright.

“For the lips of a priest should guard knowledge, and people should seek instruction from his mouth, for he is the messenger of the Lord of hosts.” (2:7)

The priest is a guardian of truth he did not invent. People have a right to sound teaching.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the first task of priests, as co-workers of the bishops, is to proclaim the Gospel of God to all (Presbyterorum Ordinis 4), and that bishops are authentic teachers of the faith (Lumen Gentium 25). Irenaeus lived this verse: he wrote to protect his people from teachings that twisted the Scriptures, setting out the faith received from the apostles.

2 Timothy 3:14–17 · Continue in what you have learned

Setting.

The letter presents itself as Paul’s last testament to Timothy, written from prison as his death draws near (4:6). Many scholars think it was written by a disciple in Paul’s name, while others hold it is Paul’s own; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way. Timothy had learned the faith from his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (1:5) and from Paul himself.

Structure.

  • v. 14: continue in what you have learned, knowing from whom you learned it.
  • v. 15: from childhood you have known the sacred writings, which lead to salvation through faith in Christ.
  • vv. 16–17: all scripture is inspired by God and useful for teaching, reproof, correction and training.

Key words.

  • theopneustos, inspired by God: Literally “God-breathed”. The Scriptures come from the breath, the Spirit, of God, while being truly written by human authors.
  • paideia, training: The word for the upbringing of a child. Scripture forms a person over a lifetime, not only in moments of study.

Verse by verse.

“But as for you, continue in what you have learned and firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it” (3:14)

Faith is received from trustworthy people. Knowing from whom we learned is part of knowing that it is true.

“All scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness” (3:16)

At first “scripture” meant what we call the Old Testament. The Church, guided by the Spirit, recognized the apostolic writings with it as one Bible.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council quotes this verse in teaching that the books of Scripture, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, have God as their author, and teach firmly, faithfully and without error the truth God wanted put into writing for our salvation (Dei Verbum 11; CCC 105–107). Irenaeus was a living example of verse 14: as a boy in Asia Minor he heard St Polycarp, who had known the apostle John, and he could say from whom he had learned (in substance, Eusebius, Church History 5.20).

John 14:26–30 · He will teach you everything

Setting.

At the Last Supper Jesus prepares his disciples for his departure. He promises the Advocate, gives them his peace, and tells them that his going to the Father is reason for joy. The hour of the Passion is near: “the ruler of this world is coming.”

Structure.

  • v. 26: the Advocate, sent by the Father in my name, will teach you and remind you.
  • v. 27: my peace I give you, not as the world gives.
  • vv. 28–29: rejoice that I go to the Father; I tell you before it happens.
  • v. 30: the ruler of this world is coming; he has no power over me.

Key words.

  • paraklētos, Advocate: One called to stand beside another, to defend, comfort and guide. The Spirit continues Jesus’ own presence with the disciples.
  • hypomimnēskō, to remind: The Spirit does not bring a new Gospel but makes the Church remember and understand what Jesus said.

Verse by verse.

“But the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you everything, and remind you of all that I have said to you.” (14:26)

Here is the root of the Church’s living Tradition: the Spirit keeps Jesus’ words alive and leads the Church into their depth.

“Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. I do not give to you as the world gives.” (14:27)

The world’s peace depends on the absence of trouble. Christ’s peace remains even in the hour of the Cross.

“If you loved me, you would rejoice that I am going to the Father, because the Father is greater than I.” (14:28)

The Church reads this in the light of the faith that the Son is equal to the Father. St Augustine explains it of the Son in the form of a servant he took for our sake (in substance, Tractates on John); other Fathers read it of the Father as the source from whom the Son is eternally begotten.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, recalls to the Church all that Christ said and guides her into all truth (CCC 243, 729). St Irenaeus wrote that where the Church is, there is the Spirit of God, and where the Spirit of God is, there is the Church and every grace (in substance, Against Heresies 3.24.1). He also called the Son and the Spirit the “two hands” of the Father, by which God forms and saves the human person (in substance, Against Heresies 4, preface; 5.6.1).

Questions people ask

Who was St Irenaeus?

He was born in Asia Minor, probably around AD 130–140, and as a youth heard St Polycarp of Smyrna, a disciple of the apostle John. He became a priest at Lyons in Gaul, and after the bishop Pothinus died in the persecution of 177, he succeeded him. His great work, Against Heresies, defended the faith against the Gnostics by appealing to the Scriptures and to the teaching handed down by the bishops in succession from the apostles. When Pope Victor threatened to cut off the Churches of Asia over the date of Easter, Irenaeus urged peace, living up to his name (Eusebius, Church History 5.24). He died around AD 200; later tradition honours him as a martyr, though early sources are silent. In 2022 Pope Francis declared him a Doctor of the Church, with the title “Doctor of Unity”.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Irenaeus came from the East and served in the West; he belongs to the whole Church. For Syro-Malabar Catholics, who treasure an apostolic faith received from Mar Thoma Shleeha and kept through the centuries, his teaching is close to home: the faith is known by its apostolic roots and kept in communion. His famous saying, that the glory of God is the human person fully alive (in substance, Against Heresies 4.20.7), is a fitting prayer for every family.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks the Corinthians to forgive and console a man who had hurt them. Is there someone in my family or parish whom I need to welcome back?
  • Irenaeus could say from whom he had learned the faith. Who handed the faith on to me, and to whom am I handing it on?
