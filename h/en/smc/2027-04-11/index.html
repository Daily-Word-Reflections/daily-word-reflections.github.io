Word for the day

“Jesus said to him, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.”” John 14:6

Reflection

On this third Sunday of the Resurrection the Church opens with a prophecy about who belongs in God's house. Isaiah speaks to two kinds of people who expected to be shut out. The foreigner thought, “The LORD will surely separate me from his people”; the eunuch, who could have no children to carry on his name, said, “I am just a dry tree.” God answers both with a promise beyond their hopes. To the eunuchs who keep his covenant he says, “I will give, in my house and within my walls, a monument and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off.” And to the foreigners who love his name he says, “these I will bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer… for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.” Jesus quoted this verse when he cleansed the temple (Mark 11:17). After Easter the promise comes true in a way no one could have imagined: in the Acts of the Apostles one of the very first to be baptised from beyond Israel is an Ethiopian eunuch, who was reading Isaiah in his chariot when Philip found him (Acts 8:26–39). The Risen Lord gathers the outcasts. In his Church no one is a dry tree, and no nation is a stranger. We Indians, whom the Gospel reached through the Apostle Thomas, are ourselves the foreigners whom God brought to his holy mountain.

The second reading takes us into the council in Jerusalem, where the apostles have been arrested again for preaching the name of Jesus. Some want to put them to death. Then a remarkable man stands up: “a Pharisee in the council named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law, respected by all the people.” He was the teacher at whose feet Saul of Tarsus had studied (Acts 22:3). Gamaliel reminds the council of other movements that rose and vanished, and gives advice of real wisdom: “keep away from these men and let them alone; because if this plan or this undertaking is of human origin, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them.” The Church remembers Gamaliel with honour: a devout Jewish teacher whose fear of God made him just. His words have been proved by two thousand years of history: empires that tried to destroy the Church have fallen, and the Church remains, not because of her own strength but because her work is of God. The apostles are flogged and released, and Luke records their response: “As they left the council, they rejoiced that they were considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name. And every day in the temple and at home they did not cease to teach and proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.” In the temple and at home: the faith was handed on in public worship and around family tables, as it has been in Kerala for centuries.

Paul's letter to the Ephesians begins with one long hymn of blessing, in the form of the great Jewish prayers of praise: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places.” In one breathless sentence Paul unfolds the whole plan of God. We were chosen in Christ “before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” We were destined “for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ.” “In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace that he lavished on us.” God's purpose is “to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” And then Paul speaks to us directly: “In him you also, when you had heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and had believed in him, were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit; this is the pledge of our inheritance.” The seal is the mark of ownership; the pledge is the first instalment, the advance that guarantees the full payment. The Holy Spirit given in Baptism and Chrismation is heaven already begun in us. The great prayer of thanksgiving in the Holy Qurbana, the Anaphora of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, one of the most ancient Eucharistic prayers in the world, sings this same blessing: praise to the Father who has done all this for us through his Son and in the Holy Spirit.

The Gospel brings us to the Last Supper, where Jesus speaks to frightened disciples: “Do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father's house there are many dwelling places… I go to prepare a place for you.” Then Saint Thomas asks the question that every honest disciple has asked: “Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” We Saint Thomas Christians should love this question. Our father in faith did not pretend to understand; he asked. And because he asked, we received one of the greatest sentences in the Gospel: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” Jesus does not give Thomas a map; he gives himself. The way to the Father is not a road we must find but a Person we must follow. The same Thomas who said ‘we do not know the way’ would one day kneel before the Risen Lord and say, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28), and would walk the way to India and lay down his life there. Then Philip asks, “Lord, show us the Father, and we will be satisfied,” and Jesus answers, “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” In the face of Jesus, the human face of God, we see who the Father is. And he promises that those who believe will do “greater works than these,” and that whatever we ask in his name he will do. Every Holy Qurbana ends with that promise: we have been fed by the Way, taught by the Truth, filled with the Life; now we go out on the road, asking in his name.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's readings ask: who belongs in God's house? Isaiah speaks to the foreigner who feared he would be shut out, and to the eunuch who called himself a dry tree. God says: I will give them an everlasting name, and my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.

After Easter this came true: one of the very first baptised from beyond Israel was an Ethiopian eunuch reading Isaiah. And we Indians, who received the Gospel through Saint Thomas, are the foreigners God brought to his holy mountain. In the Church no one is a stranger.

In Jerusalem the apostles are on trial. Gamaliel, a respected Pharisee and teacher of the law, gives wise counsel: if this is of human origin it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow it. Two thousand years have proved him right. The apostles are flogged, and they go out rejoicing, and every day, in the temple and at home, they proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.

Paul blesses God for every spiritual blessing: we were chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world, adopted as children, redeemed by his blood, and sealed with the Holy Spirit, the pledge of our inheritance. Our Qurbana, with the ancient Anaphora of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, sings this same blessing.

In the Gospel our own father in faith, Saint Thomas, asks: Lord, we do not know where you are going; how can we know the way? Jesus answers: I am the way, and the truth, and the life. He gives Thomas not a map but himself. And whoever has seen Jesus has seen the Father.

Thomas followed that Way all the way to India. This week, let us follow it too: welcome the stranger, trust God's work, bless the Father each morning, and bring our honest questions to Jesus. He is the way home. Alleluia! Amen.

Blessed are you, God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who have blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing. Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s readings open doors. Isaiah promises that foreigners and eunuchs, who feared they had no place among God’s people, will be brought to his holy mountain, because his house is “a house of prayer for all peoples”. In Acts the wise teacher Gamaliel advises the council to let the apostles alone, and the apostles go out rejoicing and keep proclaiming Jesus as the Messiah, in the Temple and at home. Ephesians sings the blessing of the Father who chose us, the Son who redeemed us, and the Spirit who seals us. In the Gospel Jesus tells his troubled friends that in his Father’s house there is room, and that he himself is the way there. The readings move from a promise of welcome, through witness under pressure and a hymn of blessing, to the Way who leads us home. The words to watch are house, name and way.

Isaiah 56:1–7 · A house of prayer for all peoples

Setting.

Many scholars think that chapters 56–66 of Isaiah come from the years after the return from Babylon, when the community in Jerusalem was rebuilding its life and asking who truly belonged to God’s people. Chapter 56 opens this last part of the book with a word of welcome.

Two groups felt uncertain. Foreigners had joined themselves to the Lord, perhaps during the exile. Eunuchs had been made so, often in the service of foreign courts. The Law in Deuteronomy 23:1–8 set limits on who could enter the assembly of the Lord. Here, from within Israel’s own Scriptures, the prophet speaks God’s word that those who hold fast to his covenant will not be shut out.

Structure.

  • v. 1: keep justice, for God’s salvation is near.
  • v. 2: blessed is the one who keeps the sabbath and does no evil.
  • v. 3: two fears: the foreigner’s and the eunuch’s.
  • vv. 4–5: to the faithful eunuchs: a monument and a name.
  • vv. 6–7: to the faithful foreigners: the holy mountain and the house of prayer for all peoples.

Key words.

  • sārîs, eunuch: A man who could have no children. In a world where a man lived on in his sons, the eunuch says, “I am just a dry tree.” God answers his deepest fear.
  • yād wāšēm, a monument and a name: Literally “a hand and a name”, a memorial that keeps someone’s name alive. Israel’s memorial in Jerusalem to the Jewish victims of the Holocaust takes its name, Yad Vashem, from this verse.
  • bêt təpillâ, house of prayer: The Temple is first of all a place of prayer, and it is meant for “all peoples”. Jesus quotes these words when he cleanses the Temple (Mark 11:17).

Verse by verse.

“Maintain justice, and do what is right, for soon my salvation will come, and my deliverance be revealed.” (56:1)

The order is the same as in the Gospel: God’s salvation is coming, so live rightly now.

“do not let the eunuch say, “I am just a dry tree.”” (56:3)

God does not let anyone define himself only by what he lacks.

“I will give, in my house and within my walls, a monument and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off.” (56:5)

A promise of a name that lasts, not through children, but through belonging to God’s house.

“for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.” (56:7)

This is the high point. The house of Israel’s God is meant to welcome all nations who come to him.

How the Church reads it.

In the Acts of the Apostles, Philip baptizes a eunuch from Ethiopia, a royal official returning from worship in Jerusalem and reading the prophet Isaiah (Acts 8:26–39). The Church sees in him this promise beginning to come true. The Second Vatican Council teaches that all people are called to belong to the new People of God, which is to be spread throughout the whole world (Lumen Gentium 13). The Church does not replace Israel in this; the gifts and the call of God to Israel are irrevocable (Romans 11:29; CCC 839).

Acts 5:34–42 · If it is of God

Setting.

For the second time the apostles have been arrested for preaching in the name of Jesus. An angel opened the prison, and they were found again teaching in the Temple (5:17–25). Before the council Peter said, “We must obey God rather than any human authority” (5:29). Some of the council were enraged and wanted to kill them (5:33). Then one man stands up.

Gamaliel, called “the Elder”, was one of the most honoured teachers of the Law in his day; Paul later says that he was “brought up … at the feet of Gamaliel” (22:3). Luke presents him with respect, as a wise and fair Jewish teacher. The two examples he gives were real movements. Judas the Galilean led a revolt at the census of AD 6. The Jewish historian Josephus mentions a Theudas who led a movement some years after the time of this speech, so scholars discuss whether Gamaliel had another Theudas in mind or whether Luke has arranged the speech in his own way. The point of the example is clear either way.

Structure.

  • vv. 34–35: Gamaliel has the apostles put outside and speaks to the council.
  • vv. 36–37: two leaders whose movements came to nothing.
  • vv. 38–39: his counsel: if it is of human origin, it will fail; if of God, you cannot stop it.
  • v. 40: the apostles are flogged and forbidden to speak in the name of Jesus.
  • vv. 41–42: they leave rejoicing, and every day proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.

Key words.

  • theomachoi, fighting against God: A single strong word: “God-fighters”. Gamaliel warns his colleagues that they may find themselves on the wrong side of God.
  • to onoma, the name: In the oldest manuscripts Luke says only “the name”, as Jews spoke reverently of “the Name” of God. Now the name of Jesus carries that weight: healing, preaching and suffering are all “for the name”.
  • katēxiōthēsan atimasthēnai, considered worthy to suffer dishonor: A deliberate paradox: they were honoured to be dishonoured. To share Christ’s shame is a privilege.

Verse by verse.

“But a Pharisee in the council named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law, respected by all the people, stood up” (5:34)

Not every member of the council opposed the apostles. Luke shows a Pharisee speaking for patience and fairness.

“because if this plan or this undertaking is of human origin, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them” (5:38–39)

Good advice, and Luke lets the story prove it true. The Church’s survival through the centuries is itself a kind of answer to Gamaliel’s question, though success alone is never the measure of truth.

“As they left the council, they rejoiced that they were considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name.” (5:41)

Joy after a flogging. The Holy Spirit has changed men who once ran away in fear.

“And every day in the temple and at home they did not cease to teach and proclaim Jesus as the Messiah.” (5:42)

Two places for the Gospel: the house of prayer and the family home. The Church grew in both.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls that from the beginning the core of the Church was often formed by those who had become believers “together with all their household”, and so the Christian family is called the domestic church (CCC 1655–1656; Lumen Gentium 11). St Peter writes in the same spirit as today’s passage: “rejoice insofar as you are sharing Christ’s sufferings” (1 Peter 4:13). The Church does not seek persecution, but she knows that the joy of the risen Lord is stronger than shame.

Ephesians 1:3–14 · Blessed be the God and Father

Setting.

Ephesians opens with a long prayer of blessing. In Greek, vv. 3–14 are a single sentence, one of the longest in the New Testament, as if the writer cannot stop praising. Its form is like the Jewish berakah, the blessing that begins “Blessed are you, Lord our God”.

In some of the oldest manuscripts the words “in Ephesus” are missing from 1:1, and many think the letter was meant to be read in several churches. Many scholars also think it was written by a disciple of Paul in his name; others hold that Paul wrote it. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture.

Structure.

  • v. 3: blessed be God, who has blessed us in Christ.
  • vv. 4–6: the Father chose us and destined us to be his children; “to the praise of his glorious grace”.
  • vv. 7–10: in the Son, redemption through his blood and the mystery of his will: to gather up all things in Christ.
  • vv. 11–12: our inheritance; “for the praise of his glory”.
  • vv. 13–14: you were sealed with the Holy Spirit, the pledge of our inheritance; “to the praise of his glory”.
  • ¶ The three refrains mark the work of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.

Key words.

  • anakephalaiōsasthai, to gather up: Literally “to sum up under one head”. All things in heaven and earth find their unity and meaning in Christ.
  • esphragisthēte, you were marked with the seal: A seal marked ownership and protection. The believer belongs to God and is kept by the Spirit.
  • arrabōn, pledge: A down payment, the first instalment that guarantees the rest. The Spirit we have now is a real share in the glory to come.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places” (1:3)

God blesses us first; our blessing of God is the answer. All of it is “in Christ”, a phrase that comes again and again in these verses.

“In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses” (1:7)

Redemption is the language of setting a slave or captive at liberty at a price. The price is the blood of Christ.

“as a plan for the fullness of time, to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” (1:10)

Not only souls are saved; the whole creation is drawn into Christ.

“were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit” (1:13)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism begins its teaching on the Liturgy with this hymn: the whole work of God is a blessing, and the Church answers with blessing (CCC 1077–1078). It also draws on these verses for God’s eternal plan of loving kindness, that we should be his adopted children (CCC 257). St Irenaeus built his teaching on Christ’s “recapitulation” on v. 10: Christ sums up in himself the whole history of humanity, to heal and restore it (Against Heresies, in substance; see CCC 518). The Church links the seal of the Spirit with the anointing of Chrismation, which in the Eastern Churches is given together with Baptism (CCC 1296).

John 14:1–14 · I am the way, and the truth, and the life

Setting.

It is the night of the Last Supper. Jesus has washed his disciples’ feet, Judas has gone out into the night, and Jesus has told Peter that he will deny him three times (13:36–38). The disciples are afraid. Chapters 14–17 are Jesus’ farewell words to them.

Jesus himself had been “troubled” at the tomb of Lazarus (11:33), at the thought of his hour (12:27) and at the betrayal (13:21). John uses the same verb here. The one who says “Do not let your hearts be troubled” knows what trouble is.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: trust, the Father’s house, a place prepared.
  • vv. 5–7: Thomas asks about the way; Jesus answers, “I am the way.”
  • vv. 8–11: Philip asks to see the Father; “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.”
  • vv. 12–14: greater works, and prayer in Jesus’ name.

Key words.

  • monai, dwelling places: From the verb menō, to remain or abide, one of John’s favourite words. A monē is a place to stay, not an inn for a night but a home.
  • hodos, way: Very early, the followers of Jesus were simply called “the Way” (Acts 9:2). Jesus does not only show the way; he is the way.
  • egō eimi, I am: One of the “I am” sayings of John’s Gospel (bread, light, gate, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine). They echo the name God revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14).

Verse by verse.

“Do not let your hearts be troubled. Believe in God, believe also in me.” (14:1)

Jesus asks for the same trust in himself that Israel gives to God. That is already a claim about who he is.

“In my Father’s house there are many dwelling places.” (14:2)

There is room. No one who comes to the Father through the Son will find the door shut for lack of space.

“Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?” (14:5)

Thomas asks the honest question many hearts ask. His question draws out one of the greatest sayings in the Gospel.

“I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” (14:6)

The way to the Father is not a method but a person, and he is also the goal: the truth to know and the life to share.

“Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.” (14:9)

Jesus is the face of the Father. To look at his words and deeds, and above all at his Cross, is to see what God is like.

“the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these, because I am going to the Father.” (14:12)

The “greater works” are possible only because he goes to the Father and sends the Spirit. They are his works through the Church.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine says that Christ as God is the homeland to which we go, and Christ as man is the way by which we go (Sermons, in substance). The Catechism teaches that Christ’s whole life is a revelation of the Father, and quotes v. 9 (CCC 516). It also teaches that prayer “in the name of Jesus” is prayer in communion with him, and that the Father gives whatever we ask in that name (CCC 2614). The Second Vatican Council, while it rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions, says that the Church proclaims, and must always proclaim, Christ, “the way, the truth, and the life” (Nostra Aetate 2).

Questions people ask

Does “No one comes to the Father except through me” mean that people of other religions cannot be saved?

Every salvation comes from Christ through his Church, which is his Body (CCC 846). But those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel or the Church, yet seek God with a sincere heart and, moved by grace, try to do his will as their conscience shows it, can also reach eternal salvation (CCC 847; Lumen Gentium 16). Even then they are saved through Christ, by ways known to God. This is no reason for the Church to stop proclaiming him (CCC 848).

How can believers do “greater works” than Jesus?

Not because they are greater than he is, but because he goes to the Father and works through them by the Spirit. The apostles brought the Gospel to lands Jesus never visited in his earthly life. St Augustine, quoted in the Catechism, says that the justification of a sinner is a greater work than the creation of heaven and earth (CCC 1994).

Why did Gamaliel defend the apostles?

Luke shows him as a wise and devout Pharisee, faithful to the Scriptures, who believed that God would show which movements came from him. He did not become a disciple in Luke’s account, but his fairness protected the apostles that day. His counsel shows that even those outside the Church may serve God’s plan with justice.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In today’s Gospel it is Thomas who asks, “How can we know the way?”, and to him Jesus first says, “I am the way.” The Syro-Malabar Church, whose faith goes back to the preaching of Mar Thoma, has long called its own Christian heritage the Margam, the Way, of Thomas. That way is nothing other than Christ. The Syriac Bible uses raza, mystery, for the “mystery of his will” in Ephesians, and the solemn form of our Holy Qurbana is called the Raza: in the Qurbana the plan of the Father, the redemption by the blood of the Son and the seal of the Spirit are celebrated as one. And as the apostles taught “at home”, our families keep the Gospel alive in the evening family prayer.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah gives the eunuch “a name better than sons and daughters”. Whom does my parish or my family make feel like “a dry tree”, and how could we welcome them?
  • The apostles rejoiced to suffer dishonour for the name of Jesus. What would it cost me this week to speak his name openly, at work or at home?
  • Ephesians says that I was chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world. How does it change my day to know that I am wanted by God?
  • Thomas asked, “How can we know the way?” What question would I ask Jesus today, and where do I see him answering it in the Gospel?
