Word for the day

“As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” John 9:5

Reflection

On the Third Sunday of Kaitha the Church opens with the Ten Commandments, but the first word is not a command. “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” Before God asks anything, he recalls what he has done: the rescue of a people of slaves. The commandments are the way of life for people already saved, the house rules of a father for the children he has carried out of Egypt. “You shall have no other gods before me. You shall not make for yourself an idol.” The jealous God is the lover who will not share his beloved with anything that cannot love her back. The text speaks of the damage of sin that passes from parents to children, and sets beside it the greater truth that God shows “steadfast love to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments”; the prophet Ezekiel teaches clearly that each person answers for his own sin (Ezekiel 18:20). Then come the Name and the Sabbath: “You shall not make wrongful use of the name of the Lord your God.” “Observe the sabbath day and keep it holy, as the Lord your God commanded you.” Deuteronomy gives the Sabbath a reason of mercy and of memory: rest for the worker, and “Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm.” The Church keeps the first day of the week, the day of the Lord's Resurrection, in the same spirit: rest for all, especially for those who work for us, and thanksgiving to the God who saves. The reading ends with the commandment that opens the second table of the law: “Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you.”

Isaiah sings what sounds like a love-song: “Let me sing for my beloved my love-song concerning his vineyard: My beloved had a vineyard on a very fertile hill. He dug it and cleared it of stones, and planted it with choice vines; he built a watchtower in the midst of it, and hewed out a wine vat in it; he expected it to yield grapes, but it yielded wild grapes.” Every verb is a work of love: digging, clearing, planting, building. Then the singer turns to the listeners and asks them to judge: “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it? When I expected it to yield grapes, why did it yield wild grapes?” The question is the cry of a God who has done everything and whose love has been met with fruit that tastes sour. Then the verdict, and the meaning of the song: “For the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel, and the people of Judah are his pleasant planting; he expected justice, but saw bloodshed; righteousness, but heard a cry!” In Hebrew the words sound alike: mishpat (justice) and mispach (bloodshed), tsedaqah (righteousness) and tse'aqah (a cry). Wherever people use their power to hurt the weak, the cry of the wronged goes up. Isaiah speaks to God's own people, and the Church hears it as addressed to herself. Anyone who has tended pepper vines or paddy in Kerala knows the labour and the waiting for the harvest; the Lord waits in the same way for the fruit of our lives. And in John's Gospel Jesus names himself the true vine and says, “Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit.”

Paul's letter shows how the vineyard bears fruit. He begins with the promises, “Since we have these promises, beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and of spirit, making holiness perfect in the fear of God,” and then he opens his heart: “Make room in your hearts for us.” He tells of his own trial in Macedonia: “disputes without and fears within.” “But God, who consoles the downcast, consoled us by the arrival of Titus.” The center of the reading is a distinction every Christian must learn: “Now I rejoice, not because you were grieved, but because your grief led to repentance; for you felt a godly grief... For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” Worldly grief is sorrow that looks at itself: it is shame at being found out, regret for what it cost me, and it ends in bitterness or despair. Godly grief looks at God and at the harm done to others; it ends in a turning back. The Church's tradition has seen the two in the two apostles who failed: Judas, who was filled with remorse but did not turn to the Lord, and Peter, who “went out and wept bitterly” and was restored. Saint Ambrose said of Peter (in substance): I do not find what he said in his defence; I find that he wept. Paul lists the fruits: “what earnestness this godly grief has produced in you, what eagerness to clear yourselves, what indignation, what alarm, what longing, what zeal.” This is the vineyard's grapes. The Church offers the sacrament of Reconciliation, our confession, as the place where godly grief becomes joy.

The Gospel is a story of sight. “As he walked along, he saw a man blind from birth. His disciples asked him, ‘Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?’” People then, and now, ask where to put the blame. Jesus refuses the question: “Neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God’s works might be revealed in him.” Suffering is not always a punishment; it can be the place where the work of God will be seen. “We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming when no one can work. As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” Then the strange action: “he spat on the ground and made mud with the saliva and spread the mud on the man’s eyes, saying to him, ‘Go, wash in the pool of Siloam’ (which means Sent).” The Fathers saw here the Word made flesh using earth, like the creator who made the first man from the clay, and they saw in the pool of Siloam, “Sent”, the baptism that Jesus, the Sent One, gives; baptism was long called “illumination”. The man does what he is told: “Then he went and washed and came back able to see.” His neighbours cannot believe it: “Is this not the man who used to sit and beg?” He says simply, “I am the man.” When they ask who healed him he says only, “The man called Jesus made mud, spread it on my eyes, and said to me, ‘Go to Siloam and wash.’ Then I went and washed and received my sight.” “Where is he?” “I do not know.” His sight is real, and his faith has hardly begun.

The reading then skips what follows, the questioning by those who disliked what had happened and the man's growing courage, and returns at the end: “Jesus heard that they had driven him out, and when he found him, he said, ‘Do you believe in the Son of Man?’” The Lord goes looking for the one who has been cast out. The man answers, “And who is he, sir? Tell me, so that I may believe in him.” Jesus said to him, “You have seen him, and the one speaking with you is he.” Then the whole journey comes to rest in one sentence: “He said, ‘Lord, I believe.’ And he worshiped him.” First he called Jesus “the man called Jesus”; now he says “Lord”. The eyes opened first; the heart is opened later; and the first sight of his life ends with worship. Put the four readings together and the shape of the Season of Kaitha appears: the law of the God who has saved us, the vineyard that must bear fruit, the grief that turns us back, and the light that lets us see the Lord and say, “Lord, I believe.”

Homily outline

Theme: “As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.”

On the Third Sunday of Kaitha: the God who saves before he commands, the vineyard that must bear fruit, the grief that turns us back, and the sight that ends in worship.

1. I am the Lord your God, who brought you out

  • Grace before law.
  • The Ten Words and the Lord's Day.
  • Honour your father and your mother.
“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery;” Deuteronomy 5:6
““If you love me, you will keep my commandments.” John 14:15

Apply: Keep this Sunday as a true day of rest and of the Lord, with your family.

2. What more was there to do for my vineyard?

  • The work of the beloved.
  • Wild grapes instead of grapes.
  • Justice and righteousness as fruit.
“he expected justice, but saw bloodshed; righteousness, but heard a cry!” Isaiah 5:7
“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” John 15:5

Apply: Name one fruit of justice or mercy that you will give this month.

3. A godly grief

  • Worldly grief and godly grief.
  • Judas and Peter.
  • The fruits of repentance.
“rend your hearts and not your clothing. Return to the Lord, your God, for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love,” Joel 2:13
“For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” 2 Corinthians 7:10

Apply: Make a good confession this month, and repair one wrong.

4. Lord, I believe

  • The man born blind: mud, Siloam, the Sent One.
  • Sight grows into faith.
  • Jesus goes looking for the one who was driven out.
“the Lord opens the eyes of the blind.” Psalm 146:8
“He said, “Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him.” John 9:38

Apply: Each day this week, ask the Lord to open your eyes to one person or one need you have not seen.

The God who saves gives the law, tends the vineyard, turns us back with godly grief and opens our eyes.

Let us keep his day, bear his fruit, repent with our whole heart and say, “Lord, I believe.”

Take-home resolution: This week, keep the Lord's Day, repair one wrong, and ask the Lord to open your eyes to someone you have not seen.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church opens with the Ten Commandments. But notice the first word: not “Do this”, but “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery” (Deuteronomy 5:6). God saves first, and then he asks us to live as people he has saved.

Among the commandments, the Sabbath: a day of rest for everyone, even for those who work for us. For us Christians, Sunday is the Lord's Day, the day of the Resurrection. Let us keep it: come to the Qurbana, rest, spend time with the family. And let us honour our fathers and mothers, who gave us life.

Isaiah sings of a vineyard. The owner did everything: dug it, cleared the stones, planted choice vines, built a tower. He expected grapes, and got wild grapes (Isaiah 5:2). He asks, “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it?” God has done everything for us. What fruit does he find in our families, our parishes, our streets? Justice, or bloodshed? Righteousness, or a cry?

Paul tells us how to bear fruit again: with a godly grief. Worldly grief is sorry for being caught. Godly grief is sorry for having hurt God and others, and it turns us back (2 Corinthians 7:10). Peter wept and was restored. We too can go to confession and begin again.

And in the Gospel Jesus opens the eyes of a man born blind. The man did what Jesus told him, and he saw. And when Jesus found him again, he said, “Lord, I believe,” and worshiped him (John 9:38). Let us ask the Lord today to open our eyes, so that we may see him and say the same. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus made a blind man see! He put mud on his eyes and told him to wash. The man did what Jesus told him, and he could see! Then he said, “Lord, I believe.” Jesus is the light of the world. Let us always do what Jesus says, and we will see how good he is.

Prayer

Lord our God, you brought your people out of slavery and gave them a law of love; you planted your vineyard with the care of a lover and looked for justice. Give us the grief that turns us back to you and the fruit that pleases you. As you opened the eyes of the man born blind, open ours to see your works and to worship you. Teach us to keep your Sunday with joy, to honour our parents, to love our neighbour, and to say with our whole life, “Lord, I believe.” We ask this through Jesus Christ, the light of the world, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.
