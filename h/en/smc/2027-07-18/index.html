Word for the day

“As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” John 9:5

Reflection

On the Third Sunday of Kaitha the Church opens with the Ten Commandments, but the first word is not a command. “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” Before God asks anything, he recalls what he has done: the rescue of a people of slaves. The commandments are the way of life for people already saved, the house rules of a father for the children he has carried out of Egypt. “You shall have no other gods before me. You shall not make for yourself an idol.” The jealous God is the lover who will not share his beloved with anything that cannot love her back. The text speaks of the damage of sin that passes from parents to children, and sets beside it the greater truth that God shows “steadfast love to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments”; the prophet Ezekiel teaches clearly that each person answers for his own sin (Ezekiel 18:20). Then come the Name and the Sabbath: “You shall not make wrongful use of the name of the Lord your God.” “Observe the sabbath day and keep it holy, as the Lord your God commanded you.” Deuteronomy gives the Sabbath a reason of mercy and of memory: rest for the worker, and “Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm.” The Church keeps the first day of the week, the day of the Lord's Resurrection, in the same spirit: rest for all, especially for those who work for us, and thanksgiving to the God who saves. The reading ends with the commandment that opens the second table of the law: “Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you, so that your days may be long and that it may go well with you.”

Isaiah sings what sounds like a love-song: “Let me sing for my beloved my love-song concerning his vineyard: My beloved had a vineyard on a very fertile hill. He dug it and cleared it of stones, and planted it with choice vines; he built a watchtower in the midst of it, and hewed out a wine vat in it; he expected it to yield grapes, but it yielded wild grapes.” Every verb is a work of love: digging, clearing, planting, building. Then the singer turns to the listeners and asks them to judge: “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it? When I expected it to yield grapes, why did it yield wild grapes?” The question is the cry of a God who has done everything and whose love has been met with fruit that tastes sour. Then the verdict, and the meaning of the song: “For the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel, and the people of Judah are his pleasant planting; he expected justice, but saw bloodshed; righteousness, but heard a cry!” In Hebrew the words sound alike: mishpat (justice) and mispach (bloodshed), tsedaqah (righteousness) and tse'aqah (a cry). Wherever people use their power to hurt the weak, the cry of the wronged goes up. Isaiah speaks to God's own people, and the Church hears it as addressed to herself. Anyone who has tended pepper vines or paddy in Kerala knows the labour and the waiting for the harvest; the Lord waits in the same way for the fruit of our lives. And in John's Gospel Jesus names himself the true vine and says, “Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit.”

Paul's letter shows how the vineyard bears fruit. He begins with the promises, “Since we have these promises, beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and of spirit, making holiness perfect in the fear of God,” and then he opens his heart: “Make room in your hearts for us.” He tells of his own trial in Macedonia: “disputes without and fears within.” “But God, who consoles the downcast, consoled us by the arrival of Titus.” The center of the reading is a distinction every Christian must learn: “Now I rejoice, not because you were grieved, but because your grief led to repentance; for you felt a godly grief... For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” Worldly grief is sorrow that looks at itself: it is shame at being found out, regret for what it cost me, and it ends in bitterness or despair. Godly grief looks at God and at the harm done to others; it ends in a turning back. The Church's tradition has seen the two in the two apostles who failed: Judas, who was filled with remorse but did not turn to the Lord, and Peter, who “went out and wept bitterly” and was restored. Saint Ambrose said of Peter (in substance): I do not find what he said in his defence; I find that he wept. Paul lists the fruits: “what earnestness this godly grief has produced in you, what eagerness to clear yourselves, what indignation, what alarm, what longing, what zeal.” This is the vineyard's grapes. The Church offers the sacrament of Reconciliation, our confession, as the place where godly grief becomes joy.

The Gospel is a story of sight. “As he walked along, he saw a man blind from birth. His disciples asked him, ‘Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?’” People then, and now, ask where to put the blame. Jesus refuses the question: “Neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God’s works might be revealed in him.” Suffering is not always a punishment; it can be the place where the work of God will be seen. “We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming when no one can work. As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” Then the strange action: “he spat on the ground and made mud with the saliva and spread the mud on the man’s eyes, saying to him, ‘Go, wash in the pool of Siloam’ (which means Sent).” The Fathers saw here the Word made flesh using earth, like the creator who made the first man from the clay, and they saw in the pool of Siloam, “Sent”, the baptism that Jesus, the Sent One, gives; baptism was long called “illumination”. The man does what he is told: “Then he went and washed and came back able to see.” His neighbours cannot believe it: “Is this not the man who used to sit and beg?” He says simply, “I am the man.” When they ask who healed him he says only, “The man called Jesus made mud, spread it on my eyes, and said to me, ‘Go to Siloam and wash.’ Then I went and washed and received my sight.” “Where is he?” “I do not know.” His sight is real, and his faith has hardly begun.

The reading then skips what follows, the questioning by those who disliked what had happened and the man's growing courage, and returns at the end: “Jesus heard that they had driven him out, and when he found him, he said, ‘Do you believe in the Son of Man?’” The Lord goes looking for the one who has been cast out. The man answers, “And who is he, sir? Tell me, so that I may believe in him.” Jesus said to him, “You have seen him, and the one speaking with you is he.” Then the whole journey comes to rest in one sentence: “He said, ‘Lord, I believe.’ And he worshiped him.” First he called Jesus “the man called Jesus”; now he says “Lord”. The eyes opened first; the heart is opened later; and the first sight of his life ends with worship. Put the four readings together and the shape of the Season of Kaitha appears: the law of the God who has saved us, the vineyard that must bear fruit, the grief that turns us back, and the light that lets us see the Lord and say, “Lord, I believe.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church opens with the Ten Commandments. But notice the first word: not “Do this”, but “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery” (Deuteronomy 5:6). God saves first, and then he asks us to live as people he has saved.

Among the commandments, the Sabbath: a day of rest for everyone, even for those who work for us. For us Christians, Sunday is the Lord's Day, the day of the Resurrection. Let us keep it: come to the Qurbana, rest, spend time with the family. And let us honour our fathers and mothers, who gave us life.

Isaiah sings of a vineyard. The owner did everything: dug it, cleared the stones, planted choice vines, built a tower. He expected grapes, and got wild grapes (Isaiah 5:2). He asks, “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it?” God has done everything for us. What fruit does he find in our families, our parishes, our streets? Justice, or bloodshed? Righteousness, or a cry?

Paul tells us how to bear fruit again: with a godly grief. Worldly grief is sorry for being caught. Godly grief is sorry for having hurt God and others, and it turns us back (2 Corinthians 7:10). Peter wept and was restored. We too can go to confession and begin again.

And in the Gospel Jesus opens the eyes of a man born blind. The man did what Jesus told him, and he saw. And when Jesus found him again, he said, “Lord, I believe,” and worshiped him (John 9:38). Let us ask the Lord today to open our eyes, so that we may see him and say the same. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus made a blind man see! He put mud on his eyes and told him to wash. The man did what Jesus told him, and he could see! Then he said, “Lord, I believe.” Jesus is the light of the world. Let us always do what Jesus says, and we will see how good he is.

Prayer

Lord our God, you brought your people out of slavery and gave them a law of love; you planted your vineyard with the care of a lover and looked for justice. Give us the grief that turns us back to you and the fruit that pleases you. As you opened the eyes of the man born blind, open ours to see your works and to worship you. Teach us to keep your Sunday with joy, to honour our parents, to love our neighbour, and to say with our whole life, “Lord, I believe.” We ask this through Jesus Christ, the light of the world, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The four readings of this Sunday begin with something God has already given, and then ask what grows from it. In Deuteronomy, the Ten Words open with a gift: “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt”, and only then come the commands. In Isaiah, a friend sings of a vineyard planted with every care, and asks, “What more was there to do?”, when it yielded wild grapes. Paul, writing to the Corinthians who have been hurt by his earlier letter, rejoices that their sorrow led to repentance, not despair. And in John, a man born blind is given sight by Jesus, and with sight comes faith: “Lord, I believe.” Light, grace and love come first; what God hopes for in return is trust, justice and a heart that turns. The Gospel shows that harvest in one person, who goes from darkness to worship.

Deuteronomy 5:6–16 · I am the Lord your God, who brought you out

Setting.

Deuteronomy is Moses’ great farewell address to Israel on the plains of Moab, before they cross the Jordan. In chapter 5 he calls the people together and repeats the Ten Commandments that were given at Horeb (Sinai), which are also found in Exodus 20. Moses speaks as if the people were all standing at the mountain: “The Lord our God made a covenant with us at Horeb” (5:2). The covenant belongs to every generation.

The Ten Commandments are called in Hebrew the “ten words” (Deuteronomy 4:13; 10:4). The reading today covers the first four in the Catholic way of counting (CCC 2066): the worship of the one God and no idols (vv. 6–10), the name of the Lord (v. 11), the Sabbath (vv. 12–15), and honour for father and mother (v. 16). The remaining commandments, on love of neighbour, follow in verses 17–21. Deuteronomy’s form of the Sabbath commandment differs from Exodus 20:11: Exodus gives the Lord’s rest at creation as the reason; Deuteronomy gives the memory of slavery in Egypt.

Structure.

  • v. 6: the prologue: the Lord who brought Israel out of slavery.
  • vv. 7–10: no other gods, no idols; the jealous God who shows steadfast love to a thousand generations.
  • v. 11: do not misuse the Lord’s name.
  • vv. 12–15: the Sabbath: six days of work, the seventh for the Lord; rest for all, including servants and the resident alien; remember the slavery in Egypt.
  • v. 16: honour father and mother, that your days may be long in the land.

Key words.

  • bêt ʿăbādîm, house of slavery: Egypt is called a “house” of slaves, a place where people were treated as property. The Lord’s first action in the commandments is liberation. The commandments are given to a people who have been released from slavery, to keep them so.
  • qannāʾ, jealous: Not the petty jealousy of envy but the exclusive love of a husband or wife who cannot bear to share the beloved. The prophets use the same image of the marriage covenant. It shows the passion of God’s love, and it is why idols betray the relationship.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love, faithful love: The Lord’s loyal, covenant love, which does not give up. The verse sets it beside punishment: punishment reaches to the third and fourth generation, but steadfast love reaches to the thousandth.
  • zākar, to remember: In Hebrew “remember” means more than recalling; it means to act on the memory. The Sabbath, resting with servants and strangers, is how Israel remembers what the Lord did for them.

Verse by verse.

“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery” (5:6)

Every commandment stands under this sentence. First the Lord says who he is and what he has done; then he says what he asks. Obedience is a response to love already received.

“but showing steadfast love to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments.” (5:10)

The verse before it speaks of sin’s consequences spreading through families. Here the proportion is stated: grace outruns judgement a thousand times over. The Church reads it with Ezekiel 18, where each person answers for his or her own sin, and with the mercy shown in Christ.

“Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm” (5:15)

The Sabbath is a day when no one is a slave: not the child, the servant, the stranger or even the ox. Rest is a sign of liberation, and it is a gift for all.

“Honor your father and your mother, as the Lord your God commanded you” (5:16)

The first commandment about people. Family is the first school of faith and the first place where commandments are lived.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Ten Commandments must be understood in light of the Exodus: God first liberates, and then gives the Law (CCC 2056–2057). The Church’s tradition keeps them as the path of love of God and neighbour, and Jesus did not abolish them but fulfilled them (Matthew 5:17). The Sabbath, a day of rest and remembrance, is kept by Christians on Sunday, the Lord’s Day, the day of the Resurrection (CCC 2174–2176), and the Church calls Sunday the day when the Church gathers for the Holy Qurbana.

Isaiah 5:1–7 · The song of the vineyard

Setting.

The prophet Isaiah worked in Jerusalem in the eighth century before Christ, during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. The “Song of the Vineyard” is placed at the end of the first great section of the book, the opening chapters. It is one of the most famous poems in the Old Testament. Isaiah is a son of the people he addresses, and he sings the song as a love song that turns into a legal case.

Many think the song was first sung at a vintage or harvest festival, where a singer might entertain the crowd with a love song. Isaiah begins, “Let me sing for my beloved” and the crowd is charmed. Only in verse 3 is it clear that the audience is also the accused: “inhabitants of Jerusalem and people of Judah”.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the love-song: the beloved’s vineyard, the care and the expectation; it yields wild grapes.
  • vv. 3–4: the appeal to the audience: judge between me and my vineyard.
  • vv. 5–6: the verdict: the hedge removed, the wall broken, the vineyard made a waste.
  • v. 7: the explanation: the vineyard is the house of Israel; the Lord expected justice and heard a cry.

Key words.

  • dôdî, my beloved: A word for a lover. In the poem the singer speaks for his friend, and the friend turns out to be the Lord. The prophet’s own love for his people is part of the song.
  • bĕʾušîm, wild grapes: Literally “stinking things”, sour or bad-smelling fruit, which have the look of grapes and are useless. The point is the contrast between what was expected and what grew.
  • mišpāṭ … miśpāḥ; ṣĕdāqâ … ṣĕʿāqâ, justice … bloodshed; righteousness … a cry: A sound-play in Hebrew that cannot be kept in translation: the Lord looked for mishpat, justice, and found mispach, bloodshed; he looked for tsedaqah, righteousness, and heard tse‘aqah, the cry of the oppressed.

Verse by verse.

“Let me sing for my beloved my love-song concerning his vineyard” (5:1)

Prophecy begins with love. God’s dealings with his people are the story of a lover who has given everything.

“What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it?” (5:4)

The Lord does not blame the soil or the planter. He has given everything and is saddened, not angry in a cold way. The question invites a real answer: what is wrong with us, when God has done all?

“he expected justice, but saw bloodshed; righteousness, but heard a cry!” (5:7)

The “fruit” is not religious feeling but justice among people: the cry of the poor and the wronged. Isaiah’s message to his own nation is the one that the Church hears for itself.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the vineyard also as a sign of herself and of every believer, and this is why the Lord says, “I am the vine, you are the branches” (John 15:1–5; CCC 755). Jesus’ own parable of the tenants (Matthew 21:33–43) takes up Isaiah’s song, aimed at the leaders who failed to give the owner the fruit. The Church does not read Isaiah’s song as God’s rejection of the Jewish people: Isaiah himself was a Jewish prophet speaking to his own people out of love, and Paul says clearly that God has not rejected his people (Romans 11:1, 29; Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839). The warning is for every generation of those who have been given much, including ourselves.

2 Corinthians 7:1–11 · Godly grief

Setting.

This passage comes at the emotional turning point of the letter. Paul had sent the Corinthians a severe letter, probably written “out of much distress and anguish of heart and with many tears” (2:4), and he did not know how they would respond. He waited anxiously for Titus; when Titus came with good news, his relief was great.

Verse 1 gathers the promises at the end of chapter 6 (that God will live among his people and be a Father to them) into a call to holiness: “let us cleanse ourselves” (7:1). Then Paul asks the community to open their hearts again to him, and he explains why he is joyful: the letter that hurt them led them to a change of heart. Some scholars think the “severe letter” is lost, or is part of 2 Corinthians 10–13; scholars are divided.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the promises call us to holiness.
  • vv. 2–4: “Make room in your hearts for us”; Paul’s love and pride in them.
  • vv. 5–7: affliction in Macedonia; God consoles through Titus.
  • vv. 8–9: the painful letter; sorrow that led to repentance.
  • vv. 10–11: godly grief versus worldly grief; the fruit of repentance.

Key words.

  • lypē kata theon, godly grief (literally, sorrow according to God): Sorrow that sees sin in God’s light: it is painful but hopeful, and it turns toward him. The other kind, “worldly grief”, looks only at the damage to oneself or to one’s name, and ends in bitterness or despair.
  • metanoia, repentance, change of mind: From meta (change) and nous (mind). Repentance is more than feeling sorry; it is turning life around. Paul says it “leads to salvation and brings no regret”.
  • spoudē, earnestness, eagerness: In verse 11 Paul lists what godly sorrow has produced: earnestness, eagerness to clear themselves, indignation, alarm, longing, zeal. Real repentance is full of energy.

Verse by verse.

“Make room in your hearts for us” (7:2)

Paul asks for a place in their hearts. A relationship damaged by hard words is healed by open hearts on both sides.

“But God, who consoles the downcast, consoled us by the arrival of Titus” (7:6)

God’s consolation often comes through a person. The Church sees in this how God uses ordinary friends as his instruments.

“Now I rejoice, not because you were grieved, but because your grief led to repentance” (7:9)

Paul does not enjoy causing pain. A correction made in love is justified only by the good it brings.

“For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death.” (7:10)

A summary of the Christian way of dealing with sin. Peter’s tears led to restoration; Judas’s remorse ended in despair. The difference is whether sorrow turns toward God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that interior repentance is a radical reorientation of the whole life, a return to God with all our heart (CCC 1431), and that contrition, sorrow of the soul with the resolution not to sin again, is the first step in the sacrament of Penance (CCC 1451). The Church sees in godly grief a gift of grace, since the human heart is hard and God must give a new heart (CCC 1432). It does not paralyse; it opens the door to forgiveness.

John 9:1–12, 35–38 · The man born blind

Setting.

John 9 is a single long scene: a healing (vv. 1–7), the neighbours’ questions (vv. 8–12), the inquiry before the Pharisees and the man’s parents (vv. 13–34, not read today), and the meeting with Jesus (vv. 35–41). It takes place on a Sabbath (9:14), and follows the chapters set at the Feast of Tabernacles (7–8), a feast in which light was a central image. Jesus has said in 8:12, “I am the light of the world.”

The reading omits the middle, where those who question the man, “the Pharisees” and “the Jews”, are the religious authorities, and the man’s parents fear being put out of the synagogue (9:22). This reflects tensions in the time of the Gospel’s writing, which many scholars place near the end of the first century, and must never be read as blame on the Jewish people. Jesus, his disciples, the blind man and his parents are all Jews.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the disciples’ question about sin; Jesus’ answer: God’s works are to be revealed.
  • vv. 4–5: night is coming; “I am the light of the world.”
  • vv. 6–7: the mud and the pool of Siloam; the man washes and sees.
  • vv. 8–12: the neighbours’ disbelief; “I am the man”; “Where is he?” “I do not know.”
  • (vv. 13–34 are not read: the questioning of the man, the parents and the expulsion.)
  • vv. 35–38: Jesus finds him; “Do you believe in the Son of Man?”; “Lord, I believe.”

Key words.

  • apestalmenos, sent: John explains that “Siloam” means Sent. Jesus is the one sent by the Father (9:4), and the man is sent to a pool that bears the same name. The pool of Siloam also appears in Isaiah 8:6 as “the waters of Shiloah that flow gently”.
  • phōs tou kosmou, light of the world: Light is the main image of the chapter. For John, to see is to come to faith. The healed man sees step by step: first “the man called Jesus”, then “a prophet”, then “from God”, then “Lord”.
  • proskynein, to worship, to bow down: The word used for worship of God. In John it appears only here for Jesus, as the man bows before the Son of Man. He has moved from blindness to adoration.

Verse by verse.

“Neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God’s works might be revealed in him.” (9:3)

Jesus refuses to explain every suffering as punishment for sin. He turns the disciples’ attention from blame to what God will do. This does not mean that God sends the blindness for show; it says that God can bring his works out of it.

“As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world.” (9:5)

The light does not stay far away; it comes close, with mud and water and a command to go and wash.

“Go, wash in the pool of Siloam” (which means Sent). Then he went and washed and came back able to see.” (9:7)

The man does what he is told, though he cannot yet see why. The first step of faith is obedience before sight.

“Do you believe in the Son of Man?” (9:35)

After the man has been put out, Jesus finds him. Those who have been cast out are sought by the Lord.

““Lord, I believe.” And he worshiped him.” (9:38)

The healing ends not with sight but with worship. His physical sight is the sign; his faith is the gift.

How the Church reads it.

From the earliest centuries the Church has read this story as a picture of Baptism, in which the one who washes receives light; for that reason the Catechism notes that Baptism is called “enlightenment” (CCC 1216). St Irenaeus (in substance) saw in the clay a sign that the same hand that formed Adam from the earth was now completing the man’s creation. St Augustine (in substance) taught that the blind man stands for the whole human race, born in the dark, who is lit by Christ. The Catechism also says that Jesus’ healings are signs of the kingdom, though he did not come to abolish every earthly evil (CCC 548–549).

Questions people ask

Jesus says the man was born blind “so that God’s works might be revealed”. Did God make him blind in order to show off?

No. The Church teaches that God is never the cause of moral evil, and that he allows suffering only because he can bring a greater good out of it (CCC 311–312). Jesus’ answer is first of all a refusal to blame: the man’s blindness is not a punishment for anyone’s sin. He then turns attention to what God will do. The Church sees in Christ the compassion of God for the sick and the sign of the healing that he brings (CCC 1503–1505).

Does the Sabbath commandment still apply to Christians?

The commandment remains: a day set apart for God, for worship and for rest. Christians keep it on Sunday, the first day of the week, the day of the Lord’s Resurrection, when the community gathers at the Holy Qurbana (CCC 2174–2176). Jesus teaches that the Sabbath is for people and for doing good (Mark 2:27; 3:4), and Deuteronomy already ties it to the memory of liberation and rest for everyone.

Does Isaiah’s song of the vineyard mean that God rejected the Jewish people?

No. Isaiah was a Jew speaking to his own people, as a prophet who loved them. His song was a warning, not a final verdict, and later in the same book there is a new song of the vineyard that promises restoration (Isaiah 27:2–6). The Church teaches that God does not take back his gifts and calling (Romans 11:29), and that the Jewish people remain beloved of God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Third Sunday of Kaitha, in the Syro-Malabar calendar, follows the Season of the Apostles. In Kaitha the Church walks Sunday by Sunday through the Lord’s word, and today’s readings give a good frame: the covenant given by grace, the fruit the Lord looks for, repentance, and light. The East Syriac tradition loves the language of light for Baptism. Mar Aprem (in substance) speaks of the baptised as clothed in a robe of glory. The Gospel of the blind man, who washes in a pool and comes back seeing, who then bows down in worship before Jesus, is read in that light. At the Holy Qurbana the Church also bows before the Lord who is truly present, and the people’s confession, “Lord, I believe”, is the Church’s own.

For study and teaching

  • The Ten Words begin with “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out.” How does remembering what God has already done for me change the way I keep his commandments?
  • In Isaiah, God asks “What more was there to do?” What fruit might God be looking for in my life, and what is blocking it?
  • Paul distinguishes godly grief from worldly grief. When I have failed, which kind do I usually feel, and how can I turn toward God?
  • The blind man grew in sight: “the man called Jesus”, then a prophet, then “Lord”. Where am I on that road, and what would help me say “Lord, I believe” more fully?
