Word for the day

“Nevertheless, do not rejoice at this, that the spirits submit to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” Luke 10:20

Reflection

“For here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.” Hebrews reminds us that the Church is a pilgrim people; our true citizenship is in heaven. Meanwhile we offer “a sacrifice of praise to God… the fruit of lips that confess his name,” and do not neglect to do good and share.

The seventy-two return rejoicing at their success; Jesus redirects their joy: rejoice that your names are written in heaven. Then he himself rejoices in the Holy Spirit and thanks the Father for revealing these things to infants. True joy is not in what we achieve but in belonging to God.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews says: here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come (Hebrews 13:14). The Church is a pilgrim people; our true citizenship is in heaven. Meanwhile we offer God a sacrifice of praise, the fruit of lips that confess his name, and we do not neglect to do good and to share what we have (Hebrews 13:15–16).

The seventy-two return from their mission full of joy: Lord, in your name even the demons submit to us. Jesus says: rejoice that your names are written in heaven. At that same hour Jesus rejoiced in the Holy Spirit: I thank you, Father, because you have revealed these things to infants (Luke 10:17–21).

Pilgrims travel light and keep a joyful heart. Our joy is not in our successes, but in the fact that God knows us. Today let us sing a hymn of praise, and share what we have with someone. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says your names are written in heaven. How wonderful! Sing a song to Jesus today.

Prayer

Father, Lord of heaven and earth, I praise you because you reveal yourself to the little ones. Write my name in heaven and keep me a faithful pilgrim toward the city to come. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings lift our eyes beyond this world. Hebrews reminds Christians that “here we have no lasting city” and calls them to go out to Christ and offer a sacrifice of praise. In Luke the seventy return rejoicing in their power over demons, and Jesus tells them to rejoice rather that their names are written in heaven. Our true home and our true joy are with God.

Hebrews 13:9–16 · Here we have no lasting city

Setting.

These are some of the closing exhortations of Hebrews. The readers were being drawn by “strange teachings”, perhaps about sacred meals and food laws. The author answers with the image of the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16): the bodies of the animals whose blood was carried into the sanctuary were burned outside the camp (Leviticus 16:27).

Structure.

  • vv. 9–10: hearts strengthened by grace, not by food; “we have an altar”.
  • vv. 11–12: the sin offering burned outside the camp, and Jesus who suffered outside the city gate.
  • vv. 13–14: “let us then go to him outside the camp”, seeking the city to come.
  • vv. 15–16: the sacrifice of praise, and the sacrifices of doing good and sharing.

Key words.

  • thysiastērion, altar: The author does not explain which altar he means. Many see the Cross; Catholic tradition also sees the Eucharistic table, where the one sacrifice of the Cross is made present.
  • thysia aineseōs, sacrifice of praise: In the Greek Old Testament this renders the tôdâ, the thanksgiving offering. Christian worship is a thanksgiving offered through Christ.
  • koinōnia, sharing: The word behind “to share what you have” (v. 16). Communion with God is lived out in sharing with others.

Verse by verse.

“We have an altar from which those who officiate in the tent have no right to eat.” (13:10)
“Therefore Jesus also suffered outside the city gate in order to sanctify the people by his own blood.” (13:12)

Golgotha lay outside the walls of Jerusalem (John 19:17, 20). The place of shame becomes the place of holiness.

“For here we have no lasting city, but we are looking for the city that is to come.” (13:14)

Christians are pilgrims. This does not make them careless about the earthly city; it breaks the hold of its idols on them.

“Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.” (13:16)

Worship and charity belong together: praise on the lips and bread shared with the poor.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism lists “the sacrifice of praise” among the names of the Eucharist (CCC 1330). The early Letter to Diognetus describes Christians as living in their own countries as sojourners, for whom every foreign land is a homeland and every homeland a foreign land (in substance).

Luke 10:17–21 · Rejoice that your names are written in heaven

Setting.

Luke alone tells of a second, larger mission: Jesus sends disciples ahead of him in pairs (10:1–12). Now they return with joy. The mission of these disciples anticipates the mission of the Church to all nations.

Structure.

  • v. 17: the joyful report: “even the demons submit to us”.
  • vv. 18–19: Jesus’ vision of Satan’s fall and the authority he gives.
  • v. 20: the deeper reason for joy.
  • v. 21: Jesus rejoices in the Holy Spirit and praises the Father.

Key words.

  • engegraptai en tois ouranois, written in heaven: The image of the book of life (Exodus 32:32; Daniel 12:1; Philippians 4:3). To be written there is to belong to God for ever.
  • ēgalliasato, rejoiced: A word of exultant joy, used in Mary’s “my spirit rejoices” (1:47). Here Jesus rejoices “in the Holy Spirit”: Father, Son and Spirit appear together.
  • nēpioi, infants: Little children, the simple. God reveals himself to those who receive him as a gift.

Verse by verse.

“The seventy returned with joy, saying, “Lord, in your name even the demons submit to us!”” (10:17)

A note on the text. The ancient manuscripts differ here and in 10:1. Some important ones read “seventy” (followed by the NRSV), others, also very old, read “seventy-two” (followed by the Malayalam POC). The numbers may recall the table of the nations in Genesis 10, which counts seventy nations in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in the Greek. Either way, the mission points to the whole world.

“I watched Satan fall from heaven like a flash of lightning.” (10:18)

The mission of the disciples is part of the defeat of evil. Satan’s power is already broken.

“but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” (10:20)

Success in ministry is a gift, but it is not the deepest joy. The deepest joy is to be known and loved by God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism presents this moment as a model of Jesus’ prayer: his thanksgiving to the Father, Lord of heaven and earth, for revealing the mysteries of the Kingdom to little ones (CCC 2603). The Kingdom belongs to the poor and the lowly, who receive it with humble hearts (CCC 544).

For study and teaching

  • Hebrews says, “here we have no lasting city.” What attachment in my life makes me forget that I am a pilgrim?
  • Do I rejoice more in what I achieve for God or in the fact that my name is written in heaven?
