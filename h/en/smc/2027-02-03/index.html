Word for the day

“My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to complete his work.” John 4:34

Reflection

Paul writes to Timothy as an old man near the end of his race, and his words have the weight of a last will: “I solemnly urge you: proclaim the message; be persistent whether the time is favorable or unfavorable; convince, rebuke, and encourage, with the utmost patience in teaching.” He knows what is coming: “the time is coming when people will not put up with sound doctrine, but having itching ears, they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own desires.” Our age of endless voices, each telling us what we like to hear, is not new. Timothy must stay steady: “always be sober, endure suffering, do the work of an evangelist, carry out your ministry fully.” And Paul himself is ready to give the last drop: “As for me, I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come.”

In the Gospel the Samaritan woman has just met Jesus at the well. The disciples come back and are astonished that he is speaking with a woman. She, meanwhile, “left her water jar and went back to the city.” The jar she came for is forgotten; she has found living water. She says to the people, “Come and see a man who told me everything I have ever done! He cannot be the Messiah, can he?” The disciples urge Jesus, “Rabbi, eat something,” and he answers, “I have food to eat that you do not know about.” They think someone has brought him lunch. He explains: “My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to complete his work.” Then he points to the Samaritans already walking toward him across the fields: “look around you, and see how the fields are ripe for harvesting.” One sows and another reaps. In Denha Christ is revealed even to the Samaritans, the outsiders, through a woman who simply said: come and see. We in Kerala reap what the apostle Thomas and many generations of priests, religious and catechists sowed. “Others have labored, and you have entered into their labor.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul charges Timothy: proclaim the message; be persistent whether the time is favorable or unfavorable (2 Timothy 4:2). People will look for teachers who say what their itching ears want to hear. But Timothy must do the work of an evangelist.

In the Gospel the Samaritan woman leaves her water jar and runs back to the city: come and see a man who told me everything I have ever done! She came for water and found the Messiah.

The disciples urge Jesus to eat. He says: my food is to do the will of him who sent me and to complete his work (John 4:34). Then he shows them the fields ripe for harvesting.

We reap what others have sown: the apostle Thomas, our missionaries, our parents and catechists. Let us thank them, leave our water jars, and say to someone: come and see. Amen.

For children

Children, a lady met Jesus at a well and was so happy that she forgot her water jar! She ran to tell everyone: come and see Jesus! Who can you tell about Jesus today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, at the well you gave living water to a woman who came only for water, and she ran to bring others to you. Make your Father's will our food, give us courage to proclaim your word in every season, and let us gather with joy the harvest that others have sown. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two people carry the word today, in very different circumstances. An old apostle in chains hands on his charge: “proclaim the message”. A woman of Samaria, who came for water, leaves her jar at the well and runs to tell her town. Between them stands Jesus, who says that doing the Father’s will is his food, and that the harvest is already ripe. Denha shows Christ to the world, and the world still comes to know him through those who speak of him.

2 Timothy 4:1–6 · Proclaim the message

Setting.

This letter is presented as Paul’s last word, written from prison (1:8; 2:9) to Timothy, the younger co-worker he had taken on at Lystra (Acts 16:1–3). Many hold that Paul wrote it or that it rests on his own words; many scholars think that a disciple composed it in his name and spirit after his death. Either way the Church receives it as inspired Scripture, and it reads as a father’s farewell to his son in the faith.

The passage comes straight after the famous words on Scripture (3:16–17). The charge to preach follows from them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: a solemn charge before God and Christ the Judge.
  • vv. 3–4: the reason: a time of itching ears.
  • v. 5: “As for you”: four short commands.
  • v. 6: “As for me”: the writer’s life is being poured out.

Key words.

  • kēryxon, proclaim: From the word for a herald, who announces a message he did not compose. The preacher’s task is first to hand on what has been entrusted to him, not to display his own ideas.
  • eukairōs akairōs, favorable or unfavorable: A wordplay in Greek: “in season, out of season”. Convenience, whether the preacher’s or the hearers’, does not decide when the Gospel is spoken.
  • spendomai, I am being poured out as a libation: A libation was wine poured out with a sacrifice (Numbers 28:7). The same verb appears in Philippians 2:17. The writer sees his coming death as an act of worship.

Verse by verse.

“proclaim the message; be persistent whether the time is favorable or unfavorable; convince, rebuke, and encourage, with the utmost patience in teaching.” (4:2)

Three verbs, and only the middle one is harsh. All three are held inside “the utmost patience”. Correction without patience wounds, and patience without truth leaves people where they are.

“having itching ears, they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own desires” (4:3)

The danger described lies as much in the listeners as in the teachers. People go looking for voices that say what they already want to hear. The warning applies in every age, and to every side of every argument.

“As for me, I am already being poured out as a libation, and the time of my departure has come.” (4:6)

“Departure” (analysis) was used of a ship slipping its moorings or a camp being taken down. Death is spoken of here as setting out, without fear.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council, cited by the Catechism, names preaching the Gospel as the first task of bishops, with priests as their co-workers (CCC 888). Lay people share the same mission by word and by the witness of life (CCC 905). St Paul VI wrote that people today listen more willingly to witnesses than to teachers, and if they listen to teachers it is because they are witnesses (Evangelii Nuntiandi 41). Verse 5, “endure suffering”, says the same thing in fewer words.

John 4:27–38 · My food is to do the will of him who sent me

Setting.

We are at Jacob’s well near Sychar in Samaria, at about noon (4:5–6). The disciples had gone to buy food (4:8), and Jesus has been speaking with a Samaritan woman, to whom he has just said plainly that he is the Messiah (4:25–26). Jews and Samaritans had a long and bitter separation behind them (4:9). It was also not the custom for a teacher to hold such a conversation with a woman in public, which is why the disciples are astonished.

The reading has a double movement. The woman goes into the town while the disciples stay with Jesus and misunderstand him about food. Then the townspeople come out, and Jesus shows the disciples what they are seeing.

Structure.

  • v. 27: the disciples return; astonished, they say nothing.
  • vv. 28–30: the woman leaves her jar and speaks to the town; the people set out.
  • vv. 31–34: “Rabbi, eat something”: his true food.
  • vv. 35–38: the harvest; sower and reaper.

Key words.

  • hydria, water jar: The same word as the stone jars at Cana (2:6–7). Each time, something is left behind or changed when Jesus is present. She came for this jar’s water, and now she has forgotten it.
  • brōma, food: The disciples think of bread. As so often in John, a plain word lifts to a higher level: what keeps Jesus alive is the Father’s will.
  • teleiōsō, to complete: “To complete his work”. A related word is his last word on the Cross: “It is finished” (19:30). The whole of his life is already in view at this well.

Verse by verse.

“Then the woman left her water jar and went back to the city.” (4:28)

She does not argue or explain. The one who had come to the well at noon now goes to the people of her town, with her question.

““Come and see a man who told me everything I have ever done! He cannot be the Messiah, can he?”” (4:29)

“Come and see” are the words of Philip to Nathanael (1:46). She does not claim to have all the answers. She tells what happened to her and asks a question, and that is enough to start them walking.

“My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to complete his work.” (4:34)

Jesus was tired and thirsty at the start of the chapter (4:6–7). He does not despise bread. He says that there is something he lives by even more.

“look around you, and see how the fields are ripe for harvesting.” (4:35)

The Greek says the fields are “white”. Perhaps at that very moment the people of the town are coming across the fields toward him. The harvest is not four months off. It is walking up the road.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees in this well the wonder of prayer itself: Christ comes to meet every human being, and “it is he who first seeks us and asks us for a drink” (CCC 2560). It also teaches that in Christ’s human will the will of the Father was perfectly done (CCC 2824). St Augustine, in his homilies on John, takes the jar she left as a sign of worldly desire laid aside for the sake of living water (in substance). St John Chrysostom admires her as one who did the work of an apostle, drawing a whole town by her words (in substance).

Questions people ask

Who are the “others” who have labored, in v. 38?

John does not say. Many readers think of the prophets and John the Baptist; Jesus himself has sown in this very conversation. Some scholars also see in it a look ahead to the later mission to Samaria (Acts 8). The point stays the same: no missionary starts from nothing, and every harvest depends on those who went before.

For study and teaching

  • Paul’s charge to Timothy holds truth and patience together. Which of the two do I find harder when I correct someone at home or in the parish?
  • The Samaritan woman said only what had happened to her and asked a question. To whom could I say, “Come and see”?
