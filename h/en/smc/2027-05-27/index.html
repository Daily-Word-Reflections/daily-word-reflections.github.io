Word for the day

“I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” John 6:51

Reflection

Two months after Easter, and eleven days after Pentecost, the Church stops on a Thursday to adore the gift that Jesus left on another Thursday, the night before he died. On Maundy Thursday the joy of the gift was overshadowed by the betrayal and the Passion. Today, in the Season of the Apostles, the Church celebrates the same gift in full light: the Body and Blood of Christ, given in every Holy Qurbana from the Upper Room to our own parish altar. The apostles, filled with the Spirit, did not only preach; they broke bread. The first believers “devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers” (Acts 2:42). The Church that the apostles founded has been a Eucharistic Church from the first day. Our own Mar Thoma Christians received this faith from the apostle Thomas, and the word we use for the Eucharist, Qurbana, a Syriac word for offering or sacrifice, says what it is: the offering of Christ himself.

The first reading takes us back almost two thousand years before Christ. Abram returns from battle, having rescued his nephew Lot, and a mysterious figure comes out to meet him: “And King Melchizedek of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was priest of God Most High.” He blesses Abram: “Blessed be Abram by God Most High, maker of heaven and earth; and blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand!” Melchizedek appears and disappears in three verses, yet the Bible never forgets him. The psalm says of the coming Messiah: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), and the Letter to the Hebrews sees in him an image of Christ, “resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever” (Hebrews 7:3). A king of peace and righteousness, a priest who offers bread and wine: the Church has always seen here the first outline of the Eucharist. At the altar our priest still offers bread and wine, and they become the Body and Blood of the eternal High Priest.

The second reading is a hard word to priests. Through the prophet Malachi God complains that the priests of the restored Temple are offering blind, lame and sick animals, the leftovers of the flock: “By offering polluted food on my altar. And you say, ‘How have we polluted it?’ By thinking that the Lord’s table may be despised.” God would rather the Temple doors were shut than that worship should continue carelessly. Then comes a promise that reaches far beyond Jerusalem: “For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is great among the nations, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering; for my name is great among the nations, says the Lord of hosts.” From the earliest centuries Christians read this verse as a prophecy of the Eucharist. The Didache, one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament, applies it to the Sunday breaking of bread, and St Justin and St Irenaeus did the same. Today, at every hour, somewhere from the rising of the sun to its setting, the Holy Qurbana is being offered, in Kerala and in Chicago, in Rome and in a forest chapel in Arunachal: one pure offering, Christ himself. Malachi's warning remains for us too. We can despise the Lord's table by arriving late and leaving early, by careless dress and wandering eyes, by giving God the leftovers of our Sunday.

In the Epistle Paul hands on the oldest written account of the Last Supper, written about twenty-five years after the event: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, ‘This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’” Received and handed on: this is what tradition means. Paul did not invent the Eucharist; he passed on what the Church already did. And he draws a serious conclusion: “For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes. Whoever, therefore, eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be answerable for the body and blood of the Lord. Examine yourselves, and only then eat of the bread and drink of the cup.” The Corinthians were sharing the Lord's Supper while the rich ate well and the poor went hungry. To fail to discern the Body, Paul says, is to fail to see both Christ truly present in the sacrament and Christ present in the brothers and sisters gathered around it. That is why the Church asks us to confess serious sins before receiving, to keep the Eucharistic fast, and to come to Communion reconciled with our neighbour.

The Gospel is the heart of the discourse in Capernaum: “I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” His hearers dispute: “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” Jesus does not soften his words. He makes them stronger: “Very truly, I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood have eternal life, and I will raise them up on the last day; for my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink.” True food, true drink: not a symbol only, not a pious memory, but the real Christ. This is what the Catholic Church believes and what the Council of Trent called transubstantiation: at the consecration the whole substance of the bread becomes the Body of Christ and the whole substance of the wine his Blood, while the appearances remain. And the purpose of it all is communion: “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” Whoever receives the Lord worthily carries him out into the street, the office, the kitchen and the hospital ward. Many of our parishes will carry the Blessed Sacrament in procession in these days, under the umbrellas and through the lanes, with children scattering petals. It is the same mystery: Christ who comes to us in the Qurbana wants to go with us wherever we live.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today the Church does what she did on Maundy Thursday, but now in the full light of Easter and Pentecost: she gives thanks for the Body and Blood of Christ. The word Qurbana means offering. In every Qurbana Christ offers himself, and gives himself to us.

Long before Christ, a king and priest named Melchizedek came out to meet Abram with bread and wine and blessed him. The Bible calls Jesus a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek. At our altar too, bread and wine are offered, and they become the Body and Blood of the eternal Priest.

Through Malachi God complains about careless worship: blind and lame animals, the leftovers. But he promises: from the rising of the sun to its setting, a pure offering will be made to my name in every place (Malachi 1:11). The first Christians saw here the Eucharist, offered every hour somewhere in the world. Let us never give God our leftovers.

Paul hands on what he received: on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and said, “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.” Then Paul warns: examine yourselves, and only then eat (1 Corinthians 11:24, 28). Let us come to Communion with a clean heart, reconciled with God and with our neighbour.

In the Gospel Jesus says: my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink (John 6:55). Not only a sign, but truly himself. It looks like bread, but it is the Lord. That is why we kneel, we fast before Communion, we keep silence, and we adore him.

And Jesus says: whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in them (John 6:56). We carry him out of the church. In these days many of our parishes walk in procession with the Blessed Sacrament. Let our whole life be such a procession, carrying Christ to our homes and streets. Amen.

Homily option 2 · Lord, to whom can we go?

Apply: Spend some time in adoration this week, saying Peter's words.

With Peter we answer: Lord, to whom can we go?

Take-home resolution: This week I will spend time before the Blessed Sacrament and say: Lord, to whom can we go?

Dear brothers and sisters, in today's Gospel Jesus says: this is the bread that came down from heaven, not like that which your ancestors ate, and they died. But the one who eats this bread will live forever (John 6:58). In the desert God fed Israel with manna each morning. It sustained their bodies for a time. The bread Christ gives is himself, and it gives eternal life.

But the Gospel goes on. When many of his disciples heard it, they said: this teaching is difficult; who can accept it? (John 6:60). And many turned back and no longer went about with him. Notice: Jesus did not call them back and say, I only meant a symbol. He let them go, because he had spoken the truth. The bread is truly his flesh; the cup is truly his blood.

Then he turned to the Twelve and asked: do you also wish to go away? (John 6:67). It is like Joshua's challenge to Israel: choose this day whom you will serve (Joshua 24:15). Today many people, even baptised Christians, find the mystery of the Eucharist too hard. The question comes to each of us: will I stay?

Simon Peter answered: Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God (John 6:68–69). Peter did not understand everything. But he knew Jesus, and he trusted him. The Psalm invites us: O taste and see that the LORD is good (Psalm 34:8).

On this Feast of the Body and Blood of Christ, let us learn what the Church teaches and share it with our children. Let us spend time before the Blessed Sacrament this week. And when we receive him at the Holy Qurbana, let us say with Peter: Lord, to whom can we go? Amen.

Apply: Join the Eucharistic procession or adoration in your parish.

The Body of Christ received makes the Body of Christ, the Church.

We discern his body in the bread and in the poor.

Take-home resolution: This week I will pray for my neighbour after Communion and share a meal with someone poor or lonely.

Dear friends, Paul asks the Corinthians: the cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ? Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread (1 Corinthians 10:16–17).

The Eucharist does two things at once. It gives us Christ, truly present, Body and Blood, soul and divinity. And it makes us one body in him. Saint Augustine told his people: receive what you are, the Body of Christ. The Psalm says: how very good and pleasant it is when kindred live together in unity (Psalm 133:1). After Communion, let us pray for the person beside us.

But in Corinth something was wrong. When they gathered for the Lord's supper, the rich ate first and the poor went hungry (1 Corinthians 11:21). Paul said: that is not really to eat the Lord's supper. And he warned that all who eat and drink without discerning the body eat and drink judgment against themselves (1 Corinthians 11:29). Amos had condemned those who lay on beds of ivory and were not grieved over the ruin of their people (Amos 6:4–6). To discern the body is to see Christ in the bread, and also in the poor brother at the door.

In these days many of our parishes carry the Blessed Sacrament in procession. David danced before the ark of the LORD as it was brought into the city (2 Samuel 6:14). At Emmaus the two disciples recognised the Lord in the breaking of the bread, and their hearts burned (Luke 24:30–32). The procession shows that Christ does not stay locked in the church. He walks with his people.

So let us come to the Holy Qurbana as one family. Let us pray for our neighbour after Communion. Let us share a meal this week with someone poor or lonely. And let us walk behind the Blessed Sacrament, carrying the Lord into our homes and streets. Amen.

For children

Children, at the Holy Qurbana the priest says the words of Jesus: “This is my body.” Then the bread becomes Jesus himself! It still looks like bread, but it is Jesus.

That is why we are quiet and we kneel. When you see the priest lift up the Host, say softly in your heart: “My Lord and my God!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, eternal High Priest according to the order of Melchizedek, on the night you were betrayed you took bread and wine and gave us your Body and Blood. We adore you truly present in the Holy Qurbana, offered from the rising of the sun to its setting. Give us hearts that discern your Body, in the sacrament and in our brothers and sisters. Let us never despise your table, but come to it cleansed and grateful. Feed us with the living bread, that we may abide in you and you in us, and raise us up on the last day. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, now and for ever. Amen.
