Word for the day

“I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” John 6:51

Reflection

Two months after Easter, and eleven days after Pentecost, the Church stops on a Thursday to adore the gift that Jesus left on another Thursday, the night before he died. On Maundy Thursday the joy of the gift was overshadowed by the betrayal and the Passion. Today, in the Season of the Apostles, the Church celebrates the same gift in full light: the Body and Blood of Christ, given in every Holy Qurbana from the Upper Room to our own parish altar. The apostles, filled with the Spirit, did not only preach; they broke bread. The first believers “devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers” (Acts 2:42). The Church that the apostles founded has been a Eucharistic Church from the first day. Our own Mar Thoma Christians received this faith from the apostle Thomas, and the word we use for the Eucharist, Qurbana, a Syriac word for offering or sacrifice, says what it is: the offering of Christ himself.

The first reading takes us back almost two thousand years before Christ. Abram returns from battle, having rescued his nephew Lot, and a mysterious figure comes out to meet him: “And King Melchizedek of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was priest of God Most High.” He blesses Abram: “Blessed be Abram by God Most High, maker of heaven and earth; and blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand!” Melchizedek appears and disappears in three verses, yet the Bible never forgets him. The psalm says of the coming Messiah: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), and the Letter to the Hebrews sees in him an image of Christ, “resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever” (Hebrews 7:3). A king of peace and righteousness, a priest who offers bread and wine: the Church has always seen here the first outline of the Eucharist. At the altar our priest still offers bread and wine, and they become the Body and Blood of the eternal High Priest.

The second reading is a hard word to priests. Through the prophet Malachi God complains that the priests of the restored Temple are offering blind, lame and sick animals, the leftovers of the flock: “By offering polluted food on my altar. And you say, ‘How have we polluted it?’ By thinking that the Lord’s table may be despised.” God would rather the Temple doors were shut than that worship should continue carelessly. Then comes a promise that reaches far beyond Jerusalem: “For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is great among the nations, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering; for my name is great among the nations, says the Lord of hosts.” From the earliest centuries Christians read this verse as a prophecy of the Eucharist. The Didache, one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament, applies it to the Sunday breaking of bread, and St Justin and St Irenaeus did the same. Today, at every hour, somewhere from the rising of the sun to its setting, the Holy Qurbana is being offered, in Kerala and in Chicago, in Rome and in a forest chapel in Arunachal: one pure offering, Christ himself. Malachi's warning remains for us too. We can despise the Lord's table by arriving late and leaving early, by careless dress and wandering eyes, by giving God the leftovers of our Sunday.

In the Epistle Paul hands on the oldest written account of the Last Supper, written about twenty-five years after the event: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, ‘This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’” Received and handed on: this is what tradition means. Paul did not invent the Eucharist; he passed on what the Church already did. And he draws a serious conclusion: “For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes. Whoever, therefore, eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be answerable for the body and blood of the Lord. Examine yourselves, and only then eat of the bread and drink of the cup.” The Corinthians were sharing the Lord's Supper while the rich ate well and the poor went hungry. To fail to discern the Body, Paul says, is to fail to see both Christ truly present in the sacrament and Christ present in the brothers and sisters gathered around it. That is why the Church asks us to confess serious sins before receiving, to keep the Eucharistic fast, and to come to Communion reconciled with our neighbour.

The Gospel is the heart of the discourse in Capernaum: “I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” His hearers dispute: “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” Jesus does not soften his words. He makes them stronger: “Very truly, I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood have eternal life, and I will raise them up on the last day; for my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink.” True food, true drink: not a symbol only, not a pious memory, but the real Christ. This is what the Catholic Church believes and what the Council of Trent called transubstantiation: at the consecration the whole substance of the bread becomes the Body of Christ and the whole substance of the wine his Blood, while the appearances remain. And the purpose of it all is communion: “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” Whoever receives the Lord worthily carries him out into the street, the office, the kitchen and the hospital ward. Many of our parishes will carry the Blessed Sacrament in procession in these days, under the umbrellas and through the lanes, with children scattering petals. It is the same mystery: Christ who comes to us in the Qurbana wants to go with us wherever we live.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today the Church does what she did on Maundy Thursday, but now in the full light of Easter and Pentecost: she gives thanks for the Body and Blood of Christ. The word Qurbana means offering. In every Qurbana Christ offers himself, and gives himself to us.

Long before Christ, a king and priest named Melchizedek came out to meet Abram with bread and wine and blessed him. The Bible calls Jesus a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek. At our altar too, bread and wine are offered, and they become the Body and Blood of the eternal Priest.

Through Malachi God complains about careless worship: blind and lame animals, the leftovers. But he promises: from the rising of the sun to its setting, a pure offering will be made to my name in every place (Malachi 1:11). The first Christians saw here the Eucharist, offered every hour somewhere in the world. Let us never give God our leftovers.

Paul hands on what he received: on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and said, “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.” Then Paul warns: examine yourselves, and only then eat (1 Corinthians 11:24, 28). Let us come to Communion with a clean heart, reconciled with God and with our neighbour.

In the Gospel Jesus says: my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink (John 6:55). Not only a sign, but truly himself. It looks like bread, but it is the Lord. That is why we kneel, we fast before Communion, we keep silence, and we adore him.

And Jesus says: whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in them (John 6:56). We carry him out of the church. In these days many of our parishes walk in procession with the Blessed Sacrament. Let our whole life be such a procession, carrying Christ to our homes and streets. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, in today's Gospel Jesus says: this is the bread that came down from heaven, not like that which your ancestors ate, and they died. But the one who eats this bread will live forever (John 6:58). In the desert God fed Israel with manna each morning. It sustained their bodies for a time. The bread Christ gives is himself, and it gives eternal life.

But the Gospel goes on. When many of his disciples heard it, they said: this teaching is difficult; who can accept it? (John 6:60). And many turned back and no longer went about with him. Notice: Jesus did not call them back and say, I only meant a symbol. He let them go, because he had spoken the truth. The bread is truly his flesh; the cup is truly his blood.

Then he turned to the Twelve and asked: do you also wish to go away? (John 6:67). It is like Joshua's challenge to Israel: choose this day whom you will serve (Joshua 24:15). Today many people, even baptised Christians, find the mystery of the Eucharist too hard. The question comes to each of us: will I stay?

Simon Peter answered: Lord, to whom can we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and know that you are the Holy One of God (John 6:68–69). Peter did not understand everything. But he knew Jesus, and he trusted him. The Psalm invites us: O taste and see that the LORD is good (Psalm 34:8).

On this Feast of the Body and Blood of Christ, let us learn what the Church teaches and share it with our children. Let us spend time before the Blessed Sacrament this week. And when we receive him at the Holy Qurbana, let us say with Peter: Lord, to whom can we go? Amen.

Dear friends, Paul asks the Corinthians: the cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a sharing in the body of Christ? Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread (1 Corinthians 10:16–17).

The Eucharist does two things at once. It gives us Christ, truly present, Body and Blood, soul and divinity. And it makes us one body in him. Saint Augustine told his people: receive what you are, the Body of Christ. The Psalm says: how very good and pleasant it is when kindred live together in unity (Psalm 133:1). After Communion, let us pray for the person beside us.

But in Corinth something was wrong. When they gathered for the Lord's supper, the rich ate first and the poor went hungry (1 Corinthians 11:21). Paul said: that is not really to eat the Lord's supper. And he warned that all who eat and drink without discerning the body eat and drink judgment against themselves (1 Corinthians 11:29). Amos had condemned those who lay on beds of ivory and were not grieved over the ruin of their people (Amos 6:4–6). To discern the body is to see Christ in the bread, and also in the poor brother at the door.

In these days many of our parishes carry the Blessed Sacrament in procession. David danced before the ark of the LORD as it was brought into the city (2 Samuel 6:14). At Emmaus the two disciples recognised the Lord in the breaking of the bread, and their hearts burned (Luke 24:30–32). The procession shows that Christ does not stay locked in the church. He walks with his people.

So let us come to the Holy Qurbana as one family. Let us pray for our neighbour after Communion. Let us share a meal this week with someone poor or lonely. And let us walk behind the Blessed Sacrament, carrying the Lord into our homes and streets. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this Thursday, the day of the week of the Last Supper, the Church gives thanks for the gift of the Body and Blood of Christ, and the four readings walk through the whole history of that gift. In Genesis a mysterious priest-king, Melchizedek, brings out bread and wine and blesses Abram. Through Malachi, God rebukes priests who despise his table and foretells a pure offering made to his name in every place, among the nations. Paul hands on what he received from the Lord: on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and the cup and said, “This is my body,” “This cup is the new covenant in my blood.” And in John, Jesus says plainly that his flesh is true food and his blood true drink. Figure, prophecy, institution and teaching: the readings lead from the bread of Melchizedek to the altar of every Holy Qurbana.

Genesis 14:17–20 · Bread and wine from the priest of God Most High

Setting.

Abram has just won a surprising victory. Four eastern kings, led by Chedorlaomer, had raided the cities of the plain and carried off Abram’s nephew Lot. With his household Abram pursued them, rescued Lot and brought back the captives and their goods (14:1–16). As he returns, two kings come out to meet him: the king of Sodom, and Melchizedek, king of Salem.

Melchizedek appears in the story without parents, ancestors or later history, and then disappears. Salem was understood in Israel as Jerusalem (Psalm 76:2). Psalm 110:4 speaks of a king who is “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”, and the Letter to the Hebrews applies this to Christ, a priest not by descent from Aaron but by the power of an indestructible life (Hebrews 7:1–17).

Structure.

  • v. 17: the king of Sodom comes out to meet the victorious Abram.
  • v. 18: Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, brings out bread and wine.
  • vv. 19–20a: he blesses Abram and blesses God Most High.
  • v. 20b: Abram gives him a tenth of everything.
  • ¶ After these verses the king of Sodom offers Abram the goods, and Abram refuses them (14:21–24). The contrast is deliberate: from Melchizedek Abram receives a blessing; from Sodom he will take nothing.

Key words.

  • malkî-ṣedeq, Melchizedek: The name means “my king is righteousness” or “king of righteousness”. Hebrews draws out both his name and his city: king of righteousness, and king of Salem, that is, king of peace (Hebrews 7:2).
  • ’ēl ‘elyôn, God Most High: A title of God found also in the Psalms (Psalm 78:35). Abram himself identifies God Most High with the Lord (14:22).
  • kōhēn, priest: This is the first time the Bible uses the word “priest”. Long before the priesthood of Aaron, a priest appears who offers bread and wine and blesses.

Verse by verse.

“And King Melchizedek of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was priest of God Most High.” (14:18)

Some scholars read the bread and wine as royal hospitality for weary soldiers. The verse at once adds that he was a priest, and the Fathers saw in his gift an offering: a figure of the bread and wine that Christ, the true priest-king, would make his Body and Blood.

“Blessed be Abram by God Most High, maker of heaven and earth” (14:19)

The one who blesses is greater than the one blessed (Hebrews 7:7). The father of Israel, bearer of the promise, bows to receive a blessing.

“And Abram gave him one-tenth of everything.” (14:20)

The tithe recognises Melchizedek as God’s priest. A tenth given to God says that all comes from him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Church sees in the gesture of the priest-king Melchizedek, who brought out bread and wine, a prefiguring of her own offering (CCC 1333), and that Christ is the unique high priest after the order of Melchizedek (CCC 1544). St Cyprian of Carthage, writing about the cup of the Lord, says that in Melchizedek the sacrifice of the Lord was foreshown, since he offered bread and wine, and Christ offered the same, that is, his body and blood (in substance, Letter 63).

Malachi 1:7–12 · A pure offering in every place

Setting.

Malachi is the last book of the prophets in our Bibles. The name means “my messenger” (3:1). He speaks after the return from exile, when the Temple had been rebuilt (it was dedicated about 515 BC) and worship had grown tired and careless. The Law required that animals offered to God be without blemish (Leviticus 22:20–22; Deuteronomy 15:21), but the priests were accepting the blind, the lame and the sick.

The book is built as a series of disputes. God makes a charge, the people answer “How have we…?”, and God replies. Today’s verses belong to the first charge against the priests (1:6–2:9).

Structure.

  • v. 7: the charge: polluted food on my altar; you think the Lord’s table may be despised.
  • v. 8: the proof: blind, lame and sick animals; would your governor accept them?
  • vv. 9–10: an ironic call to pray, and a wish that someone would shut the temple doors.
  • v. 11: the great promise: from the rising of the sun to its setting, a pure offering among the nations.
  • v. 12: the contrast: but you profane it.

Key words.

  • šulḥan YHWH, the Lord’s table: Malachi calls the altar the Lord’s table (1:7, 12). Paul will speak of “the table of the Lord” at which Christians share (1 Corinthians 10:21).
  • minḥâ, offering: A gift brought to God, often a grain offering of flour and oil (Leviticus 2). The word is used broadly here for every gift offered to God’s name.
  • ṭĕhôrâ, pure: Clean, fit for God, the opposite of the “polluted” food of verse 7. What God desires is not only an unblemished gift but a pure heart that offers it.

Verse by verse.

“By offering polluted food on my altar. And you say, “How have we polluted it?”” (1:7)

The priests do not see their own sin. Carelessness in worship begins in the heart that no longer honours God as Father (1:6).

“Try presenting that to your governor; will he be pleased with you or show you favor?” (1:8)

A sharp, homely question. We take care over gifts for those we honour; God deserves our best, not our leftovers.

“For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name is great among the nations, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering” (1:11)

The Hebrew uses participles with no main verb, so the verse can be read as present or future. Either way it looks beyond the one Temple in Jerusalem to worship among all nations, in every place.

How the Church reads it.

The earliest Christians read verse 11 as a prophecy of the Eucharist. The Didache, a very early Christian writing, tells believers to gather on the Lord’s day, break bread and give thanks, after confessing their sins, so that their sacrifice may be pure, and it quotes Malachi’s words about a pure sacrifice in every place (in substance, Didache 14). St Justin Martyr also applies the verse to the bread and cup of the Eucharist offered by Christians in every place (in substance, Dialogue with Trypho 41). The Council of Trent teaches that the Eucharistic sacrifice is the clean oblation foretold by Malachi, which no unworthiness of those who offer it can defile (Session 22, chapter 1).

1 Corinthians 11:23–30 · This is my body that is for you

Setting.

Paul is writing to correct an abuse. When the Corinthians gathered for the Lord’s Supper, which was then joined to a common meal, the rich ate and drank first and the poor went hungry (11:17–22). Paul says that this is not really to eat the Lord’s Supper (11:20). To bring them back he reminds them of what the Supper is, in words he had received and already handed on to them.

This is the oldest written account of the institution of the Eucharist, set down about AD 54, before the Gospels were written. Its wording is very close to Luke’s (Luke 22:19–20).

Structure.

  • v. 23a: Paul hands on what he received from the Lord.
  • vv. 23b–25: the institution: the bread, “This is my body that is for you”; the cup, “the new covenant in my blood”; twice, “Do this in remembrance of me.”
  • v. 26: every celebration proclaims the Lord’s death until he comes.
  • vv. 27–29: eating unworthily; examine yourselves; discern the body.
  • v. 30: a warning from their own community.

Key words.

  • paralambanō, paradidōmi, receive, hand on: Technical words for passing on a sacred tradition, as rabbis passed on teaching to their disciples. Paul uses the same pair for the Creed of the Resurrection (15:3). The Eucharist is not Paul’s invention; it is the Lord’s gift, handed down.
  • anamnēsis, remembrance: Not a mere thinking back. In the Bible, to remember before God is to make present and effective what God has done, as at the Passover meal (Exodus 12:14).
  • diakrinō, discern: To distinguish, to judge rightly. To discern the body is to recognise that this bread is the Lord’s Body, and also to honour the body of Christ that is the community, especially its poor (12:27).

Verse by verse.

“the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread” (11:23)

Paul ties the Eucharist to history: a certain night, a betrayal. The gift was given at the very moment of rejection.

“and when he had given thanks, he broke it and said, “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”” (11:24)

“Given thanks” is eucharistēsas in Greek, from which the name Eucharist comes. The body is “for you”: given in sacrifice.

“This cup is the new covenant in my blood.” (11:25)

The words recall the covenant at Sinai sealed with blood (Exodus 24:8) and the new covenant promised by Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:31).

“For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” (11:26)

Every Holy Qurbana looks back to the Cross and forward to the Lord’s return. It stands between Calvary and the Second Coming.

“For all who eat and drink without discerning the body, eat and drink judgment against themselves.” (11:29)

Paul is not frightening the humble away; he is calling the careless to faith and love.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the command “Do this in memory of me” is directed at the liturgical celebration, by the apostles and their successors, of the memorial of Christ, of his life, death, Resurrection and intercession with the Father (CCC 1341), and that in the liturgy this memorial makes the events of salvation present (CCC 1363). On verses 27–29 it teaches that anyone conscious of a grave sin must receive the sacrament of Reconciliation before coming to communion (CCC 1385). The Council of Trent teaches that in the Eucharist the body and blood of Christ, together with his soul and divinity, are truly, really and substantially contained (CCC 1374).

John 6:51–59 · My flesh is true food

Setting.

John does not tell the institution of the Eucharist at the Last Supper; there he tells of the washing of the feet (13:1–15). Instead he gives, in chapter 6, Jesus’ long teaching on the bread of life, spoken at Passover time (6:4) after the feeding of the five thousand. In these verses the teaching reaches its plainest and boldest words.

Until now Jesus has spoken of believing in him as the bread from heaven. From verse 51 he speaks of eating his flesh and drinking his blood. The hearers understand him literally, and he does not correct them; he says it again more strongly.

Structure.

  • v. 51: I am the living bread; the bread I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.
  • v. 52: the dispute: how can this man give us his flesh to eat?
  • vv. 53–56: four sayings about eating his flesh and drinking his blood: life, eternal life, true food, abiding.
  • vv. 57–58: as I live because of the Father, so whoever eats me will live because of me; not like the manna.
  • v. 59: the setting: the synagogue at Capernaum.

Key words.

  • sarx, flesh: The word of John 1:14, “the Word became flesh”. The flesh Jesus gives in the Eucharist is the flesh he took from Mary and offered on the Cross “for the life of the world”.
  • trōgō, to eat, to chew: From verse 54 John changes from the ordinary verb for eating to trōgō, a very physical word, to chew or munch. The change makes it hard to read Jesus’ words as a mere figure of speech.
  • menō, abide, remain: A favourite word of John (15:4–10). Communion is not a passing visit; it is a mutual dwelling: “they abide in me, and I in them.”

Verse by verse.

“I am the living bread that came down from heaven. Whoever eats of this bread will live forever; and the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” (6:51)

“For the life of the world”: the language of sacrifice. The Eucharist and the Cross are one gift.

“So Jesus said to them, “Very truly, I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.” (6:53)

Drinking blood was forbidden by the Law (Leviticus 17:10–14), because the life is in the blood. Jesus gives his own blood so that his life may be in us.

“for my flesh is true food and my blood is true drink.” (6:55)

“True” here means real, genuine. The manna was a figure; this is the reality it pointed to.

“Just as the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever eats me will live because of me.” (6:57)

The life that flows from the Father into the Son flows from the Son into those who receive him. Communion draws us into the life of the Trinity.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that in the synagogue at Capernaum Jesus prepared for the institution of the Eucharist, calling himself the bread of life come down from heaven (CCC 1338), and it quotes verse 53 to show the urgency of his invitation to receive him (CCC 1384) and verse 56 to teach that communion deepens our union with Christ (CCC 1391). St Ignatius of Antioch, writing about AD 110, warns against those who stay away from the Eucharist because they do not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins and which the Father raised up (in substance, To the Smyrnaeans 7). St Augustine, preaching on this chapter, cries out: O sacrament of devotion, O sign of unity, O bond of charity (Tractates on John 26; quoted in CCC 1398).

Questions people ask

Is the Eucharist only a symbol of Christ?

No. The Church believes that by the consecration the whole substance of the bread becomes the Body of Christ and the whole substance of the wine his Blood; the Council of Trent called this change transubstantiation (CCC 1376). Christ is present truly, really and substantially, not only as in a sign (CCC 1374). John 6 points the same way: when the hearers object, Jesus does not soften his words but repeats them more strongly, and lets many disciples go rather than turn the teaching into a figure of speech (6:66).

Who was Melchizedek, and why does he matter for the Eucharist?

Genesis tells only that he was king of Salem and priest of God Most High, that he brought out bread and wine, and that he blessed Abram. Psalm 110 and the Letter to the Hebrews make him a figure of Christ, an eternal priest who is also king. The Church sees in his offering of bread and wine a first sign of the Eucharist (CCC 1333).

Paul warns about receiving “in an unworthy manner”. Should I stay away if I feel unworthy?

No one is worthy of the Lord by his own merit; we receive him by grace. Paul is warning against careless and loveless reception, like the Corinthians who humiliated the poor at the Lord’s Supper. The Church teaches that anyone aware of a grave sin should first receive the sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 1385). For everyone else, a humble sense of unworthiness is not a barrier but the right way to come.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In our East Syriac tradition the Eucharist is called the Holy Qurbana, from a Syriac word meaning “offering”, the gift brought near to God. Malachi’s “pure offering” and Melchizedek’s bread and wine are fulfilled in it. The Anaphora of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, which our Church prays, is among the oldest Eucharistic prayers still in use, and the most solemn form of our Qurbana is called the Raza, the Mystery.

Many Syro-Malabar families keep a home custom on Pesaha Thursday: the father of the family breaks the Pesaha bread and shares it with the household, in memory of the Lord’s Supper. Today’s Thursday feast can help families connect that home table with the altar, and to remember Paul’s warning: the Lord’s Body is not rightly received where the poor are forgotten.

For study and teaching

  • Melchizedek blessed Abram before Abram gave him anything. How does the Holy Qurbana begin with God’s gift rather than with mine?
  • Malachi rebuked priests who brought God their leftovers. What would it mean for my family to bring God our best in worship: time, attention, preparation?
  • Paul says each Qurbana proclaims the Lord’s death until he comes. How can my communion today be both a memory of the Cross and a hope for his coming?
  • Jesus says, “Those who eat my flesh and drink my blood abide in me, and I in them.” Where in my week do I need to let him abide more deeply?
