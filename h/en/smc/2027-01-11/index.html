Word for the day

“that they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” John 17:21

Reflection

At the Last Supper, before he goes out to his Passion, Jesus prays not only for the apostles but “also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word”: that is, for us. And what does he ask? “That they may all be one. As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” The unity of Christians is not a matter of good organisation; its source and model is the communion of the Father and the Son. And it has a missionary purpose: when Christians are one, the world can recognise that Jesus comes from the Father. Denha is the season of manifestation, and our unity is one of the ways in which Christ is still made manifest.

Paul shows us the opposite picture. In Corinth, “when you come together as a church, I hear that there are divisions among you”. At the very meal of the Lord the better-off eat their own supper first, and “one goes hungry and another becomes drunk”. Paul is blunt: “Do you show contempt for the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?” To divide the Body at the table of the Body is a contradiction. Our parishes and families are not free of such divisions: rich and poor, old families and newcomers, groups that no longer speak to one another, quarrels even about holy things. Jesus' prayer is for us. The Holy Qurbana is the sacrament of unity, and we cannot receive it with a clear heart while we despise a brother or sister.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Corinthians were a lively, gifted community, yet Paul has to write to them: when you come together as a church, I hear that there are divisions among you (1 Corinthians 11:18). At the Lord's supper the better-off ate their own meal while the poor went hungry.

Paul asks: do you show contempt for the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing? (1 Corinthians 11:22). The meal of unity had become a sign of division.

In the Gospel Jesus prays for us, who believe through the apostles' word: that they may all be one, as you, Father, are in me and I am in you, so that the world may believe that you have sent me (John 17:21). Our unity is meant to be a window through which the world sees God.

In a week the Church begins the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity. Let us start at home: one phone call, one greeting, one apology before we next come to the altar. Amen.

For children

Children, before Jesus died, he prayed for you! He asked his Father that we would all be one big family. So be kind to everyone, even the child nobody plays with.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, on the night before you suffered you prayed that we may all be one, as you and the Father are one. Heal the divisions in my family, my parish and your Church, and never let me despise a brother or sister at your table, so that the world may believe that the Father has sent you. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings set a broken table beside a prayer for unity. In Corinth, Christians who gather for the Lord's Supper shame the poor among them. In John, on the night before he dies, Jesus prays for all who will ever believe, “that they may all be one”. Division among Christians is not a small matter of manners. It hides from the world the God whom Denha makes known.

1 Corinthians 11:17–22 · When you come together

Setting.

Paul writes from Ephesus (16:8), probably in the mid-50s, to a young and divided community in a busy Roman port. Christians then met in private houses for a shared meal, with the Eucharist as part of the gathering. Many scholars think that the better-off arrived early and ate and drank in plenty, while poorer members and slaves, who had to work, came late to find little left. Immediately after this reading Paul gives the oldest written account of the Lord's Supper (11:23–26).

Structure.

  • v. 17: “I do not commend you.”
  • vv. 18–19: divisions when you gather.
  • vv. 20–21: what you are eating is not the Lord's Supper.
  • v. 22: you show contempt for the church of God.

Key words.

  • schismata, divisions: Literally tears, as in cloth. These are not yet separate churches, but rifts within one assembly, here along lines of wealth and class.
  • kyriakon deipnon, the Lord's supper: The only place in the New Testament where this name is used. The meal belongs to the Lord, and it cannot be treated as “your own supper” (v. 21).

Verse by verse.

“For, to begin with, when you come together as a church, I hear that there are divisions among you;” (11:18)

“Church” here is the people assembled. For Paul the church takes visible shape when they gather.

“When you come together, it is not really to eat the Lord’s supper.” (11:20)

This is a shocking sentence. The rite may go on, and it is still contradicted by the way the poor are treated.

“Or do you show contempt for the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?” (11:22)

To shame a poor brother or sister is to despise God's Church. For Paul the two offences are one.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that “the Eucharist commits us to the poor”, and it quotes St John Chrysostom: you have tasted the Blood of the Lord, yet you do not recognise your brother (CCC 1397). The Eucharist makes the Church (CCC 1396), so that what is done to the unity of the people touches what is done at the altar.

John 17:20–26 · That they may all be one

Setting.

John 17 is the long prayer that closes Jesus' words at the Last Supper. He has prayed for himself and for his disciples; at v. 20 the prayer widens to those not yet born. The Church's tradition has often called this the priestly prayer of Jesus. The reader of every age is meant to hear his own name in it.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–21: for all who will believe: that they may be one, so that the world may believe.
  • vv. 22–23: the glory given, so that they may be completely one.
  • v. 24: that they may be with him and see his glory.
  • vv. 25–26: the world does not know you; I have made your name known.

Key words.

  • hen, one: The Greek is neuter: not one person, but one reality, the same word as in “The Father and I are one” (10:30). The unity of the Church has its source in the unity of God.
  • doxa, glory: The revealing brightness of God. In John it is seen above all on the Cross. Jesus gives us a share in it.
  • gnōrizō, to make known: Jesus says he has made God's name known and will do so (v. 26). Past and future join in the same work; through the Spirit the Church goes on growing in the knowledge of him.

Verse by verse.

“I ask not only on behalf of these, but also on behalf of those who will believe in me through their word,” (17:20)

Faith comes through the word of witnesses. Each of us is in this verse.

“As you, Father, are in me and I am in you, may they also be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.” (17:21)

The measure is impossibly high: the Father and the Son. And unity has a purpose outside itself. Division among Christians makes the gospel hard to believe.

“Father, I desire that those also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory,” (17:24)

He ends by asking for what every love asks: to be together. Heaven is described simply as being with him.

“so that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.” (17:26)

These are the last words of the prayer: the Father's love for the Son is to live in us. Then Jesus goes out to the garden (18:1).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls this “the prayer of the Hour of Jesus”, the priestly prayer (CCC 2746–2751), and says that the desire to recover the unity of all Christians is Christ's gift and a call of the Spirit (CCC 820). The Second Vatican Council teaches that division among Christians contradicts Christ's will and is a scandal to the world (Unitatis Redintegratio 1). St John Paul II took his encyclical's title from this verse (Ut Unum Sint, 1995). St Cyril of Alexandria, commenting on this chapter, sees our unity as a work of the Spirit and of the Lord's own body received in the Eucharist (in substance).

Questions people ask

Does Paul say that “there have to be factions”? Does God want division?

No. The tone of v. 19 is sharp, close to irony, or else it reflects the view that the last days bring testing: such strains show who is genuine. Paul has just told them he does not commend them. Elsewhere he begs them to have no divisions at all (1:10).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says humiliating those who have nothing is contempt for the Church. Who could feel out of place in my parish, and what might change that?
  • Jesus prays that we may be one “so that the world may believe”. What can I do this week to heal one division near me?
