Word for the day

“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” John 15:5

Reflection

Hebrews reminds suffering Christians that “the Lord disciplines those whom he loves.” Discipline “always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness.” Hardship received in faith is the Father's training, not his rejection.

Jesus uses the image of the vine: the Father prunes every fruitful branch so that it bears more fruit. Pruning hurts, but its purpose is fruit. And the secret of fruitfulness is simple: abide in me. Prayer, the Word and the Qurbana keep the branch joined to the vine; “apart from me you can do nothing.”

Full homily

Dear friends, the Letter to the Hebrews reminds suffering Christians that the Lord disciplines those whom he loves. Discipline always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness (Hebrews 12:6, 11). Hardship received in faith is the Father's training.

Jesus says: I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinegrower. Every branch that bears fruit he prunes to make it bear more fruit. Abide in me; apart from me you can do nothing (John 15:1–5).

Pruning hurts, but it is a sign of the gardener's love. Offer Jesus one difficulty you are going through now, and stay united to the vine through daily prayer and the Holy Qurbana. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus is the vine and we are the branches. If we stay close to Jesus we bear good fruit: kindness, patience and joy!

Prayer

Father, vinedresser of my soul, prune me with love, keep me joined to your Son, the true vine, and make me bear much fruit for your glory. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of God at work on his children to make them fruitful. Hebrews compares the trials of believers to a father's discipline, which “yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness”. In John, the Father is the vinegrower who prunes the branches so that they bear more fruit. The pruning hurts, but its purpose is life, and the condition for fruit is to abide in Christ.

Hebrews 12:4–13 · The Lord disciplines those whom he loves

Setting.

Chapter 11 of Hebrews listed the heroes of faith; chapter 12 opens by telling the readers to run their race looking to Jesus, who endured the cross (12:1–3). The readers have suffered public abuse and loss of property (10:32–34), but not yet bloodshed. The author helps them see their hardships not as signs that God has abandoned them, but as the training of sons and daughters.

Structure.

  • v. 4: you have not yet resisted to the point of blood.
  • vv. 5–6: a quotation from Proverbs 3:11–12: the Lord disciplines those he loves.
  • vv. 7–11: the argument from human fathers to the Father of spirits; discipline is painful now but fruitful later.
  • vv. 12–13: lift drooping hands, strengthen weak knees (Isaiah 35:3), make straight paths.

Key words.

  • paideia, discipline, training (vv. 5–11): The Greek word for the whole education of a child: instruction, correction and formation. It is not punishment for its own sake but upbringing.
  • gymnazō, to train (v. 11): The word from which “gymnasium” comes. Trials are the training ground of holiness, like an athlete's exercises.

Verse by verse.

“for the Lord disciplines those whom he loves, and chastises every child whom he accepts.” (12:6)
“he disciplines us for our good, in order that we may share his holiness.” (12:10)

The goal of God's training is nothing less than a share in his own holiness.

“Now, discipline always seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” (12:11)

An honest verse: it does not pretend that suffering is pleasant. But it promises fruit: peace and righteousness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the way of perfection passes by way of the Cross, and that there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle (CCC 2015). The Church does not say that God sends every evil, but that he can bring good out of all things for those who love him (Romans 8:28; CCC 313). Suffering accepted in faith becomes a share in Christ's own obedience.

John 15:1–8 · Abide in me

Setting.

These words belong to Jesus' farewell discourse at the Last Supper (John 13–17). In the Old Testament Israel is often pictured as God's vine or vineyard, sometimes disappointing its owner (Isaiah 5:1–7; Psalm 80:8–16; Jeremiah 2:21). Jesus calls himself “the true vine”: in him the people of God finally bears the fruit God looked for.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the vine, the vinegrower, the pruning; you are already cleansed by the word.
  • vv. 4–5: abide in me; apart from me you can do nothing.
  • v. 6: the branch that does not abide withers.
  • vv. 7–8: prayer answered; the Father glorified by much fruit.

Key words.

  • menō, abide, remain: The key word: it appears again and again in these verses. It means a lasting, living union, like the branch that stays joined to the vine and lives from its sap.
  • airei / kathairei, removes / prunes (v. 2): A play on words in Greek: the Father “takes away” the dead branch and “cleans” the living one. Then in v. 3 Jesus says, “You are already cleansed (katharoi)”: the word of Jesus is the first pruning.

Verse by verse.

“Every branch that bears fruit he prunes to make it bear more fruit.” (15:2)

Pruning is a sign of hope: the vinegrower prunes only the branches he expects to bear fruit.

“I am the vine, you are the branches. Those who abide in me and I in them bear much fruit, because apart from me you can do nothing.” (15:5)

Not “little”, but “nothing”. Without Christ we can do nothing that bears fruit for eternal life.

“My Father is glorified by this, that you bear much fruit and become my disciples.” (15:8)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these words to show the intimate communion between Christ and his members: “Abide in me, and I in you” (CCC 787). St Augustine, commenting on this Gospel, says that the branches are in the vine in such a way that they give nothing to the vine but receive their life from it (Tractates on John 81, in substance). The Holy Qurbana is where this abiding is nourished most of all (John 6:56).

Questions people ask

Does God cause my sufferings to punish me?

Hebrews does not say that every suffering is a punishment for a particular sin. It says that God, as a loving Father, uses hardships to form us. Jesus himself refused the idea that suffering always comes from personal sin (John 9:2–3).

For study and teaching

  • What “pruning” is God doing in my life now, and what fruit might he be preparing?
  • How do I “abide” in Christ through the week, not only on Sunday?
