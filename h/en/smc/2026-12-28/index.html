Full homily

Dear friends, the joy of Christmas is followed by tears. Herod, furious at being tricked by the Magi, killed all the children in and around Bethlehem who were two years old or under (Matthew 2:16). Matthew hears in the mothers' cry the voice of Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more (Matthew 2:18; Jeremiah 31:15).

The first reading takes us back to the beginning: Cain kills his brother Abel (Genesis 4:8). From the first family, innocent blood has cried to God. The Holy Innocents join this long line, and they are the first to die in the place of Christ.

Why does God allow this? We do not have a full answer. But Christmas tells us that God did not stay distant from suffering. He came into a world where children are killed, and he himself would be killed. He is with the innocent who suffer.

The Letter to the Hebrews speaks of those who suffered for their faith: they were stoned, sawn in two, killed by the sword; of whom the world was not worthy (Hebrews 11:37–38). The Holy Innocents could not speak, but their lives witnessed to Christ.

Today the Church in Kerala keeps Holy Childhood Day: children praying for and sharing with other children. Let us remember the innocents of today: unborn children lost to abortion, children who are abused, trafficked, starving or caught in war. Herod is still at work.

Jeremiah gives a promise: there is hope for your future, says the Lord (Jeremiah 31:17). Let us protect children in our families and parishes, defend the unborn, and support children in need. And let us comfort parents who grieve the loss of a child. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The joy of Christmas is followed at once by the tears of Bethlehem. Today’s readings draw one line of innocent blood through the whole Bible. Genesis shows the first murder, of a brother, by a man who would not master his anger. Jeremiah hears Rachel weeping and carries God’s promise that her children will come back. Hebrews remembers those who suffered for their faith and of whom “the world was not worthy”. Matthew shows the children of Bethlehem, killed by a frightened king, while the Child he sought is carried away. The key words are blood, weeping and hope.

Genesis 4:3–8 · Where the killing began

Setting.

Genesis 3 told how the first man and woman turned from God. Genesis 4 shows what follows outside the garden: sin moves from the relation with God to the relation with a brother. The story is short and bare, and the word “brother” sounds seven times in vv. 1–11.

The names may be meaningful. Abel (hebel in Hebrew) means “breath” or “vapour”, the word used in Ecclesiastes for what passes away. His life is short, and he never speaks in the story.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–5a: two offerings; the Lord regards one and not the other.
  • v. 5b: Cain’s anger and his fallen face.
  • vv. 6–7: God speaks to Cain before the crime, warning him as a father would.
  • v. 8: the field and the killing.

Key words.

  • minḥâ, offering: A gift or tribute. Both brothers bring one. The text does not explain why only Abel’s is regarded. It only notes that Abel brought the “firstlings” and “their fat portions”, the best that he had.
  • rōbēṣ, lurking: Crouching, lying in wait, like an animal at a door. Sin is pictured as a beast waiting for Cain to open.
  • təšûqâ, desire: The same rare word as in Genesis 3:16. Sin wants to possess Cain, yet the verse says he “must master it”. Temptation is real, and so is human responsibility.

Verse by verse.

“but for Cain and his offering he had no regard. So Cain was very angry, and his countenance fell.” (4:5)

The hidden wound is envy. The Hebrew says literally that it burned in him. His face tells what his heart is doing.

“If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin is lurking at the door; its desire is for you, but you must master it.” (4:7)

God does not reject Cain. Before the crime he speaks to him, questions him and offers a way out. The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, but its main sense is clear: nothing is yet decided.

“Cain said to his brother Abel, “Let us go out to the field.” And when they were in the field, Cain rose up against his brother Abel, and killed him.” (4:8)

A note on the text. The standard Hebrew text says “Cain said to his brother Abel”, and then gives no words. The words “Let us go out to the field” come from ancient versions, including the Greek and the Syriac, and both the NRSV and the POC include them. Two lines later a brother is dead.

How the Church reads it.

Jesus himself names “righteous Abel” as the first in the line of those who shed innocent blood (Matthew 23:35). Hebrews says that Christ’s blood “speaks a better word than the blood of Abel” (12:24), for Abel’s blood cries out for justice, and Christ’s for mercy. The Catechism sees in this story the anger and envy that are the consequences of original sin (CCC 2259). St John Paul II opens Evangelium Vitae with a long meditation on Cain and Abel (EV 7–9). The Syriac teacher Aphrahat, in his Demonstration on Prayer, says that Abel’s offering was accepted because of the purity of his heart (in substance).

Jeremiah 31:15–17 · Rachel weeping, and a word of hope

Setting.

Jeremiah 30–31 is often called the Book of Consolation, a bright section in a dark book. The prophet lived through the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC. The northern tribes had been taken away by Assyria more than a century earlier.

Ramah lay a few miles north of Jerusalem, in the land of Benjamin, and Jeremiah says that exiles were gathered there in chains (Jeremiah 40:1). Rachel, Jacob’s beloved wife, was the mother of Joseph and Benjamin, and so the grandmother of the great northern tribes. She had died long before; the prophet imagines her crying from her grave for her lost descendants.

Structure.

  • v. 15: the voice in Ramah: Rachel refuses to be comforted.
  • vv. 16–17: the Lord’s answer: stop weeping; they will come back.
  • ¶ “Thus says the Lord” opens both parts. God is the one who reports her grief, and God is the one who answers it.

Key words.

  • mē’ănâ ləhinnāḥēm, refuses to be comforted: A very similar phrase is used of Jacob when he thinks Joseph is dead (Genesis 37:35). God does not scold this refusal; he answers it with a promise.
  • śākār, reward: “A reward for your work”, as if her tears were labour that God takes into account.
  • tiqwâ, hope: From the same root as the verb for waiting with eager hope. Jeremiah uses the word for the future God plans for his people (29:11).

Verse by verse.

“Rachel is weeping for her children; she refuses to be comforted for her children, because they are no more.” (31:15)

The Bible lets a mother cry with no easy words offered. It gives this grief the dignity of Scripture.

“Keep your voice from weeping, and your eyes from tears; for there is a reward for your work, says the Lord: they shall come back from the land of the enemy;” (31:16)

God alone can say “stop weeping” without being cruel, because he alone can bring the children back.

“there is hope for your future, says the Lord: your children shall come back to their own country.” (31:17)

Later in this same chapter comes the promise of the new covenant (31:31–34).

How the Church reads it.

Matthew quotes v. 15 today and stops there. A reader who knows Jeremiah hears what comes next, the promise of return, and so the Gospel’s silence is not despair. Jewish tradition has also treasured Rachel as the mother who weeps and intercedes for her children; a well-known passage in the Midrash on Lamentations says that God was moved by her plea (in substance). For Christians the final answer to every Rachel is the Resurrection.

Hebrews 11:32–38 · Of whom the world was not worthy

Setting.

Hebrews 11 is the great roll call of faith. It opens by defining faith as “the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1), and it runs from Abel (11:4) through Abraham and Moses. At v. 32 the author runs out of time and hurries on.

The readers are tired Christians who have already known suffering (10:32–34). The chapter tells them that the people of God has always walked by faith, in victory and in defeat alike.

Structure.

  • v. 32: names from Judges, David, Samuel and the prophets.
  • vv. 33–35a: faith that triumphs: kingdoms won, lions’ mouths shut, fire quenched, the dead given back.
  • vv. 35b–38: faith that suffers: torture, chains, stoning, the sword, the wilderness.
  • ¶ The turning point is in the middle of v. 35. The second list is not a failure of faith. Both lists are “through faith”.

Key words.

  • etympanisthēsan, they were tortured: A kind of torture by beating. The related word appears in the account of the old scribe Eleazar in 2 Maccabees 6:19, 28, which this passage seems to remember.
  • kreittonos anastaseōs, a better resurrection: Better than the return to this life given to the widows’ sons (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:18–37). The brothers in 2 Maccabees 7 refused release in the hope of being raised by God.
  • melōtais, skins of sheep: The word used in the Greek Old Testament for the mantle of Elijah. These are prophets’ clothes.

Verse by verse.

“who through faith conquered kingdoms, administered justice, obtained promises, shut the mouths of lions,” (11:33)

Lions recall Daniel (Daniel 6). The “raging fire” of the next verse recalls the three young men (Daniel 3).

“Others were tortured, refusing to accept release, in order to obtain a better resurrection.” (11:35)

Faith here looks beyond death. Their courage rests on a promise that they did not see fulfilled.

“They were stoned to death, they were sawn in two, they were killed by the sword;” (11:37)

Zechariah son of Jehoiada was stoned in the Temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–22). An old Jewish tradition says that Isaiah was sawn in two. A note on the text: some ancient manuscripts have one more verb here, “they were tested”, and the POC reflects a text of this kind (ചിലരെ വിചാരണ ചെയ്തു); the NRSV follows those without it.

“of whom the world was not worthy.” (11:38)

A stunning reversal. The world judged them worthless and drove them out. God’s verdict is that the world did not deserve them.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Hebrews 11 a eulogy of the faith of the ancestors (CCC 147) and urges us to look to this “cloud of witnesses” (Hebrews 12:1; CCC 165). Martyrdom is “the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith” (CCC 2473). On this feast the reading lets the children of Bethlehem take their place in that long line.

Matthew 2:13–18 · A voice was heard in Ramah

Setting.

Herod feared any rival. The Magi had asked him for the “king of the Jews” (2:2), a title that belonged to him by Roman grant. When they did not come back, he acted.

No other ancient writer records this event, and Bethlehem was small. Many scholars reckon the number of boys to have been modest, perhaps a score or so. Josephus does not mention it, but he describes a Herod who had his wife Mariamne and three of his own sons put to death, and the deed fits that picture.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–15: the Child is taken to safety; Hosea 11:1 is fulfilled.
  • v. 16: Herod’s rage and the killing.
  • vv. 17–18: Jeremiah’s word is fulfilled.
  • ¶ Matthew is telling a new story of Moses: a ruler kills Hebrew boys (Exodus 1:15–22), and one child escapes to save his people.

Key words.

  • enepaichthē, tricked, made a fool of: The same verb is used when the soldiers mock Jesus as king (Matthew 27:29, 31). The powerful who cannot bear being mocked here will mock him there.
  • ethymōthē, infuriated: Burning anger, the same inner fire as Cain’s. Fear of losing power is enough to set it alight.
  • tous paidas, the children: A masculine form, so the POC rightly says “boys” (ആൺകുട്ടികളെ). Two years is a wide margin: fear calculates generously.

Verse by verse.

“and remained there until the death of Herod. This was to fulfill what had been spoken by the Lord through the prophet, “Out of Egypt I have called my son.”” (2:15)

Hosea 11:1 speaks of Israel. Matthew sees Jesus as the true Son, who sums up his people’s whole story in himself.

“he was infuriated, and he sent and killed all the children in and around Bethlehem who were two years old or under,” (2:16)

Matthew’s words are plain and restrained. He does not describe the scene; he states it.

“Then was fulfilled what had been spoken through the prophet Jeremiah:” (2:17)

Compare the wording in 2:15, “This was to fulfill”. Here, as often noted, Matthew writes “Then was fulfilled”, avoiding any suggestion that God intended this evil.

““A voice was heard in Ramah, wailing and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be consoled, because they are no more.”” (2:18)

Tradition placed Rachel’s tomb on the way to Bethlehem (Genesis 35:19). The mothers of Bethlehem join the mother of Israel in her grief.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that this event shows “the opposition of darkness to the light”, and that those who belong to Christ share his persecution (CCC 530). The Church honours these children as martyrs. St Irenaeus says that the Lord sent these children ahead of him into his kingdom (in substance). St Quodvultdeus of Carthage, in a sermon the Roman Office reads on this feast, says that these children die for Christ without knowing it, and that God’s grace gives them a crown (in substance). The feast is also a defence of the weakest: every human life is to be protected from its beginning (CCC 2270; Evangelium Vitae).

Questions people ask

How can babies be martyrs if they did not choose to die for Christ?

A martyr’s crown is above all God’s gift. These children died in place of Christ and because of him. The Church has long held that those who die for the sake of Christ without Baptism are baptized by their death for and with him (CCC 1258), and it has honoured the Innocents from early times.

Why did God warn Joseph and not the other families?

The Gospel gives no answer, and no honest reader should pretend that this is easy. Evil here comes from Herod, not from God. God does not explain suffering in the Bible by theory; he enters it. The Child saved today will not be saved from Calvary. The Catechism says that only the whole of the Christian faith answers the question of evil, and it points to the Cross and the Resurrection (CCC 309, 272).

What about children who die today without Baptism, or before they are born?

The Church entrusts them to the mercy of God, who desires that all be saved, and to Jesus’ tenderness toward children, which allows us to hope that there is a way of salvation for them (CCC 1261).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In Kerala this day is known as Kunjippaithangal, and it is kept as Holy Childhood Day (Thirubalasakhyam). The Holy Childhood Association was founded in France in 1843 by Bishop Charles de Forbin-Janson so that children might pray and share for other children, and it is a Pontifical Mission Society. So the Syro-Malabar child who gives a small coin today stands against Herod in a real way. At the Holy Qurbana we may remember children who suffer now: the unborn, the abused, the trafficked, the hungry and those caught in war. We may also remember parents who have buried a child: the Bible gives them Rachel’s voice, and Rachel’s promise.

For study and teaching

  • Before the crime God speaks to Cain: “you must master it”. What anger or envy is crouching at my door today?
  • Jeremiah 31 lets Rachel weep before God answers. How do we keep company with those who grieve, without hurrying them?
  • Hebrews says that the world was “not worthy” of those it despised. Who are the despised in my town, and how does the Church stand with them?
  • What can my parish or family do this year for children who suffer?
