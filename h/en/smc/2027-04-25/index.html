Word for the day

“Jesus said to them, “Come and have breakfast.” Now none of the disciples dared to ask him, “Who are you?” because they knew it was the Lord.” John 21:12

Reflection

This Sunday carries the name of Mar Addai. The Church of the East has always honoured Addai and his disciple Mari as the apostles who carried the Gospel to Edessa and to Mesopotamia, and our Syro-Malabar Church, which shares that East Syriac heritage, prays at most of its Qurbanas the Anaphora of the Apostles Mar Addai and Mar Mari, one of the most ancient Eucharistic prayers still in use anywhere in the world. What do we know of Addai? The historian Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, records a tradition he found in the archives of Edessa: after the Ascension, the apostle Thomas sent Thaddeus, one of the seventy disciples, to King Abgar of Edessa, who was healed and received the faith. The Syriac Teaching of Addai tells the same story with many additions. Historians cannot verify every detail, and we hold them as tradition, but the core is precious: Edessa became one of the first Christian cities, the cradle of Syriac Christianity, and its memory links Addai to Thomas, our own father in faith. In 2001, in guidelines for the Chaldean Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, the Holy See recognised the validity of this ancient anaphora even in the form used by the Assyrian Church of the East, which has no explicit narrative of the Last Supper. When our priest prays it, he prays in the words of a Church that has offered the Sacrifice in the language close to that of Jesus since the first centuries.

The readings of the Fifth Sunday of Resurrection begin with the Servant of the LORD in Isaiah. He is “deeply despised, abhorred by the nations, the slave of rulers”, yet God promises: “Kings shall see and stand up, princes, and they shall prostrate themselves.” God gives the Servant “as a covenant to the people”, to say “to the prisoners, ‘Come out,’ to those who are in darkness, ‘Show yourselves.’” The returning exiles will not hunger or thirst, “for he who has pity on them will lead them, and by springs of water will guide them.” And all creation is invited to sing: “Sing for joy, O heavens, and exult, O earth; break forth, O mountains, into singing! For the LORD has comforted his people, and will have compassion on his suffering ones.” The despised Servant who is exalted is Christ, rejected on Good Friday and raised at Easter. Paul quotes this very chapter when he writes, “See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” In the Acts of the Apostles we see the prisoner of darkness called out. Saul, “still breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord”, sets out for Damascus with letters to arrest those “who belonged to the Way”. A light from heaven flashes around him, he falls to the ground, and a voice asks: “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?” “Who are you, Lord?” “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” The Risen Lord so identifies himself with his Church that to strike the disciples is to strike him. Saul rises blind and is led by the hand into the city; for three days he neither eats nor drinks. The longer reading tells how Ananias, sent by the Lord, lays hands on him: something like scales fall from his eyes, he is baptised, and he eats and is strengthened.

The Letter to the Hebrews draws the conclusion for us who have been baptised: “Therefore, my friends, since we have confidence to enter the sanctuary by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain (that is, through his flesh), and since we have a great priest over the house of God, let us approach with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water.” Hearts sprinkled and bodies washed: the writer is thinking of Baptism. The curtain of the Temple that hid the Holy of Holies has been opened in the torn flesh of Christ, and now we may come near. Three exhortations follow: let us approach, let us hold fast to the confession of our hope, and “let us consider how to provoke one another to love and good deeds, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day approaching.” Already in the first century some Christians were staying away from the Sunday assembly. The letter's answer is not a scolding but a reminder: we need one another, and we need the meeting at the altar where the new and living way is opened. Our Qurbana begins with the veil drawn before the sanctuary in some of our churches and opened for the celebration, a living sign of this very text.

The Gospel takes us back to the lakeshore. Seven disciples have gone home to Galilee, Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, the sons of Zebedee and two others, and Peter says, “I am going fishing.” They toil all night and catch nothing. At daybreak a stranger on the beach calls: “Children, you have no fish, have you?” “Cast the net to the right side of the boat, and you will find some.” The net fills so full they cannot haul it in. The beloved disciple is the first to see: “It is the Lord!” Peter, impulsive as ever, throws on his clothes and jumps into the sea. On the shore they find a charcoal fire, fish laid on it, and bread. The Lord who once asked for five loaves now has breakfast ready, and still he says, “Bring some of the fish that you have just caught.” He wants our work joined to his gift. Then the gentle invitation: “Come and have breakfast.” No one dares to ask, “Who are you?” because they know it is the Lord. “Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish.” It is the third time he has shown himself to them after rising from the dead, and again he makes himself known at a meal. Among the seven is Thomas, the apostle who would carry the Gospel east and, by our tradition, to the shores of Kerala, and who, Eusebius tells us, sent Thaddeus, the Addai of Syriac tradition, to Edessa. A Church of fishermen, sent from a lakeshore breakfast to the ends of the earth: this is our story too. Every Sunday the Risen Lord stands on the shore of our tired week, calls us children, fills our empty nets with his grace, and says at the altar, Come and eat.

Full homily

Dear friends, this Fifth Sunday of Resurrection bears the name of Mar Addai, the apostle of Edessa. When our priest prays the Anaphora of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, he prays with one of the oldest Eucharistic prayers in the world. Eusebius records a tradition that it was the apostle Thomas, our father in faith, who sent Thaddeus, known in Syriac tradition as Addai, to Edessa.

Isaiah sings of the Servant despised by all, whom kings will one day honour. God gives him as a covenant, to say to prisoners, “Come out.” “Sing for joy, O heavens, and exult, O earth.” The despised Servant exalted is Christ, rejected on the Cross and risen at Easter.

In Acts, a prisoner of darkness is called out: Saul, breathing threats against the disciples. A light flashes, he falls, and hears, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?” “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” Christ is one with his Church. Blind for three days, Saul is then baptised and becomes the great apostle.

Hebrews says that through the blood of Jesus a new and living way is open to us: “let us approach with a true heart.” And it adds: let us not neglect to meet together, but encourage one another. Even then, some stayed away on Sunday. We need one another, and we need the altar.

In the Gospel the disciples fish all night and catch nothing. At daybreak Jesus fills their nets. The beloved disciple cries, “It is the Lord!” On the shore Jesus has a fire, fish and bread ready, and he says, “Come and have breakfast.” He takes the bread and gives it to them.

Among them is Thomas, who would bring the Gospel to our shores. Every Sunday the Risen Lord stands on the shore of our tired week, fills our empty nets with grace, and says at the altar: Come and eat. Let us recognise him, and bring someone back with us next Sunday. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, the disciples fished all night and caught nothing. Then Jesus told them where to throw the net, and it was full! And Jesus cooked them breakfast on the beach!

Every Sunday at the Holy Qurbana, Jesus says to us, ‘Come and eat.’ Let us say like John: ‘It is the Lord!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you stood on the shore at daybreak, filled the empty nets of your disciples and fed them with bread and fish. You called Saul out of darkness and made the persecutor an apostle. Through the prayers of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, and of Mar Thoma our father in faith, keep our Church faithful to the faith of the apostles. Give us confidence to approach you with a true heart, make us faithful to the Sunday assembly, and let us recognise you each time you say to us at the altar, Come and eat. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday bears the name of Mar Addai, the apostle of Edessa, and its four readings move from darkness into light and from a long night to a meal at dawn. Isaiah hears God say to prisoners, “Come out,” and to those in darkness, “Show yourselves.” In Acts a light from heaven throws Saul to the ground, and the persecutor is led by the hand, blind, into Damascus, where his new life will begin. Hebrews tells the baptized that a new and living way into God’s presence stands open, and that they must not stay away from the assembly. In John the risen Lord stands on the shore after a night of empty nets, fills the net, and feeds his disciples with bread and fish. The words to watch are light, way and Lord. The feast of St Mark the Evangelist (25 April) gives way to the Sunday this year.

Isaiah 49:7–13 · Prisoners, come out

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ. Many scholars think they come from a prophet of the exile who stands in the tradition of Isaiah of Jerusalem. The message is consolation: God is about to bring his people home in a new exodus.

Just before this reading comes the second of the four “Servant songs” (49:1–6), in which the Servant, called “Israel” (49:3), is made “a light to the nations”. Today’s verses take up that Servant, despised and enslaved, and promise that kings will bow before him. Then the road home opens.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the one deeply despised will be honoured by kings.
  • vv. 8–9a: a day of salvation: the Servant given as a covenant, prisoners called out.
  • vv. 9b–11: the new exodus: pasture on bare heights, springs of water, mountains made into a road.
  • v. 12: the scattered come from every direction.
  • v. 13: heaven, earth and mountains are called to sing.

Key words.

  • ‘ēt rāṣôn, time of favor: A moment chosen by God’s good pleasure. It is not a time we fix; it is the time God gives.
  • bĕrît ‘ām, a covenant to the people: The Servant does not only make a covenant; he is the covenant (also 42:6). Christians hear this fulfilled in the one who says, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25).
  • raḥam, to have compassion: The verb behind “he who has pity on them” (v. 10) and “will have compassion” (v. 13) is related to reḥem, the womb. Two verses later God asks, “Can a woman forget her nursing child?” (49:15). God’s compassion is a mother’s love.

Verse by verse.

“Thus says the Lord, the Redeemer of Israel and his Holy One, to one deeply despised, abhorred by the nations, the slave of rulers,” (49:7)

The despised one and the exalted one are the same. The Church has always read here the way of Christ, rejected and then raised, which the fourth Servant song (52:13–53:12) will tell in full.

“In a time of favor I have answered you, on a day of salvation I have helped you;” (49:8)

St Paul quotes this verse and adds, “See, now is the acceptable time; see, now is the day of salvation!” (2 Corinthians 6:2). The day Isaiah promised is today.

“saying to the prisoners, “Come out,” to those who are in darkness, “Show yourselves.”” (49:9)

Read beside today’s second reading, this verse describes Saul: in darkness three days, then called out into the light.

“they shall not hunger or thirst, neither scorching wind nor sun shall strike them down, for he who has pity on them will lead them, and by springs of water will guide them.” (49:10)

The Book of Revelation takes up these words for the great multitude before the throne, whom the Lamb will guide “to springs of the water of life” (Revelation 7:16–17).

“and these from the land of Syene.” (49:12)

Textual note: the traditional Hebrew text reads “the land of Sinim”. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran supports “Syene”, the town of Aswan on the southern border of Egypt, and the NRSV follows it.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the Servant of these songs as Christ. The Catechism says that the Messiah’s characteristics are revealed above all in the Servant songs, which proclaim the meaning of Jesus’ Passion (CCC 713). Simeon greets the child Jesus as “a light for revelation to the Gentiles” (Luke 2:32), and Paul and Barnabas take up Isaiah 49:6 for their own mission to the nations (Acts 13:47). Jewish readers have understood the Servant as Israel, as the song itself names him (49:3), and we read that with respect: the Church confesses that Christ takes up and fulfils the calling of Israel, which God has never revoked (Romans 11:29).

Acts 9:1–9 · Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?

Setting.

After the death of Stephen, at which Saul stood by and approved (8:1), a persecution scattered the believers of Jerusalem. Saul, a Pharisee and zealous for the law (Philippians 3:5–6), now carries the campaign to Damascus, where there were synagogues and already some disciples of Jesus.

Acts tells this event three times: here, and twice in Paul’s own speeches (22:6–16; 26:12–18). Luke never mentions a horse; that comes from later painting. Everyone in the scene is Jewish: Saul, the high priest, the disciples in Damascus, and Jesus himself. This is a quarrel within one people over Jesus, not a story of Jews against Christians.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Saul’s plan and the letters from the high priest.
  • vv. 3–4: light from heaven, the fall, the voice.
  • vv. 5–6: “Who are you, Lord?” “I am Jesus.” The command to go into the city.
  • vv. 7–9: the companions; three days blind, without food or drink.

Key words.

  • hē hodos, the Way: The earliest name of the Christian movement in Acts (also 19:9, 23; 22:4; 24:14). Before they were called Christians, the disciples were people of a road: the way of the one who said, “I am the way” (John 14:6).
  • diōkō, persecute: The voice uses this verb twice. Saul thought he was pursuing men and women; he was pursuing Christ.
  • Saoul, Saoul, Saul, Saul: Here the Greek keeps the Hebrew form of his name, not the Greek form Saulos. In 26:14 Paul says the voice spoke “in the Hebrew language”. The name is doubled, as God called “Abraham, Abraham” and “Samuel! Samuel!”

Verse by verse.

“He fell to the ground and heard a voice saying to him, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?”” (9:4)

Saul had never met Jesus in Galilee. He had seized his disciples. Yet the Lord says “me”. Here, at the very beginning of Paul’s life as an apostle, is the seed of his later teaching: “you are the body of Christ” (1 Corinthians 12:27).

““Who are you, Lord?” The reply came, “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” (9:5)

The question of a man whose certainty has fallen with him. The answer is a name: Jesus, alive.

“though his eyes were open, he could see nothing; so they led him by the hand” (9:8)

The man who came to lead others away in chains is himself led by the hand. His blindness is not a punishment but a pause, until another disciple, Ananias, lays hands on him (9:17).

“For three days he was without sight, and neither ate nor drank.” (9:9)

Three days of darkness and fasting, and then light and baptism (9:18). Many readers see here Saul’s own share in the three days of the Lord’s death and rising.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine often returns to this voice from heaven. Christ, he says, was seated in heaven and no one could touch him there, yet he cried out “Why do you persecute me?”, because the head speaks for the members, and Christ and his Church are one (in substance). The Catechism takes up Augustine’s teaching on the “whole Christ”, head and members (CCC 795). Paul himself never forgot what he had been: “I am the least of the apostles, unfit to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God” (1 Corinthians 15:9). The Church does not pray for her persecutors as enemies to be destroyed but as people Christ may still call.

Hebrews 10:19–25 · A new and living way

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews does not name its author. In the early centuries some thought it was by Paul and some did not; today most scholars think it comes from a teacher close to Paul’s circle. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture whoever its writer was.

Chapters 7–10 have shown that Christ is the true high priest. Once a year the high priest of Israel passed behind the curtain into the Holy of Holies with blood (9:7). Christ has entered the true sanctuary once for all. Today’s reading begins the great “therefore”: what we are to do now that the way is open.

Structure.

  • vv. 19–21: two reasons: we have confidence to enter, and we have a great priest.
  • v. 22: “let us approach”: faith.
  • v. 23: “let us hold fast”: hope.
  • vv. 24–25: “let us consider how to provoke one another”: love, shown in the assembly. ¶ Faith, hope and love, the three that Paul names together (1 Corinthians 13:13), give the passage its shape.

Key words.

  • parrēsia, confidence: Literally the right to speak openly, the boldness of a citizen with full rights. Through the blood of Jesus we stand before God as children, not as strangers.
  • katapetasma, curtain: The veil before the Holy of Holies. The Gospels say it was torn in two when Jesus died (Mark 15:38). Hebrews says the true curtain is his flesh: we pass to God through the body of Christ.
  • episynagōgē, meeting together: The gathering of the community (v. 25). The same noun is used in 2 Thessalonians 2:1 for our being gathered to the Lord at his coming. Every Sunday assembly is a small foretaste of that last gathering.

Verse by verse.

“by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain (that is, through his flesh),” (10:20)

The way is not a method but a person. It is “new” because it was never open before, and “living” because the one who opened it is alive.

“our bodies washed with pure water.” (10:22)

Many commentators, ancient and modern, hear Baptism in these words, joined to the sprinkling of the heart. The way into the sanctuary begins at the font.

“Let us hold fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who has promised is faithful.” (10:23)

Our firmness rests on God’s faithfulness, not on our own strength. Christian assurance is a hope, held fast.

“not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some,” (10:25)

Already in the first generation some were drifting away from the assembly. The remedy given is not a rule alone but mutual encouragement.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites this verse when it teaches that the practice of the Christian assembly goes back to the beginnings of the apostolic age (CCC 2178). St John Paul II’s letter on keeping Sunday holy, Dies Domini (1998), presents the Sunday Eucharist as the heart of the Christian week. In Hebrews the one sacrifice of Christ is never repeated, and in the Holy Qurbana it is not repeated but made present, so that the assembly enters the sanctuary by the same new and living way.

John 21:1–14 · It is the Lord!

Setting.

John 20 seems to close the Gospel: “these are written so that you may come to believe” (20:31). Chapter 21 then adds one more appearance. Many scholars think it was added by the evangelist’s disciples, who say “we know that his testimony is true” (21:24). No ancient manuscript of the Gospel lacks it, and the Church receives it as part of the inspired Gospel.

The Sea of Tiberias is the Sea of Galilee, named after the city Herod Antipas built on its shore. Seven disciples are there, among them Thomas, the apostle our Church calls its father in faith. They go back to the work they knew. Night was the usual time for fishing on the lake.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: seven disciples, Peter’s decision, a night with nothing.
  • vv. 4–6: a stranger on the shore at daybreak; the net cast on the right side.
  • vv. 7–8: the beloved disciple recognizes him; Peter leaps into the sea.
  • vv. 9–11: the charcoal fire, the bread and fish; one hundred fifty-three large fish; the net not torn.
  • vv. 12–14: “Come and have breakfast”; he gives them bread and fish; the third appearance.

Key words.

  • paidia, children: A tender word (v. 5), the address of a father or an elder to those in his care. The one who asks has the meal already prepared.
  • anthrakia, charcoal fire: This word appears in the New Testament only here and in 18:18, where Peter warmed himself and denied the Lord. Peter is brought back to a charcoal fire, and in the next verses he will be asked three times, “Do you love me?”
  • ouk eschisthē, was not torn: From schizō, the root of “schism”. At the Cross the soldiers said of Jesus’ tunic, “Let us not tear it” (19:24), using the same verb. The net of the Church holds many and is not torn.

Verse by verse.

“Just after daybreak, Jesus stood on the beach; but the disciples did not know that it was Jesus.” (21:4)

In John’s Easter stories the Risen Lord is not at once recognized: by Mary Magdalene in the garden (20:14), and now by these fishermen. He is the same Lord, but recognizing him is a gift.

“That disciple whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It is the Lord!”” (21:7)

Love sees first; Peter acts first. The beloved disciple and Peter appear together at the empty tomb as well (20:3–8). The Church needs both.

“full of large fish, a hundred fifty-three of them; and though there were so many, the net was not torn.” (21:11)

The exact number shows that someone counted. The Fathers saw in it a sign of the fullness of the Church (see below).

“Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and did the same with the fish.” (21:13)

These are the movements of the feeding of the five thousand by the same lake (6:11). The early Christians could not hear them without thinking of the Eucharist.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine notes that 153 is the sum of all the numbers from 1 to 17, and that seventeen joins the ten commandments of the law to the sevenfold gift of the Holy Spirit; the number stands for all who are saved by grace (in substance, Tractates on John 122). St Jerome reports that writers on fishes counted a hundred and fifty-three kinds, so the full net shows the Church gathering every kind of people (in substance). The Catechism points to this meal when it says that the Risen Lord established direct contact with his disciples by the sharing of a meal (CCC 645).

Questions people ask

Why did the disciples not recognize Jesus at once?

The Risen Lord is truly the same Jesus, in the same body that bore the wounds, but that body is now glorified and no longer bound by the conditions of this life (CCC 645–646). In John’s Gospel recognition comes through a word or a sign: Mary knows him when he calls her name; the fishermen know him through the full net. Faith is needed to see him, then and now.

Acts 9:7 says Saul’s companions heard the voice, but Acts 22:9 says they did not hear it. Is this a contradiction?

Luke wrote both passages and saw no contradiction. One common explanation is that the companions heard a sound but did not understand the words spoken to Saul; the Greek verb for “hear” can carry both senses. In both accounts the point is the same: the others knew something had happened, but the call was for Saul.

Does the number 153 have a hidden meaning?

John does not explain it. The number may simply record an eyewitness memory of a catch so great that it was counted. The Fathers drew lessons from it about the fullness and unity of the Church, and the Church allows such readings as long as they serve the plain sense: the Lord’s mission gathers all, and the net does not tear.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

The Syro-Malabar calendar keeps Mar Addai on the Fifth Sunday of Resurrection. The historian Eusebius, writing early in the fourth century, records a tradition from the archives of Edessa that after the Ascension the apostle Thomas sent Thaddaeus, one of the Seventy, to heal King Abgar and preach in that city; the Syriac tradition calls him Addai. How much of the story is history is debated, but the memory of an apostolic mission to Edessa is ancient and dear to all the Churches of the East. Most of our Holy Qurbanas are prayed with the Anaphora of the Apostles Mar Addai and Mar Mari, one of the oldest Eucharistic prayers still in use.

Today’s Gospel fits the day well. Thomas is in the boat; the Lord gives bread with his own hands; and Hebrews calls us to the assembly. When the sanctuary veil is drawn back and the Qurbana begins, we stand where the disciples stood on the shore: before the Lord who has already prepared the meal and asks us to bring some of our own catch.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah hears God say to prisoners, “Come out.” What darkness in my life, or in my family, needs that word today?
  • The Lord tells Saul that to persecute his disciples is to persecute him. How does this change the way I treat other Christians, and those who suffer for their faith?
  • Hebrews joins faith, hope and love to the Sunday assembly. Who in my circle has drifted from the Qurbana, and how could I encourage them?
  • The disciples worked all night and caught nothing until the Lord spoke. Where am I working hard without listening to him?
