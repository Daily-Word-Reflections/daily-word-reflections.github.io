Word for the day

“But be doers of the word, and not merely hearers who deceive themselves.” James 1:22

Reflection

James writes like a man who has watched many people come out of church unchanged. “But be doers of the word, and not merely hearers who deceive themselves.” Then he gives an image everyone understands: “For if any are hearers of the word and not doers, they are like those who look at themselves in a mirror; for they look at themselves and, on going away, immediately forget what they were like.” In Aranmula, on the banks of the Pampa, craftsmen still make the famous metal mirror, the Aranmula kannadi, polished for days until it shows a face without distortion. Nobody buys such a mirror only to forget their face. The word of God is a mirror like that: it shows us exactly who we are, both our sins and our dignity as children of God. The danger James sees is not that we refuse to look, but that we look, nod, and walk away. In this Easter season the Risen Lord has spoken to us Sunday after Sunday. The question is whether anything has changed between one Sunday and the next.

James then turns to the true test of religion. First the tongue: “If any think they are religious, and do not bridle their tongues but deceive their hearts, their religion is worthless.” A Christian who prays the long prayers of the Qurbana and then tears a neighbour to pieces on the way home has not yet understood what he prayed. Then the hands: “Religion that is pure and undefiled before God, the Father, is this: to care for orphans and widows in their distress, and to keep oneself unstained by the world.” Two things, held together: mercy towards the weakest, and purity of life. Kerala's Christians have a long history of the first. Religious sisters and brothers, priests and lay people have built homes for children without parents, for the abandoned elderly, for the sick whom no one wanted, and many of these houses are still kept going by the small gifts of ordinary parishes. James calls this not a hobby for the generous but the heart of religion. Every Easter Christian is invited to ask: whom do I visit? Which orphan, which widow, which lonely old man knows me by name?

In the Gospel Jesus walks into Jerusalem in the morning, hungry. He sees a fig tree by the road “and found nothing at all on it but leaves.” He says: “May no fruit ever come from you again!” and the tree withers. This is one of the few miracles of judgement in the Gospels, and it is a parable acted out. The prophets had used the fig tree as an image of God's people: “there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree” (Jeremiah 8:13). A tree covered in leaves promises fruit from far away; up close it has nothing to give. Leaves without fruit are what James calls hearing without doing. Jesus does not stop at the warning. When the disciples marvel, he turns the moment into a lesson on prayer: “Truly I tell you, if you have faith and do not doubt, not only will you do what has been done to the fig tree, but even if you say to this mountain, ‘Be lifted up and thrown into the sea,’ it will be done. Whatever you ask for in prayer with faith, you will receive.” The power that can wither a fruitless tree is the same power that can make a dry tree bear fruit. The Risen Lord does not want us to wither; he wants us to pray with faith and bear the fruit of the grace already given to us in Baptism and in the Holy Qurbana. In April and May many parishes run their vacation Bible classes, and children learn the words of Scripture by heart. The best lesson they can receive is to see their parents doing those words.

Full homily

Dear friends, St James gives us a picture: someone looks in a mirror, sees his face, walks away and forgets what he looks like (James 1:23–24). That, he says, is a person who hears the word and does not do it. The word of God is a mirror; it is given to change us.

Then James tells us what pure religion is: to bridle the tongue, to care for orphans and widows in their distress, and to keep ourselves unstained by the world (James 1:26–27). Not only long prayers, but a guarded tongue and open hands.

In the Gospel Jesus comes to a fig tree, hungry, and finds nothing on it but leaves. The tree withers. Leaves without fruit: that is what a life of hearing without doing looks like.

But Jesus does not stop at the warning. He tells his disciples: if you have faith and do not doubt, you can say to this mountain, be lifted up and thrown into the sea, and it will be done; whatever you ask for in prayer with faith, you will receive (Matthew 21:21–22). The Risen Lord gives us the grace to bear fruit, if we ask.

This week, let us do one word we have heard, and visit one person who is alone. Let the Lord find fruit on our branches. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus came to a tree looking for fruit, but it had only leaves. Jesus wants our lives to have fruit: kind words, helping hands, saying sorry. Today, can you do one kind thing at home without being asked? That is fruit for Jesus!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen and living, you came to the fig tree hungry and found only leaves. Do not find our lives full of words and empty of fruit. Make us doers of your word and not hearers only; bridle our tongues, open our hands to the orphan and the widow, and keep us unstained by the world. Give us faith that does not doubt, so that we may pray with boldness and bear the fruit of your grace. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today’s readings ask for fruit. James warns against hearing the word and walking away unchanged, like a man who forgets his own face. In Matthew, Jesus finds a fig tree with leaves and no fruit, and then teaches the disciples the power of prayer made in faith. Leaves without fruit and hearing without doing are the same illness; the cure is faith that acts and prayer that trusts.

James 1:22–27 · Doers of the word

Setting.

The Letter of James is a collection of practical teaching in the spirit of the wisdom books and of the Sermon on the Mount. It is addressed “to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion” (1:1), probably Jewish Christians living outside the land of Israel. Tradition links it to James, “the brother of the Lord”, leader of the Church in Jerusalem. Just before today’s reading James has said, “welcome with meekness the implanted word that has the power to save your souls” (1:21).

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: hearers who forget, like a man before a mirror.
  • v. 25: the one who looks into the perfect law of liberty and perseveres.
  • vv. 26–27: worthless religion and pure religion.

Key words.

  • poiētai logou, doers of the word: Not only listeners but people who carry the word out. In the Hebrew tradition, to hear (shema) already means to obey.
  • nomos teleios ho tēs eleutherias, the perfect law, the law of liberty: For James the law, read in the light of Christ, does not enslave; it gives true liberty. He will soon call it “the royal law”: “You shall love your neighbour as yourself” (2:8).
  • thrēskeia, religion: The outward practice of worship. James does not despise it; he says that true worship shows itself in the care of the weak and in purity of life.

Verse by verse.

“But be doers of the word, and not merely hearers who deceive themselves.” (1:22)

The danger is self-deception: to feel good about having heard a sermon and to change nothing.

“for they look at themselves and, on going away, immediately forget what they were like.” (1:24)

The word of God is a mirror that shows us our true face. A glance is not enough; we must look and stay.

“Religion that is pure and undefiled before God, the Father, is this: to care for orphans and widows in their distress, and to keep oneself unstained by the world.” (1:27)

Orphans and widows were the most defenceless in ancient society, and the Law of Moses names God as their protector (Deuteronomy 10:18). Charity and holiness belong together.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has never set faith against works. The good works of a Christian are themselves the fruit of God’s grace, which we receive in faith and which works through us; that is why they can be pleasing to God (CCC 2008, 2011). James and Paul agree: Paul speaks of “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6).

Matthew 21:18–22 · Leaves without fruit

Setting.

Jesus has entered Jerusalem and cleansed the Temple (21:12–17). The next morning, on the road back from Bethany, the fig tree incident follows. Mark tells the story in two parts around the cleansing of the Temple and adds that “it was not the season for figs” (Mark 11:13); Matthew tells it in one scene and says the tree withered at once.

Structure.

  • vv. 18–19: Jesus is hungry; a tree with leaves only; his word, and the tree withers.
  • v. 20: the disciples’ amazement.
  • vv. 21–22: the teaching on faith and prayer.

Key words.

  • sykē, fig tree: The prophets used the fig tree as an image of God’s people and of the fruit God seeks (Jeremiah 8:13; Micah 7:1). The action is a prophetic sign, like those of Jeremiah and Ezekiel.
  • mē diakrithēte, do not doubt: Literally “not be divided”, not torn between trust and distrust. James uses the same verb of the one who asks in prayer (James 1:6).
  • pistis, faith: Not confidence in our own power, but trust in God to whom nothing is impossible.

Verse by verse.

“In the morning, when he returned to the city, he was hungry.” (21:18)

A small, human detail. The Son of God knew hunger, as he knew tiredness and thirst.

“he went to it and found nothing at all on it but leaves.” (21:19)

Leaves promise fruit. The sign is a warning against any show of religion that bears no fruit: a warning to every disciple and every community, ours first.

“Whatever you ask for in prayer with faith, you will receive.” (21:22)

This promise does not make God a servant of our wishes. Prayer in faith seeks first what God wills, and receives more than it asked (CCC 2610).

How the Church reads it.

The Church does not read the withered fig tree as a sentence on the Jewish people, whom God has never rejected (Romans 11:1–2, 29; CCC 839). The Fathers saw in it a lesson for every soul and every Church: God looks for fruit, not only leaves. The second half of the reading moves from judgment to hope: the disciples who have faith are promised that their prayer will be heard.

Questions people ask

Why would Jesus curse a tree for having no fruit?

The action is not a burst of anger at a plant. It is a prophetic sign, as when the prophets broke a jar or wore a yoke to make God’s word visible. Jesus uses the tree to teach what God seeks in his people: fruit, not appearance. The sign is a warning given in love, while there is still time to bear fruit.

  • Where in my life are there leaves without fruit: signs of faith that are not yet matched by love?
