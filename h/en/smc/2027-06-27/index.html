Word for the day

“Strive to enter through the narrow door; for many, I tell you, will try to enter and will not be able.” Luke 13:24

Reflection

The Season of the Apostles ends today as it began, with a word heard from the fire. At Pentecost the Spirit came on the apostles in tongues of fire; in the first reading Moses reminds Israel of the day they stood at Horeb “while the mountain was blazing up to the very heavens, shrouded in dark clouds.” God had said to Moses, “Assemble the people for me, and I will let them hear my words, so that they may learn to fear me as long as they live on the earth, and may teach their children so.” Then the decisive line: “Then the Lord spoke to you out of the fire. You heard the sound of words but saw no form; there was only a voice.” Israel's God is not a statue that can be carried or bought; he is a living God who speaks and makes a covenant, written on two tablets of stone. Faith comes by hearing, and it lives by being taught to children. In Kerala this month the schools have reopened, and in our parishes the faith formation classes have begun again on Sunday mornings. Moses would call that holy work. The voice without form has now taken a face: “No one has ever seen God. It is God the only Son, who is close to the Father’s heart, who has made him known” (John 1:18). The apostles heard that voice, saw that face and handed both on to us.

Isaiah's reading follows the Song of the Vineyard, in which God looked for justice in his people and found bloodshed, looked for righteousness and heard a cry (Isaiah 5:7). Now come the woes. “Ah, you who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is room for no one but you, and you are left to live alone in the midst of the land!” Greed that swallows the land of the poor ends in loneliness: large and beautiful houses “without inhabitant”. Many Kerala villages know the sight of great locked houses whose owners live abroad and whose aged parents are alone. Then: “Ah, you who rise early in the morning in pursuit of strong drink, who linger in the evening to be inflamed by wine,” with music at every feast, “but who do not regard the deeds of the Lord.” Our State knows the cost of that woe in broken families and early deaths. And the deepest woe: “Ah, you who call evil good and good evil, who put darkness for light and light for darkness.” When a society renames sin as progress and calls goodness foolish, it has lost its way. Yet in the middle of the woes stands the Holy One: “the Lord of hosts is exalted by justice, and the Holy God shows himself holy by righteousness.” God is not defeated by our confusion; his holiness will shine in justice.

Paul's letter closes with practical matters, and they show us the apostolic Church at work. “Now concerning the collection for the saints”: the churches founded among the Gentiles were gathering money for the poor of the mother Church in Jerusalem. “On the first day of every week, each of you is to put aside and save whatever extra you earn.” Here, in one of the earliest Christian writings, the first day of the week, the day of the Resurrection, is already the day of giving, just as Acts tells us it was the day “when we met to break bread” (Acts 20:7). Paul then shares his plans: “I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost, for a wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.” An open door and many adversaries: that is the life of every apostle. He commends young Timothy, “let no one despise him”, and speaks warmly of Apollos. And he gives five commands that could sum up the whole Season of the Apostles: “Keep alert, stand firm in your faith, be courageous, be strong. Let all that you do be done in love.” Our Sunday offering, our help to the mission dioceses of North India and to the poor of our own parish, belongs to this same apostolic love.

In the Gospel Jesus is on the road to Jerusalem, and someone asks the question people still ask: “Lord, will only a few be saved?” Jesus does not answer with a number. He turns curiosity into a call: “Strive to enter through the narrow door.” The word for strive is the word for an athlete's struggle; salvation is a gift, but it is received by those who make the effort of conversion. Then a hard picture: the owner of the house has shut the door, and those outside knock: “Lord, open to us.” “We ate and drank with you, and you taught in our streets.” They had been near Jesus, had shared meals with him and heard him preach, yet he says, “I do not know where you come from.” Nearness without conversion is not enough. Baptism, a Christian name, a seat in church on feast days: these are graces, but they do not replace a life turned to God. And then the door opens wide: “Then people will come from east and west, from north and south, and will eat in the kingdom of God.” The apostles carried this invitation to every direction of the earth, and Mar Thoma brought it to our shores. “Indeed, some are last who will be first, and some are first who will be last.” Those who came late may be first at the table. As the Season of the Apostles closes, the Church asks us: have we entered through the narrow door ourselves, and are we holding it open for those still outside?

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the last Sunday of the Season of the Apostles. It began at Pentecost with tongues of fire, and the first reading takes us back to another fire, at Mount Horeb. There God spoke to his people: “You heard the sound of words but saw no form; there was only a voice” (Deuteronomy 4:12). Our God is not a statue; he is a God who speaks.

Moses says that the people must learn this word and teach it to their children. This month the schools have reopened and the faith formation classes have begun again. Parents, you are the first teachers of the faith. Ask your children what they have learned, and pray with them.

Isaiah warns against three woes: greed that joins field to field until no one else has room, strong drink from morning to night, and calling evil good and good evil (Isaiah 5:8, 11, 20). Our families know all three. Let us ask the Holy God to make us holy by righteousness.

Saint Paul asks the Corinthians to put something aside on the first day of every week for the poor in Jerusalem. Already then, Sunday was the day of the breaking of bread and the day of giving. And he gives us five words for the road: “Keep alert, stand firm in your faith, be courageous, be strong. Let all that you do be done in love” (1 Corinthians 16:13–14).

In the Gospel someone asks, “Lord, will only a few be saved?” Jesus does not give a number. He says: “Strive to enter through the narrow door” (Luke 13:24). It is not enough to have eaten and drunk near him; we must turn our lives to him.

But the door is open to all: people will come from east and west, from north and south, and sit at the table of the kingdom. The apostles carried this invitation to the whole world, and Mar Thoma brought it to us. As this season closes, let us enter the narrow door ourselves and hold it open for others. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: try hard to go in through the narrow door. To go through a narrow door you must leave your big bags outside! Leave outside lies, selfishness and bad words.

And Jesus says people will come from every side of the world to sit at God's table. God wants everyone in his house. This week, help a friend to come closer to Jesus!

Prayer

Lord God, you spoke to your people out of the fire at Horeb, and at Pentecost you sent the fire of your Spirit on the apostles. Through this season you have taught us the faith they handed on: keep it alive in our homes, and help us to teach it to our children. Deliver us from greed, from strong drink and from calling evil good; show yourself holy among us by righteousness. Make us generous on the first day of every week, alert, firm in faith, courageous and strong, and let all that we do be done in love. Lord Jesus, you are the narrow door: give us strength to enter, and gather people from east and west, north and south, to your table in the kingdom. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s four readings speak of hearing God and of a door. In Deuteronomy, Moses reminds Israel that at Horeb they heard the voice of God out of the fire, though they saw no form, and received the ten commandments to live by. In Isaiah, the prophet cries woe on those who heard the same law and ignored it: those who swallow up the land of the poor, who feast without regard for the Lord, who call evil good and good evil; yet the Holy God shows himself holy by righteousness. Paul, closing his first letter to Corinth, arranges a weekly collection for the poor of Jerusalem, speaks of a wide door opened for the Gospel, and sums up everything in one line: let all that you do be done in love. And in Luke, Jesus is asked whether only a few will be saved, and answers: strive to enter through the narrow door. The God who spoke at Horeb still speaks; to hear him is to enter by the narrow door of love and justice, and that door is open to people from east and west, north and south.

Deuteronomy 4:10–14 · You heard the sound of words but saw no form

Setting.

Deuteronomy presents itself as Moses’ last words to Israel in the plains of Moab, before they cross the Jordan. In chapter 4 he ends his first great speech with an urgent call to keep the law. He takes the people back in memory to Horeb, the name Deuteronomy gives to Sinai, so that a new generation may stand there in spirit, as if they themselves had been present.

These verses lead straight into the warning that follows: since you saw no form on the day the Lord spoke to you, do not make an idol in the shape of any creature (4:15–19). Israel meets God first as a voice that speaks and commands, not as an image to be looked at.

Structure.

  • v. 10: remember the day at Horeb: “Assemble the people for me,” so that they learn to fear me and teach their children.
  • v. 11: the mountain blazing to the heavens, wrapped in dark cloud.
  • v. 12: the Lord spoke out of the fire; you heard words but saw no form.
  • v. 13: he declared his covenant, the ten commandments, written on two tablets of stone.
  • v. 14: Moses is charged to teach the statutes for life in the land.

Key words.

  • qôl, voice, sound: The key word of the passage. God is known at Horeb by what he says. Faith begins in hearing: “Hear, O Israel” (6:4).
  • tĕmûnâ, form, likeness: Any visible shape. Israel saw none, so no image could stand for the Lord. This is the ground of the prohibition of idols in the next verses.
  • ‘ăśeret haddĕbārîm, the ten words: The Hebrew name for the ten commandments (also 10:4). They are words spoken by God to his people, the terms of a covenant of love, before they are rules.

Verse by verse.

“Assemble the people for me, and I will let them hear my words, so that they may learn to fear me as long as they live on the earth, and may teach their children so” (4:10)

Revelation is given to an assembly, and it is meant to be handed on in the family. Parents are the first teachers of the faith.

“Then the Lord spoke to you out of the fire. You heard the sound of words but saw no form; there was only a voice.” (4:12)

The fire shows God’s holiness; the voice shows his nearness. He does not remain silent behind the cloud.

“He declared to you his covenant, which he charged you to observe, that is, the ten commandments; and he wrote them on two stone tablets.” (4:13)

The covenant is not an idea but a way of life. God himself writes it, as later he will write his law on hearts (Jeremiah 31:33).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes Deuteronomy 4 when it explains the commandment against idols: God revealed himself at Horeb without any form, so that his people would not make a carved image of him (CCC 2129). It then explains why Christians may venerate images: by becoming man, the Son of God has introduced a new economy of images, and the honour paid to an image passes to the one it represents (CCC 2131–2132, following the Second Council of Nicaea). The Letter to the Hebrews contrasts the blazing mountain of Sinai with Mount Zion, where we come to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and warns: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking” (Hebrews 12:18–25).

Isaiah 5:8–20 · The Holy God shows himself holy by righteousness

Setting.

Isaiah preached in Jerusalem in the eighth century before Christ, a time of wealth for the powerful in Judah and of hardship for the poor. Just before this passage stands the Song of the Vineyard (5:1–7): God planted Israel like a choice vine and looked for justice, but found bloodshed; for righteousness, but heard a cry. The woes that follow show what that “bloodshed” and “cry” looked like in daily life.

Each woe begins with the Hebrew hôy, the cry of mourning used at a funeral (1 Kings 13:30). The prophet laments those who are already walking toward death. In the land of Israel each family was meant to keep its inheritance, because the land belonged to God (Leviticus 25:23); when the rich bought up field after field, the poor lost not only property but their place in the covenant people.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–10: first woe: on those who join house to house and field to field; the great houses will be empty, the fields barren.
  • vv. 11–12: second woe: on those who rise early for strong drink and feast with music, but do not regard the deeds of the Lord.
  • vv. 13–17: the consequence: exile for want of knowledge; Sheol opens its mouth; the proud are brought low, and the Lord is exalted by justice.
  • vv. 18–19: third woe: on those who drag sin like a cart and mock: let God hurry up and act.
  • v. 20: fourth woe: on those who call evil good and good evil.

Key words.

  • hôy, Ah, woe: A cry of grief over the dead. These woes are not curses spoken in anger, but the prophet’s mourning over people who are destroying themselves.
  • mišpāṭ and ṣĕdāqâ, justice and righteousness: The pair that runs through Isaiah: right dealing in the courts and faithful, upright relations in the community. Verse 16 says that God shows his holiness precisely in these.
  • qādôš, holy: Holiness in Isaiah is God’s utter otherness and majesty (6:3). But the Holy One is not distant from the poor; his holiness is seen when justice is done.

Verse by verse.

“Ah, you who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is room for no one but you” (5:8)

Greed ends in loneliness. Those who take everything are left “to live alone in the midst of the land.” The prophet Micah raises the same cry (Micah 2:2), and the story of Naboth’s vineyard shows it in action (1 Kings 21).

“For ten acres of vineyard shall yield but one bath, and a homer of seed shall yield a mere ephah.” (5:10)

A homer was ten ephahs (Ezekiel 45:11), so the harvest will be a tenth of the seed sown. The land taken by injustice will not feed its new owners.

“whose feasts consist of lyre and harp, tambourine and flute and wine, but who do not regard the deeds of the Lord” (5:12)

Isaiah does not condemn music or celebration, but feasting that forgets God and the poor.

“But the Lord of hosts is exalted by justice, and the Holy God shows himself holy by righteousness.” (5:16)

The centre of the passage. God’s holiness is not shown by pomp but by setting things right.

“Ah, you who call evil good and good evil, who put darkness for light and light for darkness” (5:20)

The last woe is the deepest: when a people changes the names of good and evil, conscience itself is darkened.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in Isaiah’s first woe her teaching on the universal destination of goods: the earth is entrusted to all humankind, and the right to private property does not abolish this original common destination (CCC 2402–2403). Pope St John Paul II quoted verse 20 in Evangelium Vitae (1995), warning that when conscience is darkened a society comes to call evil good and good evil, above all in matters of human life (in substance, Evangelium Vitae 24). St Basil the Great, preaching against greed, asked the rich man how he could call his own what he had received to share (in substance, Homily on “I will pull down my barns”).

1 Corinthians 16:1–14 · Let all that you do be done in love

Setting.

Paul wrote this letter from Ephesus, probably around AD 54–55. Now, at its close, he turns to practical matters: the collection for the poor Christians of Jerusalem, his travel plans, and the visits of Timothy and Apollos.

The collection was very dear to Paul. When Paul met the leaders of the Church in Jerusalem, they asked him to remember the poor (Galatians 2:10), and he gathered gifts from the Churches of Galatia, Macedonia and Achaia (2 Corinthians 8–9; Romans 15:25–27). For him it was more than relief: it was a sign that Gentile and Jewish Christians are one Body, and that the Gentiles, who had received spiritual blessings from Jerusalem, now share with Jerusalem in return.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: the collection for the saints: set aside something on the first day of every week; approved messengers will carry it to Jerusalem.
  • vv. 5–9: Paul’s plans: through Macedonia to Corinth; but first Ephesus until Pentecost, for a wide door has opened.
  • vv. 10–12: Timothy and Apollos: receive Timothy without fear; Apollos will come later.
  • vv. 13–14: closing charge: keep alert, stand firm, be courageous, be strong; let all be done in love.

Key words.

  • logeia, collection: A word used in everyday Greek for a gathering of money. Paul also calls it koinōnia, sharing, and charis, grace (2 Corinthians 8:4, 6). Giving is a share in God’s own gift.
  • kata mian sabbatou, on the first day of the week: Sunday, the day of the Lord’s resurrection. This is among the earliest witnesses that Christians already marked that day, as when they met to break bread at Troas (Acts 20:7).
  • thyra, door: A door for the Gospel, an opportunity opened by God (also 2 Corinthians 2:12; Colossians 4:3). Paul sees both the open door and the many adversaries.

Verse by verse.

“On the first day of every week, each of you is to put aside and save whatever extra you earn” (16:2)

Giving is regular, personal and in proportion to what one has. It is joined to the Lord’s Day.

“for a wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.” (16:9)

Opportunity and opposition often come together. Paul does not wait for an easy road before walking through the door God opens.

“Keep alert, stand firm in your faith, be courageous, be strong.” (16:13)

Four short commands, like a soldier’s orders. “Be courageous” translates a verb meaning “act like grown men”: a mature and steady faith.

“Let all that you do be done in love.” (16:14)

One line sums up the whole letter, with its hymn of love in chapter 13. Collections, plans and courage are worth nothing without love (13:3).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Christian assembly on Sunday goes back to the beginnings of the apostolic age (CCC 2178), and it links Sunday with works of mercy and care for the poor (CCC 2186). St Justin Martyr, writing in Rome around AD 150, describes the Sunday Eucharist and adds that those who are well off give what they wish, and the gift is kept by the one who presides to help orphans, widows, the sick and prisoners (in substance, First Apology 67). Paul’s collection lives on wherever the faithful give for the poor of another Church.

Luke 13:22–30 · Strive to enter through the narrow door

Setting.

Since 9:51 Jesus has been on his long journey to Jerusalem, where he will suffer. Luke reminds us of it here (v. 22). Along the way he teaches about the Kingdom, and just before this he has told the small parables of the mustard seed and the yeast (13:18–21): the Kingdom begins small and grows.

Someone now asks a question that people debated in that age: will only a few be saved? Jesus does not answer with a number. He turns the question from “how many?” to “what about you?”

Structure.

  • vv. 22–23: on the way to Jerusalem; the question: will only a few be saved?
  • v. 24: strive to enter through the narrow door; many will try and not be able.
  • vv. 25–27: the owner shuts the door; those outside knock: “We ate and drank with you”; “I do not know where you come from.”
  • vv. 28–29: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets in the Kingdom; people come from east and west, north and south.
  • v. 30: the last will be first, and the first last.

Key words.

  • agōnizesthe, strive: From the word for an athletic contest, the root of our word “agony”. Entering the Kingdom asks for effort and perseverance, not a casual wish.
  • thyra stenē, narrow door: Not a door that keeps people out, but one that is entered one by one, without the baggage of pride. Christ himself says, “I am the gate” (John 10:9).
  • oikodespotēs, owner of the house: The master of the house who gives the banquet. In the parables he stands for God, or for Christ, who will one day close the door.

Verse by verse.

“Someone asked him, “Lord, will only a few be saved?”” (13:23)

The question is about other people. Jesus will not satisfy curiosity; he speaks to the heart of the one asking.

“Strive to enter through the narrow door; for many, I tell you, will try to enter and will not be able.” (13:24)

There is still time now; the door is open. But it will not stay open forever. Today is the day to enter.

“We ate and drank with you, and you taught in our streets.” (13:26)

Being near Jesus outwardly is not enough. Knowing about him, even sharing his table, must become a life of doing what he says.

“Then people will come from east and west, from north and south, and will eat in the kingdom of God.” (13:29)

The banquet of the nations foretold by the prophets (Isaiah 25:6) will be full. The narrow door leads to a very wide table.

“Indeed, some are last who will be first, and some are first who will be last.” (13:30)

No one can presume on his place. Grace overturns our rankings.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes the Lord’s words on the narrow way when it teaches about the final destiny of the human person: they are a call to responsibility and conversion, for each of us must use our freedom with a view to our eternal destiny (CCC 1036). It teaches too that God predestines no one to go to hell, and that the Church prays that no one should be lost (CCC 1037, 1058). The Kingdom, first announced to the children of Israel, is meant to welcome people of all nations (CCC 543). The Second Vatican Council teaches that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ, but seek God sincerely and try to do his will known through conscience, may attain salvation (Lumen Gentium 16). The warning in verses 26–27 is addressed to everyone who is close to the Lord outwardly; the Church reads it first as a word to herself.

Questions people ask

Will only a few be saved?

Jesus does not give a number, and the Church does not either. The Church teaches that God desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), that Christ died for all, and that God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). At the same time, salvation is not automatic: we must freely accept God’s grace and persevere. Jesus’ answer is meant for each listener: do not count others; strive to enter yourself.

Since Israel saw no form at Horeb, are images in our churches wrong?

Deuteronomy forbids making an image to worship as God, because God is invisible. But in Christ the invisible God has become visible: “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (John 14:9). The Second Council of Nicaea (787) taught that Christians may honour images of Christ, Mary and the saints, because the honour passes to the person represented (CCC 2131–2132). We do not worship an image; we worship God alone.

Is Isaiah against owning houses and fields?

No. The law of Israel protected each family’s land as its inheritance, and the Church defends the right to private property (CCC 2403). Isaiah condemns greed that swallows up the land of others until the poor have no place. The Church teaches that property is held in stewardship, with care for the common good (CCC 2404).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Season of the Apostles our Church remembers the apostles’ mission to the nations, and we recall that Mar Thoma Shleeha opened a wide door for the Gospel in our land. Paul’s Sunday collection for the poor of another Church lives on in the offerings of Syro-Malabar parishes and eparchies for missions in India and abroad, and for the poor among us.

In the Holy Qurbana we too stand before the Lord who speaks: the readings are proclaimed, and we hear his voice, as Israel did at Horeb. Then we are fed at the table where people come from east and west. The narrow door is entered by a life of love; Paul’s words, “Let all that you do be done in love,” are a good rule to take home from the altar.

For study and teaching

  • At Horeb Israel heard God’s voice and was told to teach their children. How is God’s word handed on in my family?
  • Isaiah mourns over those who “call evil good and good evil”. Where do I see this in my own world, and where am I tempted to do it myself?
  • Paul asks for a gift set aside on the first day of every week. How do I join my Sunday with generosity to the poor?
  • Jesus answers the question “Will only a few be saved?” by saying “Strive to enter.” What would striving to enter the narrow door mean for me this week?
