Word for the day

“Moved with compassion, Jesus touched their eyes. Immediately they regained their sight and followed him.” Matthew 20:34

Reflection

Jesus is leaving Jericho, the last town before the climb to Jerusalem, where the Cross awaits him. A large crowd follows him, and by the roadside sit two blind men. They cannot see him, but they hear that he is passing by, and they cry out: “Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!” The crowd, perhaps embarrassed, perhaps impatient, tells them to be quiet. “But they shouted even more loudly, ‘Have mercy on us, Lord, Son of David!’” Their prayer is short, repeated, and stubborn. It is the prayer the Church has never stopped praying: in our Holy Qurbana and in the Prayer of the Hours we say again and again, Lord, have mercy; the Christian East has made of it the Jesus Prayer, repeated with every breath. There is always a crowd that tells us to be quiet: the voice of shame, of busyness, of people who think prayer is useless. The blind men teach us to cry louder. And notice what happens next: “Jesus stood still.” The One who has set his face towards Jerusalem stops for two beggars. No one is too small to halt the Lord on his way.

“What do you want me to do for you?” Jesus knows what they need, yet he asks. He wants them to say it, to name their deepest desire. “Lord, let our eyes be opened.” Matthew gives the reason for what follows in one phrase: “Moved with compassion, Jesus touched their eyes.” The word for compassion is the one Matthew uses when Jesus sees the crowds like sheep without a shepherd: a love that moves the depths of the heart. He does not heal from a distance; he touches. “Immediately they regained their sight and followed him.” They do not go home to enjoy their new sight; they follow him on the road that leads to Jerusalem, to the Cross and to the Resurrection. In this first week of the Season of the Apostles, the Gospel shows us what the apostles became: people whose eyes had been opened by Jesus and who followed him wherever he went. Isaiah had said that the Servant would come “to open the eyes that are blind” (Isaiah 42:7). Every Baptism, which the ancient Church called illumination, is such an opening of the eyes; every confession and every Qurbana can be another touch of the Lord's compassion.

Paul writes to the young Church of Thessalonica with a pastor's pride: “your faith is growing abundantly, and the love of every one of you for one another is increasing.” They are suffering persecution, and he does not hide it, but he sees their “steadfastness and faith during all your persecutions and the afflictions that you are enduring” as a sign that God is making them “worthy of the kingdom of God, for which you are also suffering.” The strong language about God's judgment that follows is not a call to revenge; it is the assurance that God sees, that injustice will not have the last word, and that the Lord Jesus will be revealed “to be glorified by his saints and to be marveled at on that day among all who have believed.” Christians in many places, and in our own country, have known such afflictions; the faithful of Kandhamal in Odisha in 2008 are still remembered. Paul's answer to suffering is prayer: “To this end we always pray for you, asking that our God will make you worthy of his call and will fulfill by his power every good resolve and work of faith, so that the name of our Lord Jesus may be glorified in you, and you in him”. Faith that grows, love that increases, steadfastness under trial, and a prayer for one another: this is the portrait of an apostolic Church.

Full homily

Dear friends, Jesus is leaving Jericho on his way to Jerusalem and the Cross. Two blind men by the road hear that he is passing and cry out: “Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!” (Matthew 20:30).

The crowd tells them to be quiet. But they shout even louder. There is always a crowd that tries to silence our prayer: shame, tiredness, people who say prayer is useless. Let us learn from the blind men to cry louder. Our liturgy repeats again and again, Lord, have mercy.

“Jesus stood still.” The Lord on his way to the Cross stops for two beggars. He asks, “What do you want me to do for you?” They say, “Lord, let our eyes be opened.” Moved with compassion, he touches their eyes, and they see, and they follow him (Matthew 20:32–34).

Paul praises the Thessalonians: their faith is growing and their love increasing, even under persecution. He prays that God will make them “worthy of his call” (2 Thessalonians 1:11). That is the portrait of the Church of the apostles: eyes opened, faith growing, love increasing, steadfast in trial.

In this Season of the Apostles let us cry to the Lord for mercy, tell him honestly what we need, and when he opens our eyes, follow him. Let us also pray for our brothers and sisters who suffer for their faith. Amen.

For children

Children, two blind men shouted to Jesus, “Lord, have mercy on us!” People said, “Be quiet!” but they shouted louder. Jesus stopped, touched their eyes, and they could see! Jesus always stops when we call him.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of David, you stood still on the road for two blind beggars and opened their eyes with the touch of your compassion. Have mercy on us. Open the eyes of our hearts to see you, and let us follow you on the road you walk. Make our faith grow and our love for one another increase; keep steadfast all who suffer for your name, and make us worthy of your call, that your name may be glorified in us. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about faith that holds on. Paul thanks God for the Thessalonians, whose faith and love keep growing in the middle of persecution, and lifts their eyes to the day when the Lord Jesus will be revealed and glorified in his saints. In Matthew, two blind men by the road out of Jericho cry out to the Son of David, and the louder the crowd tells them to be quiet, the louder they cry. Jesus stops, has compassion, opens their eyes, and they follow him. In the Season of the Apostles the Church learns from both: to persevere, and to keep asking for sight.

2 Thessalonians 1:3–12 · Worthy of the kingdom

Setting.

Paul founded the church at Thessalonica in Macedonia on his second journey, and had to leave in haste because of opposition (Acts 17:1–10). The young community continued to suffer. Many scholars think the Second Letter to the Thessalonians was written in Paul’s name by a disciple, because its style and its teaching on the end differ from the First Letter; others hold that Paul wrote it soon after 1 Thessalonians. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture in either case.

The language of this passage is that of Jewish apocalyptic writing: angels, fire, the day of the Lord. Its purpose is not to frighten the faithful but to comfort them: their suffering is seen by God, and justice will be done.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–4: thanksgiving for growing faith and love, and pride in their steadfastness.
  • vv. 5–10: God’s righteous judgment: relief for the afflicted, and the revealing of the Lord Jesus.
  • vv. 11–12: Paul’s prayer that God make them worthy and that Jesus be glorified in them.

Key words.

  • hypomonē, steadfastness: Endurance under a load; the strength to remain. It is the virtue of those who do not give way under trial.
  • apokalypsis, revealing: Literally an unveiling. The Lord Jesus is already reigning; on the last day what is hidden will be made plain to all.
  • axioō, make worthy: Not that we earn the kingdom by ourselves, but that God by his grace makes us fit for it. Paul prays for this; it is God’s work in us (v. 11).

Verse by verse.

“because your faith is growing abundantly, and the love of every one of you for one another is increasing.” (1:3)

Persecution has not shrunk this church; it has deepened it. Faith and love are not fixed amounts but living things that grow.

“These will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, separated from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might” (1:9)

The heart of the punishment is separation from the Lord. Judgment belongs to God alone; the persecuted are never told to take revenge (compare Romans 12:19).

“so that the name of our Lord Jesus may be glorified in you, and you in him, according to the grace of our God and the Lord Jesus Christ.” (1:12)

An exchange of glory: Christ glorified in his people, his people glorified in him. All of it is “according to the grace” of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the chief punishment of hell is eternal separation from God, in whom alone we can have the life and happiness for which we were created, and that this state is a definitive self-exclusion from communion with God (CCC 1033, 1035). It also teaches that God predestines no one to go to hell (CCC 1037). The Church prays, as Paul does here, that all may be made worthy of their call. Today, in many lands, Christians still suffer for their faith; the Church prays for them and for those who wrong them.

Matthew 20:29–34 · Lord, let our eyes be opened

Setting.

Jesus is on his last journey to Jerusalem. He has just foretold his passion for the third time (20:17–19), and the mother of James and John has asked for places of honour for her sons (20:20–28). Now, leaving Jericho, about fifteen miles from Jerusalem, he meets two blind men. The next scene is his entry into the city as the Son of David (21:1–11).

Mark tells of one blind beggar, Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46–52), and Luke of one blind man as Jesus approached Jericho (Luke 18:35–43). Matthew several times tells of two where others tell of one (as with the demoniacs in 8:28); perhaps two witnesses make the testimony sure.

Structure.

  • v. 29: leaving Jericho, a large crowd following.
  • vv. 30–31: two blind men cry out; the crowd rebukes them; they cry louder.
  • vv. 32–33: Jesus stops: “What do you want me to do for you?”
  • v. 34: compassion, touch, sight, following.

Key words.

  • eleēson, have mercy: The cry of the blind men, Kyrie eleēson, “Lord, have mercy”, became one of the oldest prayers of the Church’s liturgy.
  • huios Dauid, Son of David: A title for the Messiah, the promised king from David’s line. The blind men see who Jesus is before their eyes are opened.
  • splanchnistheis, moved with compassion: From splanchna, the inner organs: a compassion felt in the depths of one’s being. Matthew uses it of Jesus before he heals and feeds the crowds (9:36; 14:14; 15:32).

Verse by verse.

“The crowd sternly ordered them to be quiet; but they shouted even more loudly, “Have mercy on us, Lord, Son of David!”” (20:31)

The crowd following Jesus becomes an obstacle to those who want to reach him. Their persistence is a lesson in prayer.

“Jesus stood still and called them, saying, “What do you want me to do for you?”” (20:32)

A few verses earlier he asked the mother of James and John, “What do you want?” (20:21). She asked for places of glory for her sons; the blind men ask for sight.

“Moved with compassion, Jesus touched their eyes. Immediately they regained their sight and followed him.” (20:34)

They follow him on the road to Jerusalem, to the Cross. Sight is given so that we may follow.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus heard the prayer of faith during his ministry, and that the urgent cry of the blind men, “Have mercy on us, Son of David”, has been renewed in the traditional prayer to Jesus known as the Jesus Prayer (CCC 2616). St Augustine, preaching on this Gospel, says that the crowd which tried to silence the blind men stands for the noise of worldly habits, even among Christians, that hinders us from crying to Christ; we must cry the louder until he stops for us (in substance, Sermon 88).

Questions people ask

Does 2 Thessalonians teach that God takes revenge?

The passage speaks of God’s justice, in the strong language of the prophets. Its point is that suffering for the kingdom is not forgotten and that evil will not have the last word. Vengeance belongs to God, not to us (Romans 12:19), and the Christian answers persecution with prayer, even for persecutors (Matthew 5:44). The Church teaches that hell is a state of definitive self-exclusion from God, and that God wills all to be saved (CCC 1033, 1037).

For study and teaching

  • Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians’ faith and love are growing. Where do I see faith and love growing in my family or parish, and do I thank God for it?
  • If Jesus asked me today, “What do you want me to do for you?”, what would I ask for? Is it glory, or sight?
