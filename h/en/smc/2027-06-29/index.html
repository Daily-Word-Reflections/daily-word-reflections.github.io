Word for the day

“He said to him the third time, “Simon son of John, do you love me?” Peter felt hurt because he said to him the third time, “Do you love me?” And he said to him, “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” Jesus said to him, “Feed my sheep.” John 21:17

Reflection

Today the whole Church, East and West, keeps one feast for two apostles. Peter, the fisherman of Galilee, and Paul, the scholar of Tarsus who had persecuted the Church, were very different men. They once disagreed openly at Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14). Yet they preached one Gospel, served one Lord, and gave their lives for him in the same city. According to the ancient tradition of the Church, Peter was crucified in Rome during the persecution under Nero, and was buried on the Vatican hill, where the Basilica of St Peter stands over his tomb; tradition adds that he asked to be crucified head downward, judging himself unworthy to die as his Master had died. Paul, a Roman citizen, was beheaded on the road to Ostia and buried where the Basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls now stands. St Augustine said that the two apostles have one feast because they were one: though they suffered on different days, they were as one. For us in India this feast has a family warmth. Peter and Paul were the brothers in mission of Mar Thoma, our Father in faith, whose Dukhrana we will keep on Saturday. And our Syro-Malabar Church, with its own Major Archbishop and Synod, lives in full communion with the Bishop of Rome, the successor of Peter. Today we pray especially for Pope Leo XIV. It is a day of obligation: the Church gathers around the altar with the apostles.

The first two readings are a pair, like the two apostles. In the Second Book of Kings a wealthy woman of Shunem has welcomed the prophet Elisha, building a small room on her roof for him. He promises her a son: “At this season, in due time, you shall embrace a son.” The boy is born, grows, goes out to his father among the reapers, cries “Oh, my head, my head!”, and dies on his mother's lap at noon. She lays him on the prophet's bed, rides to Mount Carmel and will not let Elisha go. “So he went in and closed the door on the two of them, and prayed to the Lord.” He stretches himself on the child, mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands, the child grows warm, sneezes seven times and opens his eyes. “Take your son.” In the Acts of the Apostles the same story happens again through Peter. At Joppa a disciple named Tabitha, in Greek Dorcas, “devoted to good works and acts of charity,” has died. The widows stand weeping, showing the tunics and clothing she had made for them. Peter, like Elisha, puts everyone outside; he kneels and prays; then he says, “Tabitha, get up.” She opens her eyes and sits up. “He gave her his hand and helped her up.” The apostle does what the prophet did, and more: he does what Jesus did, for in the house of Jairus Jesus had taken a girl by the hand and said, “Talitha cum,” which means, “Little girl, get up!” (Mark 5:41). And this morning's weekday Gospel shows where Peter first saw that gesture: in his own house at Capernaum, when Jesus came to his mother-in-law, sick with fever, “and took her by the hand and lifted her up.” The authority of Peter is the power of the risen Lord, given for the poor, the widows and the dead.

Paul's reading is a page from his own life, written to Corinthians who were being impressed by boastful preachers. Paul says he will speak “as a fool”, and boast as the others do: “Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they descendants of Abraham? So am I.” Then he lists, not his successes, but his sufferings: labours and imprisonments, floggings, near death many times; beaten with rods three times, stoned once, shipwrecked three times, “for a night and a day I was adrift at sea”; dangers from rivers, bandits, Gentiles and his own people, in city and wilderness and at sea; hunger, thirst, cold and sleeplessness. He even mentions the synagogue punishment of the forty lashes minus one, which he, a Jew among Jews, accepted five times rather than abandon his people or his Lord. And above all this: “I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches. Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to stumble, and I am not indignant?” Then the surprising conclusion: “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” The great apostle remembers that his ministry began with a humiliating escape, let down in a basket through a window in the wall of Damascus. Paul's glory is not his eloquence but his wounds, carried for Christ and for the Churches. At the end of his life he could write, “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith” (2 Timothy 4:7).

The Gospel takes us to the shore of the Sea of Tiberias after the Resurrection. Jesus has prepared a charcoal fire with fish and bread for the disciples. The last time Peter stood by a charcoal fire was in the courtyard of the high priest, where he denied his Master three times (John 18:18). Now, by another charcoal fire, Jesus asks him three times: “Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?” “Yes, Lord; you know that I love you.” “Feed my lambs.” The second time: “Tend my sheep.” The third time Peter is hurt, and he answers with all his heart: “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” “Feed my sheep.” Three confessions of love heal three denials, and each confession is answered by a commission. Jesus does not give Peter a lesser task because he fell; he gives him his whole flock, the lambs and the sheep, the little ones and the great. The calendar joins to this Gospel the scene at Caesarea Philippi: “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 16:18–19), and Luke adds the Lord's prayer for him: “I have prayed for you that your own faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). Here is the Petrine ministry that the Catholic Church believes continues in the Bishop of Rome: a primacy of faith and love, of feeding and of strengthening, a ministry of unity for the whole Church. Pope St Gregory the Great called himself the servant of the servants of God, and every Pope since has carried that title. Then Jesus tells Peter how he will die: “when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will fasten a belt around you and take you where you do not wish to go.” And the last word: “Follow me.” The shepherd who feeds the sheep will one day lay down his life for them, like the Good Shepherd himself.

The weekday readings, read on the Seventh Tuesday of the Apostles, complete the picture. Paul tells the Romans: “Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law.” Love is the only debt we can never finish paying, and it is the whole law. And Mark shows us Peter's house at Capernaum: Jesus heals his mother-in-law, “and she began to serve them,” and that evening “the whole city was gathered around the door,” bringing the sick and the possessed. Pilgrims to Capernaum today are shown, under a modern church, the remains of a house that early Christians honoured and that many archaeologists identify with Peter's house. The Church built on Peter is meant to be such a house: a home where Jesus heals, where those who are healed serve, and at whose door the whole city can gather. On this feast let us thank God for the two pillars of the Church, pray for the Pope, our Major Archbishop and all our bishops, and renew our own answer to the question that Jesus asks every disciple: do you love me?

Full homily

Dear friends, today the whole Church celebrates Saint Peter and Saint Paul, the two great apostles. They were very different: a fisherman and a scholar, one who denied Jesus and one who persecuted his Church. Both were forgiven, both preached Christ, both died for him in Rome. Today is a day of obligation, and we gather with joy.

In the first reading the prophet Elisha raises a dead boy to life. In the second, Peter kneels, prays and says, “Tabitha, get up” (Acts 9:40), and gives her his hand. Peter learned that gesture from Jesus, who took his mother-in-law by the hand in his own house and lifted her up (Mark 1:31). The power of the apostles is the power of the risen Lord, given to lift up the poor and the fallen.

Paul does not boast of his success. He lists his sufferings: prisons, beatings, shipwrecks, hunger, cold, and his daily anxiety for all the Churches. “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness” (2 Corinthians 11:30). His glory is his wounds for Christ.

In the Gospel, by a charcoal fire, Jesus asks Peter three times, “Do you love me?” Three times Peter had denied him by another fire. Now three times he says, “Lord, you know that I love you.” And Jesus gives him the whole flock: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17).

This is the ministry that continues in the Pope, the successor of Peter: to feed, to strengthen the brethren, to keep the whole Church one. Our Syro-Malabar Church lives in full communion with him. Today let us pray for Pope Leo XIV, for our Major Archbishop and for all our bishops.

And let us hear the question that Jesus asks each of us: do you love me? Let us answer with Peter, “Lord, you know that I love you,” and let us show it by feeding his sheep and lifting up those who have fallen. Saint Peter and Saint Paul, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, today we celebrate Saint Peter and Saint Paul. Peter once said three times that he did not know Jesus. But Jesus forgave him and asked him three times, “Do you love me?”

Peter said, “Yes, Lord, you know that I love you!” Tonight, tell Jesus the same: “Jesus, I love you!” And pray for the Pope, who takes care of the whole Church.

Prayer

Lord God, you built your Church on the faith of the apostles and gave us today the joy of honouring Saint Peter and Saint Paul, who gave their lives for Christ in Rome. You made Peter, who had denied his Master, the shepherd of your flock, and Paul, who had persecuted your Church, its tireless apostle. Hear the prayer of your Church gathered on this feast: keep us one in faith and love around the successor of Peter. Strengthen our Pope Leo, our Major Archbishop, our bishops and priests, that they may feed your sheep with love. Give us Peter's love and Paul's zeal, the hand that lifts up the fallen and the heart that carries all the Churches. When our own hour comes, may we too have fought the good fight, finished the race and kept the faith. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of the two great apostles the readings show the risen life of Christ working through weak human servants. In 2 Kings, the prophet Elisha prays over the dead son of the Shunammite woman, and the child opens his eyes. In Acts, Peter kneels and prays beside the body of Tabitha at Joppa and says, “Tabitha, get up,” and she rises: the power that worked through the prophets and through Jesus now works through his apostle. Paul, defending his ministry, lists his labours, prisons, beatings and shipwrecks, and boasts only of his weakness. And in John, the risen Lord asks Peter three times, “Do you love me?” and entrusts him with his flock, foretelling the death by which Peter would glorify God. Peter the shepherd and Paul the missionary both gave their lives in Rome. The Church also gives the readings of the Seventh Tuesday of the Apostles, which fit the feast well: love as the fulfilling of the law (Romans 13:7–10), and Jesus healing in Peter’s own house (Mark 1:29–34).

2 Kings 4:16–21, 32–37 · Take your son

Setting.

Elisha succeeded Elijah as prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel in the ninth century before Christ. In Shunem, a village in the Jezreel valley, a wealthy woman had shown him hospitality and built a small upper room for him to stay in (4:8–10). She had no son, and her husband was old. Elisha promised her a son within the year.

The reading tells of the gift, the loss and the restoring of that child. The verses left out (22–31) tell how the mother rode to Elisha at Mount Carmel and would not leave him, and how Elisha’s servant Gehazi laid the prophet’s staff on the child without result. Elijah had raised a widow’s son in a similar way (1 Kings 17:17–24).

Structure.

  • vv. 16–17: the promise of a son, and his birth “at that season”.
  • vv. 18–21: the boy falls ill in the harvest field and dies on his mother’s lap; she lays him on the prophet’s bed.
  • vv. 32–35: Elisha shuts the door, prays, and stretches himself on the child; the child sneezes seven times and opens his eyes.
  • vv. 36–37: “Take your son.” The mother bows and takes him.

Key words.

  • ’îš hā’ĕlōhîm, man of God: The usual title of Elisha in these stories. It means one who belongs to God and speaks and acts with his power.
  • yitpallēl, he prayed: Elisha does not raise the child by his own power. He first shuts the door and prays to the Lord. Every miracle in the Bible is God’s work.
  • šeba‘ pĕ‘āmîm, seven times: Seven is the number of fullness. The child’s sneezing seven times is a sign of breath fully returned: life is the gift of God’s breath (Genesis 2:7).

Verse by verse.

“She went up and laid him on the bed of the man of God, closed the door on him, and left.” (4:21)

The mother says nothing to her husband of the death. She lays the child on the prophet’s bed, as if handing him back to the one through whom God gave him.

“So he went in and closed the door on the two of them, and prayed to the Lord.” (4:33)

Prayer comes first. Jesus too put the mourners outside before raising Jairus’ daughter (Mark 5:40), and Peter will do the same at Joppa.

“putting his mouth upon his mouth, his eyes upon his eyes, and his hands upon his hands” (4:34)

The prophet gives himself wholly, body to body, to the dead child. Some Fathers saw here a picture of the Son of God who came down to our measure to give us life.

“She came and fell at his feet, bowing to the ground; then she took her son and left.” (4:37)

Her faith is rewarded. The story ends in silent thanksgiving.

How the Church reads it.

Several Fathers read Elisha’s action as a figure of the Incarnation: the Word made himself small, stretching himself, as it were, over dead humanity to give it life. The Church sees in the prophets’ miracles a preparation for Christ, who raised the dead by his own word, and for the apostles, who would act in his name. The Catechism distinguishes such raisings, which brought people back to ordinary earthly life, from Christ’s own Resurrection to a glorious life that can no longer die (CCC 646). Elisha’s miracle points ahead; Christ fulfils it.

Acts 9:36–42 · Tabitha, get up

Setting.

After Saul’s conversion, the Church in Judea, Galilee and Samaria has peace (9:31). Peter is travelling among the communities, and at Lydda he has healed a paralysed man named Aeneas (9:32–35). Joppa, the port on the coast, the modern Jaffa beside Tel Aviv, was not far away. There a disciple named Tabitha has died.

Joppa is the town from which Jonah once fled from God’s call to the Gentiles (Jonah 1:3). In the next chapter, Peter, staying at Joppa, will receive the vision that sends him to the Gentile Cornelius (10:5–23).

Structure.

  • vv. 36–37: Tabitha, a disciple rich in good works, dies; her body is laid in the upper room.
  • vv. 38–39: two men bring Peter; the widows weep and show the clothes she made.
  • v. 40: Peter sends all out, kneels, prays, and says, “Tabitha, get up”; she opens her eyes and sits up.
  • vv. 41–42: he presents her alive; many believe in the Lord.

Key words.

  • mathētria, disciple: The feminine form of “disciple”, used only here in the New Testament. Tabitha is honoured as a true follower of Christ.
  • Tabitha, Dorkas, gazelle: Tabitha is Aramaic, Dorcas Greek; both mean gazelle. Luke gives both names, as Joppa was a city of two languages.
  • anastēthi, get up: The verb of resurrection. Peter’s words, “Tabitha, get up”, echo Jesus’ words to Jairus’ daughter, “Talitha cum” (Mark 5:41), which differ by one letter in Aramaic.

Verse by verse.

“She was devoted to good works and acts of charity.” (9:36)

Literally “full of good works and almsgiving”. Her holiness was in her needle and her generosity.

“All the widows stood beside him, weeping and showing tunics and other clothing that Dorcas had made while she was with them.” (9:39)

The poor widows are her best witnesses. Each garment tells of her love. Charity is remembered long after we are gone.

“Peter put all of them outside, and then he knelt down and prayed. He turned to the body and said, “Tabitha, get up.”” (9:40)

Peter does exactly what he had seen Jesus do in Jairus’ house (Mark 5:40–41). But he first kneels and prays: the power is not his own, but Christ’s.

“This became known throughout Joppa, and many believed in the Lord.” (9:42)

The miracle is not an end in itself. It leads people to faith in the Lord.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Peter’s ministry the continuation of Christ’s own: Jesus gave the Twelve a share in his power to heal and to raise (Matthew 10:8), and in Acts they act in his name, never in their own (Acts 3:12, 16). The Catechism teaches that the apostles were sent to continue Christ’s own mission, acting in his name and by his power (CCC 858–859). Tabitha, whose good works speak for her, is honoured in East and West as a model of practical charity, and many parish women’s groups in various lands have been named “Dorcas societies” after her.

2 Corinthians 11:21b–33 · I will boast of my weakness

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 Paul answers rival preachers at Corinth who boasted of their Jewish descent, their eloquence and their visions. Paul hates boasting, but to protect the Corinthians he plays the “fool” and boasts for a while (11:1, 16–21). Yet he turns the boasting upside down: instead of successes, he lists sufferings.

His list matches what Acts tells: stoning at Lystra (Acts 14:19), beating with rods at Philippi (Acts 16:22–23), the escape from Damascus (Acts 9:23–25). It also tells of much that Acts does not record, such as three shipwrecks, all before the famous shipwreck at Malta, which came later (Acts 27). The forty lashes minus one was a synagogue discipline (Deuteronomy 25:3 allows forty; one was held back so as not to exceed the limit). That Paul accepted it five times shows he still counted himself a member of the synagogue and kept returning to his own people.

Structure.

  • vv. 21b–23a: Hebrews, Israelites, descendants of Abraham, ministers of Christ: so am I, and more.
  • vv. 23b–27: the catalogue of labours, imprisonments, beatings, dangers and hardships.
  • vv. 28–29: daily anxiety for all the Churches; weak with the weak.
  • vv. 30–33: I will boast of my weakness; God knows I do not lie; let down in a basket at Damascus.

Key words.

  • kauchaomai, to boast: A word Paul uses more than anyone else in the New Testament. For him the only true boast is in the Lord (10:17) and in the cross (Galatians 6:14).
  • merimna, anxiety, care: His heaviest burden is not the beatings but the daily care for all the Churches. A shepherd’s heart suffers with every member.
  • astheneia, weakness: The key word of these chapters. Paul will hear the Lord say, “power is made perfect in weakness” (12:9).

Verse by verse.

“Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they descendants of Abraham? So am I.” (11:22)

Paul never denies his Jewish identity. He remains proud to belong to the people of the covenant (Romans 9:3–5; 11:1).

“Five times I have received from the Jews the forty lashes minus one.” (11:24)

These punishments were given by synagogue authorities in particular places, not by a whole people. Paul himself never blames his people as a whole; he prays for them with “great sorrow and unceasing anguish” (Romans 9:2).

“And, besides other things, I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches.” (11:28)

Here Paul speaks as a pastor. The care of souls is a heavier cross than any rod.

“If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” (11:30)

The world boasts of strength; the apostle boasts of weakness, because there Christ’s power is seen.

“but I was let down in a basket through a window in the wall, and escaped from his hands.” (11:33)

An ironic ending. Roman soldiers were honoured for being first up an enemy wall; Paul’s “glory” is to have gone down a wall in a basket.

How the Church reads it.

St John Chrysostom loved Paul above all the saints and preached seven homilies in his praise; he marvels that this one man, weak in body, carried the care of the whole world in his heart (in substance, Homilies in Praise of St Paul). Pope Benedict XVI, in the Pauline Year 2008–2009, taught that Paul’s sufferings show that the apostle is united with Christ crucified, and that his strength came from Christ alone (in substance, General Audiences on St Paul). The Church honours Peter and Paul together because both gave their lives for Christ in Rome; St Clement of Rome, writing within a generation, already holds them up as examples of endurance (in substance, 1 Clement 5).

John 21:15–19 · Feed my sheep

Setting.

The risen Jesus has appeared to seven disciples by the Sea of Tiberias. After a night of catching nothing, they have hauled in a great catch at his word, and he has given them breakfast of bread and fish on a charcoal fire (21:1–14). The only other charcoal fire in the Gospels is the one in the high priest’s courtyard where Peter denied him three times (18:18). Now, beside a charcoal fire again, Jesus heals Peter’s threefold denial with a threefold question of love.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–17: three times: “Simon son of John, do you love me?” Three times Peter answers, and three times Jesus entrusts his flock to him.
  • v. 18: the prophecy: when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will fasten a belt around you.
  • v. 19: the meaning: the death by which Peter would glorify God; then, “Follow me.”

Key words.

  • agapaō and phileō, to love: Jesus twice asks with agapaō; Peter answers with phileō, and the third time Jesus also uses phileō. Some Fathers and preachers have found a lesson here, but many scholars think John varies the words for style, since he uses both of the Father’s love for the Son (3:35; 5:20). Either way, the heart of the scene is the threefold question.
  • boske, poimaine, feed, tend: Two verbs: to feed and to shepherd, to guide and protect. Peter receives the whole care of the flock, lambs and sheep alike.
  • ta probata mou, my sheep: The flock remains Christ’s. Peter is a shepherd under the Good Shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep (10:11).

Verse by verse.

“Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?” (21:15)

Jesus calls him by his old name, as at the first call (1:42). Peter had once boasted that he would never fall away (Mark 14:29). Now he does not compare himself with the others.

“Peter felt hurt because he said to him the third time, “Do you love me?” And he said to him, “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.”” (21:17)

Peter no longer trusts his own word. He appeals to the Lord’s knowledge of his heart. Humility has replaced boasting.

“But when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will fasten a belt around you and take you where you do not wish to go.” (21:18)

Early Christian writers understood this of crucifixion. Tradition holds that Peter was crucified in Rome under Nero, and Origen, as reported by Eusebius, says he asked to be crucified head downward.

“After this he said to him, “Follow me.”” (21:19)

The last words of Jesus to Peter in John are the first words of every vocation. To shepherd is to follow, all the way to the Cross.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus entrusted a specific authority to Peter, and that after his Resurrection he confirmed it: “Feed my sheep” (CCC 553). The First Vatican Council and the Second Vatican Council cite this passage in teaching that the Lord made Peter the shepherd of the whole flock, and that this ministry continues in his successors, the bishops of Rome (Lumen Gentium 18, 22; CCC 881–882). St Augustine notes that the threefold confession answers the threefold denial, so that the tongue might show as much love as it had shown fear (in substance, Tractates on John 123). He adds that Peter is told to feed Christ’s sheep, not his own.

Romans 13:7–10 · Love is the fulfilling of the law

Setting.

These verses follow Paul’s teaching on the civil authorities. After saying “Pay to all what is due them,” he turns from debts that can be paid to the one debt that is never paid off: love.

Structure.

  • v. 7: pay all their due: taxes, revenue, respect, honour.
  • v. 8: owe no one anything except love; the one who loves has fulfilled the law.
  • vv. 9–10: the commandments are summed up in “Love your neighbor as yourself”; love is the fulfilling of the law.

Key words.

  • opheilō, to owe: Paul plays on the word: settle every debt, but keep this one always open. We can never say we have loved enough.
  • plērōma, fulfilling: Love does not cancel the commandments; it fills them up and brings them to their goal.

Verse by verse.

“Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law.” (13:8)

A debt of love grows the more it is paid.

“Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore, love is the fulfilling of the law.” (13:10)

Each commandment Paul lists protects the neighbour. Love does all that the commandments ask, and more.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses when it shows how Jesus and the apostle sum up the Decalogue in the twofold commandment of love (CCC 2055), and it teaches that charity is the soul of all the virtues (CCC 1827). On the feast of the two apostles, this is fitting: both Peter and Paul learned that love is the whole of the law, and both gave their lives for love of Christ.

Mark 1:29–34 · In Peter’s house

Setting.

This is the first day of Jesus’ ministry in Mark, a sabbath at Capernaum. He has taught in the synagogue and freed a man from an unclean spirit (1:21–28). Now he goes straight to the house of Simon and Andrew. Pilgrims from the fourth century onward venerated a house in Capernaum as Peter’s, and the remains of an early house church, later covered by an octagonal church, can be seen there today.

Structure.

  • vv. 29–31: in Simon’s house Jesus raises up his mother-in-law, and she serves them.
  • vv. 32–34: at sunset the whole town brings the sick; he heals many and silences the demons.

Key words.

  • ēgeiren, lifted up: The verb Mark will use for the Resurrection (16:6). Jesus’ touch already brings the life of Easter.
  • diēkonei, she began to serve: From the word diakonia. The one who is healed becomes a servant, like the Son of Man who came to serve (10:45).

Verse by verse.

“Now Simon’s mother-in-law was in bed with a fever, and they told him about her at once.” (1:30)

Peter was married. The first healing in his house is for his family. The disciples bring a need to Jesus at once: this is the first form of intercession.

“He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up. Then the fever left her, and she began to serve them.” (1:31)

Three gestures: he comes, he takes her hand, he lifts her up. Healing leads to service.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a sign that God has visited his people, and that he wants to heal the whole person, body and soul (CCC 1503). St Jerome, preaching on this passage, prays that Jesus may come to our house too, take our hand and lift us up from the fever of our sins, so that we may rise and serve (in substance, Homilies on Mark).

Questions people ask

Why are Peter and Paul honoured on the same day?

Both were martyred in Rome under the emperor Nero, in the mid-60s, according to ancient tradition: Peter crucified, probably near the Vatican hill, and Paul beheaded on the road to Ostia. The Church in Rome has kept their common feast on 29 June at least since the fourth century. Together they stand for the two sides of the apostolic mission: Peter the shepherd and rock of unity, Paul the apostle to the nations. Very different men, sometimes in sharp disagreement (Galatians 2:11–14), they were one in faith and in love for Christ.

Did Peter really have authority over the other apostles?

The Gospels show Peter named first in every list of the Twelve (Matthew 10:2), given the name “Rock” (Matthew 16:18), asked to strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:32), and entrusted with Christ’s flock (John 21:15–17). In Acts he leads the first community (Acts 1:15; 2:14; 15:7). The Church teaches that this authority comes from Christ, and that it is a service of unity and love, continued in the bishop of Rome (CCC 881–882).

Why did Jesus ask Peter three times?

St Augustine and many others saw the threefold question as healing Peter’s threefold denial beside another charcoal fire (John 18:18). Each question gives Peter a chance to say with his lips the love that fear had silenced. And each answer is followed by a mission. Christ does not only forgive; he trusts again.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the East Syriac tradition, Peter and Paul are honoured within the Season of the Apostles, the season after Pentecost that celebrates the Church sent to the nations. The Syro-Malabar Church, which traces its faith to Mar Thoma Shleeha, rejoices in the communion of all the apostles: the faith Thomas brought to India is the same faith Peter and Paul sealed with their blood in Rome. Our Church lives in full communion with the successor of Peter, the bishop of Rome, and prays for him in the Holy Qurbana.

The reading from Acts is close to every parish: Tabitha’s needle and the widows’ tears speak of the many quiet works of charity in our homes and parishes. Like Peter, we kneel and pray first, then reach out a hand to lift others up.

For study and teaching

  • Elisha and Peter both shut the door and prayed before acting. What place does quiet prayer have before I try to help others?
  • The widows showed Peter the clothes Tabitha had made. What will people show of my love when I am gone?
  • Paul boasts of his weakness, and his greatest burden is his care for all the Churches. How can I share that care through prayer and support for my parish and eparchy?
  • Jesus asked Peter, “Do you love me?” before saying, “Feed my sheep.” How does my love for Christ show in the way I care for those entrusted to me?
