Word for the day

“He said to him the third time, “Simon son of John, do you love me?” Peter felt hurt because he said to him the third time, “Do you love me?” And he said to him, “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” Jesus said to him, “Feed my sheep.” John 21:17

Reflection

Today the whole Church, East and West, keeps one feast for two apostles. Peter, the fisherman of Galilee, and Paul, the scholar of Tarsus who had persecuted the Church, were very different men. They once disagreed openly at Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14). Yet they preached one Gospel, served one Lord, and gave their lives for him in the same city. According to the ancient tradition of the Church, Peter was crucified in Rome during the persecution under Nero, and was buried on the Vatican hill, where the Basilica of St Peter stands over his tomb; tradition adds that he asked to be crucified head downward, judging himself unworthy to die as his Master had died. Paul, a Roman citizen, was beheaded on the road to Ostia and buried where the Basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls now stands. St Augustine said that the two apostles have one feast because they were one: though they suffered on different days, they were as one. For us in India this feast has a family warmth. Peter and Paul were the brothers in mission of Mar Thoma, our Father in faith, whose Dukhrana we will keep on Saturday. And our Syro-Malabar Church, with its own Major Archbishop and Synod, lives in full communion with the Bishop of Rome, the successor of Peter. Today we pray especially for Pope Leo XIV. It is a day of obligation: the Church gathers around the altar with the apostles.

The first two readings are a pair, like the two apostles. In the Second Book of Kings a wealthy woman of Shunem has welcomed the prophet Elisha, building a small room on her roof for him. He promises her a son: “At this season, in due time, you shall embrace a son.” The boy is born, grows, goes out to his father among the reapers, cries “Oh, my head, my head!”, and dies on his mother's lap at noon. She lays him on the prophet's bed, rides to Mount Carmel and will not let Elisha go. “So he went in and closed the door on the two of them, and prayed to the Lord.” He stretches himself on the child, mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands, the child grows warm, sneezes seven times and opens his eyes. “Take your son.” In the Acts of the Apostles the same story happens again through Peter. At Joppa a disciple named Tabitha, in Greek Dorcas, “devoted to good works and acts of charity,” has died. The widows stand weeping, showing the tunics and clothing she had made for them. Peter, like Elisha, puts everyone outside; he kneels and prays; then he says, “Tabitha, get up.” She opens her eyes and sits up. “He gave her his hand and helped her up.” The apostle does what the prophet did, and more: he does what Jesus did, for in the house of Jairus Jesus had taken a girl by the hand and said, “Talitha cum,” which means, “Little girl, get up!” (Mark 5:41). And this morning's weekday Gospel shows where Peter first saw that gesture: in his own house at Capernaum, when Jesus came to his mother-in-law, sick with fever, “and took her by the hand and lifted her up.” The authority of Peter is the power of the risen Lord, given for the poor, the widows and the dead.

Paul's reading is a page from his own life, written to Corinthians who were being impressed by boastful preachers. Paul says he will speak “as a fool”, and boast as the others do: “Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they descendants of Abraham? So am I.” Then he lists, not his successes, but his sufferings: labours and imprisonments, floggings, near death many times; beaten with rods three times, stoned once, shipwrecked three times, “for a night and a day I was adrift at sea”; dangers from rivers, bandits, Gentiles and his own people, in city and wilderness and at sea; hunger, thirst, cold and sleeplessness. He even mentions the synagogue punishment of the forty lashes minus one, which he, a Jew among Jews, accepted five times rather than abandon his people or his Lord. And above all this: “I am under daily pressure because of my anxiety for all the churches. Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is made to stumble, and I am not indignant?” Then the surprising conclusion: “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness.” The great apostle remembers that his ministry began with a humiliating escape, let down in a basket through a window in the wall of Damascus. Paul's glory is not his eloquence but his wounds, carried for Christ and for the Churches. At the end of his life he could write, “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith” (2 Timothy 4:7).

The Gospel takes us to the shore of the Sea of Tiberias after the Resurrection. Jesus has prepared a charcoal fire with fish and bread for the disciples. The last time Peter stood by a charcoal fire was in the courtyard of the high priest, where he denied his Master three times (John 18:18). Now, by another charcoal fire, Jesus asks him three times: “Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?” “Yes, Lord; you know that I love you.” “Feed my lambs.” The second time: “Tend my sheep.” The third time Peter is hurt, and he answers with all his heart: “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” “Feed my sheep.” Three confessions of love heal three denials, and each confession is answered by a commission. Jesus does not give Peter a lesser task because he fell; he gives him his whole flock, the lambs and the sheep, the little ones and the great. The calendar joins to this Gospel the scene at Caesarea Philippi: “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 16:18–19), and Luke adds the Lord's prayer for him: “I have prayed for you that your own faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). Here is the Petrine ministry that the Catholic Church believes continues in the Bishop of Rome: a primacy of faith and love, of feeding and of strengthening, a ministry of unity for the whole Church. Pope St Gregory the Great called himself the servant of the servants of God, and every Pope since has carried that title. Then Jesus tells Peter how he will die: “when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will fasten a belt around you and take you where you do not wish to go.” And the last word: “Follow me.” The shepherd who feeds the sheep will one day lay down his life for them, like the Good Shepherd himself.

The weekday readings, read on the Seventh Tuesday of the Apostles, complete the picture. Paul tells the Romans: “Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law.” Love is the only debt we can never finish paying, and it is the whole law. And Mark shows us Peter's house at Capernaum: Jesus heals his mother-in-law, “and she began to serve them,” and that evening “the whole city was gathered around the door,” bringing the sick and the possessed. Pilgrims to Capernaum today are shown, under a modern church, the remains of a house that early Christians honoured and that many archaeologists identify with Peter's house. The Church built on Peter is meant to be such a house: a home where Jesus heals, where those who are healed serve, and at whose door the whole city can gather. On this feast let us thank God for the two pillars of the Church, pray for the Pope, our Major Archbishop and all our bishops, and renew our own answer to the question that Jesus asks every disciple: do you love me?

Full homily

Dear friends, today the whole Church celebrates Saint Peter and Saint Paul, the two great apostles. They were very different: a fisherman and a scholar, one who denied Jesus and one who persecuted his Church. Both were forgiven, both preached Christ, both died for him in Rome. Today is a day of obligation, and we gather with joy.

In the first reading the prophet Elisha raises a dead boy to life. In the second, Peter kneels, prays and says, “Tabitha, get up” (Acts 9:40), and gives her his hand. Peter learned that gesture from Jesus, who took his mother-in-law by the hand in his own house and lifted her up (Mark 1:31). The power of the apostles is the power of the risen Lord, given to lift up the poor and the fallen.

Paul does not boast of his success. He lists his sufferings: prisons, beatings, shipwrecks, hunger, cold, and his daily anxiety for all the Churches. “If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness” (2 Corinthians 11:30). His glory is his wounds for Christ.

In the Gospel, by a charcoal fire, Jesus asks Peter three times, “Do you love me?” Three times Peter had denied him by another fire. Now three times he says, “Lord, you know that I love you.” And Jesus gives him the whole flock: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17).

This is the ministry that continues in the Pope, the successor of Peter: to feed, to strengthen the brethren, to keep the whole Church one. Our Syro-Malabar Church lives in full communion with him. Today let us pray for Pope Leo XIV, for our Major Archbishop and for all our bishops.

And let us hear the question that Jesus asks each of us: do you love me? Let us answer with Peter, “Lord, you know that I love you,” and let us show it by feeding his sheep and lifting up those who have fallen. Saint Peter and Saint Paul, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, today we celebrate Saint Peter and Saint Paul. Peter once said three times that he did not know Jesus. But Jesus forgave him and asked him three times, “Do you love me?”

Peter said, “Yes, Lord, you know that I love you!” Tonight, tell Jesus the same: “Jesus, I love you!” And pray for the Pope, who takes care of the whole Church.

Prayer

Lord God, you built your Church on the faith of the apostles and gave us today the joy of honouring Saint Peter and Saint Paul, who gave their lives for Christ in Rome. You made Peter, who had denied his Master, the shepherd of your flock, and Paul, who had persecuted your Church, its tireless apostle. Hear the prayer of your Church gathered on this feast: keep us one in faith and love around the successor of Peter. Strengthen our Pope Leo, our Major Archbishop, our bishops and priests, that they may feed your sheep with love. Give us Peter's love and Paul's zeal, the hand that lifts up the fallen and the heart that carries all the Churches. When our own hour comes, may we too have fought the good fight, finished the race and kept the faith. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
