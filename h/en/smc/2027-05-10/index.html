Word for the day

“The water that I will give will become in them a spring of water gushing up to eternal life.” John 4:14

Reflection

It is noon in Samaria. “Jesus, tired out by his journey, was sitting by the well.” The Lord who has now ascended to the Father once sat tired on the edge of Jacob's well, in the heat of the day, and asked a woman for water: “Give me a drink.” She is astonished, for “Jews do not share things in common with Samaritans”, and a Jewish teacher did not usually speak alone with a woman of Samaria. Jesus crosses both walls at once. He is not ashamed to need something from her. That is how God often begins with us: not with a command but with a request. He asks for a little of our time, a little of our attention, a cup of water, so that he can give far more. “If you knew the gift of God, and who it is that is saying to you, ‘Give me a drink,’ you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water.” The woman thinks of running water from a spring rather than the still water of the cistern. Jesus means something deeper: the gift of God himself.

She objects with the practical sense of a woman who has carried water every day of her life: “Sir, you have no bucket, and the well is deep.” And she asks the great question: “Are you greater than our ancestor Jacob?” Jesus answers by comparing two kinds of water. “Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again, but those who drink of the water that I will give them will never be thirsty. The water that I will give will become in them a spring of water gushing up to eternal life.” John explains later what this water is: “Now he said this about the Spirit, which believers in him were to receive; for as yet there was no Spirit, because Jesus was not yet glorified” (John 7:39). Now Jesus has been glorified. He has gone up to the Father, and the Spirit is about to be poured out. That is why we read this Gospel in the days of the novena. Every thirst we carry, for love, for meaning, for forgiveness, for peace, is in the end a thirst for God, and the Holy Spirit is the spring that God places inside us. The woman's first answer is still on the surface: “Sir, give me this water, so that I may never be thirsty or have to keep coming here to draw water.” But she has begun to ask, and the conversation that follows will make her the first missionary of her town.

Paul writes to the Thessalonians with gratitude, because the same Spirit has already been at work in them. “God chose you as the first fruits for salvation through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth.” The first fruits were the first part of the harvest, offered to God as a promise of the rest. The young Church of Thessalonica was the first harvest in its city; the Syro-Malabar Church, born from the preaching of Thomas, was among the first fruits of the Gospel in India. Paul's advice to such a Church is simple: “So then, brothers and sisters, stand firm and hold fast to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by our letter.” The faith comes to us by the living voice of the Church and by the Scriptures together. Our Mar Thoma tradition, the prayers our grandparents taught us, the way our Church celebrates the Qurbana, are not dead customs; they are channels through which the living water has reached us. Paul ends with a blessing for these days of waiting: “may our Lord Jesus Christ himself and God our Father, who loved us and through grace gave us eternal comfort and good hope, comfort your hearts and strengthen them in every good work and word.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Gospel takes us to a well in Samaria at noon. Jesus is tired, and he sits down. A woman comes to draw water, and Jesus asks her: “Give me a drink.”

She is surprised. Jews and Samaritans did not mix, and a Jewish teacher did not talk alone with a Samaritan woman. But Jesus crosses those walls. He is not ashamed to ask. God often begins with us like this: not with an order, but with a request. He asks for a little of our time, so that he can give us much more.

Jesus says: “If you knew the gift of God,” you would have asked me, and I would have given you living water. Whoever drinks ordinary water will be thirsty again. But “the water that I will give will become in them a spring of water gushing up to eternal life.” This living water is the Holy Spirit. Jesus has now gone up to the Father, and the Spirit is about to be poured out. In this novena, let us bring him our thirst.

Paul tells the Thessalonians: God chose you as first fruits. Stand firm and hold fast to the traditions you were taught. Our Church received the faith from St Thomas, and our grandparents handed it to us. These traditions are channels through which the living water has reached us. Let us keep them and hand them on.

The woman said, “Sir, give me this water.” Let that be our prayer today: Lord, give us this water. Fill us with your Spirit at Pentecost. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus was thirsty, and he asked a woman for water. Then he told her about a special water that he gives, the Holy Spirit.

When we pray, the Holy Spirit fills our heart like a little spring of water that never dries. Say today: ‘Jesus, give me your living water!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, tired from your journey you sat by the well and asked for a drink, so that you might give us the living water. Now you are glorified at the Father's right hand: pour out on us the Spirit you promised, and let him become in us a spring gushing up to eternal life. Satisfy our deepest thirst, help us to cross the walls that divide people, and keep us faithful to the traditions handed down to us from the apostles. Comfort our hearts and strengthen them in every good work and word. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of gifts that come before we ask. Paul thanks God, who chose the Thessalonians and called them through the Gospel, and he tells them to hold fast to what they were taught. At Jacob’s well a tired Jesus asks a Samaritan woman for a drink, and then offers her living water. In the week before Pentecost we know what that water is: the Gospel itself says that Jesus spoke of the Spirit (John 7:39).

2 Thessalonians 2:13–17 · God chose you as the first fruits for salvation

Setting.

Paul founded the church in Thessalonica, the chief city of Macedonia, in the face of strong opposition (Acts 17:1–9). This second letter answers a confusion: some were saying “that the day of the Lord is already here” (2:2), perhaps on the strength of a letter falsely said to be from Paul. Many scholars think the letter itself was written by a disciple in Paul’s name; others hold that it comes from Paul, who signs it with his own hand (3:17).

Just before this reading Paul has warned of a lawless one who will deceive many before the end (2:3–12). Now the tone changes completely, from warning to thanksgiving.

Structure.

  • vv. 13–14: thanksgiving: God chose you, sanctified you by the Spirit, called you through the Gospel, for glory.
  • v. 15: so stand firm and hold fast to the traditions.
  • vv. 16–17: a prayer: may the Lord and the Father comfort and strengthen you.

Key words.

  • aparchē, first fruits: The first part of the harvest, given to God as a sign that all belongs to him. Textual note: some manuscripts read ap’ archēs, “from the beginning”. In Greek the two readings differ by a single letter. The NRSV prints “first fruits” and gives “from the beginning” in its note; either way, the choice was God’s before it was theirs.
  • paradosis, tradition: Literally “what is handed on”. Paul names two ways: “by word of mouth or by our letter”. The faith reached the Thessalonians as living teaching before any of it was written.
  • paraklēsis, comfort: The word is related to Paraklētos, the name Jesus gives the Holy Spirit in John’s Gospel (14:16, 26). God’s comfort is not only a feeling; it is strength “in every good work and word”.

Verse by verse.

“God chose you as the first fruits for salvation through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth.” (2:13)

The whole Trinity is at work: the Father chooses, the Spirit makes holy, and the call leads to “the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ” (2:14). Our faith is our answer, but the first word is God’s.

“stand firm and hold fast to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by our letter.” (2:15)

Against a false message, Paul does not invent something new. He sends his readers back to what they already received. Faith is guarded by faithfulness to the apostles’ teaching.

“who loved us and through grace gave us eternal comfort and good hope,” (2:16)

Hope is a gift “through grace”. In the end it is not our strength that keeps us standing, but the love of the Father and the Son.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council cites v. 15 when it teaches that the apostles handed on what they had received, and warned the faithful to hold fast to the traditions they had learned by word of mouth or by letter (Dei Verbum 8). Sacred Tradition and Sacred Scripture flow from the same divine wellspring and move toward the same goal (Dei Verbum 9; CCC 80). They are not two rival sources but one living Word of God, entrusted to the Church.

John 4:3–15 · Jesus gives living water

Setting.

Jesus leaves Judea for Galilee, and “he had to go through Samaria”. Samaria lay on the direct road north, but in John “had to” often points to God’s plan as well. The Samaritans worshipped on Mount Gerizim, near Shechem, and accepted only the five books of Moses as Scripture. Between them and the Jews there was a long history of mutual distrust, with wrongs remembered on both sides.

Sychar was near the field Jacob bought and gave to Joseph (Genesis 33:19; 48:22; Joshua 24:32). A well called Jacob’s Well is still shown there today. It is noon, the heat of the day. The disciples have gone to buy food, and Jesus sits alone by the well.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–6: the journey, the well, Jesus tired at noon.
  • vv. 7–9: “Give me a drink”; the woman’s surprise.
  • v. 10: “If you knew the gift of God”: living water.
  • vv. 11–12: she misunderstands: no bucket, a deep well, our father Jacob.
  • vv. 13–15: water that ends thirst; “Sir, give me this water”.

Key words.

  • hydōr zōn, living water: In everyday speech it meant running water from a spring, fresher than water stored in a cistern. The woman hears it so. Jesus means more: later he cries out that rivers of living water will flow from the believer’s heart, and John adds, “he said this about the Spirit” (7:38–39).
  • dōrea, gift: “The gift of God” (v. 10). In Acts the same noun is used for the Holy Spirit: “you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit” (Acts 2:38).
  • pēgē, spring: Jacob’s well is called a “spring” in v. 6 and a “well” or cistern (phrear) in vv. 11–12. Jesus promises a pēgē (v. 14): not water drawn up with effort, but a spring rising inside the one who drinks.

Verse by verse.

“Jesus, tired out by his journey, was sitting by the well.” (4:6)

St Augustine says that Jesus was tired for our sake: by his strength he created us, and by his weakness he came to seek us (in substance, Tractates on John 15). The Son of God knew a long road and a hot day.

“Give me a drink.” (4:7)

The one who will give living water begins by asking. On the Cross he will say again, “I am thirsty” (19:28). He comes to us first as one in need, so that we may discover our own need.

“If you knew the gift of God, and who it is that is saying to you, ‘Give me a drink,’ you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water.” (4:10)

Two things she does not yet know: the gift, and the giver. The rest of the chapter is her slow discovery of both, until her whole town says, “this is truly the Savior of the world” (4:42).

“The water that I will give will become in them a spring of water gushing up to eternal life.” (4:14)

The verb “gushing up” is used elsewhere for leaping. The Spirit is not stagnant in us; he rises, moves, and carries us toward eternal life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism begins its teaching on prayer at this well. Jesus thirsts; his asking arises from the depths of God’s desire for us. Whether we realize it or not, prayer is the meeting of God’s thirst with ours; God thirsts that we may thirst for him (CCC 2560, drawing on St Augustine). The Catechism also teaches that the Spirit is the living water welling up from Christ crucified as its source and welling up in us to eternal life (CCC 694). Every Baptism is this well, and every Pentecost novena is the woman’s prayer: “Sir, give me this water.”

Questions people ask

Why does the Gospel say that Jews and Samaritans did not share things in common?

John explains the woman’s surprise for readers who did not know the region. Jews and Samaritans had been divided for centuries over the place of worship and over the Scriptures, and each side had grievances. Jesus crosses that line, as he crosses the line between a Jewish teacher and an unknown woman. He does not deny the difference; he will say that “salvation is from the Jews” (4:22). But he speaks with her as one to be given the gift of God.

For study and teaching

  • What tradition of faith did I receive “by word of mouth” from my parents or grandparents, and how am I handing it on?
  • Jesus first asks the woman for a drink. When has someone’s need become, for me, the place where I met the Lord?
