Word for the day

“do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my victorious right hand.” Isaiah 41:10

Reflection

The first Friday of Kaitha belongs to a bishop of the border, Mar Yakob of Nisibis, James of Nisibis, who died about the year 338. Nisibis, today Nusaybin on the Turkish-Syrian frontier, stood at the edge of two empires, the Roman and the Persian, and its Christians lived under the threat of war. According to the Syriac tradition he was a man of prayer and of the Scriptures who became the first bishop of the city and the teacher of the young Ephrem, who later became the great poet and Doctor of the Church; tradition also says he took part in the Council of Nicaea in 325. When the Persian armies came against the city, the people looked to their bishop, and the Church of Nisibis remembered how her prayer, and not only her walls, had kept her. We keep his memory because we belong to the same family: our liturgy, the Qurbana of Addai and Mari, comes from the Church of the East, which had its roots in this Syriac world.

The readings give a fine frame for such a life. In Isaiah, God speaks to a small and frightened people: “But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen.” The name Jacob is the name of our saint, Yakob in Syriac, and it is easy to hear the promise spoken over him: “do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my victorious right hand.” Isaiah dares to call the nation “you worm Jacob”, so small and weak, and then tells it that the Holy One of Israel is its Redeemer. The Church of Nisibis was small and exposed, and she was held by the same hand. So are we, whenever we are tired and alone: God does not choose the strong first.

In Acts the word of the Lord grows “mightily” at Ephesus, a city of magic and of the great temple of Artemis. God does “extraordinary miracles” through Paul; the sons of Sceva, who try to use the name of Jesus like a charm without belonging to him, are overpowered, and many believers confess and burn their books of magic, worth fifty thousand silver coins, before everyone. The lesson is two-sided: the name of Jesus is not a spell, and a Christian leaves the old powers openly. In Kerala some still go to astrologers, amulets and charms in time of fear; the Church asks us to put our trust in Christ alone (Catechism 2116–2117). In the Epistle Paul says that his weapons are not human: “we destroy arguments and every proud obstacle raised up against the knowledge of God, and we take every thought captive to obey Christ.” The weapon of a bishop is prayer, the word and the sacraments, and also patient teaching, as James taught young Ephrem. Paul adds that the authority was given “for building you up and not for tearing you down”.

The Gospel is the great word to those who bear witness. “Do not fear those who kill the body, and after that can do nothing more.” Jesus says it to his friends, and in the same breath tells them of a Father who forgets not one of five sparrows sold for two pennies and who has counted the hairs of their head. The call to confess him is clear: “everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God.” And when they are brought before rulers, they should not worry, “for the Holy Spirit will teach you at that very hour what you ought to say.” The Church of the East knew this hour well; in the Persian Empire thousands of her sons and daughters were martyred in the century after Nisibis, and the Thomas Christians of India honour them in their calendar to this day. On this first Friday of Kaitha, the Friday of martyrs and saints, we ask for the same fearlessness: not the boldness of the proud, but the quiet courage of those who know that they are counted and held.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church of the East remembers Mar Yakob, a bishop of Nisibis who lived about seventeen hundred years ago. He was a man of prayer, a teacher of the Scriptures, and the teacher of the young Ephrem, who became one of the greatest poets of our tradition. Our Qurbana, with its prayer of Addai and Mari, comes from this same Church.

The first reading is God's promise to Jacob: “Do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God” (Isaiah 41:10). God does not say this to the strong. He says it to a little people, even to the “worm Jacob”. He chooses the small and holds them with his right hand.

In Acts, people burned their books of magic in public. The name of Jesus is not a charm. We do not trust amulets, horoscopes or lucky days; we trust the Lord himself. If there is any such thing in our house, today is a good day to put it away.

Saint Paul tells us that our weapons are not the world's weapons. They are prayer, the word of God and love, and they are used for building up and not for tearing down (2 Corinthians 10:4, 8).

And Jesus says: “Do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows” (Luke 12:7). Not one is forgotten before God. If we acknowledge him before others, he will acknowledge us before the angels, and the Holy Spirit will teach us what to say. Let us carry this courage into the week. Amen.

For children

Children, today we remember Mar Yakob, a bishop who loved to pray and to teach children. One of the boys he taught, Ephrem, grew up to write beautiful songs for God. Jesus tells us not to be afraid, because God knows every little sparrow, and he knows you even better. So when you are afraid of the dark, of exams or of storms, say: ‘Jesus, you are with me.’

Prayer

God our Father, you chose Jacob and told him not to fear, for you were with him. We thank you for your servant Mar Yakob, bishop of Nisibis, who taught your word, led his people in prayer and was a father in the faith to the young Ephrem. Through his intercession keep your Church safe from fear and from every false trust. Teach us to put away the charms and idols that we hold, and to place our whole trust in the name of Jesus. Give us weapons that are not of this world, the word, the sacraments and love, and strengthen all who suffer for the faith, as you strengthened the martyrs of the Church of the East. Teach us by your Holy Spirit what to say, and when we are afraid, uphold us with your right hand. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The four readings of this Friday, the day of martyrs and saints in Kaitha, are one message to a small and threatened people: do not fear. In Isaiah, God speaks to Jacob, a worm of a people, and promises to hold his right hand. In Acts, at Ephesus, God works wonders through Paul, and the name of Jesus proves to be no charm but the power of a living Lord. In the Second Letter to the Corinthians, Paul says that the weapons of the apostles are not human, and that their authority is given to build up. In Luke, Jesus tells his friends not to fear those who kill the body, because the Father counts even the sparrows and the Spirit will teach them what to say. (The calendar also gives the longer forms, Acts 19:8–20, 2 Corinthians 10:3–18 and Luke 12:1–12; the notes below follow the verses printed for the day.) The day keeps the memory of Mar Yakob, James of Nisibis, a bishop on the border of two empires, and the readings are well chosen for his Church.

Isaiah 41:8–16 · Do not fear, for I am with you

Setting.

Isaiah chapters 40–55 are addressed to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC, a people who felt forgotten and small. Many scholars call this part of the book Second Isaiah. It opens with comfort (40:1) and speaks of a coming return.

Chapter 41 sets the Lord’s people against the power of the nations. In this passage God answers Israel’s fear with a series of promises, each beginning with the call “do not fear”.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–9: Israel is the chosen servant, descendant of Abraham, taken from the ends of the earth.
  • v. 10: “Do not fear, for I am with you”: three promises, to strengthen, help and uphold.
  • vv. 11–12: those who fight against Israel will come to nothing.
  • vv. 13–14: God holds Israel’s right hand; the Holy One of Israel is the Redeemer.
  • vv. 15–16: Israel will be a threshing sledge, and will rejoice in the Lord.

Key words.

  • ’al tîrā’, do not fear: The call of reassurance God speaks to Abraham (Genesis 15:1), Isaac, Jacob and Moses. It is not a command to feel no fear, but a promise: you are not alone.
  • gō’ēl, redeemer: In Israel the gō’ēl was the close relative who rescued a family member from slavery or debt (Leviticus 25:47–49). God takes that role for his people.
  • ‘ebed, servant: Israel is God’s servant: not a slave of fear but a chosen one given a task. In this part of Isaiah the word carries honour as well as duty.

Verse by verse.

“But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen, the offspring of Abraham, my friend;” (41:8)

Abraham is called God’s friend. The people’s hope rests on the friendship of God with their ancestor, and that friendship is not forgotten.

“do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my victorious right hand.” (41:10)

The reason not to fear is never our own strength, but the nearness of God: he is with us, and he is our God.

“Do not fear, you worm Jacob, you insect Israel! I will help you, says the Lord; your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel.” (41:14)

God does not flatter. He names the weakness honestly and then holds it. The word “worm” is a name for helplessness, and the answer is the Holy One.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in Isaiah’s promise the care of God for his small ones, and prays it in its own time of trial. The Catechism teaches that Israel’s election is a choice made by God’s love, and that the covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (CCC 218, 839).

Acts 19:11–20 · The name of the Lord Jesus was praised

Setting.

Paul stays in Ephesus for more than two years (19:8–10; he later speaks of three years, 20:31), teaching daily. Ephesus was a great port, home to the temple of Artemis, and well known for charms and magical writings. Luke tells how the Gospel meets this world.

Verses 13–14 tell of travelling Jewish exorcists, among them seven sons of a man called Sceva. Luke calls him a Jewish high priest; no high priest of that name is known from other sources, so he may have belonged to a priestly family or used the title himself. What matters is that these men used the name of Jesus as a formula, without belonging to him.

Structure.

  • vv. 11–12: extraordinary miracles by the hands of Paul; cloths touched by him heal the sick.
  • vv. 13–16: the sons of Sceva try the name of Jesus as a charm and are overpowered.
  • vv. 17–19: awe in Ephesus; believers confess their practices and burn their magic books.
  • v. 20: the word of the Lord grows and prevails.

Key words.

  • dynameis ou tas tychousas, extraordinary miracles: Literally “powers not of the ordinary kind”. Luke wants his readers to see that the power is God’s, working through Paul, not Paul’s own.
  • exomologoumenoi, confessing: Those who believed “confessed”: they said openly what they had done. Conversion is not only a feeling; it includes turning away from the old life, in the open.
  • ischyen, prevailed: The same word is used of a victory. The word of the Lord is not merely spread; it wins ground against the powers that held Ephesus.

Verse by verse.

“God did extraordinary miracles through Paul,” (19:11)

Luke is careful: God does the miracles. Paul is the instrument.

“But the evil spirit said to them in reply, “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?”” (19:15)

The name of Jesus is not a magic word for anyone to use. It is the name of a person, and only those who belong to him can call on it in faith.

“A number of those who practiced magic collected their books and burned them publicly; when the value of these books was calculated, it was found to come to fifty thousand silver coins.” (19:19)

The sum is very large, more than a labourer could earn in a lifetime. The new believers give up what was valuable to them, without being asked, and in the open.

“So the word of the Lord grew mightily and prevailed.” (19:20)

Luke ends the scene as he ends several in Acts, with a note of growth. The word is stronger than the old powers.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that all forms of divination, magic and sorcery are to be rejected, because they contradict the honour, respect and loving fear owed to God alone (CCC 2116–2117). The Church’s prayer of exorcism and the sacrament of Baptism are not charms: they work by the power of Christ and in faith (CCC 1237, 1673). Acts shows the answer in a city of magic: the open confession of Christ, and the setting aside of every false trust.

2 Corinthians 10:3–11 · Weapons that are not of this world

Setting.

In chapters 10–13 Paul answers critics at Corinth who say that he is bold only in his letters and weak when present. Many scholars think these chapters come from a separate letter, written before or after chapters 1–9; the Church reads the whole letter as Scripture.

Paul uses the language of war, but he turns it: the battle is not against people but against ideas and pride that stand against God. The aim is obedience to Christ and the building up of the community.

Structure.

  • vv. 3–4: we live as human beings, but our weapons are not human; they are powerful to destroy strongholds.
  • vv. 5–6: arguments and pride are destroyed; every thought is taken captive for Christ.
  • vv. 7–8: “you belong to Christ, and so do we”; authority is given to build up, not tear down.
  • vv. 9–11: the critics’ charge that Paul’s letters are strong and his presence weak; he is the same present or absent.

Key words.

  • hopla, weapons: The tools of a soldier. For Paul they are truth, righteousness, faith, prayer and the word (see Ephesians 6:11–18).
  • oikodomē, building up: The aim of all authority in the Church. Paul uses the word also of the Church as a house and of prayer in the assembly (1 Corinthians 14).
  • aichmalōtizontes, taking captive: The picture is of a prisoner of war brought home. Every thought is brought under the rule of Christ, not to destroy the mind but to set it in order.

Verse by verse.

“for the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power to destroy strongholds.” (10:4)

The only strength the apostle trusts is God’s. A stronghold here is a way of thinking that shuts out God.

“and we take every thought captive to obey Christ.” (10:5)

Faith is meant to shape the whole mind. Christ is Lord of our thinking, not only of our feelings.

“Now, even if I boast a little too much of our authority, which the Lord gave for building you up and not for tearing you down, I will not be ashamed of it.” (10:8)

The test of every authority in the Church, from the bishop to the parent, is whether it builds up.

How the Church reads it.

The Church applies Paul’s words to its ministers: authority in the Church is a service for the sake of building up, and a bishop is to teach, sanctify and govern in the name of Christ and not in his own name (CCC 894–896; Lumen Gentium 27). The Catechism also teaches that faith and reason do not conflict, since the same God gives both, so that taking every thought captive for Christ does not mean silencing the mind (CCC 159).

Luke 12:4–12 · Do not fear: you are of more value than many sparrows

Setting.

Jesus is teaching a great crowd (12:1) and begins with a warning to his disciples against hypocrisy. In today’s verses he speaks to his “friends”, a word that is rare and warm in Luke’s Gospel. The reading is about fear, and about the confidence of those who confess him.

The “synagogues, rulers and authorities” of verse 11 mean the places where the first disciples would be tried. The disciples themselves, like Jesus, were Jews, and these words are about the cost of witness, never about blaming a people (Nostra Aetate 4).

Structure.

  • vv. 4–5: do not fear those who can only kill the body; fear God, who has authority over life beyond death.
  • vv. 6–7: five sparrows, the hairs of the head: nothing is forgotten by the Father.
  • vv. 8–9: acknowledge the Son of Man before others, and he will acknowledge you before God’s angels.
  • v. 10: words against the Son of Man may be forgiven; blasphemy against the Holy Spirit will not.
  • vv. 11–12: do not worry before the authorities; the Holy Spirit will teach you what to say.

Key words.

  • philoi, friends: Jesus calls his disciples friends (see John 15:15). The one who tells them to be fearless speaks as a friend who is with them.
  • phobeisthe, fear: Two fears are set side by side: the fear of persecutors, which is to be set aside, and the “fear” of God, which is reverent awe and the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10).
  • homologēsē, acknowledge, confess: To speak publicly for someone as your own. The disciple’s confession of Jesus is answered by his confession of the disciple before God.

Verse by verse.

“I tell you, my friends, do not fear those who kill the body, and after that can do nothing more.” (12:4)

Fear is put in its place. Even the worst that human power can do does not reach the life a person has with God.

“Are not five sparrows sold for two pennies? Yet not one of them is forgotten in God’s sight.” (12:6)

Sparrows were the cheapest food in the market. If not one of them is forgotten, the disciple is certainly not forgotten.

“And I tell you, everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God;” (12:8)

Confession and acknowledgement go together. We speak for Christ, and he speaks for us.

“for the Holy Spirit will teach you at that very hour what you ought to say.” (12:12)

A promise for the hour of trial, not a reason to neglect learning. The Spirit gives words to those who have first given their lives.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that martyrdom is the supreme witness to the truth of the faith (CCC 2473), and that the “fear of the Lord” is one of the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1831). On the sparrows, it teaches that Jesus asks for childlike trust in the providence of our heavenly Father, who cares for the smallest needs of his children (CCC 305). The sin against the Holy Spirit, of which verse 10 speaks, is taught by the Church as a deliberate refusal to accept the mercy God offers, remaining in the refusal to the end (CCC 1864). Tertullian wrote that the blood of Christians is seed (Apology 50, in substance).

Questions people ask

What is the sin against the Holy Spirit that “will not be forgiven”?

The Church teaches that no sin, however grave, is beyond the mercy of God. The sin against the Holy Spirit is the deliberate and final refusal to accept that mercy, and so refusing repentance and forgiveness to the end (CCC 1864). It is not an act of weakness or a moment of anger. Anyone who is worried that they have committed this sin shows by that worry that the door is still open.

Is it wrong to wear a blessed medal or carry a cross, since Acts condemns charms?

The Church distinguishes. Sacramentals such as a cross, a medal or blessed water are signs that prepare us to receive grace and lead us to prayer; they work through the Church’s prayer and our faith, never as a spell (CCC 1667–1670). A charm or amulet trusted to protect on its own is superstition (CCC 2111). The question is always: in whom do I trust?

For the Syro-Malabar reader

On the first Friday of Kaitha the Syro-Malabar Church remembers Mar Yakob (Yakob is James, and also the Syriac form of Jacob) of Nisibis, a bishop who died about 338. According to the Syriac tradition he was the first bishop of the city, the teacher of the young Ephrem, and a man of prayer, and he is said to have taken part in the Council of Nicaea. Nisibis stood on the frontier of the Roman and Persian empires, and Ephrem’s later hymns recall the sufferings and the prayer of that city (in substance).

Our Holy Qurbana of Addai and Mari comes from the Church of the East, which grew in this Syriac world. Fridays in Kaitha remember martyrs and saints of that Church. We keep Mar Yakob’s feast with Isaiah’s word to his own namesake: do not fear, I am with you.

For study and teaching

  • God says to a “worm Jacob”: do not fear, I will help you. Where do I feel small and afraid, and can I say these words to God in prayer today?
  • The sons of Sceva used the name of Jesus without belonging to him. What do I trust, besides Christ, when I am afraid, and what could I set aside?
  • Paul says that authority is given for building up. Where do I have some authority, in a family, a school or a parish, and how can I use it to build up?
  • Jesus says that not one sparrow is forgotten. How can I remember today those who are persecuted for their faith, and pray for them and for those who wronged them?
