Word for the day

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much; and whoever is dishonest in a very little is dishonest also in much.” Luke 16:10

Reflection

Paul calls the apostles “servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries,” and says: “it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.” Everything we have, money, time, faith, is entrusted to us; we are managers, not owners.

Jesus' parable of the dishonest manager is surprising: the master praises his shrewdness, not his dishonesty. If worldly people are clever with money for their own future, Christians should be wise in using wealth for eternity, “make friends for yourselves by means of dishonest wealth”, through generosity to the poor. And the conclusion is clear: “You cannot serve God and wealth.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul calls the apostles servants of Christ and stewards of God's mysteries, and says that it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy (1 Corinthians 4:1–2). Everything we have, money, time and faith, is entrusted to us; we are managers, not owners.

In the parable of the dishonest manager Jesus says: whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much. You cannot serve God and wealth (Luke 16:10–13). Money is a good servant but a bad master.

Today the Church also remembers St Elizabeth of Hungary, a princess who saw her wealth as belonging to the poor. Let us set aside a share of our income for God and the poor, and honour the Lord with what we have. Amen.

For children

Children, everything we have is a gift from God. Today set aside a little of your pocket money for the poor!

Prayer

Lord, all I have is yours. Make me a trustworthy steward, faithful in little things, generous with the poor, and free to serve you alone. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

One word links today's readings: steward. Paul calls the apostles “stewards of God’s mysteries” who must be found trustworthy. Jesus tells of a steward who was dishonest but shrewd, and draws the lesson: be faithful in little things and you will be trusted with true riches. In the season of the Church's dedication, every Christian is reminded that what he holds belongs to Another.

1 Corinthians 4:1–5 · Stewards of God's mysteries

Setting.

The Corinthians had formed parties around their favourite preachers: “I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos” (1:12). They compared and judged their teachers. Paul answers that preachers are only servants, and that the only judge who matters is the Lord.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: what apostles are: servants and stewards, who must be trustworthy.
  • vv. 3–4: Paul does not depend on human verdicts, not even on his own conscience; the Lord judges.
  • v. 5: do not judge before the time; the Lord will reveal the hidden purposes of the heart.

Key words.

  • hypēretēs, servant (v. 1): An assistant who carries out the orders of another. The apostle does not act on his own authority.
  • oikonomos, steward (vv. 1–2): The manager of a household, often himself a slave, who looked after his master's goods and gave each servant his portion. The same word appears in today's Gospel (Luke 16:1).
  • mystēria, mysteries: God's saving plan, once hidden and now revealed in Christ. In the Eastern Churches the word came to name the sacraments as well, the Holy Mysteries entrusted to the Church's ministers.

Verse by verse.

“Moreover, it is required of stewards that they be found trustworthy.” (4:2)

Not brilliant, not popular, but trustworthy. The steward's virtue is faithfulness to the master.

“Then each one will receive commendation from God.” (4:5)

Paul ends with hope, not fear. The Lord's judgment will bring to light the hidden good as well as the hidden evil.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council and the Catechism describe ordained ministers as servants of Christ who act in his person, not by their own power (CCC 875–876). The Fathers often applied this verse to bishops and priests as dispensers of the sacraments. Every baptised person is also a steward of the gifts of grace (1 Peter 4:10).

Luke 16:1–13 · Faithful in a very little

Setting.

Luke 16 is about the right use of wealth, and follows the parables of mercy in chapter 15. The parable of the dishonest steward is one of the hardest in the Gospels. A manager, about to be dismissed for wasting his master's goods, quickly reduces the debts of his master's debtors so that they will welcome him later. The master praises him, not for his dishonesty, but for acting shrewdly.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the steward is accused and dismissed.
  • vv. 3–4: his inner monologue: “What will I do?”
  • vv. 5–7: he reduces the debts.
  • v. 8: the master praises his shrewdness.
  • vv. 9–13: Jesus' sayings on wealth: make friends with it, be faithful in little, you cannot serve two masters.

Key words.

  • phronimōs, shrewdly (v. 8): Prudently, with practical wisdom. The same word used of the wise bridesmaids (Matthew 25:2). Jesus wants his disciples to be as alert for the Kingdom as worldly people are for their own interests.
  • mamōnas, wealth, mammon (vv. 9, 11, 13): An Aramaic word for property or riches, perhaps from a root meaning “that in which one trusts”. Jesus speaks of wealth almost as a rival master.
  • bath / kor, measures (vv. 6–7): The NRSV “jugs” and “containers” translate the Hebrew measures bath (for oil) and kor (for wheat), which the POC keeps (ബത്ത്, കോർ). The debts were very large.

Verse by verse.

“for the children of this age are more shrewd in dealing with their own generation than are the children of light.” (16:8)

“Children of light” was a name some Jewish groups of the time used for the faithful, as the Dead Sea Scrolls show. Jesus' sad observation: worldly people often plan more carefully for this life than believers do for eternal life.

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much; and whoever is dishonest in a very little is dishonest also in much.” (16:10)

Character is formed in small things: a little honesty, a little punctuality, a small promise kept.

“You cannot serve God and wealth.” (16:13)

The verb is “serve as a slave”. One can use money; one cannot belong to it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that disordered love of money is a form of idolatry, quoting the Lord: “You cannot serve God and mammon” (CCC 2113). Many Fathers explained the “friends” of v. 9 as the poor, who will welcome the generous into the eternal homes; so St Augustine and St Ambrose (in substance). Wealth becomes holy when it is shared.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus praise dishonesty?

No. The master (and Jesus) praise the steward's quick, decisive prudence, not his fraud. Jesus calls him “dishonest” (v. 8). The lesson is: use present goods wisely and generously for the sake of eternal life.

For study and teaching

  • What has God entrusted to me as a “steward”, and how would I give an account of it today?
  • In what small matter of honesty or faithfulness is God asking more of me this week?
