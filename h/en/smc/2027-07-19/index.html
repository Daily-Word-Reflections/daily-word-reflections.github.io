Readings

Word for the day

“For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.” James 2:26

Reflection

The Season of Kaitha is the season of the harvest, and a harvest is judged by its fruit. Today's Epistle asks what the fruit of faith is. James places two very different people side by side: Abraham, the father of believers, and Rahab, a woman of Jericho whom the Bible calls a prostitute. “Was not our ancestor Abraham justified by works when he offered his son Isaac on the altar? You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was brought to completion by the works.” James is not contradicting Paul. Paul argues against those who thought they could earn God's favour by keeping the Law; James argues against those who thought that a belief held in the head, with a closed hand and a closed door, is enough. Both of them quote the same verse, “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (Genesis 15:6), and both know that faith is God's gift. But a gift received is a gift used. Abraham believed long before he climbed the mountain of Moriah; the climb was his faith made visible, and in that obedience, says James, faith “was brought to completion”. The Church teaches the same: faith is the beginning of salvation, and a faith that is not alive in hope and love does not unite us fully to Christ.

Then James names Rahab: “Likewise, was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she welcomed the messengers and sent them out by another road?” Her story is told in the Book of Joshua. Two spies come to Jericho; she hides them on her roof, confesses that “The Lord your God is indeed God in heaven above and on earth below” (Joshua 2:11), and asks that her family be spared. Her faith did not stay in words: she risked her life and her house. The Letter to the Hebrews also names her among the people of faith (11:31), and Matthew places her in the family line of the Messiah (Matthew 1:5). So the patriarch and the foreigner, the greatest and the least, are justified in the same way, by a living faith that opens the door. James closes with the sentence that gathers the whole passage: “For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.”

The Gospel takes us to the last teaching of Jesus in the Temple, after the seven woes. He speaks as a prophet of Israel who loves his people as only one of them could. “Therefore I send you prophets, sages, and scribes, some of whom you will kill and crucify, and some you will flog in your synagogues and pursue from town to town.” He points back over the whole long history of faithful witnesses, “from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah”, from the first righteous man killed in Genesis to the last in the order of the Hebrew Scriptures (2 Chronicles 24:20–21). The prophets whom he now sends are the apostles and martyrs whom Kaitha honours. These hard words are to be read as the Church reads them: as a warning to every generation, ours included, and not as a verdict on a people. The Church teaches that the Jewish people as a whole are not to be blamed for the death of the Lord (Catechism 597–598), and that God's call to them is irrevocable (Romans 11:29). Jesus, his mother and his first disciples were all Jews.

Then the tone turns from warning to tenderness: “Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” Every Kerala household knows the picture: at the first shadow of a kite in the sky the mother hen spreads her wings and the chicks run under them. The psalmist already said of God, “he will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge” (Psalm 91:4). It is not God who is unwilling; “you were not willing”. His love never forces. Yet the lament ends with a promise: “you will not see me again until you say, ‘Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.’” The words are from Psalm 118:26, which the crowds sing on Palm Sunday and which the Church repeats in the Sanctus of the Holy Qurbana. So we ask two questions today: what fruit does my faith bear, and have I let myself be gathered under his wings?

Homily outline

Theme: “For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.”

A faith brought to completion by obedience, a stranger's open door, and the Lord who would gather us under his wings.

1. Faith brought to completion

  • Abraham believed first.
  • The mountain of Moriah made his faith visible.
  • Faith is a gift; a gift is meant to be used.
“And he believed the Lord; and the Lord reckoned it to him as righteousness.” Genesis 15:6
“You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was brought to completion by the works.” James 2:22

Apply: Choose one act this week through which your faith will be seen.

2. The open door of Rahab

  • A stranger who believed.
  • She risked her life and her house.
  • The least and the greatest justified alike.
“But Rahab the prostitute, with her family and all who belonged to her, Joshua spared. Her family has lived in Israel ever since. For she hid the messengers whom Joshua sent to spy out Jericho.” Joshua 6:25
“By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had received the spies in peace.” Hebrews 11:31

Apply: Open your door, your table or your time to one person who is a stranger to you.

3. Wings spread, children unwilling

  • The grief of Jesus over Jerusalem.
  • God's love never forces.
  • Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.
“he will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge; his faithfulness is a shield and buckler.” Psalm 91:4
“How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” Matthew 23:37

Apply: Each evening this week, ask the Lord to gather you and the people you love under his wings.

Faith is a gift of God, and it is alive when it opens the hand and the door.

The Lord is always the one who gathers; our part is to be willing.

Take-home resolution: This week, do one act that shows your faith, open a door to a stranger, and run to the Lord's wings each evening.

Full homily

Dear friends, today's Epistle asks a simple question: what is your faith worth if nobody can see it? Saint James says: “For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead” (James 2:26).

He gives two examples. Abraham believed God, and then he obeyed God even when it was very hard. Rahab was a stranger in Jericho, yet she believed in the God of Israel and she risked her life to save two messengers. Both were justified; both had a faith that acted.

We are saved by the grace of God that we receive in faith, and that faith is alive when it shows itself in love. The believer does not earn God's love, but the one who has received it cannot keep it locked inside.

In the Gospel Jesus grieves over Jerusalem: “How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” (Matthew 23:37). We know this picture from our own courtyards. Jesus is the one who would gather us; all that is asked of us is to be willing.

This week let us ask: where is my faith only words? Let us do one good deed that costs us something, and let us run under the Lord's wings each evening. Amen.

For children

Children, have you seen a mother hen when a kite flies over? She spreads her wings and all the chicks hurry underneath. Jesus said he wants to gather us like that, and be our safe place. And what do we do? We can run to him in prayer, and we can open the door to someone who needs help, like Rahab. That is faith that works!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you gathered your people under your wings, though they were not willing. Gather us today. Make our faith alive, like the faith of Abraham who obeyed and of Rahab who opened her door; let our hands do what our lips confess. Keep us from judging any people as you alone can judge, and teach us to grieve over the world as you grieved over Jerusalem. Welcome us when we come in your name, for you are blessed, you who come in the name of the Lord, now and for ever. Amen.
