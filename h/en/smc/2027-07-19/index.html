Readings

  • Epistle James 2:21–26 · Faith is brought to completion by works

Word for the day

“For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.” James 2:26

Reflection

The Season of Kaitha is the season of the harvest, and a harvest is judged by its fruit. Today's Epistle asks what the fruit of faith is. James places two very different people side by side: Abraham, the father of believers, and Rahab, a woman of Jericho whom the Bible calls a prostitute. “Was not our ancestor Abraham justified by works when he offered his son Isaac on the altar? You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was brought to completion by the works.” James is not contradicting Paul. Paul argues against those who thought they could earn God's favour by keeping the Law; James argues against those who thought that a belief held in the head, with a closed hand and a closed door, is enough. Both of them quote the same verse, “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (Genesis 15:6), and both know that faith is God's gift. But a gift received is a gift used. Abraham believed long before he climbed the mountain of Moriah; the climb was his faith made visible, and in that obedience, says James, faith “was brought to completion”. The Church teaches the same: faith is the beginning of salvation, and a faith that is not alive in hope and love does not unite us fully to Christ.

Then James names Rahab: “Likewise, was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she welcomed the messengers and sent them out by another road?” Her story is told in the Book of Joshua. Two spies come to Jericho; she hides them on her roof, confesses that “The Lord your God is indeed God in heaven above and on earth below” (Joshua 2:11), and asks that her family be spared. Her faith did not stay in words: she risked her life and her house. The Letter to the Hebrews also names her among the people of faith (11:31), and Matthew places her in the family line of the Messiah (Matthew 1:5). So the patriarch and the foreigner, the greatest and the least, are justified in the same way, by a living faith that opens the door. James closes with the sentence that gathers the whole passage: “For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.”

The Gospel takes us to the last teaching of Jesus in the Temple, after the seven woes. He speaks as a prophet of Israel who loves his people as only one of them could. “Therefore I send you prophets, sages, and scribes, some of whom you will kill and crucify, and some you will flog in your synagogues and pursue from town to town.” He points back over the whole long history of faithful witnesses, “from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah”, from the first righteous man killed in Genesis to the last in the order of the Hebrew Scriptures (2 Chronicles 24:20–21). The prophets whom he now sends are the apostles and martyrs whom Kaitha honours. These hard words are to be read as the Church reads them: as a warning to every generation, ours included, and not as a verdict on a people. The Church teaches that the Jewish people as a whole are not to be blamed for the death of the Lord (Catechism 597–598), and that God's call to them is irrevocable (Romans 11:29). Jesus, his mother and his first disciples were all Jews.

Then the tone turns from warning to tenderness: “Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” Every Kerala household knows the picture: at the first shadow of a kite in the sky the mother hen spreads her wings and the chicks run under them. The psalmist already said of God, “he will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge” (Psalm 91:4). It is not God who is unwilling; “you were not willing”. His love never forces. Yet the lament ends with a promise: “you will not see me again until you say, ‘Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.’” The words are from Psalm 118:26, which the crowds sing on Palm Sunday and which the Church repeats in the Sanctus of the Holy Qurbana. So we ask two questions today: what fruit does my faith bear, and have I let myself be gathered under his wings?

Full homily

Dear friends, today's Epistle asks a simple question: what is your faith worth if nobody can see it? Saint James says: “For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead” (James 2:26).

He gives two examples. Abraham believed God, and then he obeyed God even when it was very hard. Rahab was a stranger in Jericho, yet she believed in the God of Israel and she risked her life to save two messengers. Both were justified; both had a faith that acted.

We are saved by the grace of God that we receive in faith, and that faith is alive when it shows itself in love. The believer does not earn God's love, but the one who has received it cannot keep it locked inside.

In the Gospel Jesus grieves over Jerusalem: “How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” (Matthew 23:37). We know this picture from our own courtyards. Jesus is the one who would gather us; all that is asked of us is to be willing.

This week let us ask: where is my faith only words? Let us do one good deed that costs us something, and let us run under the Lord's wings each evening. Amen.

For children

Children, have you seen a mother hen when a kite flies over? She spreads her wings and all the chicks hurry underneath. Jesus said he wants to gather us like that, and be our safe place. And what do we do? We can run to him in prayer, and we can open the door to someone who needs help, like Rahab. That is faith that works!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you gathered your people under your wings, though they were not willing. Gather us today. Make our faith alive, like the faith of Abraham who obeyed and of Rahab who opened her door; let our hands do what our lips confess. Keep us from judging any people as you alone can judge, and teach us to grieve over the world as you grieved over Jerusalem. Welcome us when we come in your name, for you are blessed, you who come in the name of the Lord, now and for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what God sees when he looks at his people. James looks for a faith that has become visible in deeds, as Abraham’s did on the mountain and Rahab’s did at her door. In Matthew, Jesus looks at Jerusalem, the city he loves, and speaks with the grief of a mother hen whose chicks will not come under her wings. Faith that is alive moves toward God and toward neighbour; love that is refused still goes on calling.

James 2:21–26 · Faith brought to completion

Setting.

James writes to Jewish Christians scattered among the nations (1:1). In 2:14–20 he has asked what good it is to say “I have faith” when there are no deeds, for example when a brother or sister is left hungry and cold. These verses give two examples from Scripture: Abraham, the father of faith, and Rahab, the foreign woman of Jericho.

James is not arguing against Paul. Paul speaks of the works of the law, such as circumcision, as a way to claim a place among God’s people (Galatians 2:16). James speaks of works of love, such as mercy to the poor, which show that faith is real. Both agree that faith in Christ is the root; they look at different dangers.

Structure.

  • v. 21: Abraham and the offering of Isaac (Genesis 22).
  • v. 22: faith and works work together, and faith is brought to completion.
  • v. 23: Genesis 15:6 is fulfilled; Abraham is called the friend of God.
  • vv. 24–25: the conclusion, and the second example, Rahab (Joshua 2).
  • v. 26: the picture of the body and the spirit.

Key words.

  • dikaioō, to declare righteous, to justify: In the Greek Old Testament this verb is used for God’s verdict that a person stands rightly before him. James uses it of the verdict that is shown by a life, and Paul of the gift God gives. The one word is used with different emphasis.
  • ergon, work, deed: In James a work is not a way of earning God’s favour but what faith does when it is alive: welcoming, feeding, risking oneself for others.
  • teleioō, to bring to completion: The faith was “brought to completion”. Faith is a seed that becomes fruit; the fruit does not replace the seed.

Verse by verse.

“Was not our ancestor Abraham justified by works when he offered his son Isaac on the altar?” (2:21)

Abraham’s obedience in Genesis 22 came long after the word of Genesis 15:6. James shows that the trust God first reckoned as righteousness was later proved in the hardest of tests. Abraham is “our ancestor” for Jews and for all who share his faith.

“You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was brought to completion by the works.” (2:22)

Faith is the root, works the fruit, and the two belong to one tree. The Church has always read the verse so.

“and he was called the friend of God.” (2:23)

The title comes from Isaiah 41:8 and 2 Chronicles 20:7. A friend is trusted with God’s plans, and Abraham is the friend because he trusted God completely.

“Likewise, was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she welcomed the messengers and sent them out by another road?” (2:25)

After the patriarch, a woman of the nations, once outside Israel. She is also named in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5) and among the heroes of faith (Hebrews 11:31).

“For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.” (2:26)

A faith that never acts is not a sleeping faith but a dead one, as a body without breath. The saying needs no other explanation.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith is a gift of God and a supernatural virtue, and that this gift remains in one who has not sinned against it, but “faith apart from works is dead” (James 2:26), and without hope and love it does not unite the believer fully to Christ (CCC 1815). The Church teaches that we are justified by grace through faith, not by merits of our own (CCC 1987–1992 and 2010), and that this grace is meant to bear fruit in love. Charity is the “form” of the other virtues (CCC 1827).

Matthew 23:34–39 · Jerusalem, Jerusalem

Setting.

This is the last public teaching of Jesus in Matthew before the Passion. In Matthew 23 he speaks to the crowds and his disciples about the scribes and Pharisees who “sit on Moses’ seat” (23:2). He first says that their teaching is to be heeded where it is faithful to Moses, then speaks seven woes against hypocrisy, which is a danger for every teacher of religion in every age, and then, in these verses, he turns to lament over the whole city.

Jesus himself is a Jew speaking to Jews, in the manner of the prophets, who often spoke with harsh words to their own people because they loved them. The words are not a judgment on any people as a whole. The Church teaches that what happened in Christ’s passion cannot be blamed on all Jews then living, nor on Jews today (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

Structure.

  • v. 34: Jesus sends prophets, sages and scribes, and they meet rejection.
  • vv. 35–36: the line of the righteous slain, from Abel to Zechariah.
  • v. 37: the lament over Jerusalem, with the image of the hen and her brood.
  • vv. 38–39: the house left desolate, and the promise of a return with the words of Psalm 118.

Key words.

  • episynagō, to gather together: The verb of a shepherd gathering the flock, or of exiles being brought home. Jesus wants to gather Jerusalem’s children as God long promised (Isaiah 40:11; Jeremiah 31:10).
  • erēmos, desolate, abandoned: Used in the prophets for a house or city that has lost God’s protection (Jeremiah 22:5). It is the opposite of the gathering of verse 37.
  • eulogēmenos ho erchomenos, blessed is the one who comes: From Psalm 118:26, the pilgrims’ greeting sung at the Temple gates. The crowds sang it at the entry into Jerusalem (Matthew 21:9).

Verse by verse.

“from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah, whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar.” (23:35)

Abel is the first righteous victim in Genesis 4. Zechariah is probably the priest killed in the Temple court in 2 Chronicles 24:20–22, the last book of the Hebrew Scriptures: from the first book to the last. Matthew calls him “son of Barachiah”, while Chronicles calls the priest the son of Jehoiada; scholars explain the difference in several ways, and none is certain.

“How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” (23:37)

One of the tenderest images in the Gospels. The Old Testament often speaks of God’s wings as shelter (Psalm 91:4; Ruth 2:12). Jesus speaks as God speaks, and with God’s longing. “You were not willing” is the sorrow of love that cannot force a reply.

“See, your house is left to you, desolate.” (23:38)

Many see here the sign of the Temple’s coming fall (Matthew 24:2). It is a warning spoken in grief, not in triumph.

“you will not see me again until you say, ‘Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord.’” (23:39)

The saying ends in hope: a day will come when Jerusalem welcomes him. Paul, too, speaks of the final salvation of Israel (Romans 11:25–32).

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always heard Jesus’ lament as the voice of divine love, never of hatred. At the Second Vatican Council she taught that the Jews remain very dear to God, who does not repent of the gifts he has made nor of the call he has issued (Nostra Aetate 4, citing Romans 11:28–29); Christians must never speak of them as rejected or accursed. The Catechism adds that sinners, not any one people, were the authors and the instruments of Christ’s sufferings (CCC 598). Luke records the same grief when Jesus weeps over the city as he approaches it (Luke 19:41–44).

Questions people ask

Do James and Paul contradict each other on justification?

No. Paul argues against the idea that keeping the works of the Mosaic law, such as circumcision, can win a place with God; James argues against the idea that a faith with no deeds of love can save. The Catholic Church holds both: we are justified by God’s grace through faith in Christ, and this faith must work through love (Galatians 5:6; CCC 1814–1815).

For study and teaching

  • James says that faith is brought to completion by works. Where in my week does what I believe become something people can see?
  • Jesus grieves over Jerusalem because love was refused. How do I respond when someone I love will not come back, and what can I do other than pray?
