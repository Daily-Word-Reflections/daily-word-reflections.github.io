Readings

  • Gospel Luke 11:29–32 · The sign of Jonah

Word for the day

“For just as Jonah became a sign to the people of Nineveh, so the Son of Man will be to this generation.” Luke 11:30

Reflection

The crowds around Jesus keep growing, and with the crowds grows the demand for proof. They want a sign from heaven, something so overwhelming that no one could doubt. Jesus refuses: “This generation is an evil generation; it asks for a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of Jonah.” Then he explains: “For just as Jonah became a sign to the people of Nineveh, so the Son of Man will be to this generation.” What was the sign of Jonah? A prophet who went down into the depths, swallowed up as if dead, and came back after three days to preach. Matthew spells it out: “For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth” (Matthew 12:40). We read this Gospel four weeks after Easter, and so we hear it as the disciples heard it after the Resurrection: the sign has been given. The tomb is empty. There will be no other sign greater than this one, and no sign is needed beyond it. The Church still meets people who ask for a proof that would force them to believe. She can only point, as Jesus did, to the Crucified One who rose on the third day, and to the lives of those who have met him.

Jesus then names two foreigners who will rise at the judgment and put his hearers to shame. The queen of the South travelled from the ends of the earth just to listen to Solomon's wisdom; the people of Nineveh, a pagan city, “repented at the proclamation of Jonah”. And the refrain comes twice: “see, something greater than Solomon is here!”, “see, something greater than Jonah is here!” Jesus does not speak against his own people; he speaks as a prophet of Israel, calling his generation to listen as Nineveh listened. Every generation, ours included, is in the same place. Our Syro-Malabar Church keeps the memory of Nineveh alive every year in the Moonnu Nombu, the three days' fast of the Ninevites before the Great Fast, when the faithful flock to Kuravilangad and to many other churches and fast with the hope of the people who believed a preacher of repentance. Today, in the bright weeks of Easter, the same story speaks of joy rather than sackcloth: if Nineveh changed at the voice of Jonah, how much more should we change at the voice of the One who came back from the dead, greater than Jonah and greater than Solomon.

Peter writes to Christians who are discovering that the Risen Lord does not take them out of trouble. “Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal that is taking place among you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you.” Suffering for Christ is not a sign that something has gone wrong; it is part of belonging to him. “But rejoice insofar as you are sharing Christ's sufferings, so that you may also be glad and shout for joy when his glory is revealed.” The joy of Easter is not cancelled by the Cross; it shines through it. Peter is precise: there is no glory in suffering for doing wrong, but “if any of you suffers as a Christian, do not consider it a disgrace, but glorify God because you bear this name.” The name Christian, first given at Antioch (Acts 11:26), was perhaps meant as mockery; the martyrs wore it as a crown. The fiery ordeal comes in many sizes: the student laughed at for making the sign of the Cross before a meal, the employee passed over for refusing to falsify an account, the believers in many lands who lose homes and lives for the faith. To all of them Peter gives one instruction: “let those suffering in accordance with God's will entrust themselves to a faithful Creator, while continuing to do good.” Entrust, and keep doing good. That is how the sign of Jonah is lived in an ordinary Christian life: going down into trouble without losing faith, and coming up again with the light of the Resurrection.

Full homily

Dear friends, the crowds around Jesus wanted a sign, a great proof from heaven. Jesus said: no sign will be given to this generation except the sign of Jonah (Luke 11:29).

Jonah was three days in the belly of the great fish, and then came out and preached. Jesus was three days in the heart of the earth, and then rose. We are in the season of the Resurrection: the sign has been given. The tomb is empty.

The people of Nineveh, a pagan city, repented at the preaching of Jonah. The queen of the South came from the ends of the earth to hear Solomon. And Jesus says: something greater than Jonah is here, something greater than Solomon is here (Luke 11:31–32). Our Church remembers Nineveh every year in the Moonnu Nombu. In this Easter season, let the Risen Lord change us as Jonah's voice changed Nineveh.

Peter tells the first Christians not to be surprised at the fiery ordeal. If you suffer as a Christian, do not be ashamed, but glorify God because you bear this name (1 Peter 4:12, 16). Rejoice that you share the sufferings of Christ, for you will also share his glory.

When trouble comes for our faith, let us do what Peter says: entrust ourselves to the faithful Creator and keep on doing good (1 Peter 4:19). That is the sign of Jonah lived in our own life. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, do you know the story of Jonah? He was inside a big fish for three days, and then he came out! Jesus said he would be like Jonah: three days in the tomb, and then he would rise. And he did! When you see a picture of Jonah and the fish, remember: Jesus is alive!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you went down into the heart of the earth and rose on the third day: you are the sign of Jonah given to every generation. Forgive us for asking for more proof when you have already given us yourself. Give us the humility of Nineveh to repent at your word, and the courage of your martyrs to bear your name without shame. When the fiery ordeal comes, teach us to entrust ourselves to the faithful Creator and to go on doing good, until your glory is revealed and we shout for joy. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings speak of suffering and judgment, and both point to the Resurrection. Peter tells Christians not to be surprised by the fiery ordeal, because to share Christ’s sufferings is to share his glory. In Luke, Jesus refuses the crowd a sign on demand and offers only the sign of Jonah: a preacher whose word brought a pagan city to repentance. In this season we know what that sign became: the Son of Man given back from death.

1 Peter 4:12–19 · Do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal

Setting.

1 Peter is written in Peter’s name from “Babylon” (5:13), a Christian code name for Rome, to believers scattered across Asia Minor (1:1). Many scholars think it was written by a disciple in Peter’s name after his death, others that it comes from Peter through Silvanus (5:12). Its readers suffer insults and pressure because they no longer share the life of their neighbours (4:4). The letter does not describe an organised empire-wide persecution, but the slow fire of being despised for Christ.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: do not be surprised; rejoice to share Christ’s sufferings.
  • vv. 14–16: blessed if reviled for the name; but never suffer as a wrongdoer.
  • vv. 17–18: judgment begins with the household of God.
  • v. 19: entrust yourselves to a faithful Creator, and keep doing good.

Key words.

  • pyrōsis, fiery ordeal: Literally a burning, the fire of the smelter. The letter has already said that faith is tested like gold in the fire (1:7). The fire purifies; it does not destroy.
  • Christianos, Christian: One of only three places in the New Testament where the name appears (also Acts 11:26; 26:28). It was probably first a nickname given by outsiders. Peter tells believers to carry it with honour.
  • ktistēs, Creator: Only here in the New Testament is God called by this title. The one who made us will not abandon what he made.

Verse by verse.

“Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal that is taking place among you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you.” (4:12)

Suffering for Christ is not a sign that something has gone wrong. The Master was treated so, and the disciple is not above the master.

“But let none of you suffer as a murderer, a thief, a criminal, or even as a mischief maker.” (4:15)

An honest warning. Not every hardship a Christian meets is suffering for Christ; some comes from our own faults. The word translated “mischief maker” probably means a busybody who meddles in others’ affairs.

“Therefore, let those suffering in accordance with God’s will entrust themselves to a faithful Creator, while continuing to do good.” (4:19)

The verb is the one Jesus used on the Cross: “Father, into your hands I commend my spirit” (Luke 23:46). The Christian answer to unjust suffering is trust and continued goodness, not revenge.

How the Church reads it.

Verse 18 quotes Proverbs 11:31 in its Greek form. The Second Vatican Council teaches that martyrdom, by which the disciple is made like the Master, is regarded by the Church as an exceptional gift and the highest proof of love (Lumen Gentium 42). The Church today remembers that in many lands Christians still bear the name at great cost, and she prays for them and for those who wrong them.

Luke 11:29–32 · Something greater than Jonah is here

Setting.

Some in the crowd have just said that Jesus casts out demons by Beelzebul, and others, to test him, asked for a sign from heaven (11:15–16). Jesus now answers that second group. The crowds are growing, but numbers are not faith.

Structure.

  • v. 29: an evil generation asks for a sign; only the sign of Jonah will be given.
  • v. 30: as Jonah to Nineveh, so the Son of Man to this generation.
  • vv. 31–32: two witnesses from among the nations, the queen of the South and the people of Nineveh, will rise at the judgment.

Key words.

  • sēmeion, sign: In Luke the sign is Jonah himself, a prophet whose preaching called to repentance. Matthew adds the three days and nights in the belly of the fish (Matthew 12:40), which makes the link to the Resurrection explicit.
  • genea, generation: Not a people or a race, but the people of a time who see and do not respond. The warning reaches every generation that hears the Gospel, ours included.
  • pleion, something greater: The Greek is neuter: not only “someone” but “something” greater. With Jesus the whole Kingdom of God has come near, greater than Solomon’s wisdom and Jonah’s preaching.

Verse by verse.

“it asks for a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of Jonah.” (11:29)

Jesus will not perform on demand. The signs he gives call for conversion, not for applause.

“The queen of the South will rise at the judgment with the people of this generation and condemn them,” (11:31)

The queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10:1–10) travelled far to hear Solomon. A foreign queen and a pagan city become the examples of openness. Those who seemed far away listened best.

“they repented at the proclamation of Jonah, and see, something greater than Jonah is here!” (11:32)

Nineveh repented after one short sermon from a reluctant prophet (Jonah 3:4–5). How much more should we listen to the Son himself?

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers read the sign of Jonah as the Resurrection, following Matthew, and the Easter season gives this Gospel its full meaning: the greatest sign given to the world is the Son of Man risen from the dead. Our Syro-Malabar tradition holds the people of Nineveh in honour: before the Great Fast it keeps the three-day Rogation of the Ninevites, the Moonnu Nombu, asking for the same repentance that saved that city.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus condemn the Jewish people as an “evil generation”?

No. Jesus speaks as the prophets of Israel spoke, calling his own people to conversion, and the warning is addressed to all who hear and do not respond, in every age. The Church teaches that the Jewish people may not be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598). The sign of Jonah is offered to us, and we are the generation now being asked.

For study and teaching

  • When have I suffered because of my own faults, and when for Christ? How do I tell the difference?
  • The queen of the South travelled far to hear wisdom. What effort do I make to hear the word of the One greater than Solomon?
