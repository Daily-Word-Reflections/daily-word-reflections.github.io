Word for the day

“When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Son, your sins are forgiven.”” Mark 2:5

Reflection

Paul's picture in the Epistle is taken from the Law of Moses: “Do not be mismatched with unbelievers.” The Greek word means to be yoked unequally, like an ox and a donkey under one yoke, which the Law of Moses forbids (Deuteronomy 22:10). Then follows a series of questions, each with the answer no: “what partnership is there between righteousness and lawlessness? Or what fellowship is there between light and darkness? What agreement does Christ have with Beliar? Or what does a believer share with an unbeliever? What agreement has the temple of God with idols?” In Corinth the practical question was sharing in the feasts held in pagan temples, with their sacrifices to idols and their immorality. Paul is not telling Christians to cut off their neighbours; in the first letter he says that then they would have to go out of the world (1 Corinthians 5:10). He asks them to refuse partnership in idolatry and sin.

In India, where Christians live among neighbours of many faiths, this needs care. The Church “rejects nothing that is true and holy” in other religions and urges respect, friendship and dialogue with them (Nostra Aetate 2). What we refuse is not a neighbour but a yoke: the bribe, the cruelty, the lie, the unclean practice, anything that would put another master in the place of Christ. Paul's reason is a gift before it is a command: “For we are the temple of the living God; as God said, ‘I will live in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.’” God walked among Israel in the tent and the Temple, as he walked with Adam in the garden. Now he lives in us, in the Church and in each baptised person. A temple is not a place where everything is permitted; it is a place that belongs to God. The reading ends in a promise of tenderness: “I will welcome you, and I will be your father, and you shall be my sons and daughters, says the Lord Almighty.”

The Gospel is one of the most vivid scenes in Mark. Jesus is “at home” in Capernaum, and the house is so full that there is no room even at the door. Four men arrive carrying a paralysed friend. They cannot get through the crowd, so they climb the outside stairs, dig through the roof, made of beams, branches and clay, and lower the man on his mat to the feet of Jesus. We do not know their names, only that they would not give up. Mark says: “When Jesus saw their faith.” It is their faith, and the paralytic's, that Jesus sees. Here is a mystery that every parish knows: someone is carried to Jesus by the faith of others, by a mother's prayer, a friend's visit, a catechist, a neighbour who sits by a sickbed.

Jesus does not begin with the healing of the legs. “Son, your sins are forgiven.” The man had come for his body; Jesus first gives him what he needs most, and calls him “Son”. Some scribes, sitting there, think: “It is blasphemy! Who can forgive sins but God alone?” Their premise is correct, and they ask honestly; only God can forgive sins. Jesus answers by doing the visible thing to prove the invisible: “But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins… I say to you, stand up, take your mat and go to your home.” The man stands, takes his mat and walks out before all of them, and all are amazed: “We have never seen anything like this!” Jesus forgives as God forgives, and this authority has been given to his Church in the sacrament of Reconciliation (John 20:23). On this Saturday, when many families remember the Mother of the Lord, we remember that she is the one who, at Cana, brought the need of others to her Son; she is the best of all those who carry friends to Jesus.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel four friends carry a paralysed man to Jesus. The house is full, so they climb up, make a hole in the roof, and let their friend down. We do not know their names. But Mark says: “When Jesus saw their faith” (Mark 2:5). Faith can carry another person.

And what does Jesus say to the man? He does not first say, ‘Walk.’ He says, “Son, your sins are forgiven.” The man had come for his legs, but Jesus gave him what he needed most. Only God can forgive sins, and Jesus does it, and proves it by making the man walk.

This forgiveness is given to us in the sacrament of Reconciliation. If we have been away for a long time, today's Gospel tells us that there is no hole in the roof too hard to make. The Lord is waiting.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians that we are “the temple of the living God” (2 Corinthians 6:16). God lives in us. So we refuse everything that is not worthy of his dwelling: lies, cheating, impurity, idols. At the same time we respect and love our neighbours of every faith.

On this Saturday, let us ask Mary, the Mother of Jesus, to teach us to be like the four friends: to carry others to her Son. Let us name one person today and carry them in prayer. Amen.

For children

Children, do you remember the four friends who made a hole in the roof? They loved their friend so much that they did not give up. Jesus saw how much they believed and made the man walk. You can also carry your friends to Jesus: when a friend is sick or sad, say a prayer for him. And remember, Jesus lives in you like in a little church, so be kind and clean in your words and deeds.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you see the faith of those who carry others to you, and you say to the weak: Son, your sins are forgiven. Make us temples of the living God, clean from every idol, and give us the grace to refuse every partnership with sin. Teach us to carry our friends, our families and our parish to you, and never to give up when the crowd is in the way. Heal us in body and soul, and give us the courage to come to the sacrament of your mercy. Through the prayer of your Mother, who brings the needs of her children to you, make us your dwelling place, for you are God for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the house where God lives and who may come in. In Second Corinthians Paul tells the Christians of Corinth that they are the temple of the living God, and so they must not share in the worship of idols. In Mark, a house in Capernaum is so crowded that four friends tear open the roof to bring a paralysed man to Jesus, and the Lord forgives his sins before he heals his body. The first reading says where God dwells, among his people; the second shows that the Lord who dwells among us is the one who has authority to forgive.

2 Corinthians 6:14–18 · We are the temple of the living God

Setting.

Corinth was a busy port full of temples, and Christians there were pressed to join in public meals at pagan shrines and feasts (see 1 Corinthians 8 and 10). In this passage Paul asks the community to keep the worship of the one God undivided.

Many scholars notice that this short passage (6:14–7:1) interrupts the flow between 6:13 and 7:2, and that its language has similarities with the writings found at Qumran. Some think Paul took up an earlier text; others think it is his own. The Church receives it as Scripture in either case. Paul is not asking Christians to avoid their neighbours. In another letter he says that to do so they would need to leave the world (1 Corinthians 5:9–10); the demand here is not to share in idol worship.

Structure.

  • v. 14: do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers: four contrasts, righteousness and lawlessness, light and darkness.
  • v. 15: Christ and Beliar; believer and unbeliever.
  • v. 16: the temple of God and idols; “we are the temple of the living God”, with a promise from the Old Testament.
  • vv. 17–18: come out and be separate; God will be a Father to his sons and daughters.

Key words.

  • heterozygountes, mismatched, unequally yoked: From the yoke of animals of different kinds. The law of Moses did not allow an ox and a donkey to be yoked together (Deuteronomy 22:10). The picture is of a team pulling in two directions.
  • Beliar, Beliar: A form of Belial, a Jewish name for the evil one, used here for the power opposed to Christ.
  • naos, temple, sanctuary: The inner sanctuary where God dwells. The whole community, not only the building, is God’s dwelling place.

Verse by verse.

“Do not be mismatched with unbelievers. For what partnership is there between righteousness and lawlessness?” (6:14)

Paul’s point is about shared worship and loyalty, not about daily life with neighbours. Christians do not stop being good neighbours; they cannot share in worship that denies the one God.

“For we are the temple of the living God; as God said, “I will live in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.” (6:16)

Paul joins words of Leviticus 26:11–12 and Ezekiel 37:27. The promise God made to Israel, to dwell among his people, now reaches its fullness: God dwells in the community of believers by his Spirit.

“and I will be your father, and you shall be my sons and daughters, says the Lord Almighty.”” (6:18)

The last words echo God’s promise to David’s son (2 Samuel 7:14), now extended to “sons and daughters”. Separation is not an end; it is made for the sake of belonging to the Father.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that by Baptism the Christian becomes a member of Christ and a temple of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1265). It warns that idolatry is not only the worship of pagan gods but a constant temptation to the faith, whenever a person puts something in the place of God, such as money, power or pleasure (CCC 2113–2114). The Church also teaches respect for all that is true and holy in other religions and urges Christians to dialogue and cooperation with their followers (Nostra Aetate 2). Both belong together: undivided worship of the one God, and respect and friendship with all.

Mark 2:1–12 · Son, your sins are forgiven

Setting.

Jesus has left the synagogue of Capernaum and healed Peter’s mother-in-law and many others (1:21–34), and then healed a leper (1:40–45). He now returns to Capernaum, most probably to the house of Peter (1:29). Houses in Galilee had flat roofs of beams, branches and packed earth, reached by an outside stair, which makes the friends’ action possible.

This is the first of five conflict stories in Mark (2:1–3:6) in which Jesus meets objection from some scribes. The scribes were experts in the Law, and their question is a fair one: only God can forgive sins. Mark invites the reader to see that Jesus is more than they think.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Jesus at home in Capernaum, speaking the word to a crowd that fills the house and the doorway.
  • vv. 3–4: four men carry a paralysed man; they open the roof and let him down.
  • v. 5: Jesus sees their faith and says, “Son, your sins are forgiven.”
  • vv. 6–9: the scribes question in their hearts; Jesus asks which is easier to say.
  • vv. 10–12: “the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”; the man rises, takes his mat and goes; all glorify God.

Key words.

  • exousia, authority: Not only power but the right to act. In Mark the crowds notice Jesus’ authority in teaching, over spirits, over disease, and here over sin (1:22, 27).
  • aphientai, are forgiven: The passive form is often used to avoid naming God: God forgives. Jesus speaks and acts in God’s name, and yet in his own person.
  • ho huios tou anthrōpou, the Son of Man: The title Jesus uses of himself, from the vision in Daniel 7:13–14 of one like a son of man who receives dominion from God. Here it is used on earth, in a house, over a mat.

Verse by verse.

“And when they could not bring him to Jesus because of the crowd, they removed the roof above him; and after having dug through it, they let down the mat on which the paralytic lay.” (2:4)

The friends do not give up when the door is closed. Their love is stubborn and costly. Jesus does not scold them for the damage; he praises their faith.

“When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Son, your sins are forgiven.”” (2:5)

Jesus sees the faith of those who carry and forgives first, as if the deepest need were not the legs but the heart. The word “Son” is tender: he is received as a child of God.

““Why does this fellow speak in this way? It is blasphemy! Who can forgive sins but God alone?”” (2:7)

The scribes are right in what they say about God. Only God can forgive sins. They do not yet see that God is at work in Jesus.

““I say to you, stand up, take your mat and go to your home.”” (2:11)

The visible healing is a sign of the invisible forgiveness. The man who was carried now carries his own mat.

“so that they were all amazed and glorified God, saying, “We have never seen anything like this!”” (2:12)

The story ends with praise of God. A healing that makes people glorify God is a healing in the way of Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that only God forgives sins, and that Jesus, as Son of God, says of himself, “The Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”, and gives this power to men to exercise in his name (CCC 1441–1442). The Church sees in this story the sacrament of Penance and Reconciliation, in which Christ forgives through his ministers, and also the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick, where forgiveness and healing are joined (CCC 1503–1505). The four friends have been read by many as a picture of the Church’s intercession: we are carried by the faith of others, and we carry others (in substance, a common reading of the Fathers).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus forgive sins first, when the man had come to be healed?

In the way Jesus sees, the sickness of the body is not the first need. Forgiveness opens the person to God. At the same time, Jesus does not leave the body untouched: the healing is the sign that the forgiveness is real. The Church keeps this order in the sacraments: grace comes through visible signs for the whole person.

For study and teaching

  • Paul asks Christians to keep their worship undivided. What in my life could take the place that belongs to God alone, and how can I keep the door open to my neighbours with kindness?
  • Four friends carried the paralysed man and broke through a roof. For whom could I do something costly in faith, by prayer, by visiting, by helping?
