Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” John 12:24

Reflection

Today the Church in Kerala, and with her the whole Church in India, keeps the feast of a daughter of our own soil. Anna Muttathupadathu was born on 19 August 1910 at Kudamaloor, in the Kottayam district, in a Syro-Malabar Catholic family. Her mother died when she was a few months old, and she was brought up by her mother's sister, at Muttuchira. She grew into a girl who loved prayer and who longed to belong wholly to Christ. When a marriage was being arranged for her, her biographers tell us, she did a terrible thing out of desperate love: she stepped into a pit of burning chaff, so as to be unfit for marriage, and she carried the injury to her feet for life. In 1927 she entered the convent of the Franciscan Clarist Congregation at Bharananganam, took the name Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception, and lived there a hidden life of prayer, teaching and humble work. Illness followed illness, and she bore them with a quiet smile. She died on 28 July 1946, at the age of thirty-five. Pope John Paul II beatified her at Kottayam on 8 February 1986, and Pope Benedict XVI canonised her in Rome on 12 October 2008. She is the first woman of Indian origin to be declared a saint, and the first canonised saint of the Syro-Malabar Church. Her tomb in the church at Bharananganam is visited by pilgrims of every faith, and the Syro-Malabar Church calls her Alphonsamma, as one calls a mother or an aunt.

The readings of her feast give the secret of her life. The first is from Deuteronomy: “The Lord will establish you as his holy people, as he has sworn to you, if you keep the commandments of the Lord your God and walk in his ways. All the peoples of the earth shall see that you are called by the name of the Lord.” A people consecrated to God is a visible sign to the nations; Alphonsa, in a small cell, became such a sign for Kerala and for the world. The same chapter promises blessing on the barns and on the land, and the reading closes with the rain in its season: “The Lord will open for you his rich storehouse, the heavens, to give the rain of your land in its season and to bless all your undertakings.” In July, in the monsoon, we see the sky open, and we remember that this reading is about a God who gives from heaven the gift that no field can produce for itself. Alphonsa never had a field to sow, and yet her life bore the harvest of a whole Church.

Isaiah's fourth song of the Servant goes to the heart: “Yet it was the will of the Lord to crush him with pain. When you make his life an offering for sin, he shall see his offspring, and shall prolong his days; through him the will of the Lord shall prosper. Out of his anguish he shall see light; he shall find satisfaction through his knowledge. The righteous one, my servant, shall make many righteous, and he shall bear their iniquities.” The Church reads the song in the light of Jesus, the Servant who gave himself for the many. Suffering by itself saves no one; only the suffering of Christ, offered in love, has redeeming power. But he lets his friends share in it. Saint Paul writes: “I am now rejoicing in my sufferings for your sake, and in my flesh I am completing what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church.” Alphonsa understood this. She did not look for pain, and she asked for healing as anyone would; but when the suffering came, she joined it to the cross of the Lord, and from her cell she prayed for the Church and for sinners. Her suffering was not a failure, but a kind of fruitfulness.

In the letter to the Galatians, Paul gives the words that every saint could say: “I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me. And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me.” That is what holiness means: Christ living in a person who has let go of herself. Alphonsa had nothing to boast of in the eyes of the world. She was a sick sister in a convent, who taught little children for a short time, who was often unable to work, and who died young. And yet, “it is Christ who lives in me.” The Gospel gives the picture that fits her best. Some Greeks come to Philip and say, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” Jesus answers with a parable about a grain of wheat: “unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit. Those who love their life lose it, and those who hate their life in this world will keep it for eternal life. Whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am, there will my servant be also.” Alphonsa was a grain that fell into the ground of Bharananganam. In 1946 it seemed that a young nun had died unnoticed. Today the fruit is a shrine that receives thousands of pilgrims, and countless people in hospitals, at home and in prison who have learnt from her that suffering offered with love is not wasted.

The weekday readings add a word of encouragement. Paul says to the Corinthians: “he was crucified in weakness, but lives by the power of God. For we are weak in him, but in dealing with you we will live with him by the power of God.” Weakness and power are not enemies in the Christian life: the cross is where God's power is hidden. In the Gospel Jesus goes about the villages, and when he sees the crowds, “he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” Then he says to the disciples: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” We are in Kaitha, the season of harvest. Alphonsa was one of those laborers whom the Lord sent into his field, though from a sickbed. Let us ask the Lord today to send more of them, women and men who love the poor and the sick, and let each of us ask what part of the harvest is ours: a sick neighbour to visit, a child to teach, a pain to offer.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the feast of a daughter of our own land, Saint Alphonsa. Anna was born in 1910 at Kudamaloor, near Kottayam. Her mother died when she was a baby, and she grew up with her mother's sister. She loved prayer. She longed to belong to Jesus. In 1927 she entered the convent of the Clarist sisters at Bharananganam, where she took the name Alphonsa. She was often ill, and she died on 28 July 1946, when she was only thirty-five. In 2008 Pope Benedict XVI declared her a saint, the first woman of Indian origin to be so honoured.

What made her a saint? The Gospel today gives the answer. Some Greeks come to Philip and say, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” And Jesus says: “unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit” (John 12:24). A grain that stays in the bag is safe, but it gives no harvest. A grain that falls into the earth seems to disappear, but it gives life to many.

Alphonsa was such a grain. She lived in a small convent, she was often sick, she did simple tasks. Nobody in the world noticed her. But she gave everything, her pain and her prayer, to Jesus. Today thousands come to pray at her tomb, and millions know her name. Saint Paul says the same: “I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me” (Galatians 2:19–20).

Let us not misunderstand. Suffering is not good in itself; we should treat the sick and remove pain as far as we can. But when suffering comes, and it comes to every home, we can join it to the cross of Jesus. Saint Paul says: “in my flesh I am completing what is lacking in Christ’s afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church” (Colossians 1:24). Our pain, offered in love, is never wasted.

We are in the season of Kaitha, the season of harvest. Jesus looks at the crowds and says: “The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few” (Matthew 9:37). Saint Alphonsa was one of the laborers, though she worked from a sickbed. Today let us pray for more laborers, let us visit someone who is sick or alone, and let us offer one small pain for someone we love. Saint Alphonsa, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, today is the feast of Saint Alphonsa. She was a little girl from Kerala who lost her mother as a baby, and she loved Jesus very much. When she was sick or when something hurt, she did not complain, and she gave it to Jesus. Jesus said that a seed must fall into the earth to grow into a plant. Alphonsa was like a seed, and she gave a lot of love to the world. When something is hard for you, say: ‘Jesus, I give this to you.’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, grain of wheat fallen into the earth, you died and rose to bear much fruit. We thank you for Saint Alphonsa, your faithful daughter of Kerala, who offered her suffering in love for the Church and for sinners. Grant, through her prayers, that all who are sick, lonely or in pain may find strength, and may offer what they bear with your cross. Send more laborers into your harvest, sisters, priests and lay people who love the poor. Teach us to die to ourselves so that you may live in us. Let the grain that fell at Bharananganam bear fruit in every home of our land, and let all our people come to see you. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today the Church of the Syro-Malabar rite keeps the memory of St Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception, a Franciscan Clarist sister of Kerala who died on this day in 1946, and gives her four readings alongside the readings of the weekday. The weekday readings already prepare the theme: Paul, defending his ministry, says that Christ “was crucified in weakness, but lives by the power of God”, and that we share in that weakness and in that life; Jesus, moved with pity for the crowds like sheep without a shepherd, asks his disciples to pray for labourers for the harvest and then sends them out. The saint’s readings read as a single story. Deuteronomy tells of the blessing God promises to a people who keep his covenant. Isaiah describes the Servant who pours himself out for the many, and who, through that offering, sees his offspring and the will of the Lord prospers in his hand. Paul says what that means for the believer: “I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me.” And in John, Jesus tells the Greeks who wish to see him that the grain of wheat must fall into the earth and die to bear much fruit. Alphonsa lived this word. Her life was hidden, brief and marked by long suffering, and it has borne fruit in the hearts of many.

2 Corinthians 13:1–4 · Crucified in weakness, alive by the power of God

Setting.

These are among the last words of the letter. Paul announces his third visit to Corinth and warns that he will not be lenient with those who have sinned and not repented. The principle “any charge must be sustained by the evidence of two or three witnesses” is quoted from Deuteronomy 19:15, the same rule Jesus gives for the Church (Matthew 18:16). Some in Corinth have asked for proof that Christ speaks in Paul. He answers by pointing to the Cross: Christ himself seemed weak, and yet he lives by God’s power.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: third visit; the law of two or three witnesses; a warning to those who have sinned.
  • v. 3: you ask for proof that Christ speaks in me; he is not weak toward you but powerful in you.
  • v. 4: he was crucified in weakness but lives by the power of God; we are weak in him and will live with him.

Key words.

  • astheneia, weakness: The word runs through 2 Corinthians (11:30; 12:9–10). For Paul weakness is not shameful: it is the place where God’s power appears.
  • dynamis theou, power of God: The power that raised Jesus from the dead. It is the same power at work in the apostle and in the Church.

Verse by verse.

“For he was crucified in weakness, but lives by the power of God.” (13:4)

The Cross and the Resurrection are not opposites. The weakness of Good Friday and the life of Easter are one act of God’s love.

“For we are weak in him, but in dealing with you we will live with him by the power of God.” (13:4)

The disciple shares both. Paul’s own frailty does not cancel his authority; it is the form in which Christ’s power is exercised.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Cross is the sign of God’s power: Christ died “for our sins”, and God raised him, so that the weakness of the Passion leads to the glory of the Resurrection (CCC 648). The Church teaches that Christians, by baptism, are united to the death and Resurrection of Christ and called to carry that mystery in their own lives (CCC 1227, 1521). This reading prepares the way for the readings on St Alphonsa, who lived out in her body the “weakness” Paul speaks of.

Matthew 9:35–10:1 · The harvest is plentiful

Setting.

Matthew has told of a series of Jesus’ healings (chapters 8–9). Now he sums up his work: teaching in the synagogues, proclaiming the Gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease. The sight of the crowds, “harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd”, moves him to compassion, and he calls on the disciples to pray. In the next breath, he gives the Twelve authority to continue his work (10:1). The disciples who are told to pray for labourers become the first labourers.

Structure.

  • v. 35: Jesus goes through the cities and villages teaching, proclaiming and healing.
  • v. 36: moved with compassion for the crowds, like sheep without a shepherd.
  • vv. 37–38: the harvest is plentiful and the labourers are few; ask the Lord of the harvest.
  • 10:1: he summons the Twelve and gives them authority over unclean spirits and every sickness.

Key words.

  • esplanchnisthē, he had compassion: From splanchna, the inner organs, the seat of deep feeling. Jesus is moved in his very being, as the Lord is in Hosea 11:8.
  • therismos, harvest: In the prophets, the harvest is a picture of the gathering of the nations or of the final judgment (Joel 3:13). Here it is the gathering of people into the kingdom.

Verse by verse.

“When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.” (9:36)

The picture comes from Numbers 27:17 and Ezekiel 34. The people need a shepherd, and Jesus, the Good Shepherd, comes to be that for them.

“The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few; therefore ask the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest.” (9:37–38)

The work belongs to the Lord of the harvest, and the first task of the Church is to pray. Those who pray find that they are themselves sent.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the whole Church is missionary by her nature and that every baptised person shares in this mission (CCC 849–856). The Second Vatican Council quotes this passage when it calls for prayer for vocations and for workers for the harvest (Ad Gentes 23, 39; Optatam Totius 2). Jesus’ compassion for the crowds is the model of every pastoral work, as the Church’s documents on priestly life remind us.

Deuteronomy 28:8–12 · The Lord will command the blessing

Setting.

Deuteronomy ends its long legal address with blessings and curses (chapters 27–30): Israel is shown two ways, the way of life and the way of death, and urged to choose life (30:19). Chapter 28 begins with a series of blessings for those who listen to the voice of the Lord and keep his commandments. Today’s verses come from the middle of this list. The blessings are those of a farming people: full barns, fruitful land, seasonal rain, children and cattle. Behind them stands the covenant bond: God makes Israel “his holy people”, and the nations will see that Israel is “called by the name of the Lord”.

These promises were made to Israel as a people, and as a people they depend on faithfulness to the covenant. They are not a guarantee that every faithful person will become wealthy. The Book of Job and the Psalms, and above all the Cross, show that suffering is not a sign of God’s anger and that the righteous often suffer. The deeper blessing is the one named in verse 9: to be God’s own, and to carry his name.

Structure.

  • v. 8: the Lord commands blessing on barns and on all undertakings in the land.
  • v. 9: the Lord will establish Israel as his holy people, if they keep his commandments and walk in his ways.
  • v. 10: all peoples will see that Israel is called by the name of the Lord.
  • vv. 11–12: abundance in children, cattle and crops; the heavens opened to give rain in season; Israel will lend to many nations.

Key words.

  • bĕrākâ, blessing: In the Bible, a blessing is God’s life-giving power at work in people and creation. To be blessed is first of all to be in the presence of God.
  • ‘am qādôš, holy people: “Holy” means set apart for God. Israel is holy not because of its own goodness but because the Lord has chosen and claimed it (Exodus 19:5–6).
  • nišbā‘, he swore: God swore an oath to the ancestors (Genesis 22:16–18). The blessing rests on a promise that cannot fail, even when the people do.

Verse by verse.

“The Lord will establish you as his holy people, as he has sworn to you, if you keep the commandments of the Lord your God and walk in his ways.” (28:9)

The covenant has a gift and a response. God first chooses and then asks for a life that fits the choice.

“All the peoples of the earth shall see that you are called by the name of the Lord” (28:10)

The holiness of God’s people is meant to be seen. A holy life is a witness to those who do not yet know the Lord.

“The Lord will open for you his rich storehouse, the heavens, to give the rain of your land in its season and to bless all your undertakings.” (28:12)

Rain is not earned but given from above. God’s generosity is the source of everything that grows.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the blessings of Deuteronomy in the light of Christ. The Catechism teaches that God’s blessing is his gift of life and that Christ is the fulfilment of all the blessings, in whom the Father “has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing” (Ephesians 1:3; CCC 1077–1083). The Church also teaches that the holiness of God’s people is God’s own work in them: the Church is holy because Christ, the Holy One, gives himself to her, and the holiness of the saints shows it (CCC 823–829). The saints, among them Alphonsa, are those on whom this blessing has most clearly come: those who carry the name of the Lord and whose lives make others see him.

Isaiah 53:10–12 · He shall see his offspring

Setting.

This is the end of the fourth Servant Song (52:13–53:12) in the second part of Isaiah, addressed to Israel in exile in the sixth century before Christ. A mysterious servant of the Lord suffers, is despised and put to death, and yet through his suffering many are made righteous. In the Jewish tradition the Servant has often been read as Israel itself, or as the faithful remnant, or as a prophet. Christians have read the poem as a prophecy fulfilled in Jesus: the New Testament applies it to him (Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25; Mark 10:45), and he himself speaks of his death with its words (Luke 22:37).

Today’s verses are the conclusion. After the suffering, the Lord’s purpose prospers: the Servant sees light, sees his offspring, and shares in the triumph. His suffering is not the end.

Structure.

  • v. 10: it was the will of the Lord to crush him; his life is an offering for sin; he will see his offspring and prolong his days.
  • v. 11: out of anguish he sees light; the righteous servant makes many righteous and bears their iniquities.
  • v. 12: therefore he has a portion with the great; he poured out himself to death, was numbered with sinners, bore the sin of many, and interceded for the transgressors.

Key words.

  • ’āšām, offering for sin: The guilt offering of Leviticus 5–7, an offering that makes amends for a wrong. The Servant offers his own life in place of the sacrifice of an animal.
  • yaṣdîq, he shall make righteous: Literally “he will make many to be in the right”. The Servant’s offering restores people to a right relation with God.
  • yipgā‘, he interceded: From the root for “meeting, interposing”. The Servant steps between sinners and the judgment they deserve.

Verse by verse.

“When you make his life an offering for sin, he shall see his offspring, and shall prolong his days” (53:10)

A paradox: the one offered becomes fruitful. His suffering does not end in loss but in a family, a people born from his offering.

“The righteous one, my servant, shall make many righteous, and he shall bear their iniquities.” (53:11)

The Servant is not only an example but a source. He bears what others cannot bear and gives them a share in his righteousness.

“because he poured out himself to death, and was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.” (53:12)

Jesus quotes the phrase “numbered with the transgressors” on the night of his arrest (Luke 22:37). Even on the Cross he prays for those who crucify him (Luke 23:34).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s death fulfils the prophecy of the Suffering Servant: “Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures” (CCC 601), and that by his obedience unto death Jesus fulfilled the substitution of the Servant who “shall make many righteous” (CCC 615). The Church also teaches that Christ’s passion gives new meaning to human suffering: it can be united to his redemptive passion (CCC 1505, 1521; Colossians 1:24). This is the teaching in which the Church sees the life of St Alphonsa. She did not value suffering for its own sake; but when it came, she placed it in the hands of Christ, trusting that nothing offered in love is lost.

Galatians 2:19–21 · Crucified with Christ

Setting.

Paul has just told how he opposed Peter at Antioch when Peter drew back from table fellowship with Gentile believers (2:11–14). He explains why: no one is justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ (2:16). Today’s verses are the heart of the argument, and one of the most personal passages in his letters. “Through the law I died to the law” does not mean the law is evil (3:19–25; Romans 7:12) but that the believer has been freed through Christ from the law’s power to condemn, and now lives to God.

The central sentence is a confession of love: the Son of God “loved me and gave himself for me”. For Paul, the Gospel is not first an idea but a person who loves each one.

Structure.

  • v. 19: through the law I died to the law, so that I might live to God.
  • v. 20: I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live but Christ who lives in me; I live by faith in the Son of God who loved me and gave himself for me.
  • v. 21: I do not nullify the grace of God; if justification came through the law, Christ died for nothing.

Key words.

  • synestaurōmai, I have been crucified with: A Greek verb in the perfect tense: it happened once and its effect remains. The believer’s old self was nailed to the Cross with Christ in Baptism (Romans 6:6).
  • pistis, faith: Here not mere belief but trusting surrender to the person who loved and gave himself. Faith is the way Christ’s life becomes the believer’s life.
  • charis, grace: The undeserved gift of God’s love. Paul says that to seek justification by law alone would make the Cross pointless.

Verse by verse.

“I have been crucified with Christ; and it is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me.” (2:19–20)

This is the goal of Christian life: not to copy Christ from outside, but for Christ to live in us.

“And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me.” (2:20)

The Cross is personal: “for me”. Paul can say this because the love that gave itself was addressed to him by name.

“I do not nullify the grace of God” (2:21)

Grace is not set aside by a religion of effort. The Cross is the proof that no one can save himself.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes “who loved me and gave himself for me” when it speaks of the love of the Heart of Jesus for each person (CCC 478), and teaches that the Cross is the unique sacrifice of Christ, in which each believer is invited to share (CCC 618). It teaches that our justification comes from the grace of God and that, since grace is God’s gift, our own good works are his gifts in us (CCC 1996–2001, 2006–2011). In Baptism we are united to the death of Christ so that we may walk in newness of life (Romans 6:3–4; CCC 1227). A saint is a person in whom this has become visible: someone of whom it can truly be said that Christ lives in her.

John 12:20–26 · Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies

Setting.

It is the last public scene of Jesus’ ministry in John, in Jerusalem during Passover week. The crowds have just welcomed him on Palm Sunday (12:12–19). Some Greeks, probably Gentiles who worshipped the God of Israel, ask Philip to see Jesus. Philip, whose name is Greek and who comes from Bethsaida, tells Andrew, and together they tell Jesus. Jesus does not simply receive them. He declares that “the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified”: the coming of the Gentiles is a sign that his hour has arrived, the hour of the Cross and Resurrection through which he will draw all people to himself (12:32).

He then explains in a parable what the hour will mean: the grain must die to bear fruit. The same law holds for his followers.

Structure.

  • vv. 20–22: some Greeks wish to see Jesus; Philip and Andrew bring them.
  • v. 23: the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified.
  • v. 24: the grain of wheat must fall into the earth and die to bear much fruit.
  • v. 25: those who love their life lose it; those who hate their life in this world keep it for eternal life.
  • v. 26: whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am my servant will be; the Father will honour him.

Key words.

  • hōra, hour: In John, the “hour” is the time of Jesus’ Passion, death and glorification. Until now it “had not yet come” (2:4; 7:30); now it has.
  • doxazesthai, to be glorified: For John, Jesus’ glory is revealed in the Cross. He is lifted up on the Cross, and lifted up in glory.
  • misei tēn psychēn, hates his life: A Semitic way of speaking that means to love less, to put second (compare Luke 14:26). It does not mean contempt for life, but that the life given to God is worth more than clinging to it.

Verse by verse.

“Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” (12:21)

The whole Gospel is answered in this request. Everyone who comes to the Church is asking to see Jesus. The Church’s task is to show him.

“Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” (12:24)

Death is not the end of the grain but the beginning of the harvest. The image holds for Christ, for his martyrs and for every life given in love.

“Whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am, there will my servant be also.” (12:26)

The disciple follows into the same place: the Cross, and then the glory. Being with Christ is the reward of service.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, preaching on this chapter, saw in the grain Christ himself, who died and then rose in many members, so that the Church is the harvest sown by his death (in substance, Tractates on John 51). The Catechism teaches that Christ’s sacrifice makes it possible for his disciples to be associated with his Passion (CCC 618), and that the way to holiness passes by the Cross, for there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle (CCC 2015). It also teaches that those who unite their own suffering and death to Christ’s receive a share in his fruitfulness (CCC 1521). The martyrs and saints bear witness to this: their hidden lives become a source of life for others.

Questions people ask

Who was St Alphonsa?

Alphonsa was born on 19 August 1910 at Kudamaloor in Kerala and was baptised Anna (called Annakutty). Her mother died soon after her birth, and she was brought up by relatives. When a marriage was being arranged for her, her biographers tell us, she stepped into a pit of burning chaff so as to be unfit for marriage, and she carried the injury to her feet for life. She joined the Franciscan Clarist Congregation at Bharananganam in 1927, received the habit and the name Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception in 1928, and made her first vows in 1931 and her final vows in 1936. She lived a quiet life of prayer, work and patient suffering from illness, and died on 28 July 1946 at the age of thirty-five. Blessed Alphonsa was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1986, and Pope Benedict XVI canonised her in Rome on 12 October 2008. She is the first woman of India raised to the altars of the Church, and her tomb at Bharananganam is a place of pilgrimage for many people.

Does God want people to suffer?

No. God does not will suffering for its own sake, and the Church asks us to ease suffering and to seek healing wherever we can. But suffering that cannot be removed can be offered, in union with Christ, and can become a place of love and fruitfulness. This is the meaning of today’s readings: the Servant offers himself and bears fruit; the grain dies and bears much fruit. St Alphonsa did not value suffering for its own sake; she offered what came to her with trust in God, and it became a gift to others. The Church honours her for her love, not for the pain itself.

Do the blessings promised in Deuteronomy mean that good people will always be prosperous?

No. Deuteronomy speaks to Israel as a people in covenant with God, and the blessing is the life of a community that lives by God’s law. The Bible itself also shows that the righteous often suffer (the Book of Job, many Psalms, the Servant of Isaiah 53), and the Cross is the fullest sign of this. The deepest blessing is to belong to God and to carry his name. Material gifts, where they come, are to be received with thanks and shared with others.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St Alphonsa belongs to the Syro-Malabar Church, and her feast is kept in our rite on the day of her death, 28 July. Born in Kerala, formed in a Christian family and in the life of the Church, she gave her whole life to Christ in the quiet service of a convent. Each year pilgrims come to her tomb at Bharananganam, including many who are sick or burdened. The Church teaches us to honour the saints by imitation as well as by prayer: Alphonsa’s life asks us not to look for extraordinary things but to do small, hidden things with great love, and to place our sufferings in the hands of the Lord. In the Holy Qurbana, we too bring our lives as an offering together with Christ, the grain that died and rose. The readings of the day are a good moment for the family to pray for the sick and for all who suffer in many lands, and for those who have wronged them.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that Christ “was crucified in weakness, but lives by the power of God.” Where do I experience weakness, and can I believe that God’s power is at work there?
  • Deuteronomy says the people of God are to be “called by the name of the Lord” so that all nations see it. What does my life say about the One whose name I carry?
  • Paul says, “It is no longer I who live, but it is Christ who lives in me.” What would it mean this week to let Christ live in my work, my family and my suffering?
  • Jesus says the grain must fall into the earth and die. What small hidden act of love could I offer today, like St Alphonsa, for someone who will never know?
