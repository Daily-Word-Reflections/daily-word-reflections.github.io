Word for the day

“But she answered him, “Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.”” Mark 7:28

Reflection

Today the Gospel takes Jesus out of Galilee. Mark says that “he set out and went away to the region of Tyre”, a Gentile coastal region on the Mediterranean, in what is now southern Lebanon. He enters a house and wants no one to know he is there, perhaps seeking rest with his disciples. “Yet he could not escape notice.” A woman hears of him and comes at once, because her little daughter is tormented by an unclean spirit. Mark is careful to tell us who she is: “the woman was a Gentile, of Syrophoenician origin.” She is outside the people of the covenant, a woman, a foreigner, without any claim on a Jewish teacher. She bows down at his feet and begs him to cast the demon out of her daughter. Every parent who has prayed at night for a sick or troubled child knows what was in her heart.

Jesus' answer sounds hard to our ears: “Let the children be fed first, for it is not fair to take the children's food and throw it to the dogs.” We should not soften it too quickly, but we should read it well. Jesus speaks of an order in God's plan: the promises were made first to Israel, the children of the household, and the Messiah came first to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. The word he uses can mean the little dogs of the house, not the wild dogs of the street. And the word “first” leaves a door open: if the children are fed first, then someone else is fed afterwards. The woman hears the open door and walks through it with faith and wit: “Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs.” She does not argue that she deserves the bread. She trusts that there is so much bread at this table that even the crumbs are enough for her daughter. She calls him “Sir”, or “Lord”, the only person in Mark's Gospel to address Jesus with that title. And Jesus answers, “For saying that, you may go; the demon has left your daughter.” She goes home and finds the child lying quietly on the bed, healed.

Mark places this story just after Jesus has declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19), and just before he feeds a crowd in Gentile territory (Mark 8:1–10). The bread of the children is about to overflow the table. In this Season of the Apostles the Church remembers how that happened: after the Resurrection and Pentecost the apostles carried the bread of the Gospel to the nations, and the household of God was opened to all. We, the Christians of India, are among those who were once outside the table. Through the apostle Thomas, as our tradition holds, the bread came to our shores. Every Holy Qurbana we celebrate is a sign that the Syrophoenician woman's hope was right: there is enough at the Lord's table for all.

Paul's letter speaks of a very practical question in Corinth: may Christians eat meat that has been offered in pagan temples? Some say, “all of us possess knowledge”: we know that idols are nothing, so we may eat anything. Paul agrees with the doctrine but corrects the attitude: “Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up. Anyone who claims to know something does not yet have the necessary knowledge; but anyone who loves God is known by him.” Then he proclaims the heart of the faith in words that sound like an early creed: “yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist.” This is the Shema of Israel, “Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord alone” (Deuteronomy 6:4), opened up to include Jesus within the one God. We say the same in the Creed every Sunday. But Paul's point is that right doctrine must be held with love. The one who knows the truth must use it to build up the weak, not to show off or to wound.

Both readings meet in a single lesson. The proud knowledge of the Corinthians puffed them up; the humble faith of a foreign mother won her daughter's healing. It is not always the one who knows most who receives most, but the one who trusts most and loves most. Today let us pray for parents who carry a child's illness or struggle to the Lord; for those who feel they have no claim on God; and for ourselves, that our knowledge of the faith may become love that builds up.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Jesus goes to the region of Tyre, outside Israel. He wants to stay hidden, but a mother finds him. She is a Gentile, a foreigner, and her little daughter is tormented by an evil spirit. She falls at his feet and begs for help.

Jesus answers with words that sound hard: the children must be fed first; it is not fair to give the children's bread to the dogs. He is testing her, and he is speaking of God's plan, which began with Israel. But notice the word “first”: after the children, someone else will be fed.

The woman does not give up. She says: “Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs” (Mark 7:28). She does not claim that she deserves the bread. She only trusts that there is so much bread at the Lord's table that a crumb will be enough. Jesus rejoices in her faith, and her daughter is healed.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians that “knowledge puffs up, but love builds up” (1 Corinthians 8:1). The clever Corinthians were proud of what they knew. The foreign mother knew little, but she trusted much and loved much. And Paul reminds us of our faith: there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ.

We too were once outside the table. Through the apostles, the Gospel reached our land. Now at every Holy Qurbana we receive not crumbs but the Bread of life itself. Let us pray with this mother's persistence for the children we love, and let us use what we know of the faith to build others up. Amen.

For children

Children, a mother came to Jesus because her little girl was sick. Jesus tested her, but she did not give up! She said that even a crumb from his table would be enough. Jesus was so happy with her faith that he healed her daughter. When you pray, do not give up. Keep asking Jesus with trust, because he loves you very much.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you could not be hidden from the mother who came to you for her daughter, and you rejoiced in her humble and persistent faith. Look on the children we carry in our hearts: heal the sick, deliver those who are oppressed, and bring back those who have wandered. Teach us to trust that there is bread enough at your table for all. One God and Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we exist, keep us in the true faith and make our knowledge humble and our love strong, so that we may build up and not puff up. With the Holy Spirit be glory to you for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the one God whose love reaches beyond every boundary. Paul tells the Corinthians that knowledge puffs up but love builds up, and gives them one of the earliest Christian confessions: one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ. In Mark, a Gentile mother from the region of Tyre asks Jesus to heal her daughter, and her humble, quick-witted faith wins the healing. The one Lord is Lord of all, and humble love opens the way to him.

1 Corinthians 8:1–6 · One God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ

Setting.

The Corinthians had written to Paul with questions (7:1), and here he turns to a practical one: may Christians eat meat that had been offered to idols? In a Greek city much of the meat sold in the market came from temple sacrifices, and meals with friends were often held in temple dining halls. Some Corinthians said, “We know that idols are nothing, so we may eat anything.” Paul agrees with the truth they know, but he corrects the pride with which they hold it. Chapters 8 to 10 treat the question; today’s verses lay the foundation.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: knowledge puffs up, love builds up; whoever loves God is known by him.
  • v. 4: no idol really exists; there is no God but one.
  • v. 5: there are so-called gods and lords.
  • v. 6: the confession: one God, the Father, from whom are all things; one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things.

Key words.

  • gnōsis, knowledge: The Corinthians prized spiritual knowledge. Paul does not despise it, but knowledge without love makes a person proud and careless of others.
  • oikodomeō, build up: Literally “build a house”. Love builds the community like a house; Paul uses the word often in these chapters (8:10; 10:23; 14:4).
  • heis theos… heis kyrios, one God… one Lord: Paul takes the Shema, “The Lord is our God, the Lord alone” (Deuteronomy 6:4), and unfolds it: “God” is said of the Father, “Lord” of Jesus Christ. Jesus is placed within the confession of the one God of Israel.

Verse by verse.

“Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up.” (8:1)

A short sentence that judges much clever religion. Truth must be spoken and held in love (see 13:2).

“but anyone who loves God is known by him.” (8:3)

Paul turns the question around. What matters most is not what we know about God but that God knows us, and to be known by God is to be loved by him.

“yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist.” (8:6)

Little more than twenty years after the Resurrection, Paul speaks of Jesus as the one through whom all things were made, the work of God alone. This verse is a seed of the Creed: “one Lord Jesus Christ… through him all things were made.”

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes the Second Council of Constantinople, which draws on this verse: “one God and Father from whom all things are, one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom all things are, and one Holy Spirit in whom all things are” (CCC 258). The whole Trinity works as one in creation and salvation, each Person according to what is proper to him. On the question that follows, the Catechism quotes Paul’s warning about wounding a brother’s weak conscience, by which one sins against Christ (CCC 1789, citing 8:12): love for the neighbour’s conscience must guide how we use our freedom.

Mark 7:24–30 · Even the dogs under the table

Setting.

Jesus has just taught that what makes a person unclean comes from the heart, not from food (7:14–23). Now he goes into Gentile territory, the region of Tyre on the Phoenician coast, north of Galilee. He wants to stay hidden, perhaps to rest with his disciples. There a Gentile woman comes to him. Mark calls her a Greek, a Syrophoenician by birth: a woman of another people and religion.

The story is told after a meal of five thousand where twelve baskets of bread were left over (6:43) and before the feeding of four thousand in a Gentile area (8:1–9). Bread runs through these chapters.

Structure.

  • v. 24: Jesus goes to Tyre and wants no one to know.
  • vv. 25–26: a Gentile woman falls at his feet and begs him to deliver her daughter.
  • v. 27: the hard answer: let the children be fed first.
  • v. 28: her reply: even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.
  • vv. 29–30: for that word her daughter is delivered; she goes home and finds the child well.

Key words.

  • Hellēnis, Syrophoinikissa, a Gentile, of Syrophoenician origin: Mark gives both her language and culture (Greek) and her birth (Phoenician of Syria). She is wholly outside Israel.
  • kynaria, dogs, little dogs: The diminutive suggests house dogs, not street dogs. Jesus’ words still sound hard. Many commentators see him testing and drawing out her faith; she takes up his own image and turns it into a plea.
  • prōton, first: “Let the children be fed first.” The word leaves the door open: Israel first, then the nations. Paul says the same: the Gospel is “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” (Romans 1:16).

Verse by verse.

“Let the children be fed first, for it is not fair to take the children’s food and throw it to the dogs.” (7:27)

Jesus’ mission was first to Israel, God’s children by covenant. His word is not a refusal of the woman but a statement of order in God’s plan.

“But she answered him, “Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.”” (7:28)

She is the only person in Mark who wins an argument with Jesus. She does not deny Israel’s place; she asks only for a crumb, sure that a crumb of his power is enough.

“So she went home, found the child lying on the bed, and the demon gone.” (7:30)

Jesus heals from a distance, without seeing the child. The mother believed his word and went home; she found it true.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in this woman a model of humble and persevering prayer. St Augustine, preaching on Matthew’s account of the same woman, praises her humility: she did not take offence at being compared to a dog, but accepted it and so was raised up (in substance, Sermon 77). The story also shows the Gospel going out to all peoples, as the Lord willed: the crumbs from Israel’s table become the bread of the nations, and the Church is sent to all (CCC 849).

Questions people ask

Was Jesus being harsh or prejudiced toward the woman?

Jesus’ words do sound hard, and the Gospel does not soften them. But the whole story shows his purpose: he does heal her daughter, and he praises her answer. He tests her faith and lets it shine. He came first to Israel, as God had planned, and through Israel to all nations. The woman understood that, and believed that there was enough for her too.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says knowledge puffs up but love builds up. When I share what I know about the faith, does it build others up?
  • The Syrophoenician mother would not give up. For whom am I praying with that kind of humble persistence?
