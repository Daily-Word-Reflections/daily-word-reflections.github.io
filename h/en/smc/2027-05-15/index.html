Word for the day

“The threshing floors shall be full of grain, the vats shall overflow with wine and oil.” Joel 2:24

Reflection

On the last day of the Season of the Resurrection, on the eve of Pentecost, our Church turns to the Mother of God under a title that belongs to farmers and to the soil: the Protectress of Harvest. In the lands of Mesopotamia where the East Syriac tradition grew up, the middle of May is the time when the ears of grain ripen and the harvest is gathered; it is fitting to place our fields and our bread under the care of the Mother of the One who called himself the bread of life. The Syro-Malankara calendar keeps a similar commemoration today, of the Holy Mother of God for the ears of grain. It is a feast of the earth, and it is also a feast of heaven, because the readings chosen for it lead us straight to the festival that Christians will celebrate tomorrow. The first reading is short: “Three times in the year you shall hold a festival for me.” The second of those festivals is “the festival of harvest, of the first fruits of your labor, of what you sow in the field.” This is the Feast of Weeks, counted seven weeks from the first sheaf, “fifty days” (Leviticus 23:16); in Greek it was called Pentecost, the fiftieth day. On that very festival, in Jerusalem, the Holy Spirit came down on the apostles gathered with Mary, and the first harvest of the Church was gathered in: “that day about three thousand persons were added” (Acts 2:41).

The prophet Joel speaks to a land that had been stripped by locusts and drought. Now God promises a reversal: “Do not fear, O soil; be glad and rejoice, for the LORD has done great things!” The pastures grow green, the fig tree and the vine bear again, the early and the later rain come in their season. “The threshing floors shall be full of grain, the vats shall overflow with wine and oil. I will repay you for the years that the swarming locust has eaten”. Every farmer in Kerala understands those words, in a month when the fields wait for the monsoon that usually arrives around the beginning of June, and many families still pray for the rain before sowing. But Joel goes further than the harvest: “Then afterward I will pour out my spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions.” Tomorrow Peter will stand up in Jerusalem and say: “this is what was spoken through the prophet Joel” (Acts 2:16). The rain on the fields becomes the sign of a greater rain, the Spirit poured out on men and women, young and old, servants and masters alike. Mary, the humble servant of the Lord, is the first to receive that rain in fullness: at the Annunciation the Holy Spirit came upon her, and in the upper room she prays with the apostles for the same Spirit to be poured upon the Church.

Paul's words to the Romans may seem far from the fields, but they speak of God's faithfulness to his first harvest, Israel. “A hardening has come upon part of Israel, until the full number of the Gentiles has come in. And so all Israel will be saved”. Paul, himself a son of Israel, insists: “as regards election they are beloved, for the sake of their ancestors; for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.” The Church has learned to read these words with great respect for the Jewish people, the people of the covenant never revoked, to whom belong the patriarchs, the prophets and, according to the flesh, Christ himself and his Mother. Mary is the daughter of Zion in whom the hope of Israel came to its fruit; Elizabeth greeted her: “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb” (Luke 1:42). Paul ends with a word about all of us: “For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all.” The harvest God desires is a harvest of mercy, in which no one is left in the field.

That is exactly what the Gospel says. “Take care that you do not despise one of these little ones; for, I tell you, in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father in heaven.” Then Jesus tells of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine and goes after the one that went astray, and rejoices over it more than over the ninety-nine. “So it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” The Protectress of Harvest is not only the guardian of rice and wheat; she is the Mother who watches over the little ones and the lost, as a farmer's wife watches over every sheaf. Mothers in Kerala homes know this: when one child is far away or has stopped praying, the rosary is said with that child's name. The weekday Gospel of the unforgiving servant adds a warning that fits a harvest feast: we who have received everything as a gift must forgive “from your heart” (Matthew 18:35), or the harvest is spoiled. And the weekday Epistle gives Paul's cry, “woe to me if I do not proclaim the gospel!” (1 Corinthians 9:16): the Lord of the harvest needs labourers. So on this last evening of Easter we bring to Mary our fields and our work, our children near and far, and our hearts, asking her to prepare them, like good soil, for the rain of the Holy Spirit tomorrow.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this last day of the Season of the Resurrection, the eve of Pentecost, our Church honours the Blessed Virgin Mary as Protectress of Harvest. In the lands where our East Syriac tradition was born, mid-May is the time of the grain harvest, and it is fitting to place our fields under the care of the Mother of Christ, the Bread of life.

The first reading tells Israel to keep “the festival of harvest, of the first fruits of your labor” (Exodus 23:16). That harvest festival was counted fifty days from Passover. Its Greek name is Pentecost. Tomorrow we keep that very festival, the day on which the Holy Spirit came and the first harvest of the Church was gathered: three thousand people baptised.

Joel promises a ruined land: “The threshing floors shall be full of grain” (Joel 2:24). Then he promises more: “I will pour out my spirit on all flesh” (Joel 2:28). Our farmers know what it is to wait for the rain. On this eve of Pentecost, Mary teaches us to wait for the greater rain, the Holy Spirit, as she waited with the apostles.

Paul reminds us that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). God is faithful to Israel, his first harvest, the people of Mary. And God wishes to show mercy to all.

Jesus says: “it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost” (Matthew 18:14). Our Mother does not forget one sheaf in the field. She prays for every child, especially the one who has gone astray.

So let us bring Mary our fields and our work, our children near and far, and our hearts. Let us forgive whoever has hurt us, so that our heart may be good soil tomorrow for the Holy Spirit. Holy Mother of God, pray for us. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, who gives us rice and bread? God gives the rain and the sun, and farmers work hard. Today we ask Mother Mary to take care of the farmers and the fields.

Tomorrow is Pentecost, the birthday of the Church, when God sent the Holy Spirit. Let us ask Mary to help us get our hearts ready!

Prayer

Holy Mother of God, Protectress of Harvest, you are the good soil from which came forth Christ, the Bread of life. We place under your care our fields and gardens, the rain and the seasons, the work of our hands, and the bread on our tables. Watch over the little ones and over all who have gone astray, that not one may be lost. On this eve of Pentecost, pray with us as you prayed with the apostles, that the Father may pour out his Spirit on our sons and daughters, on the old and the young, and that the harvest of mercy may be gathered in. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On this last day of the Resurrection season the Church honours Mary as Protectress of Harvest, and the readings are full of fields, grain and a coming gift. Exodus commands Israel to keep the festival of harvest, the feast of first fruits that would later be called Pentecost, which the Church celebrates tomorrow. Joel promises threshing floors full of grain after the locusts, and then something greater: God will pour out his spirit on all flesh. Paul looks at the great harvest of the nations and of Israel and declares that God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable. In Matthew the Father’s will is that not one little one be lost. The words to watch are harvest, pour out and mercy. Mary, who waited in prayer for the Spirit with the apostles, stands at the threshold of the feast.

Exodus 23:14–16 · The festival of harvest

Setting.

These verses belong to the Book of the Covenant (Exodus 20:22–23:33), the first collection of laws given at Sinai. They set Israel’s year round three great pilgrim festivals, each tied to the farmer’s calendar and each turned into a memory of what God had done.

In the land of Israel barley ripens in spring and wheat some weeks later; grapes, olives and summer fruit are gathered in autumn. Israel was to bring the work of its hands before the Lord at each turning of the year. Later texts call the harvest festival the Feast of Weeks, kept seven weeks after the barley (Leviticus 23:15–16; Deuteronomy 16:9–10). In Greek it was called Pentecost, “the fiftieth day”.

Structure.

  • v. 14: three times a year a festival for the Lord.
  • v. 15: Unleavened Bread in the month of Abib, the month of the Exodus; no one empty-handed.
  • v. 16: Harvest, the first fruits; and Ingathering at the end of the year.

Key words.

  • ḥag, festival: A pilgrim feast, from a root that may mean to go round or to dance. These were days when the people went up together to the Lord’s sanctuary.
  • ’ābîb, Abib: “Ears of grain”: the month when the barley is in the ear, in spring. Israel’s new year of freedom began with the new grain.
  • bikkûrîm, first fruits: The first and best of the crop, given back to God before the rest is used. The first fruits declare that the whole harvest is a gift.

Verse by verse.

“Three times in the year you shall hold a festival for me.” (23:14)

The year itself becomes a path of worship. Israel’s calendar teaches that time belongs to God.

“No one shall appear before me empty-handed.” (23:15)

Not a tax but a thanksgiving. Those who have received from God come before him with something of what he gave.

“You shall observe the festival of harvest, of the first fruits of your labor, of what you sow in the field.” (23:16)

This harvest feast is the one the New Testament calls Pentecost (Acts 2:1). On that day God gathered his own first fruits: about three thousand persons (Acts 2:41).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Israel’s feasts as fulfilled in Christ without being emptied. Paul calls the Risen Christ “the first fruits of those who have died” (1 Corinthians 15:20), and James says that God gave us birth by the word of truth “so that we would become a kind of first fruits of his creatures” (James 1:18). The Catechism teaches that on the day of Pentecost, when the seven weeks of Easter had come to an end, Christ’s Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit (CCC 731). On a Marian feast of the harvest it is fitting to remember that the Church calls Mary the most excellent fruit of the redemption (CCC 508): in her, first and most fully, Christ’s work bore its harvest.

Joel 2:21–29 · I will pour out my spirit

Setting.

The book of Joel cannot be dated with certainty; many scholars place it after the Exile, when the Temple stood again. It begins with a terrible plague of locusts, which the prophet sees as a sign of the Day of the Lord, and with a call to fasting and repentance: “rend your hearts and not your clothing” (2:13).

Then the tone changes: “the Lord became jealous for his land, and had pity on his people” (2:18). Today’s verses are God’s answer. The land, the animals and the children of Zion are told not to fear. In the Hebrew Bible verses 28–29 begin a new chapter (3:1–2).

Structure.

  • vv. 21–24: do not fear, soil, animals, children of Zion: the rain, the grain, the wine and oil.
  • vv. 25–27: the lost years restored; God in the midst of Israel; no more shame.
  • vv. 28–29: afterward: the spirit poured out on all flesh, on sons and daughters, old and young, slaves male and female.

Key words.

  • hammôreh, the early rain: The autumn rain that softens the ground for sowing. The same Hebrew word can also mean “teacher”, and some ancient translations, including the Latin Vulgate, read “the teacher of righteousness”. The NRSV reads “early rain”.
  • šāpak, pour out: The verb for pouring water or oil in abundance. God’s spirit is not given drop by drop but poured like the rain of verse 23.
  • kol-bāśār, all flesh: Every human being, in weakness and mortality. The gift is no longer only for a prophet or a king, as with Samuel and David, but for the whole people, without regard to sex, age or status.

Verse by verse.

“Do not fear, O soil; be glad and rejoice, for the Lord has done great things!” (2:21)

The earth itself is addressed. The land that the locusts stripped will rejoice. Mary will sing the same: “the Mighty One has done great things for me” (Luke 1:49).

“The threshing floors shall be full of grain, the vats shall overflow with wine and oil.” (2:24)

Grain, wine and oil are the three great gifts of the land. Christians cannot hear them without thinking of the bread and wine of the Holy Qurbana and the oil of anointing.

“I will repay you for the years that the swarming locust has eaten” (2:25)

A verse of deep comfort. God does not only stop the loss; he restores what was lost.

“Then afterward I will pour out my spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy” (2:28)

Peter quotes these verses on the day of Pentecost to explain what the crowd is seeing (Acts 2:16–21). Joel’s “afterward” has become “in the last days”.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in Joel the promise fulfilled at Pentecost, as Peter himself proclaims (Acts 2:17). The Catechism, teaching on the Holy Spirit in the time of the promises, sees the prophets awaiting a new outpouring of the Spirit on the whole people (CCC 715). The fruitfulness of the land and the outpouring of the Spirit belong together: the God who sends rain on the fields sends his Spirit on hearts. This is why the Church blesses fields and seed and prays for good harvests: the Creator of the earth is the Giver of life.

Romans 11:25–32 · The gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable

Setting.

In Romans 9–11 Paul wrestles with a grief that touches him personally: most of his own people, Israel, have not accepted Jesus as the Messiah (9:1–5). Has God’s word failed? Paul’s answer grows chapter by chapter. Just before this reading he compares Israel to a cultivated olive tree and the Gentile believers to wild branches grafted in, and warns the Gentiles not to boast (11:17–24).

Now he reveals a “mystery”, a part of God’s plan once hidden and now made known. These verses are among the most important in the New Testament for how Christians must think of the Jewish people.

Structure.

  • v. 25: the mystery: a partial hardening on Israel until the full number of the Gentiles comes in.
  • vv. 26–27: and so all Israel will be saved, as the prophets foretold.
  • vv. 28–29: enemies as regards the gospel, beloved as regards election: God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable.
  • vv. 30–32: disobedience and mercy for Gentiles and for Israel alike: God will have mercy on all.

Key words.

  • mystērion, mystery: In Paul not a riddle but God’s saving plan, now revealed. Even Israel’s “no” has a place in the plan of mercy.
  • ametamelēta, irrevocable: Literally “not to be regretted”. God does not change his mind about the gifts he gave Israel: the covenants, the law, the worship, the promises (9:4). This word is the heart of the reading.
  • eleos, eleeō, mercy, to have mercy: The word returns four times in three verses, and the passage ends on it. Mercy, not merit, is the last word for Gentiles and Jews alike.

Verse by verse.

“a hardening has come upon part of Israel, until the full number of the Gentiles has come in.” (11:25)

Paul says “part”, not all, and “until”, not forever. He himself, and all the first apostles, were Israelites.

“And so all Israel will be saved” (11:26)

Paul does not explain how or when. He leaves it in God’s hands, as the doxology that follows shows: “O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!” (11:33).

“As regards the gospel they are enemies of God for your sake; but as regards election they are beloved, for the sake of their ancestors” (11:28)

The Greek says only “enemies”; the words “of God” are supplied by the translation. However one renders it, Paul’s point is that Israel remains beloved by God because of the patriarchs.

“for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.” (11:29)

The Second Vatican Council quotes this verse when it speaks of the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; Lumen Gentium 16).

“For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all.” (11:32)

No one can stand before God by his own record. All, Gentile and Jew, receive mercy as a gift.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that the Jews remain very dear to God, for the sake of the patriarchs, since God does not take back the gifts he bestowed or the choice he made (Nostra Aetate 4, quoting this passage). The Catechism adds that the Jewish faith, unlike other religions, is already a response to God’s revelation in the Old Covenant, and it quotes Romans 11:29 (CCC 839). It also teaches that Christ’s glorious coming is linked to the recognition of the Messiah by “all Israel” (CCC 674). In 2015 the Holy See’s Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews took the very words of verse 29 as the title of a document on Catholic and Jewish dialogue. The Church never speaks of Israel as replaced: she is grafted on to Israel’s root.

Matthew 18:10–14 · Not one of these little ones

Setting.

Chapter 18 is Matthew’s discourse on life in the community of disciples. It begins when the disciples ask who is greatest, and Jesus places a child among them (18:1–5). He warns against causing a little one to stumble (18:6–9). Today’s verses follow: the little ones are not to be despised, and the one who strays must be sought. Then comes the teaching on correcting a brother and on forgiving seventy-seven times.

Structure.

  • v. 10: do not despise the little ones; their angels see the Father’s face.
  • vv. 12–13: the parable of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine.
  • v. 14: the Father’s will: that not one be lost.

Key words.

  • mikroi, little ones: In Matthew, both children and the humble, ordinary disciples, those easily overlooked or easily led astray.
  • planaō, go astray: To wander, to be led off the path. Luke’s parable speaks of a sheep that is “lost” (Luke 15:4); Matthew speaks of one that has strayed, a member of the community who has wandered.
  • thelēma, will: “It is not the will of your Father”: the same word as in the Our Father, “your will be done” (6:10). The Father’s will is that none be lost.

Verse by verse.

“Take care that you do not despise one of these little ones; for, I tell you, in heaven their angels continually see the face of my Father in heaven.” (18:10)

The little ones may be unimportant to us, but they are so important to God that their angels stand always in his presence.

“What do you think? If a shepherd has a hundred sheep, and one of them has gone astray, does he not leave the ninety-nine on the mountains and go in search of the one that went astray?” (18:12)

Verse 11 is missing in the NRSV. A few manuscripts add “For the Son of Man came to save the lost”, probably taken from Luke 19:10; the oldest manuscripts do not have it.

“So it is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should be lost.” (18:14)

In Luke the parable ends with joy in heaven; in Matthew it ends with the Father’s will, as a charge to those who care for the community.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism cites verse 10 for the Church’s faith in guardian angels: from its beginning until death human life is surrounded by their watchful care and intercession (CCC 336). The Fathers loved the shepherd who goes to the mountains as a picture of the Word who came down to seek lost humanity. In Matthew the parable is also a rule for pastors and for every Christian: no one in the community may be written off.

Questions people ask

Does Romans 11 mean that the Jewish people are enemies of God?

No. Paul says that in relation to the gospel many have not yet accepted it, and that this has opened the door to the Gentiles; but in the same breath he says they are “beloved” and that God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable. The Church teaches that the Jews may not be presented as rejected or accursed by God (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598, 839). Christians are to approach the Jewish people with respect, gratitude and love.

Why does the Syro-Malabar Church keep a feast of Mary for the harvest?

The Church of the East has long kept days asking Mary’s protection over the work of the fields, and this commemoration in mid-May belongs to that tradition. It is not a nature festival placed beside the faith: it asks the Mother of the Lord, who bore the true Bread from heaven, to pray for those who sow and reap, and for a just sharing of the earth’s fruits.

Do we each have a guardian angel?

The Church teaches that from its beginning until death human life is surrounded by the watchful care and intercession of angels, and that beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd leading him to life (CCC 336, quoting St Basil). Matthew 18:10 is one of the texts on which this faith rests.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In our tradition Mary is honoured at the turning points of the farmer’s year, and this commemoration falls in the season of the first harvests. It also falls on the eve of Pentecost, Israel’s feast of harvest, when God gathered the first fruits of the Church. Mary was in the upper room with the apostles, praying for the Spirit (Acts 1:14). The Catechism says that she aided the beginnings of the Church by her prayers, imploring the gift of the Spirit (CCC 965).

It is a good day for farming families to pray in the Holy Qurbana for the fields, for rain in its season, for those who work the land and those who go hungry, and for a just sharing of the harvest. Tomorrow the Resurrection season gives way to the Season of the Apostles, and the Church, like a field ready for the sickle, waits for the Spirit.

For study and teaching

  • Israel was told not to come before God empty-handed. What first fruits of my time, work or money do I bring to God?
  • Joel promises that God will restore “the years that the swarming locust has eaten”. What lost years or losses in my life do I need to entrust to God?
  • How do I speak about the Jewish people, and how does Romans 11:29 call me to change?
  • Who are the “little ones” in my parish who are overlooked or have strayed, and what can I do to seek them?
