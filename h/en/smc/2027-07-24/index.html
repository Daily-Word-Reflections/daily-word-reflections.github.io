Word for the day

“Instead you ought to say, “If the Lord wishes, we will live and do this or that.”” James 4:15

Reflection

Saint James paints a scene from the market. A trader says, “Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a town and spend a year there, doing business and making money.” There is nothing wrong with planning, and James does not forbid it. What he exposes is the presumption in the plan, the tone of a man who speaks as if he were the lord of tomorrow. “Yet you do not even know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes.” Anyone who has seen the morning mist on the hills of Idukki or Munnar in the monsoon knows how quickly it is gone when the sun rises. The psalmist prays the same truth: “So teach us to count our days that we may gain a wise heart” (Psalm 90:12). Then comes the remedy: “Instead you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wishes, we will live and do this or that.’” Many Kerala families keep the habit in their speech: ‘if God helps’. It is not a phrase to excuse laziness; it is the confession that every plan is made inside God's providence. “The human mind plans the way, but the Lord directs the steps” (Proverbs 16:9).

James adds two sharp sentences. “As it is, you boast in your arrogance; all such boasting is evil.” And then the line that every conscience hears: “Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, commits sin.” The Church teaches that sin is not only what we do but also what we fail to do, in thought, word, deed and omission. Every good examination of conscience asks not only what we have done but what we have left undone. The man in James's market had no plan to harm anyone. His fault was that God and the neighbour did not appear in the plan at all: the good he could do today he put off for a tomorrow that he does not own.

The Gospel begins with a small quarrel among the apostles. John says, “Master, we saw someone casting out demons in your name, and we tried to stop him, because he does not follow with us.” It is the voice of the group: he is not one of us. Jesus answers, “Do not stop him; for whoever is not against you is for you.” The same breadth of heart was in Moses when Joshua wished to silence Eldad and Medad, who were prophesying in the camp: “Are you jealous for my sake? Would that all the Lord's people were prophets” (Numbers 11:29). The Church teaches, of course, that the fullness of the means of salvation is found in the Catholic Church. But she also teaches that “many elements of sanctification and of truth” are found outside her visible boundaries (Catechism 819), and that good done in the name of Christ by others is not to be forbidden with envy. This is the same lesson as on Tuesday: the spirit of rivalry is the opposite of the wisdom from above.

Then the Gospel turns toward the Cross: “When the days drew near for him to be taken up, he set his face to go to Jerusalem.” Isaiah's servant had said, “I have set my face like flint” (Isaiah 50:7). Jesus sends messengers ahead to a Samaritan village, and the villagers do not receive him because his face is set toward Jerusalem. James and John, whom the Lord called Sons of Thunder (Mark 3:17), say, “Lord, do you want us to command fire to come down from heaven and consume them?” They remember Elijah, who once called down fire (2 Kings 1:10), and the Church has met this prophet more than once this week. But Jesus “turned and rebuked them. Then they went on to another village.” No fire, no argument, no revenge. He who has set his face to the Cross moves on to another village. It is the way of the Lamb. In many homes Saturday is kept in honour of the Mother of the Lord, and in her we see the opposite of the arrogant plan: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). Her whole life was an ‘if the Lord wishes’.

Full homily

Dear friends, you have seen the mist on the hills in the morning. It is thick, and then it is gone. Saint James says that our life is like that: “You are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes” (James 4:14).

So he gives us a lesson: when we plan, we should say, “If the Lord wishes, we will live and do this or that” (James 4:15). We may plan, but we are not the lords of tomorrow. And he adds a sentence for each of us: “Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, commits sin” (James 4:17). What good do you know and keep putting off?

In the Gospel John tells Jesus: “We tried to stop him, because he does not follow with us.” Jesus replies: “Do not stop him; for whoever is not against you is for you” (Luke 9:49–50). God is glad when anyone does good in his name. Let us not be jealous of other people's good works.

Then Jesus sets his face toward Jerusalem. A village refuses him, and James and John want fire from heaven. Jesus rebukes them, and they go on to another village (Luke 9:55–56). The Lord is gentle; he does not take revenge, because he is going to the Cross for us.

This week let us offer our plans to God, do one good thing that we have put off, and say a thank-you for someone's good work. Amen.

For children

Children, have you seen mist on the hills? In the morning it is thick and white, and then the sun comes and it is gone! Our life is like that, so we must always do good today and say, “If God wishes.” And if you see someone who is not your friend doing good, do not stop them: clap for them! Prayer

Lord God, our lives are like a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes, and our tomorrows are in your hands. Teach us to count our days and gain a wise heart. Let us make our plans in humility and say with sincere hearts, “If the Lord wishes.” Keep us from boasting, and move us to do today the good that we know. Give us the breadth of heart of Jesus, who did not forbid the stranger who cast out demons in his name; and give us his gentleness, who set his face toward Jerusalem and refused to call down fire. Through Mary, who said “Let it be with me according to your word”, and through Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings correct a kind of pride. James tells the confident planners, who say they will go to this town and make this profit, that life is a mist and that every plan should be made under the words “If the Lord wishes”. In Luke, the disciples want to stop a man who is casting out demons in Jesus’ name because he is not one of their group, and later want fire to fall on a village that will not receive Jesus. Jesus answers both times with a correction and a wider heart. God is the Lord of our plans, and his grace is not confined by our boundaries.

James 4:13–17 · If the Lord wishes

Setting.

James has just spoken against judging a neighbour (4:11–12). Now he turns to a different arrogance: the traveling merchant who plans a year of business as if the future were his own. The merchants of the Mediterranean world moved from city to city, and James knows the type. He does not condemn business or planning; he condemns planning that leaves God out.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the planners: “today or tomorrow we will go…”.
  • v. 14: the reply: you do not know what tomorrow will bring; life is a mist.
  • v. 15: the right attitude: “If the Lord wishes…”.
  • vv. 16–17: boasting is evil; and the sin of knowing the good and not doing it.

Key words.

  • atmis, mist, vapour: The word is used of smoke or of breath on a cold morning. Life is brief and cannot be held, a theme of Psalm 39:5–6 and Psalm 144:4.
  • alazoneia, arrogance, boasting: A word for a show-off who claims more than he has. James calls it “evil”, not because it is loud but because it takes God’s place.
  • hamartia, sin: Verse 17 defines sin as failing to do the good one knows: sin of omission as well as of commission.

Verse by verse.

“Yet you do not even know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes.” (4:14)

A sober reminder. James is not gloomy; he wants us to live wisely, aware that each day is a gift.

“Instead you ought to say, “If the Lord wishes, we will live and do this or that.”” (4:15)

Not a mere formula to be recited, but a way of living: the plan is made, and held open to God’s will. Paul uses the same phrase (1 Corinthians 4:19; Acts 18:21).

“As it is, you boast in your arrogance; all such boasting is evil.” (4:16)

Self-reliance that forgets God is not a small fault. James names it for what it is.

“Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, commits sin.” (4:17)

A searching verse for the conscience: it is not enough to avoid doing wrong; we are also answerable for the good we leave undone.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that God’s providence guides all things, and that Jesus asks us to abandon ourselves with childlike trust to the providence of the heavenly Father, who cares for the smallest needs of his children (CCC 305). The Catechism also teaches that sin may be committed by omission as well as by commission (CCC 1853). The words “If the Lord wishes” are not an excuse for idleness: they are the other side of the Our Father, “Your will be done”.

Luke 9:49–56 · Whoever is not against you

Setting.

Luke 9 is the turning point of the Gospel. After Peter’s confession and the Transfiguration, “he set his face to go to Jerusalem” (9:51), and the long journey narrative begins, lasting to Luke 19:28. The two stories in today’s reading show how the disciples must learn on the road: John’s concern about an outside exorcist, and James and John’s anger at a Samaritan village.

Jews and Samaritans had been long divided, and many Jews travelling between Galilee and Jerusalem avoided Samaritan territory. The Samaritans worshipped on Mount Gerizim, so a party on its way to the Temple at Jerusalem touched an old disagreement. Luke will later make a Samaritan the model of neighbourly love (10:30–37; 17:11–19).

Structure.

  • vv. 49–50: John reports an exorcist who does not follow with them; Jesus: “Do not stop him”.
  • v. 51: Jesus sets his face toward Jerusalem, the days of his being “taken up” drawing near.
  • vv. 52–53: messengers to a Samaritan village; the village does not receive him.
  • vv. 54–56: James and John want fire from heaven; Jesus rebukes them; they go on.

Key words.

  • analēmpsis, being taken up: The word in v. 51 points beyond the Cross to the Ascension. Luke sees Jesus’ journey to Jerusalem as a journey to glory through death.
  • ekballō, to cast out: The same word appears for the exorcist (“casting out demons”) in v. 49. The unnamed man is doing the work Jesus himself does, in Jesus’ name.
  • epitimaō, to rebuke: Jesus uses the same word of rebuking evil spirits and storms. He rebukes his own disciples’ wish for vengeance as something to be driven out.

Verse by verse.

“we saw someone casting out demons in your name, and we tried to stop him, because he does not follow with us.” (9:49)

The disciples’ reason: he does not belong to our group. They do not deny that the work is genuine.

“Do not stop him; for whoever is not against you is for you.” (9:50)

Jesus refuses to draw the circle narrowly. Compare Mark 9:38–41. Elsewhere he says that whoever is not with him is against him (Luke 11:23): the two sayings together warn against both indifference and narrowness.

“When the days drew near for him to be taken up, he set his face to go to Jerusalem.” (9:51)

“Set his face” echoes Isaiah 50:7, the servant who sets his face like flint. He goes toward Jerusalem knowing what awaits him.

“Lord, do you want us to command fire to come down from heaven and consume them?” (9:54)

The echo is of Elijah and the fire in 2 Kings 1:10–12. Some later manuscripts add “as Elijah did”, and, after v. 55, the words “You do not know what spirit you are of, for the Son of Man has not come to destroy the lives of people but to save them”. These additions are probably not original, but they express the sense of the story well.

“But he turned and rebuked them.” (9:55)

Jesus turns and faces them. The prophet Elijah called down fire; the Son of Man refuses. His way is not destruction.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has seen in Jesus’ answer to John a call to recognise good wherever it is found: the Second Vatican Council teaches that many elements of sanctification and truth are found outside the visible structure of the Catholic Church, and that these, as gifts belonging to Christ’s Church, impel toward Catholic unity (Lumen Gentium 8; Unitatis Redintegratio 3). The refusal of fire teaches that the Gospel is never to be spread by force: no one is to be compelled to embrace the faith (Dignitatis Humanae 10).

Questions people ask

Does “whoever is not against you is for you” mean that all religions and Churches are the same?

No. Jesus is speaking about a man who casts out demons in his own name, and he teaches his disciples not to be jealous of good done in his name by someone outside their circle. The Church teaches that Christ founded one Church and that the fullness of the means of salvation subsists in the Catholic Church, while she rejoices in the grace and truth found in other Churches and treats others with respect (Lumen Gentium 8, 15; Nostra Aetate 2). Jealousy and violence are never the Church’s way.

For study and teaching

  • James reminds us that we do not know what tomorrow will bring. What plan of mine do I need to hold more openly, saying “If the Lord wishes”?
  • The disciples wanted to stop someone who was not “one of us”, and to punish those who would not welcome Jesus. Where am I tempted to limit God’s work to my own group, or to answer rejection with anger?
