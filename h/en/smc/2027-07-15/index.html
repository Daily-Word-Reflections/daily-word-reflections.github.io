Word for the day

““Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven.” Matthew 10:32

Reflection

The Church today keeps the memory of a mother and a child. According to the ancient Acts of the martyrs, which come down to us with some details we cannot verify, Julitta was a noble widow of Iconium who fled from the persecution of the emperor Diocletian, about the year 304, with her small son Cyricus, whom we call Kuriakose, who was about three years old. They were seized at Tarsus in Cilicia, the city of St Paul. The governor questioned the mother, and she confessed Christ. The little boy, taken up in the governor's arms, is said to have repeated after her, in his child's voice, “I am a Christian.” In anger the governor threw him down the steps of the tribunal, and he died. Julitta was tortured and beheaded. The details may have grown in the telling, but the memory of the Church has been steady: the faith of a mother and the faith of her child were stronger than the sword. Churches of the East and of the West honoured them early, and the name Kuriakose is cherished by Christians in Kerala to this day.

The readings for the day are chosen to help us understand them, and the seasonal readings of Kaitha speak to the same courage in ordinary life. James begins with a surprising command: “My brothers and sisters, whenever you face trials of any kind, consider it nothing but joy, because you know that the testing of your faith produces endurance; and let endurance have its full effect, so that you may be mature and complete, lacking in nothing.” Joy here is not a pleasant feeling. It is the steady confidence that nothing God allows is wasted. And James knows that we are not strong: “If any of you is lacking in wisdom, ask God, who gives to all generously and ungrudgingly, and it will be given you.” The only condition is “in faith, never doubting”, for the doubter is like a wave tossed by the wind. A person under trial does not first need a plan; he first needs to ask, and to keep on asking, with a heart that holds to God.

Mark's Gospel gives the same lesson in a hard scene. Jesus is hungry and sees a fig tree in leaf, but it has no fruit. He says to it, “May no one ever eat fruit from you again,” and the next morning the tree is withered to its roots. This is a sign in the manner of the prophets, who often pictured Israel as a tree that should have borne fruit (Hosea 9:10; Micah 7:1). Mark places the tree on either side of the cleansing of the Temple, and the message is for every believer and every community: leaves without fruit, religion without justice and love, cannot stand before God. Peter notices the tree, and Jesus turns the moment into a lesson on prayer: “Have faith in God. Truly I tell you, if you say to this mountain, ‘Be taken up and thrown into the sea,’ and if you do not doubt in your heart, but believe that what you say will come to pass, it will be done for you.” The mountain is whatever stands before the work of God and looks immovable. Then the surprise: “Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father in heaven may also forgive you your trespasses.” Faith that moves mountains cannot live beside a grudge.

The readings of the martyrs carry us from prayer to witness. In Daniel the three young men walk in the middle of the furnace: “They walked around in the midst of the flames, singing hymns to God and blessing the Lord.” Then Azariah stands in the fire and prays. His prayer, which the Church reads as the Prayer of Azariah, confesses the sins of the people, asks God not to abandon them for the sake of his name, and offers what remains when every sacrifice has been taken away: a contrite heart and a humble spirit. They had already said to the king what the martyrs would say after them: “If our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the furnace of blazing fire and out of your hand, O king, let him deliver us. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods and we will not worship the golden statue that you have set up.” That “but if not” is the faith of the martyrs: they trust God whether he rescues them or not. In the Acts of the Apostles Stephen, “full of grace and power”, is dragged before the council by false witnesses, and “all who sat in the council looked intently at him, and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.” The first martyr shows that witness shines before it bleeds.

The letter to the Hebrews gives the root of it all: “By faith we understand that the worlds were prepared by the word of God.” Abel, Enoch, Noah and Abraham each lived by what they could not see: “And without faith it is impossible to please God, for whoever would approach him must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him.” Abraham “set out, not knowing where he was going”, and lived in tents, for “he looked forward to the city that has foundations, whose architect and builder is God.” Jesus's own words in Matthew complete the picture and give a mother and a child their strength: “See, I am sending you out like sheep into the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” “When they hand you over, do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say; for what you are to say will be given to you at that time; for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.” A three-year-old could say no more than “I am a Christian”, and that was exactly what the Spirit gave. “Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father. And even the hairs of your head are all counted. So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” And the promise on which all martyrs rest: “Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven.”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers a mother and her little son. Julitta and her three-year-old Kuriakose were arrested at Tarsus, about the year 304, at the time of Emperor Diocletian. The ancient Acts tell us that the mother confessed Christ, and the little boy said the same: “I am a Christian.”

How can a child be so brave? Jesus himself answers: “When they hand you over, do not worry about how you are to speak or what you are to say; for what you are to say will be given to you at that time; for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you” (Matthew 10:19–20). The courage of the martyrs is the Spirit's gift.

And their courage is not so far from our own life. James says today, “Whenever you face trials of any kind, consider it nothing but joy” (James 1:2). He does not say that trials are pleasant. He says that faith tested produces endurance. If we lack wisdom, we are to ask God, who gives generously and ungrudgingly.

In the Gospel Jesus teaches us to pray with faith and to forgive: “Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father in heaven may also forgive you” (Mark 11:25). A grudge is a mountain that no prayer can move.

This week let us forgive one person and say one plain word of faith. “Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven” (Matthew 10:32). Saint Julitta and Saint Kuriakose, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Kuriakose was only three years old, but he said bravely, “I am a Christian.” When someone asks you about Jesus, you can say it too, kindly and happily. Jesus says that he knows each of us, even every hair on our head!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you called a mother and her little child to confess your name before the judge and you made them stronger than the sword. Give us a faith that rejoices in trials, that asks you for wisdom without doubt, and that forgives before it prays. When we are asked who we are, let your Spirit speak in us; when we are afraid, remind us that no sparrow falls without the Father. By the prayers of Saint Julitta and Saint Kuriakose, keep our children in your faith and bring us with them to the city whose builder is God. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.
