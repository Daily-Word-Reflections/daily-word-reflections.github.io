Word for the day

Reflection

The liturgical year ends with a crown. On the last Sunday of Qudash Edtha the Church proclaims Christ the King. Pope Pius XI instituted the feast in 1925 (Quas Primas) for a world that was trying to organise itself without God; the question is no less urgent today.

The readings show two kinds of kingship. Melchizedek, “king of Salem” (peace) and “priest of God Most High”, brings out bread and wine and blesses Abraham: a mysterious figure of Christ, King and Priest, who offers bread and wine. Centuries later Israel demands “a king like other nations”, and God tells Samuel: “they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them.” Every time we choose earthly powers over God, we repeat Israel's demand.

Before Pilate, bound and condemned, Jesus says: “My kingdom is not from this world.” His kingship does not come from armies, money or votes; it comes from the truth. “For this I was born… to testify to the truth. Everyone who belongs to the truth listens to my voice.” His throne is the Cross; his crown is of thorns.

Revelation shows the end: heaven opened, a rider called Faithful and True, his robe dipped in blood, his name “The Word of God”, and on his robe: “King of kings and Lord of lords.” The Church, the Bride, ends her year by bowing before her King and Bridegroom. The question for each of us: does Christ reign in my home, my work, my phone, my choices?

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear brothers and sisters, the liturgical year ends today with a crown. We proclaim Christ the King. Next Sunday a new year begins with the announcement of his coming; today we kneel before him as Lord of all.

In the first reading a mysterious king appears: Melchizedek, king of Salem, which means peace, and priest of God Most High. He brings bread and wine and blesses Abraham. The Fathers of the Church saw in him a figure of Christ, King of peace and eternal Priest, who offers bread and wine that become his Body and Blood.

Then we hear a sad story. Israel tells Samuel: “Give us a king like other nations.” God says: “They have not rejected you; they have rejected me as their king.” Brothers and sisters, how often do we do the same? We let money be king, or power, or what people say, or our phone. Christ is not removed from his throne by atheists only; he is pushed aside quietly by Christians who let other things rule.

Before Pilate Jesus stands bound. Pilate asks, “Are you a king?” Jesus answers: “My kingdom is not from this world… I came to testify to the truth.” His throne is the Cross; his crown is of thorns; his power is love. And Revelation shows him at the end: King of kings and Lord of lords.

So today, at the end of the year, let us ask honestly: does Christ reign in my home? in my business? in my words? in my screen? Let us give him the crown today. Jesus, reign in me. Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, on this last Sunday of the liturgical year we proclaim Christ as King of the universe. We sing, we crown His image, we carry Him in procession. But let us remember what kind of crown He wore on earth: a crown of thorns.

When Pilate asked Jesus whether He was a king, Jesus answered: my kingdom is not from this world. He came to testify to the truth, and everyone who belongs to the truth listens to His voice (John 18:36–37). Pilate had soldiers, a palace and the power of Rome. Jesus had nothing but truth and love. Yet two thousand years later Pilate is remembered only because his name is in our Creed, and Jesus reigns in the hearts of millions.

Above the Cross the soldiers nailed an inscription: this is the King of the Jews (Luke 23:38). They meant it as a mockery, but it was the truth. Christ reigns from the Cross. His power is not to take life but to give it. And the first one to recognise His kingship was a dying criminal who said: Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom. Jesus answered: today you will be with me in Paradise (Luke 23:42–43).

So the real question today is not whether Christ is King of the universe. He is, whether the world accepts it or not. The question is whether He is King of my heart. Does He reign over my tongue, my money, my phone, my family, my work, my politics? Or have I kept some area of my life closed to Him, a little kingdom of my own?

As we end this liturgical year, let us open every door to Christ. Let us serve as He served, especially the least of His brothers and sisters. And when we receive Him in this Holy Qurbana, let us say with the good thief: Jesus, remember me in your kingdom. That prayer is enough to save a whole life.

Homily option 3 · Teaching homily: the Kingship of Christ in the Church's teaching

Pope Pius XI established this feast in 1925 (Quas Primas), when nations were turning away from God. Its message is even more urgent today.

1. Why Christ is King

  • By nature: He is the Son of God (Colossians 1:15–17).
  • By conquest: He redeemed us by His blood (1 Peter 1:18–19).
  • By right: all authority given to Him (Matthew 28:18).
📖 Daniel 7:13–14
📖 Revelation 1:5–6

Apply: Pray the Creed slowly, attentive to the words about Christ.

2. His Kingdom: already and not yet

  • Present in mystery in the Church (Lumen Gentium 3).
  • Growing like a seed.
  • Fulfilled at His coming in glory (CCC 671).
📖 Isaiah 9:6–7
📖 1 Corinthians 15:24–28

Apply: Pray daily: Thy kingdom come.

3. Christ and society

  • Christians must bring the Gospel into public life (CCC 2105).
  • Justice, peace, dignity of life, care for the poor.
  • Freedom of conscience respected; the Gospel proposed, not imposed.
📖 Psalm 72:12–14
📖 Matthew 25:31–40

Apply: Do one concrete act of justice or charity this week.

Christ's Kingship is the foundation of our hope and our mission.

Every Christian shares His royal office by serving.

Take-home resolution: This week I will pray Thy kingdom come with attention and do one act of justice.

Dear brothers and sisters, this feast is younger than many of us think. Pope Pius XI established it in 1925, in the encyclical Quas Primas. Europe had just come out of a terrible war, ideologies were rising that put the State or the race in the place of God, and many nations were living as if God did not exist. The Pope proclaimed: Christ is King, of individuals, of families and of societies.

Why is Christ King? First, by His very nature: He is the eternal Son of God, through whom and for whom all things were created (Colossians 1:15–17). Second, by conquest: He redeemed us, not with silver or gold, but with His precious blood (1 Peter 1:18–19). Third, because the Father has given Him all authority in heaven and on earth (Matthew 28:18). Daniel saw one like a son of man receiving an everlasting dominion (Daniel 7:13–14).

His Kingdom is already present and not yet complete. The Second Vatican Council says that the Church is the Kingdom of Christ already present in mystery (Lumen Gentium 3). It grows quietly like a mustard seed. But it will be fulfilled only when Christ comes again in glory, when He hands over the Kingdom to the Father and God will be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:24–28; CCC 671). Until then we pray every day: Thy kingdom come.

Does this feast have anything to say to society? Yes. The Catechism teaches that Christians have the duty to bring the spirit of the Gospel into the life of society, its customs, laws and structures (CCC 2105). That means defending the dignity of every human life from conception to natural death, working for justice and peace, caring for the poor and the migrant. It does not mean forcing faith on anyone; the Church respects freedom of conscience. We propose Christ by our witness.

Through Baptism we share in Christ's royal office, which means to serve (CCC 786). The King washed feet. So let us end this liturgical year by renewing our allegiance to Christ, and let us show it by serving the least of His brothers and sisters. Then His Kingdom will come, a little more, in our homes and in our land.
