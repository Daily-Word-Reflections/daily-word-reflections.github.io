The Ascension of our Lord

Readings

  • First Reading 2 Kings 2:1–12 · Elijah is taken up to heaven
  • Second Reading Acts 1:6–11 · As they were watching, he was lifted up
  • Epistle 1 Timothy 2:1–7 · One mediator between God and humankind
  • Gospel Luke 24:44–53 · He withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven

Word for the day

“While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” Luke 24:51

Reflection

Forty days after Easter the Church celebrates the Ascension of our Lord. The Acts of the Apostles tells us that after his suffering Jesus “presented himself alive to them by many convincing proofs, appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God” (Acts 1:3), and then, from the Mount of Olives, he was taken up. Our Syro-Malabar Church keeps this day on the Thursday, as the ancient Church did, as a Feast of our Lord and a day of obligation; in the Syriac tradition it is called Sullaqa, the going up. (The Latin Church in India celebrates it this year on Sunday 9 May.) What do we celebrate? Not that Jesus has left us. The Creed says: “He ascended into heaven, and is seated at the right hand of the Father.” The same Jesus who was born of the Virgin Mary, who died and rose in his body, now enters with that body, glorified and bearing the wounds, into the glory of God. For the first time a human nature, our nature, is at the right hand of the Father. That is why the Church can sing on this day with joy rather than sadness. Where the Head has gone, the members hope to follow (Catechism 661). And from there, Peter will say at Pentecost, “Being therefore exalted at the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this that you both see and hear” (Acts 2:33). The Ascension is the hinge between Easter and Pentecost.

The calendar opens the feast with a story from the Old Testament that the first Christians read as a prophecy of this day: the taking up of Elijah. The old prophet knows that the Lord is about to take him, and three times he tells his disciple Elisha to stay behind, at Gilgal, at Bethel, at Jericho. Three times Elisha answers, “As the Lord lives, and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” At the Jordan Elijah strikes the water with his mantle and they cross on dry ground. Then the master asks, “Tell me what I may do for you, before I am taken from you.” Elisha asks a great thing: “Please let me inherit a double share of your spirit.” The answer is a condition: “if you see me as I am being taken from you, it will be granted you”. And then “a chariot of fire and horses of fire separated the two of them, and Elijah ascended in a whirlwind into heaven.” Elisha keeps watching and cries out, “Father, father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!” Elisha saw, and so he received the spirit of his master; the mantle that fell from Elijah became his, and with it he struck the Jordan and it parted again. The disciples of Jesus are in Elisha's place. They watch their Master go up, and ten days later they will receive, not a double share of a prophet's spirit, but the Holy Spirit himself, the Spirit of the Son. Jesus had said it plainly: “it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you” (John 16:7). The mantle of Christ is the Church's mission, and it has fallen on us.

In the Acts of the Apostles the disciples ask one last question: “Lord, is this the time when you will restore the kingdom to Israel?” Even after the Resurrection they are thinking of a kingdom like the kingdoms they knew. Jesus does not scold them; he redirects them: “It is not for you to know the times or periods that the Father has set by his own authority. But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.” The kingdom will come, but not by a calendar we can study; it will come through witnesses filled with the Spirit, moving outward from Jerusalem. One of those witnesses, Thomas, would carry the Gospel to the ends of the earth as Kerala then was. “When he had said this, as they were watching, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight.” In Scripture the cloud is the sign of God's presence: the cloud that led Israel in the desert, the cloud that filled the Temple, the cloud of the Transfiguration. Jesus is not travelling through space to a distant place; he is entering the presence of God, where he is near to every place. And then two men in white robes ask the question that the Church hears every year on this feast: “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven? This Jesus, who has been taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven.” The Ascension does not give us permission to stand staring at the sky. It sends us back to Jerusalem, to prayer, to the Spirit and to the work of witness, until he comes again.

The Epistle shows us what Christ does now at the right hand of the Father. “First of all, then, I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for everyone, for kings and all who are in high positions, so that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and dignity.” Why should the Church pray for everyone, including rulers who may not know God? Because God “desires everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth. For there is one God; there is also one mediator between God and humankind, Christ Jesus, himself human, who gave himself a ransom for all”. Notice the words “himself human”. The one Mediator who has entered heaven is a man, our brother, who has not laid aside our nature. The Letter to the Hebrews says that “he entered into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf” (Hebrews 9:24), and that “he always lives to make intercession” for those who approach God through him (Hebrews 7:25). When our Church prays in the Holy Qurbana for the Pope and the bishops, for rulers and for the whole world, for the sick and the departed, she prays with the ascended Christ and through him. Our prayers for the saints' intercession and for one another do not compete with his one mediation; they share in it, as members share in the life of the Head (Catechism 2634–2636).

Luke's Gospel ends with the same event told in a few unforgettable lines. Jesus opens their minds to understand the Scriptures: “Thus it is written, that the Messiah is to suffer and to rise from the dead on the third day, and that repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things.” He promises the gift of the Father: “so stay here in the city until you have been clothed with power from on high.” Then “he led them out as far as Bethany, and, lifting up his hands, he blessed them. While he was blessing them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.” The last image the apostles kept of Jesus on earth was of his raised, pierced hands, blessing them. Like a priest at the end of the Qurbana, he blesses and goes, and the blessing remains. And the disciples' response is surprising: “they worshiped him, and returned to Jerusalem with great joy; and they were continually in the temple blessing God.” Joy, not sorrow; worship, not loss. Acts adds that in the upper room they “were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). From tomorrow the Church enters these days of waiting with the Blessed Virgin Mary; many families and parishes pray a novena to the Holy Spirit, as Pope Leo XIII asked in 1897. Let us go up the mountain today with the apostles, receive his blessing, and come down with great joy to wait and pray for the Spirit.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, forty days after Easter Jesus went up to heaven. Today we celebrate his Ascension, a great Feast of our Lord and a day of obligation.

In the first reading Elijah is taken up to heaven in a chariot of fire. His disciple Elisha would not leave him, and asked for “a double share of your spirit” (2 Kings 2:9). Because Elisha saw his master go, he received his spirit and his mantle. The apostles saw Jesus go up, and ten days later they received the Holy Spirit.

In Acts the apostles ask about the kingdom. Jesus answers, “you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you will be my witnesses... to the ends of the earth” (Acts 1:8). Then a cloud took him out of their sight, and two angels said, “Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking up toward heaven?” (Acts 1:11). The Ascension does not tell us to stare at the sky. It sends us to be witnesses.

Paul tells Timothy that there is “one mediator between God and humankind, Christ Jesus, himself human” (1 Timothy 2:5). A man, our brother, is now at the right hand of the Father, praying for us. That is why we pray for everyone, for rulers, for the sick, for the whole world.

In the Gospel Jesus lifts up his hands and blesses his disciples, and while he is blessing them he is carried up into heaven. And they go back “with great joy” (Luke 24:52). His blessing remains over us.

From tomorrow, with Mary and the apostles, we wait and pray for the Holy Spirit. Let us begin a novena in our homes. Christ has gone ahead to prepare a place for us. Alleluia! Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, today we celebrate the Ascension of our Lord. Forty days after Easter, while he was blessing his disciples, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven (Luke 24:51). But he did not leave behind the body that was born of Mary and nailed to the Cross. He took our human nature with him into heaven.

Daniel saw in vision one like a human being coming with the clouds of heaven, and to him was given dominion and glory (Daniel 7:13–14). That is the Ascension. A man, our brother, now sits at the right hand of the Father. Hebrews says: we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses. Let us therefore approach the throne of grace with boldness (Hebrews 4:15–16).

The Psalm asks: who shall ascend the hill of the LORD? Lift up your heads, O gates, that the King of glory may come in (Psalm 24:3, 7). Christ has gone in, and he said: in my Father's house there are many dwelling places. I go to prepare a place for you (John 14:2). Heaven is not a dream. It is our home, and the door has been opened by one of us.

So Paul tells us: if you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God (Colossians 3:1). That does not mean neglecting the earth. It means living here with our hearts set there. In every Holy Qurbana the priest invites us to lift up our minds on high. Let us really lift them up.

This week let us think a little each day about heaven, our true home. Let us give one worry each day to the Lord at the Father's right hand. And let us live on earth as people whose Head is already in glory. Alleluia! Amen.

Dear friends, Luke tells us what Jesus did just before his Ascension. He said: everything written about me in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms must be fulfilled. Then he opened their minds to understand the scriptures (Luke 24:44–45).

Long ago, when Ezra read the book of the law to the people, the Levites gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading (Nehemiah 8:8). Now the risen Lord himself gives the sense of the whole Bible: it speaks of him. Until Pentecost, let us read the Gospel of the day each morning, and ask him to open our minds too.

Then he said: repentance and forgiveness of sins is to be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things (Luke 24:47–48). Jonah preached unwillingly, and yet the people of Nineveh believed God (Jonah 3:5). The apostles would preach willingly, and the Gospel would reach the ends of the earth, even our own Kerala through Mar Thoma.

Finally, while he was blessing them, he was carried up into heaven. And they worshiped him, and returned to Jerusalem with great joy; and they were continually in the temple blessing God (Luke 24:52–53). The Psalm sings: God has gone up with a shout; sing praises to our King (Psalm 47:5–6). They were not sad. They knew he had not abandoned them, and they waited in praise.

So in these days before Pentecost let us read his word, invite one person back to confession or to the Holy Qurbana, and gather our families for the novena to the Holy Spirit. Let us wait as the apostles waited, with Mary, in joy and in prayer. Alleluia! Amen.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, King of glory, today you ascended above the heavens and took our human nature to the right hand of the Father. As you blessed your apostles with raised hands, bless us also, and fill us with their great joy. You are the one mediator between God and humankind: intercede for us, for our families and for the whole world. Do not let us stand idly looking up, but send us back to pray and to bear witness. Prepare our hearts, with your Mother Mary, for the Spirit whom you have promised, and draw us, your members, to the place where you, our Head, have gone before us. Amen. Alleluia!
