Word for the day

“For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and him crucified.” 1 Corinthians 2:2

Reflection

Paul arrived in Corinth after a hard experience in Athens, where his learned speech on the Areopagus had won only a few believers (Acts 17:32–34). Corinth was a rich port city that loved clever talk and public speakers. Paul decided to do the opposite of what would impress it. “When I came to you, brothers and sisters, I did not come proclaiming the mystery of God to you in lofty words or wisdom. For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and him crucified.” He does not hide his own condition: “And I came to you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling.” Yet the Church of Corinth was born from that trembling preacher, because something other than eloquence was at work: “My speech and my proclamation were not with plausible words of wisdom, but with a demonstration of the Spirit and of power, so that your faith might rest not on human wisdom but on the power of God.” We read this in the season of the Resurrection, and it reminds us that the Risen Lord is the Crucified Lord. He showed Thomas his hands and his side (John 20:27); his wounds were not erased by Easter but glorified. That is why a Syro-Malabar Christian keeps Friday as the day of the Cross even in the weeks of Easter joy, and why the crucifix and the empty tomb are never separated in our faith.

In the Gospel Jesus calls the crowd and says: “Listen and understand: it is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but it is what comes out of the mouth that defiles.” The context is a question about washing hands before meals according to the tradition of the elders. The care of the Pharisees for purity was sincere, and the debate is one within the family of Israel, among teachers who all loved the Law; the Church reads it without any contempt for the Jewish people, from whom Jesus, his mother and the apostles came. Jesus moves the question from the hands to the heart. When Peter asks for an explanation, he says: “But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and this is what defiles. For out of the heart come evil intentions, murder, adultery, fornication, theft, false witness, slander. These are what defile a person, but to eat with unwashed hands does not defile.” Notice where the list begins: with evil intentions, the thoughts no one else sees. And notice where it ends: false witness and slander, sins of the tongue. Between the hidden thought and the spoken word lies the whole of our moral life. Outward observance matters, and the Church keeps her fasts and her rites with love; but Jesus will not let any rite take the place of a clean heart.

Today a great deal of what ‘comes out of the mouth’ comes out through the fingers. A message forwarded without checking, a rumour about a neighbour's daughter passed on in a family group, a mocking comment under a priest's photograph: Jesus' list has not gone out of date. False witness and slander now travel in seconds to hundreds of people. The question Jesus asks is not first about our phones but about our hearts: what is inside, that comes out so easily? Paul's answer is the Crucified. A heart that has decided to know Christ crucified becomes slow to condemn, because it knows what it cost to forgive its own sins. Tomorrow the month of May begins, and in many of our homes the Marian month devotion will be prayed each evening. The Blessed Virgin Mary is the model of the clean heart: of her the Gospel says twice that she treasured the words and events of her Son and pondered them in her heart (Luke 2:19, 51). What came out of her mouth was the Magnificat. Let us ask our Mother to teach us, through this month, to keep our hearts clean, so that our words, spoken or typed, may build up and not destroy.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul came to Corinth, a city that loved fine speeches, and he decided to know nothing among them except Jesus Christ, and him crucified (1 Corinthians 2:2). He came in weakness, fear and much trembling, and yet a Church was born.

Why? Because his preaching was a demonstration of the Spirit and of power, so that their faith might rest not on human wisdom but on the power of God (1 Corinthians 2:4–5). In this Easter season, let us remember: the Risen Lord is the Crucified Lord. He still bears his wounds, now shining with glory.

In the Gospel Jesus says: it is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth (Matthew 15:11). What comes out of the mouth comes from the heart: evil intentions, murder, adultery, theft, false witness, slander (Matthew 15:18–19).

Today many words come out not only through our mouths but through our phones. Before we speak or forward anything about another person, let us ask: is it true, is it kind, is it necessary?

Tomorrow the month of May begins, the month of our Blessed Mother, who kept the words of her Son in her heart. Let us ask her for a clean heart, so that our words may always build up. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says that what makes us bad is not the food we eat, but the bad words that come out of our heart. Tonight, let us ask Jesus for a clean heart, so that only kind words come out of our mouths!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings look beneath the surface. Paul reminds the Corinthians that he came to them not with polished rhetoric but with the message of Christ crucified, so that their faith would rest on God’s power and not on human cleverness. In Matthew, Jesus teaches that what defiles a person is not food taken in through the mouth but evil that comes out of the heart. God looks at the heart; the Cross is the power that changes it.

1 Corinthians 2:1–5 · Nothing except Jesus Christ, and him crucified

Setting.

Corinth was a rich, busy port city where public speakers were admired for their eloquence. Paul founded the Church there around AD 50–51, after his visit to Athens (Acts 17–18). By the time of this letter the Corinthians were dividing into parties around favourite preachers (1:12). Paul has just said that the message of the Cross is foolishness to the world but the power of God (1:18), and now he recalls how he first preached to them.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: not lofty words; only Christ crucified.
  • v. 3: Paul’s own weakness and fear.
  • vv. 4–5: the Spirit and power, so that faith may rest on God.

Key words.

  • mystērion / martyrion, mystery / testimony: A real textual variant in v. 1. Some ancient manuscripts read “mystery”, others “testimony”. The NRSV follows “mystery”; the POC follows “testimony”. Both fit Paul’s thought.
  • estaurōmenon, crucified: A perfect participle: Christ remains the Crucified One. The risen Lord still bears his wounds (John 20:27).
  • apodeixis, demonstration: A technical word of the schools of rhetoric for a convincing proof. Paul’s proof was not an argument but the work of the Spirit in the lives of those who believed.

Verse by verse.

“I did not come proclaiming the mystery of God to you in lofty words or wisdom.” (2:1)

Paul was not against learning; he was against anything that puts the preacher in place of the message.

“For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and him crucified.” (2:2)

This is not a narrowing but a centre. Everything Paul teaches, the Resurrection included, flows from this.

“And I came to you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling.” (2:3)

The great apostle was afraid. God’s strength showed itself in his weakness, as the Lord would tell him: “power is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9).

“so that your faith might rest not on human wisdom but on the power of God.” (2:5)

Faith built on a speaker’s charm falls when the speaker fails. Faith built on God stands.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read these verses as a rule for every preacher and catechist: the aim is not to win admiration but to bring people to Christ. St Augustine, himself a trained teacher of rhetoric, taught in On Christian Teaching that eloquence must serve truth and that the preacher’s life speaks louder than his words (in substance). The Catechism teaches that faith rests on the authority of God who reveals, not on the evidence of human reasoning alone (CCC 156).

Matthew 15:10–20 · What comes out of the heart

Setting.

Some Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem have asked why Jesus’ disciples do not wash their hands before eating, as “the tradition of the elders” required (15:1–2). This ritual washing was a matter of religious purity, not hygiene. The Pharisees were devout men who wanted to bring holiness into daily life. Jesus’ dispute with them is a debate within Judaism about what God truly asks. Today’s reading is his answer, first to the crowd, then to the disciples.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: to the crowd: not what goes in, but what comes out, defiles.
  • vv. 12–14: the disciples report the Pharisees’ offense; the plant not planted by the Father; blind guides.
  • vv. 15–20: to the disciples: the heart is the source of evil; washing hands does not defile.

Key words.

  • koinoō, defile: Literally “make common”, that is, unfit for what is holy. Jesus moves the question of holiness from the surface to the heart.
  • kardia, heart: In the Bible, not only feelings but the inner centre of the person, where thought, will and decision are found.
  • parabolē, parable: Peter calls the saying a parable (v. 15): a short, puzzling word that needs to be opened. The disciples still need to be taught.

Verse by verse.

“it is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but it is what comes out of the mouth that defiles.” (15:11)

Words come from the heart. A person is known by what comes out of him: speech, attitudes, actions.

“they are blind guides of the blind.” (15:14)

A warning to every teacher in the Church first of all. Those who guide others must first let themselves be enlightened.

“But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and this is what defiles.” (15:18)

Jesus does not despise outward practice; he teaches what gives it value.

“to eat with unwashed hands does not defile.” (15:20)

Mark, telling the same story, adds that by this Jesus “declared all foods clean” (Mark 7:19). Matthew, probably writing for a community close to Jewish practice, keeps the focus on hand-washing.

How the Church reads it.

The list of evils in v. 19 largely follows the order of the second part of the Ten Commandments: murder, adultery, theft, false witness. Jesus is not abolishing the law but bringing it to the heart. The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that the heart is the seat of the moral personality (CCC 2517), and it says that the New Law purifies the heart, the root of human acts (CCC 1968).

For study and teaching

  • Does my faith rest more on a favourite preacher or on the power of God? What would change if that preacher were gone?
  • Jesus says evil comes out of the heart. What have I said or done this week that shows me what my heart needs?
