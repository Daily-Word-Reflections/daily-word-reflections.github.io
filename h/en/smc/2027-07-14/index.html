Readings

  • Epistle 2 Corinthians 4:14–5:1 · Our inner nature is renewed day by day; a building from God

Word for the day

“Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” John 12:24

Reflection

Kaitha is the season of harvest, and today the readings tell us how a harvest begins: with a seed that goes under the ground. Paul writes to the Corinthians from a life of daily pressure, and the verse that opens our reading is the root of his courage: “because we know that the one who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also with Jesus, and will bring us with you into his presence.” Just before, he had described himself and his companions as treasure carried in clay jars: “We are afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed.” What keeps the jar from breaking is not its own strength but the faith that the One who raised Jesus will raise us. And Paul adds that none of this is for himself: “Yes, everything is for your sake, so that grace, as it extends to more and more people, may increase thanksgiving, to the glory of God.” A suffering that is offered for others is already becoming thanksgiving.

Then comes one of the most consoling sentences in Scripture: “So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” Anyone who has watched a parent grow old, or who feels the years in his own knees at the Sunday Qurbana, knows the first half of that sentence. Paul wants us to believe the second half just as firmly: the person who prays, forgives and gives is growing younger in what matters. He does not make light of pain; he himself had been beaten, shipwrecked and stoned. But he weighs it against glory: “For this slight momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all measure, because we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen; for what can be seen is temporary, but what cannot be seen is eternal.” The word “preparing” matters: suffering joined to Christ is not wasted; it is work being done in us.

The reading ends with an image from Paul's own trade, for he was a tentmaker (Acts 18:3): “For we know that if the earthly tent we live in is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” A tent is a home for a journey, folded away when the journey ends. Paul does not despise the body, for the Church believes in the resurrection of the body, the very flesh we now carry; but he knows that our present life is a tent, and that God is preparing a house which no monsoon, no fire and no death can bring down. For the families of Kerala who have laid parents and children in the churchyard, this is no poetry. It is the ground on which they stand when they say at the grave: we believe in the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come.

The Gospel puts this hope in the hands of Jesus. Some Greeks had come to Philip saying, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus”, and Jesus answers by saying that the hour has come: “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified.” Then he shows what glory looks like: “Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” Every farmer who sows in the monsoon soil knows this. A seed kept in a tin is safe and useless; a seed buried in wet earth seems lost and becomes a field. Jesus is speaking first of himself, for the Cross is the grain falling, and the Church is the harvest. Then he opens the saying to all who follow: “Those who love their life lose it, and those who hate their life in this world will keep it for eternal life.” To “hate” one's life is a way of speaking in Semitic idiom, meaning to love it less than him. “Whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am, there will my servant be also.” The martyrs of the Church are the grain that fell: the blood of the martyrs, the early Christians said, is the seed of the Church. Even Jesus is not spared the trembling: “Now my soul is troubled.” He does not pretend. He asks the question that every one of us has asked and then gives himself to the Father: “No, it is for this reason that I have come to this hour. Father, glorify your name.” And the voice from heaven answers: “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.”

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul tells us today, “We do not lose heart.” Whoever has watched a parent grow old, or has felt the weight of years, understands how much those words are worth. Our outer nature is wasting away, he says, but our inner nature is being renewed day by day (2 Corinthians 4:16).

Why does Paul speak with such confidence? Because he knows that the One who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also (2 Corinthians 4:14). Our troubles are real, but they are slight compared with the glory being prepared. We look not at what can be seen, but at what cannot be seen.

Paul was a tentmaker, and he says that our earthly body is like a tent. When it is folded up, God has a building ready, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens (2 Corinthians 5:1). That is why we stand at the grave of our dear ones with hope.

In the Gospel Jesus says, “Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit” (John 12:24). A seed in a tin is safe and useless. A seed in the wet earth looks lost and becomes a field. Jesus himself is that grain, and so are all who give their lives for others.

This week let us find one place where we can let a seed fall: a sharp word we keep back, an hour we give to someone old or sick, a quarrel we end. He who serves Jesus must follow him, and where he is, there will his servant be also (John 12:26). Amen.

For children

Children, a seed looks lost when the farmer puts it in the mud, but then it becomes a green plant with many grains! Jesus said that is how love works. When we give something up for someone else, God makes something beautiful grow from it.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, grain of wheat fallen into the earth, you died to bring forth a great harvest. Give us the courage not to lose heart when our bodies grow weak or our hopes seem buried. Renew our inner life day by day; teach us to look at what cannot be seen; and make us willing to fall into the earth and die to ourselves, so that the grace we receive may grow into thanksgiving for many. Prepare for us, and for all our beloved dead, the house not made with hands. You live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about a death that bears fruit. Paul says that his body is wasting away while his inner self is renewed day by day, and he looks past what can be seen to a house God has prepared. In John, Jesus says that the hour of his glory is the hour when a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, and he invites the one who serves him to follow where he goes. What looks like loss is the way the Father brings life to many.

2 Corinthians 4:14–5:1 · We do not lose heart

Setting.

Paul has just described the apostle’s life as a treasure carried in clay jars (4:7), always being given up to death for Jesus’ sake so that the life of Jesus may show in us (4:10–12). Today’s verses give the reason he does not give up: the one who raised Jesus will raise us.

The passage then moves into chapter 5, where Paul uses the image of a tent and a house. Tents were Paul’s own trade (Acts 18:3), and the tent that is taken down is his body and its earthly life. What follows in 5:1 is not a denial of the body but a hope that God will give a lasting home.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: the ground of hope: God raised Jesus and will raise us; everything is for your sake, for thanksgiving to God’s glory.
  • v. 16: therefore we do not lose heart: outer nature wasting, inner nature renewed.
  • vv. 17–18: the slight, passing affliction weighed against eternal glory; looking at what is unseen.
  • 5:1: the earthly tent destroyed; a building from God, eternal in the heavens.

Key words.

  • ho esō hēmōn anthrōpos, our inner nature (literally, the inner man): The self that God is making new, as opposed to the outer self that ages and suffers. It is not a part that escapes the body; it is the person whom grace is renewing from within, as Paul says in Romans 12:2.
  • skēnos, tent: A temporary dwelling. The word calls up Israel’s tent in the wilderness, a home that moves on, and the Word who “pitched his tent” among us (John 1:14). Our bodily life is a pilgrim’s tent; the end of the journey is God’s house.
  • baros doxēs, weight of glory: Paul sets “weight” against “slight”. In Hebrew “glory” (kābôd) also means heaviness, importance. Our troubles, however real, are light beside the glory that will weigh them down.

Verse by verse.

“because we know that the one who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also with Jesus” (4:14)

The Resurrection of Jesus is the pledge of ours. Paul’s courage is not optimism about his circumstances, but faith in what God has already done.

“So we do not lose heart. Even though our outer nature is wasting away, our inner nature is being renewed day by day.” (4:16)

Paul does not say that the outer self does not matter or that it does not hurt. He speaks of two things happening at once: ageing and renewal. Baptism has begun a new life in us that daily prayer and the sacraments feed.

“because we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen; for what can be seen is temporary, but what cannot be seen is eternal.” (4:18)

Faith is a way of seeing. The affliction is seen, the glory is not yet seen, but Paul trusts the unseen as more real.

“we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.” (5:1)

The hope is a home. Paul does not describe it in detail; he is content to say that it comes from God and lasts.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in these verses the Christian meaning of death: through Christ, death opens into the house of the Father, and the Church prays for the dead in that hope (CCC 1010). The Catechism also teaches that the renewal of the inner self begins in Baptism and goes on through a life of grace (CCC 1265–1266). The passage is often read at funerals for this reason.

John 12:23–28 · The grain of wheat

Setting.

In Jerusalem for the Passover, some Greeks asked to see Jesus (12:20–22). Their request is the sign for which he has waited. Throughout the Gospel he has said that his “hour” had not yet come (2:4; 7:30; 8:20). Now he says: “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified.” The glory John speaks of is the glory of the Cross and Resurrection together.

Verses 27–28 are John’s version of Gethsemane. Jesus’ soul is troubled; he faces the prospect of death and chooses the Father’s will. The Father answers with a voice from heaven, as at the baptism (Mark 1:11) and the Transfiguration (Mark 9:7).

Structure.

  • v. 23: the hour of glory has come.
  • v. 24: the parable of the grain of wheat.
  • vv. 25–26: those who give up their life keep it; the servant follows Jesus and is honoured by the Father.
  • vv. 27–28: the troubled soul; “it is for this reason that I have come”; the Father’s voice.

Key words.

  • kokkos sitou, grain of wheat: A single seed. The picture is plain to any farmer: the seed buried in the ground seems to disappear, and from its death comes a harvest. Jesus applies it to himself and to his followers.
  • psychē, life, soul: The word can mean both biological life and the whole self. To “hate” life “in this world” is a Semitic way of saying: to love it less than God. It is not contempt for life, which is God’s gift.
  • doxazō, to glorify: In John, glory is shown in the lifting up on the Cross (12:32–33). The Father has glorified his name in Jesus’ signs and will glorify it again in the Resurrection.

Verse by verse.

“Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” (12:24)

The seed is not destroyed; it changes. Jesus’ death will not be the end of him but the beginning of a harvest, the Church gathered from every nation, beginning with the Greeks who came.

“Whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am, there will my servant be also.” (12:26)

Discipleship is following to the place where Jesus goes, which includes the Cross. And the promise is the same: where he is, there the servant will be.

“No, it is for this reason that I have come to this hour.” (12:27)

Jesus feels the fear, and he does not hide it. He does not ask to be saved from the hour, because the hour is the reason he came.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the grain of wheat as the Paschal mystery: the death and Resurrection of Christ, from which the Church and the Eucharist come. St Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom, wrote (in substance) that he was God’s wheat, to be ground by the teeth of wild beasts to become Christ’s pure bread. The Catechism teaches that Jesus’ death was the voluntary offering of his life out of love for the Father and for us (CCC 606–609), and that his “hour” is the hour of the Cross and Resurrection.

Questions people ask

Paul says that “what can be seen is temporary”. Does this mean our life and body in this world do not matter?

No. The Church teaches that the body is good and that it will rise: God made it, Christ took our flesh, and the Resurrection includes the body. Paul’s point is about where we place our final trust. Because the things we see do pass, we should not stake our lives on them alone; and because God has promised a house that lasts, we can care for the body, for the sick and for creation with hope, not with despair.

For study and teaching

  • Paul sets “slight” affliction against an “eternal weight of glory”. What burden of mine might look different if I weighed it against that glory?
  • Jesus says the grain must fall and die to bear fruit. Where is God asking me to let something go so that others may receive life?
