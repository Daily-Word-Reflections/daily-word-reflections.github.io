Word for the day

“Then the owner of the vineyard said, ‘What shall I do? I will send my beloved son; perhaps they will respect him.’” Luke 20:13

Reflection

Paul is near the end of his first letter to Timothy, the young bishop of Ephesus, and he gathers everything into one solemn charge. He does not give it in his own name alone. He calls two witnesses into the room: “In the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession, I charge you to keep the commandment without spot or blame until the manifestation of our Lord Jesus Christ.” The first witness is the God who gives life to all things: in this Easter season we hear that name with new ears, for the God who gives life is the God who raised Jesus from the tomb. The second witness is Jesus standing before Pilate. He was bound, mocked and alone, and yet he spoke the truth calmly: he was a king, and his kingdom was not from this world. That quiet answer is what Paul calls “the good confession”. One verse earlier Paul reminded Timothy that he too had made the good confession “in the presence of many witnesses”, most likely on the day of his Baptism. Every Christian has stood where Timothy stood. At the font we, or our godparents for us, renounced Satan and confessed the faith; at every Holy Qurbana we confess it again in the Creed. Paul's charge is that we keep that confession clean until the Lord appears.

Then the letter breaks into praise, as if Paul cannot mention the coming of Christ without singing. God “will bring about at the right time” the appearing of his Son: “he who is the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords. It is he alone who has immortality and dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see; to him be honor and eternal dominion. Amen.” These lines sound like a hymn the Church at Ephesus already sang. They tell us two things at once. God is utterly beyond us: no eye can bear his light, no mind can hold him. And yet this same God has made himself known, for the One who stood before Pilate is the image of the invisible God. In the Risen Christ the unapproachable light has come near. On Easter evening the disciples touched him and ate with him. Our Syro-Malabar Qurbana keeps both truths in its prayers: it adores the hidden majesty of God with deep bows and veiled gifts, and in the same celebration it gives us the Body and Blood of the Lord to eat and drink. Holy fear and holy nearness belong together.

In the Gospel Jesus tells a parable in the Temple during his last days in Jerusalem. A man plants a vineyard, leases it to tenants and goes away. At harvest he sends a slave for his share, then a second, then a third; each is beaten and sent away empty-handed. “Then the owner of the vineyard said, ‘What shall I do? I will send my beloved son; perhaps they will respect him.’” The tenants reason coldly: “This is the heir; let us kill him so that the inheritance may be ours.” They throw him out of the vineyard and kill him. Jesus tells this story a few days before he himself is led outside the city wall to be crucified. The parable is aimed at the leaders who were plotting against him, and Luke says they understood it; it is not a charge against the Jewish people, whom the Church honours as the first tenants of God's vineyard and to whom God's gifts and calling remain irrevocable. The story is a mirror for us. The vineyard is now the Church, and we are its tenants. God still sends his messengers, still asks for the fruit of faith, mercy and justice. The rejected Son is now the Risen Son: the very next line of the Gospel recalls the psalm, “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone.” Easter is God's answer to the tenants. What men threw out of the vineyard, God set at the corner of a new house, and he has given that house to us. The question for this Monday is simple: when the Owner sends for the fruit, what will he find in my part of the vineyard?

Full homily

Dear friends, on this Monday of the fourth week of Easter, Paul gives Timothy a solemn charge, and Jesus tells a story that is hard to forget.

Paul calls two witnesses: the God who gives life to all things, and Christ Jesus, who made the good confession before Pilate. Jesus stood bound and alone, and spoke the truth calmly. Timothy made his own good confession at his Baptism. So did we. Paul's charge is for us too: keep that confession clean until the Lord comes.

Then Paul sings: God is “the King of kings and Lord of lords”, who “dwells in unapproachable light”. No one can see him. And yet in the Risen Christ that light has come near: the disciples touched him and ate with him. In the Holy Qurbana we bow before the hidden majesty of God, and we receive the Lord himself.

In the Gospel the owner of the vineyard sends servant after servant, and at last he says: “I will send my beloved son; perhaps they will respect him.” They kill him. Jesus told this story days before he was led outside the city to die. It was a warning to those who plotted against him; it is a mirror for us.

Today we are the tenants. The vineyard is the Church. God still asks for fruit: faith, mercy, justice. And God has answered the tenants with Easter: the rejected stone has become the cornerstone.

So let us ask ourselves today: what fruit will the Owner find in my part of the vineyard? Let us give him a good share this week. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about a vineyard. The owner sent his own dear son, but the farmers did not welcome him. Jesus was talking about himself!

God has given us his vineyard, the Church, to look after. Every kind thing you do is a sweet grape for God!

Prayer

God our Father, you give life to all things, and you raised your beloved Son whom the builders rejected. Make us faithful tenants of your vineyard, the Church. Keep our baptismal confession clean until the appearing of our Lord, give us courage to speak the truth as he spoke it before Pilate, and let our lives bear the fruit you seek. To you, King of kings and Lord of lords, who dwell in unapproachable light, be honour and eternal dominion, with your Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings today put a son in front of hostile power. Paul charges Timothy “in the presence of God” and of Christ Jesus, who “made the good confession” before Pontius Pilate, and he ends with a hymn to the God who dwells in unapproachable light. In Luke, the owner of a vineyard sends servant after servant to his tenants, and last of all his beloved son, whom they throw out and kill. In this season the Church hears the parable knowing how the story ended: the Son who was rejected is risen. The key words are confession, appearing and heir.

1 Timothy 6:13–16 · The good confession

Setting.

The First Letter to Timothy closes with a solemn charge to a young leader of the church at Ephesus. The letter presents itself as Paul's; many scholars think it was written later in Paul's name by a disciple, while others hold that it is his own. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way.

Just before these verses Paul reminds Timothy of the confession he made “in the presence of many witnesses” (6:12), most likely at his baptism or his ordination. Now he sets that confession beside the confession of Christ himself.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the charge, given before God the giver of life and before Christ the faithful witness.
  • v. 14: keep the commandment until the Lord appears.
  • vv. 15–16: a hymn to God, the only Sovereign, who alone has immortality.

Key words.

  • homologia, confession: To say the same thing, to agree openly. It is used of Timothy's confession in v. 12 and of Christ's in v. 13. The Christian's word of faith echoes the word Christ spoke at his trial.
  • epiphaneia, manifestation, appearing: A shining forth. The Pastoral Letters prefer this word for the Lord's return, and they also use it for his first coming (2 Timothy 1:10).
  • aprositos, unapproachable: Found only here in the New Testament. God is not far away by choice; his light is simply beyond what creatures can reach, unless he comes near to us.

Verse by verse.

“In the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession, I charge you” (6:13)

This is the only place in the letters of Paul where Pilate is named. The Creed keeps the same memory: “suffered under Pontius Pilate”. Our faith is tied to a real trial, on a real day.

“It is he alone who has immortality and dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see; to him be honor and eternal dominion. Amen.” (6:16)

Many scholars think Paul is quoting a hymn the church already sang. The God no one can see has been made known in the Son (John 1:18).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism recalls that before Pilate Christ declared that he came into the world “to bear witness to the truth”, and that the Christian is called not to be ashamed of testifying to the Lord (CCC 2471). Every confession of faith made at baptism, in the Creed of the Holy Qurbana, or before a hostile world shares in that first confession. The doxology of v. 16 has passed into Christian prayer: we adore a God who is beyond us, and we approach him only because he has approached us.

Luke 20:9–16 · I will send my beloved son

Setting.

Jesus is teaching in the Temple in the last days before his Passion. The chief priests, the scribes and the elders have just asked him by what authority he acts (20:1–8). He answers with a parable told to the people, but aimed, as Luke says, at those leaders (20:19).

Every listener would have heard Isaiah's song of the vineyard (Isaiah 5:1–7), where the vineyard is the house of Israel. Absentee landlords and quarrels with tenants were also a familiar part of life in Galilee.

Structure.

  • v. 9: the vineyard planted and leased.
  • vv. 10–12: three servants, each treated worse.
  • v. 13: the owner's decision: “my beloved son”.
  • vv. 14–15a: the plot and the killing outside the vineyard.
  • vv. 15b–16: the owner's response, and the people's cry, “Heaven forbid!”

Key words.

  • huios agapētos, beloved son: The words of the Father's voice at the Jordan (Luke 3:22). The parable has its hearers understand who the last messenger is.
  • isōs, perhaps: Only Luke has the owner say “perhaps they will respect him”. It is the hope of a father who keeps trying, even when the servants have been beaten.
  • klēronomos, heir: The tenants think that by killing the heir they will gain the inheritance. In the Easter faith the opposite happens: the heir is raised and shares the inheritance with all who are his (Romans 8:17).

Verse by verse.

“I will send my beloved son; perhaps they will respect him.” (20:13)

The question “What shall I do?” is the question of a God who will not give up on his vineyard.

“So they threw him out of the vineyard and killed him.” (20:15)

Luke and Matthew put the throwing out before the killing. Hebrews remembers that Jesus suffered outside the city gate (Hebrews 13:12).

“He will come and destroy those tenants and give the vineyard to others.” (20:16)

The vineyard is not destroyed; the unfaithful tenants are. The warning is to those entrusted with the care of God's people in every age, the shepherds of the Church included.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers, St Irenaeus among them, read the servants as the prophets and the son as Christ (in substance). The Church reads the parable as a word about her Lord's Passion, and never as a charge against the Jewish people: Luke shows that the people hung on his words, and that the leaders of that day were the ones addressed (19:48; 20:19; CCC 597). In the Resurrection the rejected Son becomes, in the verse that follows the reading, the cornerstone (20:17; Psalm 118:22).

Questions people ask

Does “give the vineyard to others” mean that God has rejected Israel?

No. In Luke the parable is addressed to the leaders who rejected Jesus, and the people themselves cry out against the thought of such an ending. Paul teaches that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29), and the Church holds that the Jewish people remain beloved of God (CCC 839). The new tenants are the apostles and their successors, who will be judged by the same standard.

For study and teaching

  • Where am I asked today to make “the good confession”, at work, at home or among friends?
  • The owner keeps sending, even after the servants are beaten. Where has God kept sending to me when I did not respond?
