Word for the day

“He said, “The one who showed him mercy.” Jesus said to him, “Go and do likewise.”” Luke 10:37

Reflection

A lawyer asks, “What must I do to inherit eternal life?” and then, “Who is my neighbour?” Jesus answers with a story in which the despised Samaritan is the one who stops, binds the wounds and pays the cost. The question changes: not “Who is my neighbour?” but “Will I be a neighbour?”

Paul describes the same love from the inside: “Let love be genuine… outdo one another in showing honour… extend hospitality to strangers… live peaceably with all.” The Church, God's house, is meant to be the inn where the wounded are cared for, and every Christian is sent out to the road to Jericho.

Full homily

Dear friends, a lawyer asks: what must I do to inherit eternal life? And then: who is my neighbour? Jesus answers with a story in which a despised Samaritan is the one who stops, binds the wounds and pays the cost (Luke 10:30–35). The question changes: not who is my neighbour, but to whom can I be a neighbour?

Paul says: let love be genuine; love one another with mutual affection; rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep (Romans 12:9–15). This is the way of life in God's house.

The Fathers of the Church compared the Church to the inn where the Samaritan brought the wounded man: a house of healing. This week let us practise love that stops: when we meet a need on the road, let us not pass by. Amen.

For children

Children, the good Samaritan stopped and helped the hurt man. If you see someone sad today, go close and help!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Good Samaritan of humanity, you stopped for me when I was wounded. Make me a neighbour to all, with genuine love that stops, binds and pays the cost. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings describe love that is real. Paul gives a string of short commands: "Let love be genuine", bless your persecutors, weep with those who weep. In the Gospel a lawyer asks, "who is my neighbor?", and Jesus answers with the Samaritan who stopped on the road. In God's house love is not a feeling only; it is the way to eternal life.

Romans 12:9–18 · Let love be genuine

Setting.

In Romans 1–11 Paul has explained the gospel: all have sinned, and God justifies sinners by his grace through faith in Christ. Chapter 12 turns to life. It begins: "present your bodies as a living sacrifice" (12:1), and describes the Church as one body with many members (12:4–8). Today's verses show what that body looks like in daily life.

Structure.

  • vv. 9–13: love within the community: genuine, warm, zealous, hopeful, generous, hospitable.
  • vv. 14–16: love toward everyone: blessing persecutors, sharing joy and sorrow, humility.
  • vv. 17–18: love in conflict: no revenge, peace with all as far as possible.

Key words.

  • agapē anypokritos, genuine love: Literally "love without hypocrisy". A hypokritēs was an actor wearing a mask. Christian love is not a performance.
  • philadelphia / philostorgos, brotherly love / mutual affection: Paul uses words from family life. In the Church we love one another as brothers and sisters, with the warm affection of a family.
  • philoxenia, hospitality: Literally "love of strangers". Paul says "pursue" it (v. 13): do not only wait for guests, but go after the chance to welcome.

Verse by verse.

“Let love be genuine; hate what is evil, hold fast to what is good;” (12:9)

Genuine love is not weak. It hates evil and holds on to good, like someone holding something precious with both hands.

“Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep.” (12:15)

The second is often easier than the first. To rejoice sincerely in another's success, without envy, is a sign of real love.

“Do not repay anyone evil for evil” (12:17)

Paul echoes Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:39, 44). Peace with all is asked "so far as it depends on you" (v. 18): we cannot control others, but we can refuse to answer evil with evil.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes St Paul's praise of love and teaches that charity animates and inspires the practice of all the virtues (CCC 1826–1827). St Augustine's short rule, "Love, and do what you will", means that when the root of a life is genuine love, good actions grow from it (in substance; Homilies on 1 John 7).

Luke 10:25–37 · Who is my neighbour?

Setting.

Jesus is on his long journey to Jerusalem (Luke 9:51–19:27). A lawyer, an expert in the law of Moses, asks a test question. Jesus lets him answer from the Scriptures: Deuteronomy 6:5 (love of God) and Leviticus 19:18 (love of neighbour).

The road from Jerusalem down to Jericho drops about a thousand metres in some twenty-seven kilometres, through lonely desert hills where robbers could hide. Jews and Samaritans were divided by long hostility over the Temple and the Scriptures (see John 4:9). A Samaritan is the last hero Jesus' hearers would expect.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–28: the question about eternal life and the two commandments.
  • v. 29: "And who is my neighbor?"
  • vv. 30–32: the man left half dead; the priest and the Levite pass by.
  • vv. 33–35: the Samaritan's compassion and care.
  • vv. 36–37: Jesus' question and command: "Go and do likewise."

Key words.

  • plēsion, neighbour: Literally "the one nearby". The lawyer wants to know where the circle of duty ends. Jesus changes the question: not "who is my neighbour?" but "who acted as a neighbour?"
  • esplanchnisthē, he was moved with pity: The same verb as the father of the prodigal son (15:20) and Jesus himself (7:13). The Samaritan feels in his inner self what God feels.
  • dēnarion, denarius: About a day's wage for a labourer (Matthew 20:2). Two denarii could pay for several days at the inn, and the Samaritan promises more.

Verse by verse.

“And who is my neighbor?” (10:29)

The lawyer asks "wanting to justify himself". He wants a limit. Love that looks for limits is not yet the love the law commands.

“But a Samaritan while traveling came near him; and when he saw him, he was moved with pity.” (10:33)

The priest and the Levite "saw" and passed by; the Samaritan "saw" and came near. They may have feared ritual impurity from touching a body that seemed dead (Leviticus 21:1), but the parable gives them no excuse. The Samaritan takes the risk and pays the cost.

“Go and do likewise.” (10:37)

The lawyer cannot bring himself to say "the Samaritan"; he says "the one who showed him mercy". Jesus sends him out to become that kind of person.

How the Church reads it.

Many Fathers, among them Origen, St Ambrose and St Augustine, read the parable also as a picture of salvation: the wounded man is Adam, fallen on the way from paradise; the Samaritan is Christ, who comes near to us; the oil and wine are the sacraments; the inn is the Church; and the promise to return is Christ's second coming (in substance). Pope Francis devotes the second chapter of the encyclical Fratelli Tutti to this parable, asking each of us which character we are.

For study and teaching

  • Romans 12:15 asks me to rejoice with those who rejoice. Whose success do I find it hard to celebrate, and why?
  • Jesus turns the question "Who is my neighbour?" into "Who acted as a neighbour?" (Luke 10:29, 36). Whom have I passed by on the other side, and how can I come near?
