Word for the day

“He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things.” Luke 1:52–53

Reflection

Paul's great blessing opens the mystery of the whole year: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing… he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” God's plan is “to gather up all things in him.”

Mary's Magnificat is the Church's song of that plan seen from below: God looks on the lowliness of his servant, scatters the proud, lifts up the lowly, fills the hungry. As the year ends and Annunciation approaches, the Church sings with Mary, the first of the redeemed and the image of the Church herself.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul's great blessing opens the mystery of the whole year: blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love (Ephesians 1:3–4).

Mary sings the same joy in her Magnificat: my soul magnifies the Lord, for he has looked with favour on the lowliness of his servant. He has brought down the powerful from their thrones and lifted up the lowly (Luke 1:46–52). Mary's song is the Church's song.

As the season of the Dedication of the Church comes to an end, let us thank God with Mary for the blessings of a whole year. This evening, sit together as a family, name five blessings of this year, and pray a rosary of thanksgiving. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Two hymns of praise stand side by side today. Ephesians opens with a great blessing of God for his plan, chosen “before the foundation of the world” and fulfilled in Christ. Luke gives us the Magnificat, Mary’s song for what God has done for her and for his people. One hymn looks at God’s plan from eternity; the other sees it come true in a humble young woman of Nazareth.

Ephesians 1:3–12 · Blessed be the God and Father

Setting.

In Greek, verses 3–14 form one single long sentence, a hymn that seems to run out of breath in praise. It takes the form of the Jewish berakah, a prayer that begins “Blessed be God” and then lists his works. Paul blesses the Father for what he has done in Christ, and the passage moves from eternity (v. 4) to the fullness of time (v. 10).

Structure.

  • v. 3: the blessing of the Father for every spiritual blessing in Christ.
  • vv. 4–6: chosen and destined to be his adopted children.
  • vv. 7–8: redemption through his blood and forgiveness.
  • vv. 9–10: the mystery of his will: to gather up all things in Christ.
  • vv. 11–12: our inheritance, so that we may live for the praise of his glory.

Key words.

  • eulogētos / eulogia, blessed / blessing: God blesses us with gifts; we bless God with praise. The same word goes down and comes back up.
  • anakephalaiōsasthai, to gather up: Literally “to bring under one head”. St Irenaeus built his teaching of “recapitulation” on this word: Christ takes up the whole story of humanity and of creation and heals it in himself.
  • mystērion, mystery: Not a riddle but God’s hidden plan, now revealed. The Syriac tradition speaks of raza, the mystery made present in the liturgy.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places,” (1:3)
“just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” (1:4)

Before anything existed, God thought of us in his Son. The aim of the choice is holiness, and its atmosphere is love.

“as a plan for the fullness of time, to gather up all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth.” (1:10)

The whole universe has one centre and one goal: Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism opens its teaching on the liturgy with this hymn: in the liturgy the Father is blessed as the source of every blessing (CCC 1077–1078). It also calls God’s plan, revealed in Ephesians 1, a “plan of loving kindness” conceived before the creation of the world (CCC 257).

Luke 1:46–55 · My soul magnifies the Lord

Setting.

Mary has gone in haste to visit Elizabeth, and Elizabeth has called her “blessed” (1:42–45). Mary answers not by speaking about herself but by praising God. Her song is woven from Old Testament texts, above all the song of Hannah (1 Samuel 2:1–10), another woman whose child was God’s gift.

Structure.

  • vv. 46–50: what God has done for Mary.
  • vv. 51–53: what God does in the world: the proud scattered, the lowly lifted up.
  • vv. 54–55: what God has done for Israel, faithful to his promise to Abraham.

Key words.

  • megalynei, magnifies: To make great, to declare great. Mary cannot make God greater, but she makes his greatness known.
  • tapeinōsis, lowliness: Low estate, humble condition. It is not first a virtue Mary claims but the smallness God chose to look upon.
  • eleos, mercy: In the Greek Old Testament it often renders the Hebrew ḥesed, God’s faithful covenant love. The word appears at the centre (v. 50) and the end (v. 54) of the song.

Verse by verse.

“for he has looked with favor on the lowliness of his servant. Surely, from now on all generations will call me blessed;” (1:48)

A prophecy fulfilled every time the Church honours Mary. Her blessedness comes from God’s look, not from her own greatness.

“He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly;” (1:52)

The verbs are in the past tense, as if God’s reversal were already done. In Christ it has begun.

“according to the promise he made to our ancestors, to Abraham and to his descendants forever.” (1:55)

The song ends with Abraham. What God is doing in Mary is the fulfilment of a very old promise.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls the Magnificat “the song both of the Mother of God and of the Church” (CCC 2619). St Ambrose, commenting on this Gospel, wishes that the soul of Mary be in each of us to magnify the Lord, and her spirit in each of us to rejoice in God (in substance).

Questions people ask

Did Mary really compose such a hymn?

Mary was a young Jewish woman whose prayer was formed by the psalms and the songs of Scripture. The Magnificat is built from those texts, as a devout person of her time could pray. A few old Latin manuscripts attribute the song to Elizabeth, but almost all manuscripts and the whole tradition of the Church give it to Mary.

For study and teaching

  • Ephesians says God chose me “before the foundation of the world”. How would my day change if I truly believed this?
  • Mary praises God for looking on her lowliness. Where in my life do I need to let God look on my smallness rather than hide it?
