Word for the day

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” Luke 14:27

Reflection

The First Letter of John opens with a breathless sentence, the words of an old man who cannot forget what he saw as a young one: “We declare to you what was from the beginning, what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we have looked at and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life.” Heard, seen, looked at, touched: four verbs piled one on another. John insists that the faith rests on a real encounter with a real person. The Word who was with the Father from the beginning became flesh, ate with fishermen, invited Thomas to put his hand into his side. “This life was revealed, and we have seen it and testify to it.” Now the Lord has ascended and is no longer seen in that way. How then do we meet him? John answers: through the witness of the apostles, handed on in the Church. “We declare to you what we have seen and heard so that you also may have fellowship with us; and truly our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ.” The chain is clear: the apostles saw; they declared; we who hear them enter into fellowship with them, and through them with the Father and the Son. Our Church received that witness from Thomas, the apostle who touched the wounds. In these days before Pentecost, the Spirit is preparing the apostles to begin declaring.

The Gospel shows what that fellowship costs. “Now large crowds were traveling with him; and he turned and said to them, ‘Whoever comes to me and does not hate father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and even life itself, cannot be my disciple.’” The word “hate” shocks us. In the language of the Bible it is a way of saying “prefer less”; Matthew gives the same saying as “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me” (Matthew 10:37). Jesus is not asking us to stop loving our families; he who gave us the commandment to honour father and mother asks that no love, not even the dearest, take his place. Then he speaks plainly: “Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” A crowd follows for a day; a disciple follows to the end.

Jesus gives two little parables of good sense. A man who wants to build a tower first sits down and estimates the cost, so that he is not mocked: “This fellow began to build and was not able to finish.” A king going to war with ten thousand against twenty thousand first sits down and considers whether he can win. Both sit down before they begin. Discipleship is not an enthusiasm of one evening; it is a decision taken with open eyes. “So therefore, none of you can become my disciple if you do not give up all your possessions.” Finally he speaks of salt: “Salt is good; but if salt has lost its taste, how can its saltiness be restored?” A disciple who has stopped paying the cost loses his flavour. Many in Kerala know what that means: families where the faith has become only a custom, where the name is Christian but the taste is gone. Salt, Leviticus says, was offered with every sacrifice as “the salt of the covenant” (Leviticus 2:13). Today the Latin Church remembers Our Lady of Fatima, who asked three poor children to pray, to do penance and to offer sacrifices for sinners. Mary is the disciple who counted the cost at Nazareth and paid it at the foot of the Cross. In the upper room she prayed for the Spirit who alone can give us the courage to finish the tower we have begun.

Full homily

Dear friends, the First Letter of John begins with an old apostle's memory: “what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we have looked at and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life.” John had touched Jesus. Our faith rests on a real meeting with a real person.

Now the Lord has ascended. How do we meet him? Through the witness of the apostles, handed on in the Church. “We declare to you what we have seen and heard so that you also may have fellowship with us.” We received this witness from St Thomas, who touched the Lord's wounds.

In the Gospel, large crowds follow Jesus. He turns and says: unless you put me before father and mother, before even your own life, you cannot be my disciple. He does not ask us to love our families less, but to love him most. “Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” A crowd follows for a day; a disciple follows to the end.

Jesus speaks of a man who builds a tower without counting the cost, and cannot finish. Many of us have begun towers: a promise at a retreat, a resolution after confession. Let us finish them. And he speaks of salt that loses its taste. Faith that becomes only a habit loses its taste. Let us restore it.

Today the Latin Church remembers Our Lady of Fatima. Mary asked three children to pray and offer sacrifices. She herself counted the cost at Nazareth and paid it at the Cross. In the upper room she prayed for the Spirit. Let us ask her to pray for us, that the Spirit may give us courage to follow to the end. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: before you build a big tower, think if you can finish it! Following Jesus is like building a tower: we must keep going even when it is hard.

At Fatima, Mary asked three children to pray every day. You can pray too! Today, say a Hail Mary for someone who is far from Jesus.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Word of life, you were heard, seen and touched by your apostles, and through their witness we have fellowship with the Father and with you. Give us the courage to put you before every other love, to carry our cross and follow you to the end. Teach us to count the cost and to finish what we have begun, and keep the salt of our faith from losing its taste. Through the prayers of the Virgin Mary, who paid the cost of discipleship at the foot of the Cross and prayed with the apostles for the Spirit, send us your Holy Spirit. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The two readings stand at the two ends of discipleship. John begins his letter with the joy of witnesses who heard, saw and touched the Word of life, and who share that life so that others may have fellowship with the Father and the Son. In Luke, Jesus turns to the crowds walking with him and tells them honestly what following him costs: the cross, the first place in the heart, all one has. The fellowship is a gift; the cost is the way to keep it. In this last week of the Resurrection season, both readings ask whether we have truly met the Risen One, and whether we will follow him all the way.

1 John 1:1–3 · What we have seen and touched

Setting.

The First Letter of John comes from the same circle as the Fourth Gospel, near the end of the first century. Tradition names the apostle John as its author; many scholars think it was written by an elder of the community that treasured his witness. The letter does not name its readers, but it is plain that some had left the community (2:19) and were denying that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh (4:2). These opening verses answer them before the letter has even greeted anyone: the life of God was not an idea; it was seen and touched.

Structure.

  • v. 1: what was from the beginning, heard, seen, looked at, touched: the word of life.
  • v. 2: an aside: this life was revealed, and we testify to it.
  • v. 3: the purpose: that you may share our fellowship, which is with the Father and his Son Jesus Christ.
  • ¶ The Greek sentence is long and breathless, and the main verb, “we declare”, arrives only in verse 3. The writer seems to stumble over his own wonder.

Key words.

  • ap’ archēs, from the beginning: The words recall the first line of the Gospel, “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1), and the first line of Genesis. What the disciples touched in Galilee was the eternal Word.
  • psēlaphaō, to touch, handle: A physical word: to feel with the hands. Luke uses the same verb when the Risen Lord says, “Touch me and see” (Luke 24:39). Faith rests on a real body, not on a vision.
  • koinōnia, fellowship, communion: A sharing in something held in common. It is the word for the life of the first community in Jerusalem (Acts 2:42) and for our share in the body and blood of Christ (1 Corinthians 10:16).

Verse by verse.

“what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we have looked at and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life” (1:1)

Four verbs climb from hearing to touching. The writer speaks for the apostolic witnesses, and the Church still speaks with that “we” whenever she proclaims the Gospel.

“this life was revealed, and we have seen it and testify to it, and declare to you the eternal life that was with the Father” (1:2)

“With the Father” echoes John 1:1, “the Word was with God.” The life that appeared in time was with the Father before all time.

“so that you also may have fellowship with us; and truly our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ.” (1:3)

There is no private road to the Father. We enter communion with God by entering the communion of those who saw and believed: the apostolic Church.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council opens its Constitution on Divine Revelation with these very verses (Dei Verbum 1), because they say what revelation is: God showing himself so that we may share his life. The Catechism uses them to describe the joy of handing on the faith: the first disciples burned to proclaim Christ and invite every age to share their communion with him (CCC 425). St Augustine, preaching on this letter, recalls that the Risen Lord let his disciples touch his hands and side, and says that the Word of life was handled so that we might believe (in substance).

Luke 14:25–35 · Count the cost

Setting.

Jesus is on his long journey to Jerusalem (9:51), and the crowds are growing. He has just told the parable of the great dinner, in which the host’s house must be filled (14:15–24). Now he turns round to the people walking behind him. Walking with him in a crowd is not yet the same as being his disciple, and he wants them to know the difference.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–27: two conditions: to put him before every human bond, and to carry the cross.
  • vv. 28–30: the parable of the tower: sit down and estimate the cost.
  • vv. 31–32: the parable of the king going to war.
  • v. 33: the conclusion: give up all your possessions.
  • vv. 34–35: salt that has lost its taste.

Key words.

  • miseō, hate: A Semitic way of speaking that sets two loves side by side: to love one less than the other. Matthew gives the sense: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me” (Matthew 10:37). Jesus does not cancel the commandment to honour parents; he asks for the first place.
  • apotassomai, give up, say farewell: In verse 33 the word means to take leave of, to say goodbye. The disciple does not despise what he has, but holds it loosely, ready to let it go for Christ.
  • mōrainō, to lose taste: Literally “to become foolish”, from the same root as mōros, fool. Salt that has lost its savour is like a disciple who has lost his wisdom: nothing remains to give flavour.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever does not carry the cross and follow me cannot be my disciple.” (14:27)

Luke’s hearers knew what it meant to see a condemned man carry his cross to execution. Jesus speaks this on the road to Jerusalem, where he will carry his own.

“For which of you, intending to build a tower, does not first sit down and estimate the cost” (14:28)

The parables do not ask us to be calculating with God. They ask us not to begin lightly. Discipleship begun in enthusiasm must be finished in faithfulness.

“Salt is good; but if salt has lost its taste, how can its saltiness be restored?” (14:34)

Salt in the ancient world was often impure, and its true salt could wash out, leaving only grey dust. A disciple who keeps the name but loses the life is like that.

How the Church reads it.

St Gregory the Great asks how we can be told to hate those we are commanded to love, and answers that we love them rightly when we refuse to listen to them whenever they would lead us away from God (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 37). The Catechism takes up verse 33 when it teaches poverty of heart: Jesus bids his disciples prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544). Each state of life lives this differently, but no Christian is excused from it.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus really want us to hate our families?

No. The word “hate” is a Semitic way of saying “love less, put second”, as Genesis says that Jacob “loved Rachel more than Leah” and then calls Leah “unloved” (Genesis 29:30–31). Jesus himself commands honour for parents (Mark 7:10). He asks for the first place, and a heart that loves God first loves its family better, not less.

For study and teaching

  • The Letter of John speaks of hearing, seeing and touching. Where do I meet the Risen Lord in a way that is real and not only an idea?
  • What would it mean for me to “sit down and estimate the cost” of following Christ this year, in my family, my work and my money?
