Word for the day

“Keep awake therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” Matthew 25:13

Reflection

James writes to poor Christians, many of them labourers who waited at the end of the day for wages that did not always come. He does not tell them to stop waiting; he teaches them how to wait. “Be patient, therefore, beloved, until the coming of the Lord. The farmer waits for the precious crop from the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains.” Every farmer in Kerala knows this patience. He ploughs and sows, and then he can only look at the sky and wait for the rains of June and the rains of October; no amount of worry will bring a cloud one day sooner. Yet his waiting is not idle. He mends the bunds, he weeds, he watches. That is the waiting of a Christian in the Easter season. The Lord is risen; he has ascended to the Father; he will come again in glory. Between his rising and his return we live like farmers between sowing and harvest. “You also must be patient. Strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.” James then names the sin that grows quickly among people who are tired of waiting: “Beloved, do not grumble against one another, so that you may not be judged. See, the Judge is standing at the doors!” When we are anxious, we turn on each other. He points instead to the prophets and to Job, who suffered without letting go of God, and to what Job discovered at the end: “how the Lord is compassionate and merciful.” Finally, a short word about speech: “let your ‘Yes’ be yes and your ‘No’ be no.” A person who lives before the coming Lord does not need oaths to be believed; his plain word is enough.

The Gospel tells the same truth in the language of a village wedding. In the custom of the time the bridegroom came, often late at night, to bring his bride to his father's house, and young women went out with lamps to light the procession and enter the feast with him. “Ten bridesmaids took their lamps and went to meet the bridegroom. Five of them were foolish, and five were wise.” Notice what is not different between them. All ten were invited, all ten had lamps, all ten were waiting, and all ten fell asleep: “As the bridegroom was delayed, all of them became drowsy and slept.” The difference is the oil. “When the foolish took their lamps, they took no oil with them; but the wise took flasks of oil with their lamps.” Then the cry at midnight: “Look! Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him.” The foolish ask to borrow, and they are refused, not out of meanness but because some things cannot be lent. No one can lend me his faith, his love or his years of prayer at the last minute; the oil must be my own. The ending is the most serious line of the parable: “and the door was shut.” This is not a story to frighten us but to wake us up. In the Easter season the Church hears it with new ears. The Bridegroom who was “taken away” on Good Friday (Matthew 9:15) has come back from the grave, and the wedding feast has already begun at every altar. Our lamp was lit at Baptism, from the light of the Risen Christ. The oil that keeps it burning is charity, daily prayer, the Holy Qurbana and confession. In many Kerala homes a lamp is still lit at dusk and the family gathers before it to pray; the parable asks whether the lamp of the heart is burning as faithfully as the lamp in the front room.

James and Matthew fit together. The farmer waits with patience and keeps working; the wise bridesmaids sleep, but they are ready when the cry comes. Christian waiting is neither panic nor laziness. It is the calm readiness of people who know that the One who is coming is not a stranger but the Bridegroom who loves them, the Risen Lord who has already shown them his hands and his side. “Keep awake therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” That is not a threat to those who love him; it is an invitation to live every ordinary day as a day on which he might knock.

Full homily

Dear friends, St James says: be patient until the coming of the Lord. Look at the farmer. After he sows, he waits for the early and the late rains (James 5:7). He cannot hurry the clouds, but he does not sit idle either. He watches, he weeds, he keeps the field ready.

That is how a Christian waits in the Easter season. Christ is risen; he will come again. While we wait, James warns us: do not grumble against one another. Impatient people turn on each other. Let us strengthen our hearts instead, and let our yes be yes and our no be no.

In the Gospel Jesus tells of ten bridesmaids. All ten were invited, all had lamps, and all fell asleep while the bridegroom was delayed. The difference was the oil. Five had brought it; five had not. And when the cry came at midnight, the foolish could not borrow it.

Some things cannot be borrowed. No one can lend you faith, love or a life of prayer at the last minute. That oil we gather day by day: in prayer, in the Holy Qurbana, in confession, in kindness to the poor.

The Bridegroom has already come back from the grave, and his feast has begun at our altar. Let us keep the lamp lit at our Baptism burning, so that whenever he knocks, we are ready to go in with him. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about girls with lamps waiting for a wedding. The wise ones brought extra oil. Your prayers and your kind acts are oil for the lamp in your heart!

Tonight, when the family prays, look at the lamp or candle and say: ‘Jesus, keep my heart shining for you!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Bridegroom of the Church, you rose from the dead and you will come again in glory. Give us the patience of the farmer who waits for the rain, and the readiness of the wise who kept oil in their lamps. Keep us from grumbling against one another, make our yes a true yes, and let the light lit in us at Baptism burn with love until the cry is heard at midnight and we go in with you to the wedding feast. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The risen Lord will come again, and both readings teach us how to wait. James asks for the patience of a farmer who waits for the rains, and of the prophets and Job, who endured. In the Gospel ten bridesmaids wait for a bridegroom who is delayed; all of them fall asleep, but only five have oil for their lamps when the cry goes up at midnight. Waiting well is not anxious watching of the clock, but steady faithfulness that keeps the lamp supplied. The words to watch are patient, coming and keep awake.

James 5:7–12 · Be patient until the coming of the Lord

Setting.

The letter of James is a practical letter, full of short teachings like the wisdom books of the Old Testament. Just before today’s verses James has spoken sharply to the rich who withheld the wages of their workers (5:1–6). Now he turns to the poor and the wronged and tells them how to live while they wait for the Lord’s justice.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: be patient like the farmer; the coming of the Lord is near.
  • v. 9: do not grumble against one another; the Judge is at the doors.
  • vv. 10–11: examples of endurance: the prophets and Job; the Lord is compassionate.
  • v. 12: do not swear; let your yes be yes and your no be no.

Key words.

  • makrothymēsate, be patient: Literally “be long of spirit”, the opposite of a short temper. The same quality is named among the fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22).
  • parousia, coming: The word for the arrival of a king or an official visit. In the New Testament it is the glorious coming of Christ at the end.
  • hypomonē, endurance: Staying under a load without giving way. It is the endurance of Job, who did not let go of God in his suffering.

Verse by verse.

“Be patient, therefore, beloved, until the coming of the Lord. The farmer waits for the precious crop from the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains.” (5:7)

In the land of Israel the early rains came in autumn, at sowing, and the late rains in spring, before harvest (see Deuteronomy 11:14). The farmer cannot make them come; he can only wait and keep working.

“Strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.” (5:8)

Patience is not weakness. It needs a strong heart.

“Beloved, do not grumble against one another, so that you may not be judged. See, the Judge is standing at the doors!” (5:9)

Hardship tempts a community to turn on itself. James reminds them that the Judge is near, so there is no need to judge one another.

“You have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful.” (5:11)

Job’s story ends not with his suffering, but with God’s mercy. That is the “purpose of the Lord”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism names patience among the fruits of the Holy Spirit (CCC 1832). On v. 12 it teaches, with the words of Jesus in Matthew 5:33–37 and of St James, that Christians should not swear lightly, and that the Church understands this teaching not to exclude an oath taken for a grave and right reason, as before a court (CCC 2153–2154).

Matthew 25:1–13 · Keep awake

Setting.

This parable comes in Jesus’ last great discourse in Matthew (chapters 24–25), about the end of the age and his coming. It is the first of three parables in chapter 25: the ten bridesmaids, the talents, and the judgment of the nations.

In a village wedding of that time, the bridegroom went to fetch his bride and brought her to his home, often at night, and young women went out with lamps to meet the procession and lead it to the feast. The details of such customs are not fully known, but the picture is clear: a joyful meeting, and a door that is shut at the right time.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: ten bridesmaids, five foolish without oil, five wise with flasks of oil.
  • v. 5: the bridegroom is delayed; all sleep.
  • vv. 6–9: the midnight cry; the foolish ask for oil and are refused.
  • vv. 10–12: the bridegroom comes; the ready go in; the door is shut; “I do not know you.”
  • v. 13: keep awake.

Key words.

  • phronimoi / mōrai, wise / foolish: The wise are the prudent, who think ahead. Mōrai is from the same family as the word Paul uses for the “foolishness” of the Cross (1 Corinthians 1:18). Here foolishness is to live as if the Lord would never come.
  • apantēsis, to meet: A word used of going out to welcome an arriving king or guest and escort him in. Paul uses it for meeting the Lord at his coming (1 Thessalonians 4:17).
  • grēgoreite, keep awake: The word that ends the parable and runs through Jesus’ teaching on the end. It is not about never sleeping, since all ten slept; it is about being ready.

Verse by verse.

“Ten bridesmaids took their lamps and went to meet the bridegroom.” (25:1)

All ten go out. All are invited, all have lamps, all want to meet him. The difference is hidden at first.

“As the bridegroom was delayed, all of them became drowsy and slept.” (25:5)

The delay is part of the parable. The early Church learned to live faithfully through a long wait.

“No! there will not be enough for you and for us; you had better go to the dealers and buy some for yourselves.” (25:9)

This sounds unkind, but the parable is teaching something else: readiness cannot be borrowed at the last moment. No one can live another person’s faith for them.

“Truly I tell you, I do not know you.” (25:12)

Compare Matthew 7:21–23: it is not enough to say “Lord, Lord”. To be known by him is to have lived in friendship with him.

“Keep awake therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” (25:13)

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council takes up this parable: since we know neither the day nor the hour, we must, as the Lord advised, watch constantly, so that when our earthly life is over we may enter with him into the marriage feast (Lumen Gentium 48, in substance). Many Fathers saw in the oil the good works of love and mercy, which no one can hand over to another at the last moment (in substance). The parable of the judgment that follows (25:31–46) says the same: we are ready for the Lord when we have loved him in the least of his brothers and sisters, and that love is itself his gift in us.

Questions people ask

Were the wise bridesmaids selfish when they refused to share their oil?

In real life we are told to share what we have. But a parable makes one point sharply. The oil here stands for something that cannot be lent: a life of faith and love built up over time. At the last moment each person stands before the Lord with his or her own life.

For study and teaching

  • James compares patience to a farmer waiting for rain. What am I waiting for from God, and what work can I keep doing while I wait?
  • What is the “oil” in my lamp, and how will I add to it this week?
