Word for the day

“You also must be patient. Strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.” James 5:8

Reflection

Kerala knows how to wait for rain. The farmer who has prepared the field cannot make the monsoon come; he can only repair the bunds, keep the seed ready, and watch the sky. Saint James uses exactly this picture for the Church in the season of Kaitha, the time of growth and harvest: “Be patient, therefore, beloved, until the coming of the Lord. The farmer waits for the precious crop from the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains.” The land of Palestine, like Kerala, had two rainy seasons, the early rain of autumn that softens the ground for sowing and the later rain of spring that fills the ears of grain; our own Edavappathi and Thulavarsham are their cousins. The farmer does not complain that the rain comes in its own time. “You also must be patient. Strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.”

Patience in James is not passivity. It is a strength of the heart, a holding firm. He gives the community three practical rules for the waiting time. First, “do not grumble against one another, so that you may not be judged. See, the Judge is standing at the doors!” Long waiting makes people short-tempered, in the parish as in the family, and the complaint against a neighbour is the first sign of a heart that has stopped looking for the Lord. Second, look at the prophets and at Job: “As an example of suffering and patience, beloved, take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord. Indeed we call blessed those who showed endurance. You have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful.” Job lost everything and said, “the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord”; and in the end the Lord's purpose showed itself as compassion. Third, keep your word: “let your ‘Yes’ be yes and your ‘No’ be no, so that you may not fall under condemnation.” People who are waiting for the Lord should be people whose word can be trusted, who do not need oaths to be believed.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks of the great coming. Mark's thirteenth chapter, often called the little apocalypse, uses the language of the prophets: the sun darkened, the moon not giving its light, the stars falling, the powers of heaven shaken. This is the language of the end of an age, the old world shaking to make room for the new. Out of the shaking comes the one sure thing: “Then they will see ‘the Son of Man coming in clouds’ with great power and glory. Then he will send out the angels, and gather his elect from the four winds, from the ends of the earth to the ends of heaven.” The elect are gathered from the ends of the earth, and no corner of the world, no village on a distant hill, is forgotten. The Church in India, which according to tradition received the Gospel from Mar Thoma at the edge of the known world, hears in this promise that nobody is too far to be gathered.

Then Jesus teaches by the fig tree, as the farmer teaches by the rain: “From the fig tree learn its lesson: as soon as its branch becomes tender and puts forth its leaves, you know that summer is near. So also, when you see these things taking place, you know that he is near, at the very gates.” The fig tree gives no timetable, only a sign that a season is turning. Elsewhere Jesus says plainly that “about that day or hour no one knows, neither the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father” and tells us to “keep alert” (Mark 13:32–33). The Church does not read charts and dates of the end; she lives each day as a day of the Lord's nearness. And the promise that makes this possible is the last sentence of the reading: “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away.” The monsoon passes, the year passes, a life passes; his word remains. For this reason we can wait without fear: the one who is coming is the one who has already come, who is with us in the Holy Qurbana, and who will come again.

The passage ends the day with a prayer for the patient heart. If the waiting seems long, remember that the farmer sows in the rain and reaps in the sun. The Lord is “compassionate and merciful,” and he knows how long a human being can wait. Let us use the waiting time as Kaitha teaches: to strengthen our hearts, to speak without complaint, to keep our word, to pray for those who are still far away, and to look for the signs of summer in the green leaves of every day.

Full homily

Dear friends, in Kerala we know what it is to wait for rain. The farmer repairs his bund, keeps the seed ready, and looks at the sky. He cannot make the rain come. Saint James uses this picture today: “Be patient, therefore, beloved, until the coming of the Lord. The farmer waits for the precious crop from the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains” (James 5:7).

Patience is not laziness. It is strength of heart: “Strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near” (James 5:8). James gives three practical rules for the waiting time. Do not grumble against one another. Learn endurance from the prophets and from Job, who said, “the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord” (Job 1:21). And keep your word: let your ‘Yes’ be yes and your ‘No’ be no (James 5:12).

In the Gospel Jesus speaks about his coming in glory. The sun darkens and the stars fall, which is the language of the Bible for the end of one age and the beginning of another. But in the middle of it all there is one sure thing: “Then they will see ‘the Son of Man coming in clouds’ with great power and glory” (Mark 13:26). And he will gather his chosen ones from the ends of the earth, even from the smallest village on the farthest hill.

Jesus gives us a lesson from the fig tree: when the branch becomes tender and puts out leaves, you know that summer is near (Mark 13:28). The signs are small: a leaf, a kind word, a prayer in a hard time. We do not know the day or the hour; the Father alone knows. So Jesus says: “keep alert” (Mark 13:33).

And then the promise: “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away” (Mark 13:31). The monsoon passes, the years pass, we pass; his word remains. So let us wait like the farmer: busy, trusting, without grumbling, saying every day: Come, Lord Jesus. Amen.

For children

Children, when a farmer plants seeds, he must wait for the rain. He cannot make it come, but he does not stop working. Jesus wants us to wait for him in the same way: be patient, be kind, do not grumble, and keep your promises. Jesus will come again, and his words will never pass away. Say with me: Come, Lord Jesus!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you are coming, and we do not know the day or the hour. Strengthen our hearts as you strengthened the prophets and Job, and as the farmer waits for the rain, teach us to wait for you without grumbling and without fear. Keep our word true and our hands busy while we wait. Gather your elect from the ends of the earth, and let us be among them, with all those who prayed and worked for us. Your words will not pass away; let them be our rock when the monsoon rains pour and the rivers rise. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about waiting for the Lord’s coming. James tells his readers to be patient like a farmer who waits for the early and late rains, to stop grumbling at one another, and to take the prophets and Job as examples of endurance. In Mark, Jesus describes the day when the Son of Man will come in clouds with power and glory and gather his chosen ones from the four winds, and he tells the disciples to learn from the fig tree and to trust that his words will never pass away. The Church’s waiting is neither fear nor calculation but patient, watchful hope.

James 5:7–12 · Be patient until the coming of the Lord

Setting.

James has just warned the rich who oppress the poor (5:1–6). Now he turns to the poor and suffering believers, who may be tempted to take matters into their own hands or to complain against each other. His word to them is patience. The farmer’s picture suits the land of Israel: the early rains come in autumn and the late rains in spring, and the crop depends on both (Deuteronomy 11:14; Jeremiah 5:24).

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: be patient like the farmer until the coming of the Lord, which is near.
  • v. 9: do not grumble against one another; the Judge stands at the door.
  • vv. 10–11: the prophets and Job as examples of endurance; the Lord is compassionate and merciful.
  • v. 12: do not swear oaths; let your “Yes” be yes and your “No” be no.

Key words.

  • makrothymeō, to be patient: Literally to be “long of spirit”, slow to anger and slow to give up. The word is used of God’s own patience with sinners.
  • hypomonē, endurance: A steady holding on under pressure. Job is the model: he did not curse God even in his sorrow.
  • parousia, coming, presence: The word for the arrival of a king or the coming of Christ in glory. The early Christians prayed Maranatha, “Our Lord, come!” (1 Corinthians 16:22).

Verse by verse.

“The farmer waits for the precious crop from the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains.” (5:7)

The farmer works, but he cannot make the rain fall. A Christian also does what he can and leaves the harvest to God.

“Beloved, do not grumble against one another, so that you may not be judged.” (5:9)

Complaining against each other is the first fruit of impatience. The Judge is already near, so there is no need to judge one another.

“You have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful.” (5:11)

The story of Job ends in God’s mercy, not in his silence. The words “compassionate and merciful” echo the name God gave himself in Exodus 34:6.

“let your “Yes” be yes and your “No” be no” (5:12)

James echoes the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:34–37). A person of integrity needs no oath to be believed.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Church awaits the Lord’s glorious return, which is certain though its day and hour are unknown (CCC 673–677). It also teaches that the Lord’s words about oaths do not forbid solemn oaths for grave and right reasons, as in a court of law, but call all Christians to truthfulness in ordinary speech (CCC 2150–2155). The Church has long held up Job’s endurance as a model for the sick and sorrowing, and the great commentary of St Gregory the Great, the Moralia on Job, shows how seriously the Fathers took the book.

Mark 13:24–31 · The Son of Man coming in clouds

Setting.

Jesus is on the Mount of Olives, looking across at the Temple, in the last days of his earthly life. Four disciples have asked him when the Temple will fall and what the sign will be (13:1–4). The whole chapter is an apocalyptic discourse: it speaks of wars, persecution and the destruction of Jerusalem, and then of the end of the age. The language of sun, moon and stars comes from the prophets (Isaiah 13:10; 34:4; Joel 2:10) and the scene of the Son of Man comes from Daniel 7:13–14. Such language is not a weather report but a way of saying that the order of the old world gives way to the reign of God.

Mark does not give a timetable. He gives a call to watch: the disciples are to read the signs as one reads the fig tree, with attention and without panic.

Structure.

  • vv. 24–25: after the distress, the sun and moon are darkened and the powers of heaven are shaken.
  • v. 26: the Son of Man comes in clouds with great power and glory.
  • v. 27: he sends the angels to gather his elect from the four winds.
  • vv. 28–29: the lesson of the fig tree: when you see these things, know that he is near.
  • vv. 30–31: this generation will not pass away before all this happens; heaven and earth will pass, but my words will not.

Key words.

  • eklektoi, the elect, the chosen: Those whom God has called and gathered, the whole people of God. The gathering from the “four winds” recalls God’s promise to bring home his scattered people (Deuteronomy 30:4; Zechariah 2:6).
  • engys, near: Used of summer, and of the Lord. The Lord is near “at the very gates”. Nearness here is as much a matter of readiness as of time.
  • genea, generation: Can mean the people living at one time, or a kind of people. Verse 30 is one of the hardest in the Gospels (see below).

Verse by verse.

“Then they will see ‘the Son of Man coming in clouds’ with great power and glory.” (13:26)

Daniel’s vision of one like a son of man receiving the kingdom is now fulfilled in Jesus. The one who is about to be judged is the one who will come as Judge and King.

“Then he will send out the angels, and gather his elect from the four winds, from the ends of the earth to the ends of heaven.” (13:27)

The picture is not of terror but of gathering. The end is the final coming together of God’s scattered people.

“Truly I tell you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have taken place.” (13:30)

Interpreters have understood this in different ways. Many hold that it refers to the generation that would see the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, which Jesus describes as the first great sign of the end; others take “generation” as the whole people of faith, or take “all these things” as the signs that are the beginning. All agree that Jesus gives no date, as the next verse says (13:32).

“Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away.” (13:31)

The sure ground of hope is not the calendar but the word of Christ. Everything else may change; his promise will not.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s glorious return awaits the end of history and that the Church’s task is to watch and pray, for “about that day or hour no one knows” (CCC 668–677; Mark 13:32). It teaches that the Last Judgment will come when Christ returns in glory, and that God alone knows the hour (CCC 1040). The Church rejects any attempt to calculate the date. St Augustine, in a letter to Bishop Hesychius, wrote that Christians should not try to determine the time of the Lord’s coming but should live always ready for it (in substance, Letter 199).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus say the world will end in the lifetime of those who heard him?

Not in that sense. Mark’s Jesus says in the very next verse that nobody knows the day or hour, not even the Son (13:32). Verse 30 is explained by the Church in different ways, most often as a reference to the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, which Jesus speaks of as an early sign, or to the whole generation of those who see the signs begin. What is clear is that Christ calls his disciples to be ready every day.

For study and teaching

  • James says the farmer waits for the early and the late rains. Where in my life must I wait patiently, doing what I can and leaving the rest to God?
  • Jesus says that his words will not pass away. Which of his words do I most need to hold on to this week?
