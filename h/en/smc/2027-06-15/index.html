Word for the day

“the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news brought to them.” Matthew 11:5

Reflection

Today's Gospel opens with a sentence that fits this Season of the Apostles: “Now when Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples, he went on from there to teach and proclaim his message in their cities.” Matthew has just given us the great missionary discourse of chapter 10, in which Jesus sends the Twelve to proclaim the kingdom. The one who sends is himself the first missionary. Then the scene shifts to a prison. John the Baptist, who had prepared the way, is in Herod's fortress; the Jewish historian Josephus names it as Machaerus, east of the Dead Sea. From his cell John hears “what the Messiah was doing,” and he sends his disciples with a question: “Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?” It is a startling question from the man who had pointed to Jesus at the Jordan. John had announced an axe at the root of the trees and a fire that would burn the chaff (Matthew 3:10–12). Now he hears of a Messiah who heals, forgives and eats with sinners, while the prophet himself sits in chains. Some of the Fathers thought John asked for the sake of his disciples, so that they might go to Jesus themselves; others saw in the question the real night of a faithful man. Either way, the question is ours too, whenever God does not act as we expected.

Jesus does not answer with a title. He answers with what is happening: “Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news brought to them.” Every phrase echoes the prophet Isaiah, who had promised that in the day of salvation “the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5), and that the Anointed One would “bring good news to the oppressed” (Isaiah 61:1). John knew these Scriptures well; he would understand. The list ends not with the most spectacular sign, the raising of the dead, but with the poor hearing good news. That is the sign by which the kingdom can always be recognised, in every age and in every parish. Then comes a beatitude that is also a warning: “And blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.” The word means to stumble over something in the road. Many stumble over Jesus: over his mercy to sinners, his silence before power, his Cross, and later over the weakness of his Church. Blessed is the one who keeps walking.

Paul's letter to the Colossians deals with a different stumbling block. Some teachers in Colossae were offering a religion of rules about food and festivals, of fear of “the elemental spirits of the universe,” and of special visions. Paul says: “If with Christ you died to the elemental spirits of the universe, why do you live as if you still belonged to the world? Why do you submit to regulations, ‘Do not handle, Do not taste, Do not touch’?” Such rules “refer to things that perish with use; they are simply human commands and teachings.” They have “an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-imposed piety, humility, and severe treatment of the body, but they are of no value in checking self-indulgence.” Paul is not condemning fasting or discipline. The Church has always fasted, as our Syro-Malankara brothers and sisters begin to do tomorrow in the Apostles' Fast, and as our own Church does in the Great Fast and on Wednesdays and Fridays. What Paul rejects is discipline cut off from Christ: rules that make us proud, rituals kept out of fear of unseen powers, practices that look holy but leave the heart unchanged. A Christian who has died and risen with Christ in Baptism does not need to fear auspicious hours or the evil eye, nor to buy God's favour by human regulations. Fasting in Christ is love that trains the heart; fasting without Christ is only hunger. John's question and Paul's warning meet here: the centre of our faith is not a system of rules or a Messiah of our own design, but the person of Jesus, seen in his works of mercy and followed without stumbling.

Homily outline

Theme: “the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news brought to them.”

John's question from prison, the signs of the kingdom, and a faith centred on Christ rather than on human regulations.

1. Died with Christ

  • The elemental spirits of the universe.
  • Human commands and teachings.
  • Discipline that flows from union with Christ.
“A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you; and I will remove from your body the heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh.” Ezekiel 36:26
“If with Christ you died to the elemental spirits of the universe, why do you live as if you still belonged to the world?” Colossians 2:20

Apply: Offer one small fast this week consciously in union with Christ, and give up one practice born of fear rather than faith.

2. Are you the one who is to come?

  • The prophet in prison.
  • When God does not act as we expect.
  • Asking Jesus himself.
“Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then the lame shall leap like a deer, and the tongue of the speechless sing for joy.” Isaiah 35:5-6
“and said to him, “Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?”” Matthew 11:3

Apply: Bring your deepest question to Jesus in prayer this week instead of keeping it to yourself.

3. Blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me

  • The poor hear good news.
  • Stumbling over the humble Christ.
  • Becoming a sign of the kingdom.
“The spirit of the Lord God is upon me, because the Lord has anointed me; he has sent me to bring good news to the oppressed,” Isaiah 61:1
“And blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.”” Matthew 11:6

Apply: Do one act of mercy for a poor or sick person this week, so that someone sees a sign of Christ.

Our faith is not a system of rules or fears but a person, Jesus, the one who is to come.

He shows himself in the signs of mercy; blessed are we if we do not stumble over his humility.

Take-home resolution: This week, offer a small fast in union with Christ, bring your hardest question to him in prayer, and become a sign of his kingdom for someone in need.

Full homily

Dear friends, John the Baptist was in prison. He had prepared the way for Jesus, and now he sent a question: “Are you the one who is to come, or are we to wait for another?” (Matthew 11:3). Even a great prophet can have a dark night. If you have ever asked God, are you really there?, you are not alone.

Jesus did not scold John. He sent him an answer from the Scriptures: “the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news brought to them” (Matthew 11:5). Look at what God is doing, he says. These are the signs of the kingdom.

Then Jesus adds: “blessed is anyone who takes no offense at me.” Many people stumble over Jesus: he is too humble, too merciful, he goes to the Cross. Blessed are we if we keep following him even when he does not act as we expected.

Saint Paul warns the Colossians against a religion of rules without Christ: do not touch, do not taste, do not handle. These rules look wise, but they do not change the heart. Fasting and discipline are good, but only when they come from love of Christ, not from pride or from fear of unseen powers. We have died and risen with Christ; we belong to him.

So let us keep our eyes on Jesus himself. Let us look for the signs of his kingdom, especially where the poor are loved, and let us become such signs for others. Blessed are we if we do not stumble over him. Amen.

For children

Children, John the Baptist was in prison and he was sad. He asked Jesus, “Are you really the one?” Jesus said, “Look: blind people see, sick people are healed, poor people hear good news!” When you help someone in Jesus' name, people can see that Jesus is real. Be a sign of Jesus this week!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you sent your apostles to proclaim the kingdom and you went ahead of them to teach and to heal. When we sit in the prisons of doubt and disappointment, as John did, send us your answer: open our eyes to see the blind receiving sight and the poor hearing good news. Keep us from stumbling over your humility and your Cross. You have made us die and rise with you in Baptism: take away every fear of unseen powers and every pride in our own practices, and let our fasting and prayer flow from love of you. Blessed are we if we take no offense at you. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.
