Word for the day

““Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” Matthew 7:7

Reflection

February in Kerala is the beginning of the hot months. The paddy fields of the Kole lands and Kuttanad lie dry after the harvest, the wells begin to fall, and by the afternoon the whole land seems to wait for a rain that will not come until the summer showers. Paul gives words to that waiting. “I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory about to be revealed to us. For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the children of God.” Creation was subjected to futility, “in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay and will obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God.” Then comes one of the boldest images in Scripture: “We know that the whole creation has been groaning in labor pains until now.” The groan of the world is not the cry of a dying thing, but of a mother about to give birth.

We groan too. “And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly while we wait for adoption, the redemption of our bodies.” The Great Fast teaches us to feel this groan honestly. When we are hungry in the late afternoon, when a habit we have given up pulls at us, when prayer feels dry, we are standing with the whole creation in its labour. Paul does not call this despair; he calls it hope. “For in hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what is seen? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience.” Many parishes hold their Lenten retreat, the dhyanam, in these weeks, and families come back from those days of listening with the same discovery: the waiting itself is a school of hope.

Into this waiting Jesus speaks his invitation to pray. “Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” He is not describing a vending machine, but a father. “Is there anyone among you who, if your child asks for bread, will give a stone? Or if the child asks for a fish, will give a snake? If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him!” The Father does not always give what we name, but he never gives a stone for bread. And the same passage ends with the golden rule: “In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” The one who knocks at God's door learns to open his own door to others.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul says that the whole creation has been groaning in labour pains until now (Romans 8:22). In these hot February days, when the fields are dry and the wells begin to fall, we can almost hear that groan. But it is the groan of a mother about to give birth, not of something dying.

We too groan as we wait. In this fast we feel hunger, we feel the pull of the habits we have given up. Paul says: in hope we were saved; and if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience (Romans 8:24–25).

Jesus teaches us how to wait: by prayer. Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you (Matthew 7:7). Our Father in heaven will never give us a stone when we ask for bread. He may not give what we name, but he always gives what is good.

And he ends with the golden rule: do to others as you would have them do to you (Matthew 7:12). Let us knock at God's door this week, and open our own door to others. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: ask, and you will receive. God is a good Father who loves to give good things. What will you ask him for today? And what good thing will you give to someone else?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, with the whole creation we groan and wait for the glory to come. Teach us to hope for what we do not see and to wait for it with patience. You told us to ask, to search and to knock; give us the confidence of children before the Father who never gives a stone for bread, and make us generous to others as we want them to be to us. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings teach us to ask and to wait. Paul hears the whole of creation groaning like a woman in labour, and says that we groan with it while we wait in hope. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells his disciples to keep asking, seeking and knocking, because the one they ask is their Father. The fast is a season for this kind of longing. The key words are hope, groaning and ask.

Romans 8:18–25 · Creation waits with us

Setting.

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome around AD 57, probably from Corinth, to a church he had not yet visited. Romans 8 is the high point of the letter's first part. It describes life in the Spirit: we are children of God and heirs with Christ, “if, in fact, we suffer with him” (8:17). Our passage takes up that suffering, and sets it against what is to come.

Structure.

  • v. 18: the thesis: present suffering against coming glory.
  • vv. 19–22: creation waits, subjected to futility, groaning in labour.
  • vv. 23–25: we also groan, and we hope for what we do not see.

Key words.

  • apokaradokia, eager longing: A rare word, found in the New Testament only here and at Philippians 1:20. It suggests someone craning the neck to see what is coming.
  • mataiotēs, futility: The word the Greek Old Testament uses for “vanity” in Ecclesiastes. Creation cannot reach its goal; it is like a work left unfinished.
  • aparchē, first fruits: The first part of the harvest, which is a pledge of the rest. The Spirit we already have is the beginning of what we shall receive.

Verse by verse.

“I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory about to be revealed to us.” (8:18)

The man who writes this has known beatings and shipwreck (2 Corinthians 11:23–27). He is not speaking lightly of pain. He is weighing it against something.

“for the creation was subjected to futility, not of its own will but by the will of the one who subjected it, in hope” (8:20)

Most commentators take “the one who subjected it” to be God, after the sin of Adam, when the ground was cursed (Genesis 3:17). The last two words matter most: even this subjection was “in hope”.

“We know that the whole creation has been groaning in labor pains until now;” (8:22)

Labour pains hurt, but they are the pains of a birth. Paul does not say that the world is dying. He says that something is about to be born.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage to show the common destiny of the material world and of humanity (CCC 1046–1047). St Irenaeus uses it to teach that creation itself will be renewed and not thrown away (in substance; Against Heresies V, 32). Pope Francis recalls v. 22 at the opening of Laudato Si' (no. 2), saying that the earth itself is among the most abandoned and maltreated of our poor.

Matthew 7:7–12 · How much more your Father

Setting.

We are near the end of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7). Jesus has already taught his disciples the Our Father (6:9–13) and has told them not to worry, because the Father knows their needs (6:25–33). Now he returns to prayer. Luke keeps a parallel in his own teaching on prayer (Luke 11:9–13), and there the Father's good gift is the Holy Spirit.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: three commands and three promises.
  • vv. 9–11: two small examples from family life, and an argument from the lesser to the greater.
  • v. 12: the rule for how we treat others.

Key words.

  • aiteite, zēteite, krouete, ask, search, knock: All three Greek commands are in the present tense, which suggests continuing action: keep asking, keep searching, keep knocking.
  • posō mallon, how much more: A familiar way of arguing in Jewish teaching, from the small case to the great. If flawed parents know how to give, what of God?
  • ho nomos kai hoi prophētai, the law and the prophets: The same phrase stands at 5:17, at the start of the Sermon's teaching on the Law. Together they frame everything between.

Verse by verse.

““Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened for you.” (7:7)

“It will be given” and “will be opened” do not name who acts, a way of speech that points reverently to God.

“If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him!” (7:11)

Jesus says “who are evil” almost in passing, and he goes on to speak of our love for our children. Even wounded human love is a small window on the Father.

““In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets.” (7:12)

The Book of Tobit has it in a negative form (4:15), and Rabbi Hillel is remembered for a similar saying. Jesus gives it as a positive command: do not wait, begin.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Jesus teaches a filial boldness in prayer (CCC 2610), and it deals honestly with the complaint that our prayer is not heard (CCC 2735–2737). It also names this verse the Golden Rule, one of the rules that always apply in moral decisions (CCC 1789). St Augustine, writing to the widow Proba, says that God does not need to be told our wishes, but that in prayer our desire is stretched so we can receive what he wants to give (in substance).

Questions people ask

I have asked and knocked for years. Why has the door not opened?

The verse does not promise that we will get exactly what we name. It promises that the Father gives good things and never a stone. Paul can say a few lines later that “we do not know how to pray as we ought” (Romans 8:26). Keep praying, and if it helps, speak with a priest about what you are asking (CCC 2735–2737).

For study and teaching

  • Where in my life am I groaning, and can I see it as labour rather than as an ending?
  • Which part of Matthew 7:7 do I find hardest to keep on doing: asking, searching or knocking?
