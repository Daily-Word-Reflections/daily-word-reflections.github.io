Readings

  • Epistle Galatians 5:1–6 · Faith working through love
  • Gospel Mark 7:31–37 · Jesus heals a deaf man

Word for the day

“They were astounded beyond measure, saying, “He has done everything well; he even makes the deaf to hear and the mute to speak.”” Mark 7:37

Reflection

Paul's letter to the Galatians is the most passionate thing he ever wrote, and today's reading opens with its battle-cry: “For freedom Christ has set us free. Stand firm, therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.” The Galatians were Gentiles who had come to faith in the Risen Christ through Paul's preaching. Then teachers arrived who told them that faith and Baptism were not enough: to belong fully to God's people, the men must also be circumcised and the whole Law of Moses taken up. Paul's answer is fierce because so much is at stake. If a Gentile must first become a Jew in order to be saved by Christ, then Christ's death and Resurrection are not enough, and grace has become a reward to be earned. “You who want to be justified by the law have cut yourselves off from Christ; you have fallen away from grace.” Paul is not attacking the Jewish people or their faithful observance of the covenant God gave them; he himself was a Jew who loved the Law. He is defending the truth that the Gentiles are welcomed into the family of Abraham by grace, through faith in the Risen Lord. Easter is liberation: the Lord who broke the chains of death does not hand us a new set of chains.

But freedom in Christ is not freedom to do as we please. Paul ends with a sentence that holds together what Christians have too often pulled apart: “For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything; the only thing that counts is faith working through love.” Faith is not a private opinion that leaves life unchanged; it works, and the work it does is love. The Catholic faith has always insisted on both halves: we are saved by grace through faith, not by our own merits; and the faith that saves is living faith, which shows itself in love. As Saint James says, “faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead” (James 2:17). A few verses later in Galatians Paul will say that the whole law is summed up in a single commandment, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Christian freedom is the freedom of a son or daughter in the Father's house, no longer working for wages but serving out of love.

The Gospel takes Jesus into the Decapolis, the ten Greek-speaking cities on the far side of the Sea of Galilee, a largely Gentile region. There “they brought to him a deaf man who had an impediment in his speech.” Mark describes every gesture with care. Jesus “took him aside in private, away from the crowd,” as if to spare him embarrassment; he puts his fingers into his ears, spits and touches his tongue; then, “looking up to heaven, he sighed and said to him, ‘Ephphatha,’ that is, ‘Be opened.’” The Lord who could heal with a word chooses to heal with touch, with his own body. These are already sacramental signs: God's grace comes to us through what can be seen and touched. “And immediately his ears were opened, his tongue was released, and he spoke plainly.” In the ancient Church this Gospel was acted out at Baptism: the bishop touched the ears and mouth of the one to be baptised and said, Ephphatha, so that the newly baptised might hear the word of God and speak his praise. The Latin rite of Baptism still keeps it. The crowd's cry, “He has done everything well,” echoes the first page of the Bible, where God saw everything he had made and it was very good. In the Risen Christ creation is being made new. In the Syro-Malabar Holy Qurbana, before the Gospel is proclaimed, the people sing Halleluia: ears opened to hear the Gospel, tongues loosed to praise.

Full homily

Dear friends, Paul writes to the Galatians with a battle-cry: For freedom Christ has set us free. Stand firm, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.

Some teachers had told these Gentile Christians that faith in Christ was not enough. Paul says: if you try to earn salvation, you cut yourselves off from grace. The Risen Lord who broke the chains of death does not give us new chains.

But Christian freedom is not doing whatever we like. Paul says: the only thing that counts is faith working through love. Faith that does not love is dead. Faith that loves lives in true freedom.

In the Gospel people bring a deaf man to Jesus. Jesus takes him aside, touches his ears and tongue, looks up to heaven, sighs and says: Ephphatha, Be opened. At once he hears and speaks.

The Lord still heals us through signs he can touch us with: water, oil, bread and wine. In every sacrament he says over us, Ephphatha. And the people cry: He has done everything well.

Today, let us ask him to open our ears to his word and our lips to praise him and to speak kindly. And let our faith work through love. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus met a man who could not hear. Jesus touched his ears and said, ‘Ephphatha!’ which means ‘Be opened!’ And the man could hear!

Let us ask Jesus to open our ears to listen to God and to our parents, and our mouths to say kind words.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen Saviour, you won our freedom, and you opened the ears of the deaf and loosed the tongue of the mute. Say over us your word, Ephphatha: open our ears to hear your Gospel and our lips to proclaim your praise. Keep us from every yoke of slavery, and let our faith be faith working through love, so that all may say of your work in us, He has done everything well. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the season of the Resurrection the Church thinks of those who have just been baptized and of all of us who were raised with Christ in baptism. Paul tells the Galatians that Christ has released them from slavery, and that what counts is “faith working through love”. In the Gospel Jesus opens the ears and loosens the tongue of a man who could not hear or speak well. Freedom in Paul and opening in Mark are one gift: the risen Lord releases what was bound so that we can hear his word and speak his praise. The words to watch are freedom, yoke and Ephphatha.

Galatians 5:1–6 · For freedom Christ has set us free

Setting.

Paul had brought the Gospel to the Galatians, Gentiles in central Asia Minor. After he left, other teachers came and told them that to belong fully to God’s people they must also be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses. For Paul this was not a small matter of custom. If a Gentile is told that circumcision is needed in order to be justified, then Christ alone is no longer enough. Chapters 3–4 have argued this from Abraham and from the two sons of Abraham; today’s verses draw the conclusion.

Paul is not attacking the Jewish people or Jewish practice. He was a Jew himself, and he speaks here to Gentile believers about what is required of them. His point is that righteousness comes by God’s grace in Christ, received in faith, and not by taking on the Law as a condition.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the thesis: “For freedom Christ has set us free”; stand firm.
  • vv. 2–4: a solemn warning: to seek justification by the Law is to be cut off from Christ.
  • v. 5: the Christian way of waiting: by the Spirit, through faith, in hope.
  • v. 6: what really counts: faith working through love.

Key words.

  • eleutheria, freedom: Not the right to do anything at all, but release from slavery into sonship. A few verses later Paul adds: “do not use your freedom as an opportunity for self-indulgence, but through love become slaves to one another” (5:13).
  • zygos, yoke: The wooden beam on the neck of an ox. Jewish teachers could speak warmly of the “yoke of the Law” as a joyful service. Paul uses the image for anything that would put a believer back under slavery, and Jesus offers his own yoke as easy (Matthew 11:29–30).
  • energoumenē, working: The word from which “energy” comes. Faith is not idle. It is alive and at work, and its working is love.

Verse by verse.

“For freedom Christ has set us free. Stand firm, therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.” (5:1)

The freedom is already given. Paul does not tell them to win it, but to “stand firm” in what they have received.

“You who want to be justified by the law have cut yourselves off from Christ; you have fallen away from grace.” (5:4)

Strong words. To make anything else the ground of our justification is to step away from grace, which is the only ground.

“the only thing that counts is faith working through love.” (5:6)

This is one of the clearest lines in Paul on faith and love together. Faith that saves is not a bare opinion; it is faith that loves. By grace, love is the very life of faith.

How the Church reads it.

The Council of Trent took up this verse when it taught that in justification faith is joined with hope and charity, since faith without them does not unite a person perfectly with Christ (Decree on Justification, chapter 7, in substance). Catholics and Lutherans, in their Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999), were able to say together that we are accepted by God by grace alone, through faith in Christ, and not because of any merit of ours, and that this grace renews the heart and moves us to good works. Our good works are themselves the fruit of grace.

Mark 7:31–37 · Ephphatha: be opened

Setting.

Jesus has been in the region of Tyre, where he healed the daughter of a Gentile woman (7:24–30). Now he takes a long road: north to Sidon and then down to the east side of the Sea of Galilee, into the Decapolis, the “ten cities”, a largely Gentile region. Earlier, the people of this same region had begged him to leave (5:17). Now they bring a man to him.

Mark keeps the Aramaic word that Jesus spoke, Ephphatha. It is one of the few places where the Gospels let us hear the Lord’s own language, the sister tongue of our Syriac.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–32: the journey; a deaf man with a speech impediment is brought.
  • vv. 33–34: Jesus takes him aside, touches ears and tongue, looks up, sighs, and speaks.
  • v. 35: the healing.
  • vv. 36–37: the command to silence, and the crowd’s astonished praise.

Key words.

  • mogilalos, with an impediment in his speech: A very rare Greek word. In the Greek Old Testament it appears only in Isaiah 35:6, “the tongue of the speechless sing for joy”, in a promise of the day when God comes to save. Mark is hinting that this day has come.
  • ephphatha, be opened: Aramaic, which Mark translates for his readers. The command is spoken to the man, not only to his ears: the whole person is opened.
  • estenaxen, he sighed: A deep groan. Jesus does not heal from a distance or without feeling. Paul uses the same family of words for creation and the Spirit groaning for redemption (Romans 8:22–26).

Verse by verse.

“He took him aside in private, away from the crowd, and put his fingers into his ears, and he spat and touched his tongue.” (7:33)

The healing begins with a meeting apart. The man cannot hear words, so Jesus speaks to him by touch, in a language he can understand.

“Then looking up to heaven, he sighed and said to him, “Ephphatha,” that is, “Be opened.”” (7:34)

He looks to the Father before he speaks. The healing comes from the communion between the Son and the Father.

“He has done everything well; he even makes the deaf to hear and the mute to speak.” (7:37)

Many readers hear an echo of creation, when God saw that everything he had made was very good (Genesis 1:31), and of Isaiah 35:5–6. In Jesus, God is making things new.

How the Church reads it.

Very early this healing was joined to baptism. St Ambrose explained to the newly baptized in Milan the rite called “Ephphatha”, in which the bishop touched their ears and nostrils before baptism, so that they might be opened to the word and to the fragrance of Christ (On the Mysteries, in substance). The Church sees in this man every believer whom Christ opens to hear the Gospel and to confess the faith. Those who have received the Word must also speak it: the crowd in the Decapolis could not keep silent.

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus tell them to tell no one, and why do they disobey?

Mark often shows Jesus asking for silence about his miracles. He did not want to be taken for a mere wonder-worker; who he is would be fully seen only on the Cross and in the Resurrection. The people cannot hold back their joy. Now that he is risen, the command is the opposite: go and proclaim it (Mark 16:15).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “Stand firm” in the freedom Christ has given. What old yoke do I keep picking up again?
  • Jesus took the man aside before he healed him. Where could I go apart with the Lord this week, so that he can open my ears and loosen my tongue?
