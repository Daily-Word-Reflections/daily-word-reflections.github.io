Word for the day

“And the slave said, “Sir, what you ordered has been done, and there is still room.”” Luke 14:22

Reflection

Saturday in the second week of Kaitha puts before us two tables: the assembly of the Church in James, and the banquet of the kingdom in Luke. James begins with a question that cuts: “My brothers and sisters, do you with your acts of favoritism really believe in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ?” His picture is simple and true to life. “If a person with gold rings and in fine clothes comes into your assembly, and if a poor person in dirty clothes also comes in, and if you take notice of the one wearing the fine clothes and say, ‘Have a seat here, please,’ while to the one who is poor you say, ‘Stand there,’ or, ‘Sit at my feet,’ have you not made distinctions among yourselves, and become judges with evil thoughts?” The assembly of the Church, which gathers around the Lord's table, is the one place where no one should be placed by his clothes or his wallet. In our own parishes, at the feast-day lunch and at the Sunday Qurbana, the question is just as real: who is welcomed first, who is asked to speak, who is given the good seat, and who stands at the back?

James then says what God does: “Listen, my beloved brothers and sisters. Has not God chosen the poor in the world to be rich in faith and to be heirs of the kingdom that he has promised to those who love him?” God does not despise the rich; but he has a special tenderness for the poor, and the Church's teaching speaks of a preferential love for them. James reminds a community in trouble that the very people they flatter are often those who drag them into court, and “you have dishonored the poor.” The remedy is what he calls the royal law, the law of the King: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Moses had already forbidden partiality in a court: “you shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great: with justice you shall judge your neighbor” (Leviticus 19:15). Whoever loves in this way does well; whoever flatters the great and scorns the small breaks the law of love, however religious he may seem.

In Luke's Gospel Jesus is a guest at table in the house of a leading Pharisee. One of the guests, hearing him talk of invitations and of the resurrection, says a pious thing: “Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!” Jesus answers with a story. “Someone gave a great dinner and invited many. At the time for the dinner he sent his slave to say to those who had been invited, ‘Come; for everything is ready now.’ But they all alike began to make excuses.” The excuses are polite, and the things they name are good things: “I have bought a piece of land, and I must go out and see it”; “I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I am going to try them out”; “I have just been married, and therefore I cannot come.” Land, work and family are not evil. But each guest puts a good thing before the invitation, and the host's “everything is ready now” goes unanswered. Saint Gregory the Great read in these three excuses the land of pride and possession, the oxen of busy affairs, and the wife of comfort; whatever the reading, the lesson is that the call of God cannot always wait until our business is finished.

Then the host's anger turns into a welcome. “Go out at once into the streets and lanes of the town and bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame.” The slave goes, and comes back: “Sir, what you ordered has been done, and there is still room.” There is still room: it is the sentence that has driven the Church's mission since the first apostles. The master sends him again to the roads and lanes, “and compel people to come in, so that my house may be filled.” The word “compel” is not an order to force anyone, for the faith can never be forced; it is the host's insistent, urgent love that will not take a polite refusal from those who have never been asked. The parable is not a verdict on any people: it is a mirror held up to each of us who has heard the call, and has found a reason to wait. Put James and Luke together: the poor in the assembly are the guests of the banquet, and the one who prefers the well-dressed to the stranger has already found his excuse. Where is the Qurbana's table set? In the middle of the street, with room for one more. In the Season of Kaitha, the season of harvest, the Church prays that the house will be filled.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the readings put two tables before us. In the first, James asks: “Do you with your acts of favoritism really believe in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ?” (James 2:1). A person with gold rings comes into the assembly, and we give him the best seat. A poor person in dirty clothes comes in, and we say, “Stand there.”

James says: this is not the Lord's way. God has chosen the poor to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom (James 2:5). The law that rules the Christian assembly is the royal law: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself” (James 2:8). In our churches nobody should be placed higher or lower because of his clothes or his money.

In the Gospel, Jesus tells of a man who gave a great dinner and invited many. “Come; for everything is ready now.” But they all made excuses (Luke 14:17–18): a field, oxen, a wedding. These are all good things. But when God calls, they cannot be put first.

Then the master sends his servant to the streets and lanes to bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame. The servant says, “there is still room” (Luke 14:22). That is the heart of God: there is always room for one more. We should be the same.

This week let us look at our excuses, and let us look at the person nobody notices. Let us greet that person, sit with him, and invite him. And let us answer the Lord's call: “Come; for everything is ready now.” Amen.

For children

Children, God invited everybody to a great feast, even those whom nobody invited. When someone is alone at school or in the church, you can say, “Come and sit with us. There is still room!”

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, host of the great supper, you invite us and tell us that everything is ready. Forgive us the excuses we have put before your call, and the favouritism we have shown in your house. Teach us to love our neighbour as ourselves; make us glad to see the poor in your Church, and give us a heart that makes room for one more. Fill your house with those who have never been asked, and bring us all to the table of your kingdom, where you live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about who sits at God’s table. James scolds a community that gives the best seat to the person in fine clothes and tells the poor person to stand, when God has chosen the poor to be rich in faith. In Luke, a guest at a dinner exclaims, “Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!”, and Jesus answers with a story of a great banquet where those first invited make excuses and the host fills his house with the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame. God’s table is wide, and the question is whether we will come.

James 2:1–8 · God chose the poor

Setting.

James now turns from trials and prayer to a very concrete example of faith in life: how the assembly treats people. The “assembly” in verse 2 is the Greek word synagōgē, the gathering of the Christian community, a reminder that these first believers were Jewish Christians who still used the language of the synagogue.

James is not describing every rich person. He is describing a situation in the community: a visitor with gold rings and fine clothes is given honour, and a poor visitor is pushed aside. The verses about the rich who “oppress you” and “drag you into court” (2:6) point to the experience of these believers, who in some places had been exploited by wealthy and powerful people. James is not hating the rich, who are called to conversion like everyone else; he is protecting the dignity of the poor.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the principle: faith in the glorious Lord does not go with favouritism.
  • vv. 2–4: the scene: the rich man and the poor man; the seat of honour and the place on the floor.
  • vv. 5–7: God’s choice: the poor, rich in faith, heirs of the kingdom; the community’s own contempt.
  • v. 8: the royal law: love your neighbour as yourself.

Key words.

  • prosōpolēmpsia, favouritism (literally, receiving by the face): The word is built from “to lift up a face”, to favour someone because of outward appearance. The Bible uses it of what God does not do: “God shows no partiality” (Romans 2:11). The community that shows it contradicts the God it worships.
  • nomos basilikos, royal law: The law of the King, or the supreme law. James uses it for the command in Leviticus 19:18, which Jesus placed beside the love of God as the heart of the Law (Matthew 22:39). It is “royal” because the Kingdom’s own rule is love.
  • plousious en pistei, rich in faith: The poor have no earthly security; so they are open to receive from God. James does not say the poor are automatically holy; he says that God has a special love for them.

Verse by verse.

“do you with your acts of favoritism really believe in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ?” (2:1)

James puts it as a question about belief. Faith in the glorious Lord, who made himself poor, cannot live alongside the judging of people by their wealth.

“Has not God chosen the poor in the world to be rich in faith and to be heirs of the kingdom that he has promised to those who love him?” (2:5)

The Church’s “preferential love” for the poor rests on verses like this. God’s choice is not because the poor are better but because they are the ones who most need him and are most likely to trust him.

“You do well if you really fulfill the royal law according to the scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”” (2:8)

James is fair: the answer to favouritism is not coldness but love. When love is the rule, the seat at the table is for everyone.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that all human beings have the same dignity, made in the image of God, and that this equal dignity requires us to work for fairer and more humane conditions (CCC 1934–1935). The Church’s care for the poor, which has always been part of her life, is not an extra but a way of being faithful to the Lord who chose to be poor himself (CCC 2444).

Luke 14:15–24 · The great dinner

Setting.

Jesus is at a meal in the house of a leading Pharisee on a Sabbath (14:1). He has just told the guests to invite “the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind” rather than those who can repay (14:12–14). One of the guests, hearing this, exclaims a pious blessing about the banquet of the kingdom of God. Jesus answers with a parable.

In the ancient Near East, as many commentators note, a feast often had two invitations: first a notice, then, when the meal was ready, a messenger to call the guests. Those who had accepted the first invitation now make excuses at the second, which was a serious insult to the host.

Structure.

  • v. 15: the guest’s exclamation: blessed is the one who eats bread in the kingdom.
  • vv. 16–17: the host invites many; the servant: “Come; for everything is ready now.”
  • vv. 18–20: three excuses: the field, the oxen, the marriage.
  • vv. 21–23: the master’s anger; the poor and the crippled; then the roads outside: “there is still room.”
  • v. 24: the closing word.

Key words.

  • paraiteisthai, to excuse oneself, to beg off: Each guest says the same thing, “please accept my regrets”. The excuses are not wicked: a field, a farm animal, a new marriage. The tragedy is that good things are put ahead of the invitation.
  • anankason, compel, urge strongly: The servant is to press the strangers so that they will not stay outside through shyness, because they feel unworthy to come. It is the urgency of hospitality. The Church has always taught that faith must be a willing answer and cannot be forced (CCC 160).

Verse by verse.

“Blessed is anyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!” (14:15)

The guest is right. Jesus does not correct him but asks whether he and others will actually come.

“But they all alike began to make excuses.” (14:18)

The excuses are ordinary. The danger of the kingdom invitation is not that people refuse it with hatred, but that they say, “Not now; I am busy.”

“bring in the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame.” (14:21)

These are the very people Jesus told his host to invite (14:13). The master’s house is not full of the “right” people but of those who know their need.

“Sir, what you ordered has been done, and there is still room.” (14:22)

The great generosity of the master: even after the poor of the town are inside, there is still room. God’s house is always larger than we expect.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in the banquet an image of the kingdom and also of the Eucharist, the “sacred banquet” in which Christ is received and a pledge of future glory is given to us (CCC 1402). She also hears in the story an invitation to all nations. It must not be read as if God has rejected Israel: Paul writes that God’s gifts and his call are irrevocable (Romans 11:29), and the Church teaches that the Jewish people remain beloved of God (Nostra Aetate 4). The parable warns each of us, whoever we are, not to put anything before the invitation.

Questions people ask

Does the parable mean that God has turned away from those who were first invited?

No. The parable is told at table to a group of Pharisees, and Jesus says it to warn everyone who is present, including his own disciples, that excuses can keep any of us from the feast. The first invited were people who had the promises; Paul says that God does not take back his call (Romans 11:29). In Luke’s second volume, the Acts of the Apostles, the first believers are Jews who accept the invitation, and then it goes out also to the Gentiles. The parable shows the generosity of God, who keeps widening the table; it does not say that anyone is cast out for what they are.

For study and teaching

  • James says that faith and favouritism cannot go together. Whom do I treat as less important because of clothes, money or position, and how will I change that?
  • The guests in the parable said, “please accept my regrets.” What good thing in my life most often keeps me from the Lord’s invitation, and what is my next step?
