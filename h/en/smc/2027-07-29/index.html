Word for the day

“Jesus said to her, “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?”” John 11:25–26

Reflection

Of all the friends of Jesus, the house at Bethany seems to have been the one where he felt most at home. The Gospel of John says it in a single sentence: “Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus.” Bethany lay about two miles from Jerusalem, on the road he walked on his way to the feast, and there a woman named Martha welcomed him into her home. We know her from three scenes. In Luke she is the one busy with “many tasks,” who comes to the Lord and says: “Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to do all the work by myself?” In John, at the death of her brother, she is the one who runs out to meet him on the road and makes a confession of faith. And at the supper in Bethany six days before the Passover, “Martha served.” Today the Church honours her as a saint, a woman who loved the Lord in the practical ways of the kitchen and the road, and who also found the words of faith.

The reading from John is one of the greatest passages of the Gospel. Lazarus “had already been in the tomb four days,” and “many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them about their brother.” When Martha hears that Jesus is coming, she goes out to meet him, while Mary stays at home. Her first words are full of grief and of trust: “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him.” Jesus says, “Your brother will rise again.” Martha answers with the faith of her people, “I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.” And then Jesus lifts her faith higher: “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?” The resurrection is not only an event of the last day; it is a person standing on the road in front of her. Martha's answer, in the verse that follows the reading, belongs among the great confessions: “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.” It is the faith that Peter confesses at Caesarea Philippi, and it is spoken by a woman, before Lazarus has been raised, in the middle of her tears.

The reading from the First Letter of John explains why Martha's service and her faith belong together: “Beloved, let us love one another, because love is from God; everyone who loves is born of God and knows God. Whoever does not love does not know God, for God is love.” Love is not first our achievement. “In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins.” Martha's kitchen, her bread and her fire were an answer to a love that had come first. “No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God lives in us, and his love is perfected in us.” And the letter ends with the words that could stand over every Christian home: “God is love, and those who abide in love abide in God, and God abides in them.” Kerala is famous for its hospitality, for the cup of tea and the rice offered to every guest; the letter to the Hebrews says that “some have entertained angels without knowing it.” Martha was a woman who knew how to welcome Christ in a guest.

The weekday readings meet her at the same door. James says: “Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.” In John, Martha does precisely this: when Jesus comes, she draws near, runs to meet him, and speaks to him without fear. James also says, “Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you,” and he warns against speaking evil of one another and judging a neighbour. In Luke 10 the two sisters are tempted to judge each other, one for being busy, the other for being idle; the Lord does not tell Martha to stop serving, but to stop being “worried and distracted by many things.” He says, “there is need of only one thing,” and Mary has chosen it. The Gospel of the day then speaks with an unexpected voice: “I came to bring fire to the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled! I have a baptism with which to be baptized, and what stress I am under until it is completed!” The fire is the love of God that Jesus came to set alight, and the baptism is his death. Even at Bethany, the raising of Lazarus will lead directly to the chief priests' decision to put Jesus to death (John 11:53). Love of this kind is costly, and it divides households between those who accept it and those who refuse it.

We are in Kaitha, the season of harvest, and Martha is a saint of the harvest. The work of the harvest is also the work of serving: of feeding the workers, washing the pots, welcoming the guest, and keeping the household going. The Gospel does not tell us that this work is a lesser thing; it says that it must be done with the heart of Mary, sitting at the feet of the Lord. In the Holy Qurbana we do both: we sit and listen to the word, and then we rise and serve at the table of the poor. Let us ask Saint Martha to teach us both how to run and meet the Lord, and how to sit and listen; and let us answer in our own lives the question that Jesus asked her: “Do you believe this?”

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Church remembers Saint Martha, the friend of Jesus. We know her from three scenes. In Luke she is the busy woman who welcomes Jesus into her home and cooks for him. In John she is the sister who runs to meet Jesus when her brother has died. At the supper in Bethany, “Martha served.” The Gospel of John says: “Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus” (John 11:5).

The Gospel today is one of the most beautiful of all. Lazarus has been four days in the tomb. Martha hears that Jesus is coming and goes out to meet him. Her words are full of grief and trust: “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him” (John 11:21–22). And Jesus answers: “I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live, and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this?” (John 11:25–26).

This is the heart of our faith. Resurrection is not only an idea for the last day. It is a person: Jesus himself. And Martha answers, in the next verse: “Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world” (John 11:27). She says it in the middle of her tears. When we stand at a grave, in our own grief, the same question is put to us: Do you believe this?

Saint John writes in the second reading: “Beloved, let us love one another, because love is from God… God is love” (1 John 4:7–8). Martha's service was an answer to love. Our hospitality in Kerala, the cup of tea, the meal for the guest, is a way of loving God, if we do it with a heart that is also sitting at the Lord's feet. The Lord does not scold Martha for serving; he asks her not to be “worried and distracted by many things” (Luke 10:41).

The weekday readings say: “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you” (James 4:8). And Jesus says: “I came to bring fire to the earth” (Luke 12:49), the fire of love. Let us pray today: Lord, light that fire in our homes. Let us serve like Martha and listen like Mary, and answer when he asks: Do you believe this? Saint Martha, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Martha was a friend of Jesus who lived in Bethany. She cooked and served for him with love. When her brother Lazarus died, she ran to Jesus and said, ‘I believe that you are the Son of God!’ And Jesus brought Lazarus back to life. At home, you can be like Martha: help set the table, welcome guests with a smile. And you can be like Mary too: sit still and say a prayer to Jesus. Say with me: Jesus, I believe!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you loved Martha and her sister and her brother, and you were at home in their house. You are the resurrection and the life; teach us to answer, as Martha did, ‘Yes, Lord, I believe.’ Give us hearts that run to meet you, hands that serve in love, and ears that listen at your feet. Heal our worry and our distraction; keep us from judging one another. Light in our hearts the fire of your love, and let our homes in Kerala be houses of welcome where you are loved in every guest. Through the prayers of Saint Martha, comfort all who grieve, and raise us with all your friends to the life that does not end. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The weekday readings call for a decision. James gives a string of short commands to those who try to serve God and the world at once: submit to God, resist the devil, draw near to God, humble yourselves, and stop judging your neighbour. In Luke, Jesus says he has come to cast fire on the earth, longs for his “baptism”, the Passion, to be fulfilled, and warns that he brings division, because to follow him cuts through even the closest ties. Today the Church also remembers St Martha of Bethany, the sister of Mary and Lazarus, and gives two readings for her: the first letter of John, “God is love”, and the Gospel in which Martha, grieving for her brother, hears Jesus say, “I am the resurrection and the life”, and answers with a confession of faith. The day’s message is that love and faith ask for a whole heart, and that the one who asks for it is himself the resurrection and the life.

James 4:7–12 · Submit yourselves to God

Setting.

James has just spoken sharply about quarrels and cravings among Christians (4:1–6) and said that friendship with the world is enmity with God. Now he gives ten short commands, in the style of the prophets’ call to repentance. They describe one movement: turn from the devil and double-mindedness, draw near to God, and live humbly with others. The reading ends with a warning against slander and judging, which he treats as a sin against the law of love.

Structure.

  • v. 7: submit to God; resist the devil and he will flee.
  • vv. 8–9: draw near to God; cleanse your hands and purify your hearts; lament and mourn.
  • v. 10: humble yourselves before the Lord and he will exalt you.
  • vv. 11–12: do not speak evil against one another; there is one lawgiver and judge; who are you to judge your neighbour?

Key words.

  • hypotagēte, submit: A military and social word for placing oneself in order under another. Before God it means a willing act of trust, not fear.
  • dipsychoi, double-minded: Literally “two-souled”: people who try to belong both to God and to the world. James uses the word of the one who doubts in 1:8.
  • engisate, draw near: In the Old Testament, priests “drew near” to God in the Temple (Exodus 19:22; Leviticus 10:3). James tells every believer to draw near, with clean hands and a pure heart, as the Psalm says (24:4).

Verse by verse.

“Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.” (4:7)

Resistance is possible because it begins with obedience to God. The devil flees not from our strength but from our belonging to the Lord.

“Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.” (4:8)

God’s nearness answers ours. The first step is a human one, yet God has long been seeking us.

“Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you.” (4:10)

It is the same promise as the Lord’s in the Gospel (Luke 14:11) and of Mary’s song (Luke 1:52). God raises the lowly.

“So who, then, are you to judge your neighbor?” (4:12)

Only God knows hearts. When we judge, we take a place that is not ours.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer (CCC 2559), and that we should not judge others rashly, speak evil of them, or run down their good name (CCC 2477–2479). The Church sees in James’s call to “draw near” the movement of conversion and penance, which begins with God’s own first love for us (CCC 1427–1429). The letter’s call to resist the devil is part of the Church’s teaching that Christians are in a spiritual battle in which they can count on God’s grace (CCC 409, 2846–2849).

Luke 12:49–53 · I came to bring fire to the earth

Setting.

Jesus is on the way to Jerusalem (9:51), teaching his disciples and the crowds about readiness for the coming of the Son of Man (12:35–48). Then his tone turns to himself. Three short sayings speak of fire, of a baptism, and of division. They are among the hardest sayings in the Gospel because they seem to contradict the angels’ song of peace at his birth (2:14).

The fire is generally understood to mean the Holy Spirit, who will come at Pentecost (Acts 2:3), and also the purifying judgment that his coming brings. The “baptism” is his Passion (compare Mark 10:38). The “division” is the result of the choice his coming forces on each person and each household. Verse 53 echoes Micah 7:6, a prophecy of division within families before the day of the Lord.

Structure.

  • v. 49: I have come to cast fire on the earth, and I wish it were already kindled.
  • v. 50: I have a baptism to receive, and I am under stress until it is completed.
  • vv. 51–53: not peace but division; households are divided, father against son, mother against daughter.

Key words.

  • pyr, fire: In the Bible, fire is a sign of God’s presence (Exodus 3:2), of purification (Malachi 3:2–3) and of the Spirit (Acts 2:3). It does not only destroy; it refines.
  • baptisma, baptism: From a verb meaning “to immerse”. Here it means being plunged into death, the Passion, from which Jesus will rise.
  • synechomai, I am under stress: The verb means to be pressed from all sides, held in tension. It shows that Jesus approaches his Passion not with indifference but with a deep longing to complete the Father’s will.

Verse by verse.

“I came to bring fire to the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled!” (12:49)

The fire is lit by Christ’s Passion and kindled at Pentecost. He desires that it burn in every heart.

“I have a baptism with which to be baptized, and what stress I am under until it is completed!” (12:50)

Jesus is not merely a teacher of wisdom; he goes to meet his Passion as the work for which he came.

“Do you think that I have come to bring peace to the earth? No, I tell you, but rather division!” (12:51)

The peace he brings is real, but it is not the peace of avoiding every choice. Christ’s call asks a decision, and where people decide differently, division can come, even in homes.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that fire is a symbol of the transforming energy of the Holy Spirit, and quotes this verse in speaking of the Spirit (CCC 696). It teaches that Christ’s whole life leads to his “baptism”, the Passion, which is the Father’s plan (CCC 536, 607–609). On division in families, the Catechism says that family ties are important but not absolute: a person’s vocation comes from God, and loyalty to Christ may bring misunderstanding and, at times, pain (CCC 2232–2233). The Church does not read this passage as a licence for hostility, since the same Lord commands his followers to love their enemies and to seek peace with all (Matthew 5:44; Romans 12:18). Christians are asked to remain faithful to Christ with gentleness, and to pray for those who oppose them.

1 John 4:7–16 · God is love

Setting.

The First Letter of John was written for a community troubled by a split in its own ranks, around the end of the first century. Its writer insists that knowing God cannot be separated from loving the brothers and sisters, and that the heart of faith is the love of God shown in the sending of his Son. This reading is given for St Martha because she is the woman of Bethany who loved the Lord and served him in her home (Luke 10:38; John 12:2).

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: love one another, for love is from God; the one who does not love does not know God, for God is love.
  • vv. 9–10: God’s love was shown in sending his Son; love is not that we loved God but that he loved us first.
  • vv. 11–12: since God has loved us, we ought to love one another; no one has seen God, but if we love, God abides in us.
  • vv. 13–16: the Spirit is the sign of this abiding; to confess that Jesus is the Son of God is to abide in God; God is love.

Key words.

  • agapē, love: The word John uses of God’s own love, without selfishness, poured out on others. To say that “God is love” is to say that God is a communion who gives himself.
  • hilasmos, atoning sacrifice: The offering that removes sin and restores friendship with God. It echoes the Day of Atonement and is fulfilled in the Cross.
  • menein, to abide: A favourite word in John for staying in lasting communion. God abides in us, and we in him.

Verse by verse.

“Whoever does not love does not know God, for God is love.” (4:8)

This is one of the shortest and deepest sentences in the Bible. All that we say of God must be said in the light of love.

“In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins.” (4:10)

The initiative is always God’s. We love because we have first been loved.

“No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God lives in us, and his love is perfected in us.” (4:12)

God is invisible, but the love of Christians for one another makes him visible. Love is the Church’s chief witness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism begins its teaching on the Trinity and on God with these words: God is love, and the whole life of God is a gift of himself (CCC 214, 220, 221, 257). It teaches that the Cross is the revelation of this love (CCC 604) and that the love of God and neighbour is the sum of the commandments (CCC 2055, 2196). The saints, including Martha in her service, show that loving is not a feeling but a way of life.

John 11:17–26 · I am the resurrection and the life

Setting.

Lazarus of Bethany, brother of Martha and Mary, has died, and Jesus arrives four days later. In the Gospel, Bethany is about two miles from Jerusalem, so many from the city have come to console the sisters. John says “the Jews” here simply of these neighbours and mourners who had come to comfort the family (11:19, 31, 45). Jesus comes to a house in grief. Martha, the active sister, goes out to meet him, while Mary stays at home (compare Luke 10:38–42).

Martha’s words are a mixture of grief and faith: “If you had been here, my brother would not have died.” Yet she adds, “even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask.” Jesus answers with promise, and then with one of the great “I am” sayings of John’s Gospel. Martha’s confession that follows (11:27) is, in John, one of the clearest professions of faith: “You are the Messiah, the Son of God, the one coming into the world.”

Structure.

  • vv. 17–20: Lazarus has been in the tomb four days; Martha goes out to meet Jesus; Mary stays at home.
  • vv. 21–22: Martha: if you had been here my brother would not have died; even now God will give you what you ask.
  • vv. 23–24: Jesus: your brother will rise again; Martha: I know he will rise at the resurrection on the last day.
  • vv. 25–26: Jesus: I am the resurrection and the life; those who believe in me, even though they die, will live; do you believe this?

Key words.

  • anastasis, resurrection: The “rising again” of the dead. Martha shares the faith of many pious Jews of the time, based on Daniel 12:2, that the dead will rise on the last day.
  • egō eimi, I am: Jesus’ solemn way of speaking in John, echoing the divine name (Exodus 3:14). The “resurrection” is not only a future event; it is a person standing before her.
  • pisteuō, I believe: In John, not an opinion but the entrusting of oneself to Christ. “Do you believe this?” is the question to every reader.

Verse by verse.

“Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that God will give you whatever you ask of him.” (11:21–22)

Martha’s prayer is honest. She says her pain, and she says her hope, in the same breath.

“I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live” (11:25)

Jesus does not only promise a resurrection; he is the resurrection. Life is to be found in union with him.

“Do you believe this?” (11:26)

A question addressed to Martha and to every Christian at a graveside. Faith is the answer of the whole person to the One who speaks.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus ties faith in the resurrection to his own person: “I am the Resurrection and the life” (CCC 994). It teaches that death is the end of earthly pilgrimage, yet for those who die in Christ it is a passage to life (CCC 1006, 1010–1014). St Augustine, preaching on the raising of Lazarus, heard in the story a call to rise from the death of sin as well as from the tomb (in substance, Tractates on John 49). The Church sees in Martha both the faithful servant and the believer who confesses Christ in the hour of loss.

Questions people ask

Who was St Martha, and is she the same as Mary Magdalene?

Martha was the sister of Mary and Lazarus, who lived at Bethany near Jerusalem and were friends of Jesus. Luke tells how she welcomed Jesus into her home and was busy with serving, and how the Lord told her gently that only one thing is needed (10:38–42). In John she confesses her faith when Lazarus dies (11:20–27), and she serves again at the supper six days before the Passover (12:2). Mary of Bethany, her sister, is not the same person as Mary Magdalene, who is a different woman from Magdala in Galilee. Martha is honoured as a model of hospitality, service and faith, and the readings of the day show that the two sides belong together: service grows from love, and love is confessed in faith.

Does Jesus really want to bring division?

Jesus does not want hatred or quarrels. He is called the Prince of Peace and gives his peace to his disciples (John 14:27). But the Gospel calls for a decision, and people in the same family may answer differently. When that happens, the Church asks believers to remain loyal to Christ with patience, love and prayer, and not to look for conflict.

For study and teaching

  • James tells us to “draw near to God.” What is one concrete step I can take today to draw near, in prayer or in forgiveness?
  • Jesus says, “I came to bring fire.” Where do I feel the fire of the Spirit in my life, and where am I trying to keep it small?
  • Martha says her grief and her hope together to Jesus. What do I need to say to him honestly today?
