Word for the day

“He said, “The one who showed him mercy.” Jesus said to him, “Go and do likewise.”” Luke 10:37

Reflection

Two weeks after Pentecost the Season of the Apostles shows us the Church on the move. The first reading is the beginning of Moses' great farewell speech on the plains of Moab, at the edge of the promised land. He recalls the word God spoke at Mount Horeb, forty years earlier: “You have stayed long enough at this mountain. Resume your journey, and go into the hill country of the Amorites.” Then the promise: “See, I have set the land before you; go in and take possession of the land that I swore to your ancestors, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.” Horeb, or Sinai, was the mountain of the covenant, the place where God had spoken. It would have been easy to stay there for ever. But the covenant was given for a journey. So it is with Pentecost. The apostles did not stay in the Upper Room; the Spirit sent them out to Samaria, to Antioch, to Rome, and, as our tradition holds, to the coasts of India with Mar Thoma. Every parish, every prayer group and every family is tempted to settle down on its own mountain of good experiences. The Lord says to us too: you have stayed long enough; resume your journey. The longer reading adds that Moses could not carry the people alone and appointed wise leaders, telling the judges: “hear out the small and the great alike; you shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God’s.” The Church on the move shares her burdens and is just to the small.

Isaiah opens his book with a lawsuit in which heaven and earth are the witnesses: “Hear, O heavens, and listen, O earth; for the Lord has spoken: I reared children and brought them up, but they have rebelled against me. The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master’s crib; but Israel does not know, my people do not understand.” The image is homely and painful. A farm animal knows the hand that feeds it and finds its way back to its own manger, but God's own children have forgotten him. The Fathers loved to see in this verse the ox and the donkey at the manger of Bethlehem, where the Lord of Israel lay in a feeding trough, recognised by animals and by shepherds. Isaiah describes a land wounded from head to foot, “bruises and sores and bleeding wounds; they have not been drained, or bound up, or softened with oil.” Remember those words when we come to the Gospel. And yet even here there is hope: “If the Lord of hosts had not left us a few survivors, we would have been like Sodom.” God always keeps a remnant, and through the remnant he begins again.

Paul answers questions the Corinthians had written to him about marriage. Some among them, it seems, were saying, “It is well for a man not to touch a woman,” as though the married love of husband and wife were somehow unholy. Paul disagrees. Husband and wife belong to each other: “The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband.” What is striking, in a world where wives were often treated as the property of their husbands, is that Paul speaks of perfect mutuality: neither has authority over his or her own body; each belongs to the other. Married couples may abstain for a time by agreement in order to give themselves to prayer, and then come together again. Then he adds: “I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has a particular gift from God, one having one kind and another a different kind.” Marriage is a gift; consecrated celibacy is a gift. Neither is to be despised, and each is a true path to holiness. Our Syro-Malabar Church, which crowns the bride and groom in the sacrament of Marriage and whose families have given so many priests and religious sisters to the world, knows both gifts. Today let us pray for married couples, especially those going through difficulties, and for all who have given their bodies and lives to God in priesthood and religious life.

The Gospel is one of the best-loved stories Jesus ever told. A lawyer asks, “Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?” He knows the answer himself: love God with all your heart, soul, strength and mind, and your neighbour as yourself. But “wanting to justify himself, he asked Jesus, ‘And who is my neighbor?’” He wants a boundary: who counts, and who does not? Jesus answers with a story. A man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho is stripped, beaten and left half dead. A priest passes by on the other side; so does a Levite. We are not told why, and we should not mock them; perhaps they feared ritual impurity, perhaps robbers. Jesus, a Jew speaking to a Jew, then names the one person his hearers least expected to be the hero: “But a Samaritan while traveling came near him; and when he saw him, he was moved with pity.” The Samaritan does all that Isaiah said no one had done for wounded Israel: he binds the wounds and pours in oil and wine. He takes him to an inn, pays, and promises to come back. Then Jesus turns the lawyer's question around: not, who is my neighbour, but “Which of these three, do you think, was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of the robbers?” The lawyer cannot bring himself to say “the Samaritan”; he says, “The one who showed him mercy.” “Go and do likewise.” The Fathers saw in the Samaritan Christ himself, who came down to our wounded humanity, poured in the oil and wine of the sacraments, brought us to the inn of the Church and promised to return. Those who have been healed by such a Samaritan cannot pass by on the other side: not by the accident victim on the national highway, nor by the migrant worker far from home, nor by the neighbour of another caste or another religion.

Full homily

Dear friends, two weeks after Pentecost, the readings show us a Church on the move. At Mount Horeb God said to his people, “You have stayed long enough at this mountain. Resume your journey” (Deuteronomy 1:6–7). The apostles did not stay in the Upper Room; the Spirit sent them out, and Mar Thoma came even to our shores.

We too can settle on our own mountain: our group, our habits, our comfort. The Lord says to us: you have stayed long enough. Go out to the family you do not know, to the parishioner who has stopped coming, to those who have never heard of Christ.

Isaiah weeps over a people who forgot God: “The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master’s crib; but Israel does not know” (Isaiah 1:3). A farm animal knows who feeds it. Do we remember the One who feeds us every day? Let our homes show to whom they belong.

Saint Paul speaks to married couples: husband and wife belong to each other, and should give themselves to each other with love; they may pray together and support each other. And he says, each has a particular gift from God (1 Corinthians 7:7). Marriage is a gift; the life of priests and sisters is a gift. Let us honour both and pray for both.

In the Gospel a man lies wounded on the road. A priest and a Levite pass by. A Samaritan, the one the listeners least expected, stops, binds his wounds, pours in oil and wine, and takes him to an inn. Jesus asks: who was a neighbour? “The one who showed him mercy.” “Go and do likewise” (Luke 10:37).

The Fathers saw in the Samaritan Jesus himself, who stopped for us, healed us with the sacraments and brought us to the inn of the Church. Healed by him, we cannot pass by on the other side. Let us become neighbours to the injured, the migrant, the lonely and the stranger, whatever their caste or religion. Amen.

For children

Children, a man was hurt on the road. Two people walked past without helping. But a Samaritan stopped, cleaned his wounds and took him to a safe place.

Jesus says to us: “Go and do likewise.” This week, look for someone who needs help, at school, at home or on the road, and help them!

Prayer

Lord God, you led your people from the mountain of the covenant toward the land you promised, and at Pentecost you sent your apostles to the ends of the earth. Do not let us settle on our own mountain, but send us out. Forgive us when we have forgotten you, though the ox knows its owner. Bless husbands and wives with faithful love, and those you have called to give themselves wholly to you with joy in their gift. Lord Jesus, true Samaritan, you came near to our wounds and poured in oil and wine: make us neighbours to everyone we meet, until you come again. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s four readings move from a mountain to a roadside. In Deuteronomy, Moses recalls the Lord’s word at Horeb: “You have stayed long enough at this mountain”; the people must set out and take the land promised to their ancestors. In Isaiah, God grieves over a people wounded from head to foot, whose sores have not been bound up or softened with oil. Paul answers the Corinthians’ questions about marriage, teaching that husband and wife belong to each other in mutual self-giving. And in Luke, Jesus tells of a Samaritan who stops on the road, pours oil and wine on a stranger’s wounds and binds them up. The God who sends his people out from the mountain sends them to the wounded, and the love he asks of us begins at home and reaches to the stranger. In the Season of the Apostles, the Church hears again: go, and do likewise.

Deuteronomy 1:5–8 · You have stayed long enough at this mountain

Setting.

Deuteronomy is set in the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, in the fortieth year after the Exodus (1:3). Israel is about to enter the land, and Moses, who will not cross the Jordan with them, speaks his last words. The book is mostly made of his great speeches, in which he retells the journey and renews the covenant. Its name comes from the Greek for “second law”, because the Law of Sinai is here repeated and explained.

Deuteronomy calls the mountain of the covenant Horeb, the name it uses for Sinai. Israel had camped there for about a year (Numbers 10:11–12). Today’s verses are the start of Moses’ first speech: the journey begins with God’s command to leave.

Structure.

  • v. 5: beyond the Jordan, in Moab, Moses begins to explain the law.
  • v. 6: God’s word at Horeb: you have stayed long enough at this mountain.
  • v. 7: resume your journey: the regions of the land, as far as the Euphrates.
  • v. 8: see, I have set the land before you; go in and take possession, as I swore to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

Key words.

  • hô’îl mōšeh bē’ēr, Moses undertook to expound: The verb bē’ēr means to make plain, to explain clearly. Deuteronomy is the Law not only given but taught, so that each generation may understand and love it.
  • rab-lākem šebet, you have stayed long enough: Literally “much for you is the dwelling”: enough sitting still. Sinai was the place of covenant, but it was not the goal. God’s people are a pilgrim people.
  • yāraš, take possession: To inherit, to occupy. The land is a gift sworn to the patriarchs, yet Israel must walk into it. God’s gifts are received by those who set out.

Verse by verse.

“Beyond the Jordan in the land of Moab, Moses undertook to expound this law as follows:” (1:5)

Before the people enter the land, they are taught. Faith that will last in a new place needs a clear understanding of God’s word.

“The Lord our God spoke to us at Horeb, saying, “You have stayed long enough at this mountain.” (1:6)

It was good to be at the mountain where God spoke, but God does not let his people settle there. The experience of God is meant to send us out.

“See, I have set the land before you; go in and take possession of the land that I swore to your ancestors, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob” (1:8)

The promise to the patriarchs (Genesis 12:7; 26:3; 28:13) is about to be fulfilled. God keeps his word across centuries.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council calls the Church a pilgrim people, on the way to the heavenly homeland (Lumen Gentium 48–50). Pope Francis recalls that this dynamic of “going forth” runs through the whole word of God, from Abraham and Moses to the apostles, and that today all of us are called to go forth from our comfort zone to reach the peripheries (Evangelii Gaudium 20). St Gregory of Nyssa, in his Life of Moses, taught that the soul that truly seeks God never stands still, but always goes on to more (in substance).

Isaiah 1:1–9 · No soundness in it

Setting.

Isaiah son of Amoz preached in Jerusalem in the eighth century before Christ, from the time of King Uzziah to that of Hezekiah (about 740 to 700 BC). Verse 1 is the heading for the whole book. The chapter then opens like a court case: heaven and earth are called as witnesses, as in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:1), and God sets out his complaint against his children.

The picture of a land burned and occupied, with Jerusalem left alone like a hut in a field (vv. 7–8), fits the invasion of the Assyrian king Sennacherib in 701 BC, when many towns of Judah were taken and Jerusalem alone survived. Many scholars date this passage to that time.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the heading: Isaiah’s vision in the days of four kings of Judah.
  • vv. 2–3: the charge: I reared children, and they rebelled; even the ox and the donkey know better.
  • v. 4: a lament over a sinful nation that has forsaken the Holy One of Israel.
  • vv. 5–6: the people as a body beaten from head to foot, its wounds untended.
  • vv. 7–9: the land devastated; Zion left like a hut; only a few survivors kept by the Lord.

Key words.

  • ḥāzôn, vision: The word for what a prophet sees and hears from God. It covers the whole book: Isaiah’s message is not his own opinion but what God showed him.
  • qĕdôš yiśrā’ēl, the Holy One of Israel: Isaiah’s own title for God, used about twenty-five times in the book and rarely elsewhere. It goes back to his vision in the Temple: “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts” (6:3). The Holy One has bound himself to Israel.
  • śārîd, survivors: Those left after disaster. The thought of a faithful remnant, kept by God, runs through Isaiah (10:20–22). Paul quotes verse 9 to speak of God’s mercy in keeping a remnant (Romans 9:29).

Verse by verse.

“I reared children and brought them up, but they have rebelled against me.” (1:2)

God speaks first as a father, not as a judge. The sin is painful because the love was great.

“The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master’s crib; but Israel does not know, my people do not understand.” (1:3)

Christian art and preaching placed the ox and the donkey at the manger in Bethlehem, drawing on this verse: the animals knew their Lord when many did not.

“they have not been drained, or bound up, or softened with oil.” (1:6)

Oil was the ancient medicine for wounds. The people lie wounded and no one tends them. Today’s Gospel answers this verse: the Samaritan pours oil on the wounds and binds them up (Luke 10:34).

“If the Lord of hosts had not left us a few survivors, we would have been like Sodom, and become like Gomorrah.” (1:9)

Even in judgment God does not let his people be wiped out. The remnant is a sign that his mercy is greater than their sin.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has always read the prophets’ rebukes as addressed first to herself, not to others: whenever God’s people forget him, the warning applies. The Catechism teaches that the prophets formed God’s people in the hope of salvation and that the poor and humble of the Lord, the remnant, would bear that hope (CCC 64). St Paul takes up verse 9 to show that God’s faithfulness to Israel has never failed, because he has always kept a remnant chosen by grace (Romans 9:29; 11:5).

1 Corinthians 7:1–7 · Husband and wife belong to each other

Setting.

Here a new section of the letter begins. The Corinthians had written to Paul with questions, and he takes them up one by one, each time with “Now concerning” (7:1, 25; 8:1; 12:1; 16:1). The first is about marriage and sexual life.

Some at Corinth had gone to the opposite extreme from the ones Paul rebuked in chapter 6. Thinking the body unimportant or unspiritual, they said, “It is well for a man not to touch a woman,” and some married couples seem to have stopped living together as husband and wife. Paul answers that marriage is good, that husband and wife owe each other their love, and that any abstaining must be by agreement, for a time, for prayer.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the Corinthian slogan: it is well for a man not to touch a woman.
  • v. 2: Paul’s answer: each man should have his own wife and each woman her own husband.
  • vv. 3–4: mutual duty and mutual belonging of husband and wife.
  • v. 5: abstain only by agreement, for a set time, for prayer, then come together again.
  • vv. 6–7: a concession, not a command; Paul wishes all were as he is, but each has his own gift from God.

Key words.

  • opheilē, what is owed, due: Translated “conjugal rights”. Literally a debt. Paul uses the language of mutual obligation for love: each spouse owes the other himself or herself.
  • ek symphōnou, by agreement: From the word for harmony, “sounding together”. Even a holy reason such as prayer must be decided together, not imposed by one on the other.
  • charisma, gift: Paul calls both marriage and celibacy a gift of God (v. 7). Neither is a lower road; each is a calling.

Verse by verse.

“Now concerning the matters about which you wrote: “It is well for a man not to touch a woman.”” (7:1)

The NRSV rightly prints the second half as the Corinthians’ own words. Paul quotes the slogan in order to correct it.

“The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband.” (7:3)

Paul begins with the husband’s duty to his wife, and then states the same for the wife. In the ancient world this equal language was striking.

“For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does; likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does.” (7:4)

The sentence is perfectly balanced. Each belongs to the other. This is not domination but mutual gift, the opposite of “All things are lawful for me.”

“Do not deprive one another except perhaps by agreement for a set time, to devote yourselves to prayer, and then come together again” (7:5)

Prayer and married love are not rivals. Paul knows that times of fasting and prayer belong to Christian life, and also that the union of husband and wife is holy and protects their faithfulness.

“I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has a particular gift from God” (7:7)

Paul was unmarried, and he valued that freedom for the Gospel (7:32–35). But he does not make his own path the rule for all.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that both the sacrament of Matrimony and virginity for the Kingdom of God come from the Lord himself, and that esteem for virginity and the Christian understanding of marriage support each other (CCC 1620). Quoting the Second Vatican Council, it teaches that the acts by which spouses are intimately united are noble and honourable, and express and foster their mutual self-giving (CCC 2362; Gaudium et Spes 49). Pope St John Paul II wrote that virginity and marriage are two ways of expressing and living the one mystery of the covenant of God with his people (Familiaris Consortio 16).

Luke 10:25–37 · Go and do likewise

Setting.

Jesus has set his face toward Jerusalem (9:51), and just before this a Samaritan village refused to receive him (9:52–53). James and John wanted to call down fire; Jesus rebuked them. Now, on the same road, Jesus tells a story in which a Samaritan is the hero.

Samaritans lived in the hill country between Judea and Galilee. They worshipped the God of Israel and kept the five books of Moses, but on Mount Gerizim, not in Jerusalem (John 4:20). For centuries there was bitterness on both sides, so much that John can say Jews and Samaritans did not share things in common (John 4:9). The road from Jerusalem down to Jericho drops about a thousand metres through lonely desert country, and was known for robbers.

Structure.

  • vv. 25–28: the lawyer’s question about eternal life; the two great commandments; “do this, and you will live.”
  • v. 29: the second question: “And who is my neighbor?”
  • vv. 30–35: the parable: the man attacked; the priest and the Levite pass by; the Samaritan is moved with pity and cares for him.
  • vv. 36–37: Jesus’ question turned round: who became a neighbour? “Go and do likewise.”

Key words.

  • nomikos, lawyer: Not a lawyer of the courts, but an expert in the Law of Moses. He answers well, joining Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18, the same two commandments Jesus names elsewhere (Mark 12:29–31).
  • plēsion, neighbour: Literally “the one near”. The lawyer asks whom he must count as near. Jesus answers by asking who came near to the wounded man.
  • splanchnizomai, to be moved with pity: A strong word for compassion felt in the depths of the body. Luke uses it of Jesus at the widow’s funeral at Nain (7:13) and of the father who sees his lost son (15:20). The Samaritan feels as God feels.

Verse by verse.

“You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength, and with all your mind; and your neighbor as yourself.” (10:27)

The lawyer quotes his own Scriptures, and Jesus praises the answer. The commandment of love is not new to the Gospel; it is the heart of the Law given to Israel.

“But wanting to justify himself, he asked Jesus, “And who is my neighbor?”” (10:29)

The question looks for a limit: whom may I leave out? The parable will remove the limit.

“But a Samaritan while traveling came near him; and when he saw him, he was moved with pity.” (10:33)

The priest and the Levite saw him and passed by; the Samaritan saw him and came near. The difference is not in the seeing but in the heart.

“He went to him and bandaged his wounds, having poured oil and wine on them.” (10:34)

Wine to clean, oil to soothe: the ordinary medicine of the time. Then the Samaritan gives his animal, his time and his money, and promises to return.

“He said, “The one who showed him mercy.” Jesus said to him, “Go and do likewise.”” (10:37)

The lawyer does not say “the Samaritan”, perhaps unwilling to name him, but he gives the right answer. Jesus ends not with a definition but with a mission.

How the Church reads it.

Origen records an interpretation he received from an elder: the wounded man is Adam, Jerusalem is paradise and Jericho the world, the robbers are hostile powers, the priest and Levite stand for the Law and the prophets, the Samaritan is Christ, the inn is the Church, and the Samaritan’s promise to return is the Second Coming (in substance, Homilies on Luke 34). The Church has kept both readings together: Christ is the Good Samaritan who has come near to our wounds, and he sends us to do likewise. Pope St John Paul II called the parable the heart of the Gospel’s teaching on suffering: we are not allowed to pass by on the other side (in substance, Salvifici Doloris 28–30). Pope Francis built a whole chapter of Fratelli Tutti (2020) on this parable.

Questions people ask

Why did the priest and the Levite pass by?

The parable does not say. Some suggest they feared becoming ritually unclean by touching a body that might be dead (Leviticus 21:1), but they were going down from Jerusalem, and the story gives no reason. Jesus is not accusing priests or Levites as a group, nor the Jewish people; he and his hearers were Jews, and the lawyer’s own answer comes from the Law. The parable warns every religious person, ourselves first, that worship and duty do not excuse us from mercy.

Who were the Samaritans, and why does it matter that the hero is one?

They were a people of the old northern kingdom’s land who worshipped the God of Israel on Mount Gerizim and kept the books of Moses. Between them and the Jews of Judea there was long mistrust (John 4:9). By making a Samaritan the one who shows mercy, Jesus breaks down the walls we build between “our people” and “others”. Luke shows the same openness when Jesus heals ten lepers and only the Samaritan returns to give thanks (17:16).

Does Paul look down on marriage in 1 Corinthians 7?

No. He corrects those who did. He insists that husband and wife give themselves to each other, calls any abstaining a matter of mutual agreement for a short time, and names both marriage and celibacy as gifts of God (7:7). The Church teaches that Matrimony is a sacrament and that virginity and marriage support each other (CCC 1620).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Season of the Apostles our Church remembers how the Gospel went out from Jerusalem to the nations, and how Mar Thoma Shleeha brought it to our land. Moses’ words, “You have stayed long enough at this mountain,” speak to every Syro-Malabar parish and eparchy: the faith we received is meant to go out, and our missionaries in India and abroad carry it on. Every Holy Qurbana ends with a blessing that sends us back into the world; we leave the altar to find the wounded on the road.

Today’s readings also speak to our families. In the Syro-Malabar wedding the bridegroom ties the minnu and gives the manthrakodi, signs of the gift of himself to his bride and of the life they will share. Paul’s teaching that husband and wife belong to each other is lived in such homes. Our hospitals, palliative care and parish charities, and every family that takes in the sick and the old, continue the work of the Good Samaritan.

For study and teaching

  • God said, “You have stayed long enough at this mountain.” Where in my faith have I settled down, and where is God asking me to set out?
  • Isaiah sees a people with wounds that no one has bound up. Whose wounds in my family or parish have been left untended?
  • Paul speaks of husband and wife belonging to each other by mutual agreement and gift. What would help married couples in my parish to live this more joyfully?
  • The Samaritan gave his oil, his wine, his animal, his money and his time. Which of these is hardest for me to give, and to whom is Christ sending me this Sunday?
