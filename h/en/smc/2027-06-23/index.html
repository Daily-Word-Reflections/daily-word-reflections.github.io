Readings

Word for the day

“Just so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents.” Luke 15:10

Reflection

Paul had promised to visit Corinth, and then changed his plans. Some in the community seized on it: here is a man who says yes and no in the same breath; how can we trust his gospel? The opening of his second letter is his answer, and it is a lesson in integrity for every pastor and every Christian. “Indeed, this is our boast, the testimony of our conscience: we have behaved in the world with frankness and godly sincerity, not by earthly wisdom but by the grace of God, and all the more toward you.” Paul's boast is not his eloquence, his miracles or the number of his converts. It is a clear conscience. The word translated ‘sincerity’ is sometimes explained as something held up to the sunlight and found without a flaw. Paul has nothing to hide: “For we write you nothing other than what you can read and also understand”. No double meanings, no hidden agenda, no smooth words that say one thing and intend another. And this integrity is not his own achievement; it comes “by the grace of God.”

Then Paul says something tender about the bond between an apostle and his people: “on the day of the Lord Jesus we are your boast even as you are our boast.” On the last day, a pastor will not be proud of his buildings or his projects but of his people, and a people will be proud of a pastor who was honest with them. In this Season of the Apostles we pray for our priests and bishops, that they may be able to say with Paul that they have behaved among us with frankness and godly sincerity; and we pray for ourselves as a parish, that we may be their joy on that day. Integrity is also asked of every lay Christian: in the account books of a shop, in the marks of an examination, in the promises of a marriage. A yes that is truly yes is the beginning of holiness.

The Gospel is the second of the three parables of the lost in Luke 15. They are told because “the tax collectors and sinners were coming near to listen to him. And the Pharisees and the scribes were grumbling and saying, ‘This fellow welcomes sinners and eats with them.’” Luke also shows Jesus eating in the homes of Pharisees (Luke 14:1); he is not against them. But he wants every listener, in every age, to see the heart of God. “Or what woman having ten silver coins, if she loses one of them, does not light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it?” Think of a village house in the old days, with small windows and an earthen floor, and a woman on her knees with a lamp and a broom, moving every mat and pot, refusing to rest until she hears the clink of the coin. A silver coin was about a day's wage for a labourer; for a poor woman, one coin in ten mattered greatly. Jesus dares to compare God to this woman. God is not only the shepherd who goes out into the hills; he is also the housewife who searches the corners of her own home. Some of the lost are far away in the wilderness; others are lost inside the house, inside the parish, inside the family, unnoticed in a dark corner. The coin cannot search for itself; it can only be found. And when it is found, she does not keep her joy private: “she calls together her friends and neighbors, saying, ‘Rejoice with me, for I have found the coin that I had lost.’ Just so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents.”

The Fathers noticed that the coin bears the image of the king. St Gregory the Great saw in it the image of God stamped on every human being; sin covers the image with dust, but does not erase it. Tomorrow the Church celebrates the Birth of John the Baptist, whose whole mission was to call people to repentance and to prepare the way for the One who came to seek and to save the lost. On the eve of his feast, let us light a lamp in our own house: examine our conscience, sweep away what hides the image of God in us, and go to the sacrament of Reconciliation, where heaven rejoices over one sinner who repents.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul had changed his travel plans, and some people in Corinth said he could not be trusted. He answers: “this is our boast, the testimony of our conscience: we have behaved in the world with frankness and godly sincerity… by the grace of God” (2 Corinthians 1:12).

Paul has nothing to hide. His words mean what they say. That is a lesson for all of us, priests and people: let our yes be yes, in our accounts, our studies, our promises and our marriages.

In the Gospel Jesus tells of a woman who loses one of her ten silver coins. She lights a lamp, sweeps the house and searches carefully until she finds it. Then she calls her friends: “Rejoice with me, for I have found the coin that I had lost.”

God is like that woman. He searches for us, even when we are lost inside his own house, in the dark corners of the parish or the family. The coin bears the image of the king; we bear the image of God, and he does not rest until it shines again.

Tomorrow we celebrate the birth of John the Baptist, who called people to repentance. Let us light a lamp in our hearts, examine our conscience and go to confession, so that heaven may rejoice over us. And let us go and search for someone who is lost. Amen.

For children

Children, a woman lost a silver coin. She lit a lamp and swept the whole house until she found it, and then she had a party with her friends! Jesus says that God looks for each of us like that, because we are precious to him. Is there a child at school who feels alone? Be kind to them today!

Prayer

Lord God, you search for every one of your children as a woman searches her house for a lost coin, and you rejoice with all your angels over one sinner who repents. Light the lamp of your Word in us, sweep away the dust that hides your image in our souls, and bring back to you all who are lost, near or far. Give our pastors and our people frankness and godly sincerity, so that on the day of the Lord Jesus we may be each other's joy. On the eve of the birth of John the Baptist, prepare our hearts for conversion. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul tells the Corinthians that his boast is a clear conscience: he has dealt with them sincerely, by the grace of God and not by worldly cleverness. In Luke, a woman lights a lamp and sweeps her house until she finds one lost coin, and Jesus says there is joy before the angels over one sinner who repents. An honest heart before God, and a God who searches for the lost: the two readings belong together.

2 Corinthians 1:12–15 · The testimony of our conscience

Setting.

Second Corinthians is the most personal of Paul’s letters. Relations with the church at Corinth had been strained: Paul had changed his travel plans (1:15–2:1), and some accused him of being unreliable, saying yes and no at the same time (1:17). Before answering the charge he appeals to his conscience and to their own knowledge of him.

Structure.

  • v. 12: Paul’s boast: the testimony of his conscience; he has acted with sincerity by God’s grace.
  • vv. 13–14: he writes plainly; on the day of the Lord they will be each other’s boast.
  • v. 15: his first plan: to visit them twice, for a double favour.

Key words.

  • kauchēsis, boast: Paul uses the word family of boasting more in this letter than anywhere else. He refuses to boast in himself, but he may boast in what God’s grace has done (10:17).
  • eilikrineia, sincerity: Purity of motive, transparency. Paul has no hidden agenda; what he writes is what he means.

Verse by verse.

“Indeed, this is our boast, the testimony of our conscience: we have behaved in the world with frankness and godly sincerity, not by earthly wisdom but by the grace of God” (1:12)

Ancient manuscripts differ here: some read “frankness” (or simplicity), others “holiness”. NRSV follows the first; the Malayalam POC follows the second. Either way, Paul credits his good conduct to God’s grace, not to his own cleverness.

“that on the day of the Lord Jesus we are your boast even as you are our boast.” (1:14)

Paul looks at the quarrel in the light of the last day. In the end, pastor and people will be each other’s joy before Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that conscience is a person’s most secret core and sanctuary, where he is alone with God, whose voice echoes in his depths (CCC 1776, quoting Gaudium et Spes 16). A good and pure conscience is enlightened by true faith (CCC 1794). Paul’s example shows that a clear conscience is not self-praise: it comes from living before God by grace.

Luke 15:8–10 · The lost coin

Setting.

Luke 15 begins with tax collectors and sinners coming near to listen to Jesus, and Pharisees and scribes grumbling that he welcomes sinners and eats with them (15:1–2). Jesus answers with three parables of the lost: a sheep, a coin and a son. This is the shortest of the three, and the only one with a woman at its centre.

Structure.

  • v. 8: a woman loses one of ten silver coins, lights a lamp, sweeps and searches.
  • v. 9: she finds it and calls her friends to rejoice.
  • v. 10: so there is joy before the angels over one sinner who repents.

Key words.

  • drachmē, silver coin: A Greek silver coin, worth roughly a day’s wage. Ten of them were a large sum for a poor household; some think they may have been her savings or part of her dowry.
  • synchairō, rejoice with: The finder cannot rejoice alone; joy calls for companions. Heaven rejoices with God over every sinner found.

Verse by verse.

“does not light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it?” (15:8)

A village house had few windows and a floor of beaten earth. Finding a small coin meant light, effort and patience. God searches for us with the same care.

“Just so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner who repents.” (15:10)

The coin does nothing to be found; the woman does everything. Repentance is our part, but it begins with God’s search.

How the Church reads it.

St Gregory the Great saw the woman as the Wisdom of God: the lamp is the light of Christ shining in the clay vessel of his humanity, and the house is swept as the conscience is stirred, until the lost image of God in man is found again (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 34). The Catechism turns to the third parable of this chapter, the prodigal son, to describe conversion and the Father’s joy at the return (CCC 1439).

Questions people ask

Why does Jesus use a woman in this parable?

Luke often pairs a man and a woman: Simeon and Anna, the man with the mustard seed and the woman with the yeast (13:18–21), the shepherd and the woman here. Jesus speaks to every listener from ordinary life. The searching shepherd and the searching woman both show how God seeks the lost.

For study and teaching

  • Paul could appeal to the testimony of his conscience. Is there any matter in which my conscience is not at peace, and what step would bring it peace?
  • The woman lit a lamp and swept until she found the coin. Who is someone I could seek out this week with that kind of patience?
