Word for the day

“But as for that in the good soil, these are the ones who, when they hear the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance.” Luke 8:15

Reflection

In the first days of June the southwest monsoon reaches Kerala, and across the paddy fields of Palakkad and Kuttanad, in the hill farms of Idukki and Wayanad, farmers prepare the ground and sow. Today's Gospel could have been told in a Kerala village. “A sower went out to sow his seed; and as he sowed, some fell on the path and was trampled on, and the birds of the air ate it up. Some fell on the rock; and as it grew up, it withered for lack of moisture. Some fell among thorns, and the thorns grew with it and choked it. Some fell into good soil, and when it grew, it produced a hundredfold.” The first surprise is the sower himself. He throws seed generously, even where it seems wasted: on the beaten path, on the thin soil over rock, among the thorns. God does not ration his word to those who seem likely to receive it. Every person, even the hardest, hears the word at least once. Then Jesus cries out, “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!” The parable is not only a description; it is an appeal.

Jesus himself explains it: “Now the parable is this: The seed is the word of God.” The path is the heart where “the devil comes and takes away the word from their hearts, so that they may not believe and be saved”: we hear on Sunday and have forgotten by the time we reach the church gate. The rock is the heart that receives the word with joy but has no root: a retreat, a feast, a moment of fervour, and then in the time of testing the faith falls away. The thorns are the heart that hears, but “as they go on their way, they are choked by the cares and riches and pleasures of life, and their fruit does not mature.” Notice the three thorns: cares, riches and pleasures. Worry and wealth and comfort are not obviously sins, but they can quietly choke the word. And the good soil: “these are the ones who, when they hear the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance.” Hold it fast, and wait. A farmer does not dig up his seed every morning to see if it has sprouted. He keeps the field, clears the weeds and trusts the rain. Fruit in the spiritual life takes the same patience.

Paul's letter shows what happens when a community is still poor soil. “I fed you with milk, not solid food, for you were not ready for solid food. Even now you are still not ready, for you are still of the flesh. For as long as there is jealousy and quarreling among you, are you not of the flesh?” The sign of spiritual infancy in Corinth was not a lack of gifts; it was faction. “For when one says, ‘I belong to Paul,’ and another, ‘I belong to Apollos,’ are you not merely human?” Paul refuses to be the head of a party: “What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you came to believe, as the Lord assigned to each. I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.” Parishes and dioceses can still divide around personalities, preferences and old grievances, and every such quarrel keeps a community on milk. The cure Paul offers is to look away from the servants to the Lord of the harvest: “For we are God’s servants, working together; you are God’s field, God’s building.” The Church is not the property of any preacher or group. She is God's field, sown by the apostles, watered by their successors, and made to grow by God alone.

The Latin Church today remembers the Martyrs of Uganda, St Charles Lwanga and his companions. Missionaries had arrived in Buganda only in 1879. Within a few years young men at the court of King Mwanga II, many of them royal pages, had received the word with joy and held it fast. When the king demanded what their faith forbade, they refused. On 3 June 1886, at Namugongo, Charles Lwanga and a group of companions were burned to death; others were killed in the months before and after, the youngest, Kizito, still a boy of about fourteen. Anglican Christians died with them. Pope Paul VI canonised the twenty-two Catholic martyrs in 1964 and in 1969 became the first Pope to visit Africa, going to Namugongo. Today Uganda's Church is one of the most vigorous in the world. The seed that fell on that good soil bore fruit a hundredfold, exactly as the parable says, with patient endurance.

Full homily

Dear friends, the monsoon has come and the farmers are sowing. Jesus tells us about a sower who throws his seed everywhere, on the path, on the rock, among thorns and on good soil. God is that generous sower: he gives his word to everyone.

The seed is the word of God (Luke 8:11). Some hear it and the devil takes it away at once. Some receive it with joy, but have no root, and fall away when trouble comes. Some hear it, but the cares, riches and pleasures of life choke it. Which soil am I?

The good soil, Jesus says, is the heart that holds the word fast and bears fruit with patient endurance (Luke 8:15). Holding fast means remembering, praying over the word, living it. Patient endurance means not giving up when fruit is slow.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians that their quarrels show they are still infants: one says, I belong to Paul, another, I belong to Apollos. Paul says: I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. You are God's field. Let us never divide our parish into parties; we all belong to Christ.

Today the Latin Church remembers the Martyrs of Uganda, young men who received the Gospel only a few years before they died for it. The seed fell on good soil, and today their Church is full of life. May God's word find such soil in us. Amen.

For children

Children, a farmer threw seeds. Some fell on the path, some on rocks, some among thorns, and some on good soil, where they grew into a big harvest.

Prayer

Lord God, Lord of the harvest, you sow your word in every heart, on the path, on the rock and among the thorns. Break up the hard ground in us, give our faith deep roots, and clear away the cares, riches and pleasures that choke your word. Keep us from jealousy and factions, for we are your field and your building, and you alone give the growth. By the prayers of the Martyrs of Uganda, give us honest and good hearts that hold your word fast and bear fruit with patient endurance. Through Christ our Lord, to whom with you and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings use the picture of seed and soil. Paul tells the quarrelling Corinthians that he planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth, and that they are God’s field. In Luke, Jesus tells the parable of the sower and explains it: the seed is the word of God, and only the heart that holds it fast with patience bears fruit. The Church hears today that ministers are only servants of the growth, and that every hearer must become good soil.

1 Corinthians 3:1–9 · You are God’s field

Setting.

In chapter 2 Paul has spoken of the hidden wisdom of God, revealed by the Spirit to those who are spiritual (2:6–16). Now he turns to the Corinthians themselves. They prided themselves on wisdom and spiritual gifts, but their rivalries over preachers show that they are still beginners. Apollos was an eloquent Jewish Christian from Alexandria who taught in Corinth after Paul had founded the church there (Acts 18:24–28).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: Paul could not speak to them as spiritual people; he gave them milk, not solid food.
  • vv. 3–4: their jealousy and slogans (“I belong to Paul”, “I belong to Apollos”) show they are still of the flesh.
  • vv. 5–7: Paul and Apollos are only servants; one planted, one watered, but God gave the growth.
  • vv. 8–9: planter and waterer work together and each receives his wage; you are God’s field, God’s building.

Key words.

  • sarkinoi, people of the flesh: Not people who have bodies, but those whose thinking and behaviour are still ruled by merely human ways. For Paul “flesh” is human nature left to itself, closed to the Spirit.
  • nēpioi, infants: Little children who cannot yet take solid food. The Corinthians thought themselves mature; Paul says they are babies in Christ, which is no shame for a beginner but is a shame for those who should have grown.
  • synergoi, fellow workers: Literally “co-workers”. Paul and Apollos are God’s fellow workers and also co-workers with each other: not rivals but labourers in the same field.

Verse by verse.

“I fed you with milk, not solid food, for you were not ready for solid food.” (3:2)

A good teacher gives what the hearers can receive. The Letter to the Hebrews uses the same picture (Hebrews 5:12–14).

“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth.” (3:6)

The first two verbs describe single past actions; the third, in the Greek imperfect tense, suggests that God kept on giving growth. The ministers came and went; God’s work continued.

“For we are God’s servants, working together; you are God’s field, God’s building.” (3:9)

Three times in one verse Paul says “God’s”. The Church does not belong to its preachers. The next verses develop the picture of the building, with Christ as its only foundation (3:11).

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council, describing the Church through images drawn from Scripture, calls her the cultivated field, the tillage of God, and also God’s building, quoting this verse (Lumen Gentium 6; CCC 755–756). The Church has always applied Paul’s words to her ministers: they are servants through whom God acts, and the fruit is his. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this chapter, observes that Paul makes himself and Apollos small so that the Corinthians would stop dividing over them (in substance, Homilies on 1 Corinthians 8).

Luke 8:4–15 · The seed is the word of God

Setting.

Luke has just described Jesus going through cities and villages proclaiming the good news, with the Twelve and certain women who provided for them out of their resources (8:1–3). Large crowds are now gathering. The parable of the sower appears in Matthew, Mark and Luke, each with an explanation; Luke’s telling is shorter and lays special stress on hearing the word and holding it fast.

Structure.

  • v. 4: a great crowd from town after town.
  • vv. 5–8: the parable: seed on the path, on rock, among thorns, in good soil; “Let anyone with ears to hear listen!”
  • vv. 9–10: the disciples ask its meaning; the secrets of the kingdom are given to them.
  • vv. 11–15: the explanation: the seed is the word of God; four kinds of hearers.

Key words.

  • mystēria, secrets: Not puzzles but God’s plan, once hidden and now being revealed in Jesus. It is given as a gift to those who come close and ask, as the disciples do here.
  • kalē kai agathē, honest and good: Literally “beautiful and good”, a Greek phrase for a person of noble character. In Luke the good soil is a heart that is sincere and well disposed.
  • hypomonē, patient endurance: Steadfastness that holds on under pressure. Luke alone adds that the good soil bears fruit “with patient endurance”: fruit does not come in a day.

Verse by verse.

“A sower went out to sow his seed” (8:5)

In Palestine seed was often scattered broadly, sometimes before ploughing. The sower is generous; he does not first measure the soil.

“The seed is the word of God.” (8:11)

Luke states plainly what the seed is. The same word of God that Jesus sows will later spread through the apostles, so that in Acts “the word of God continued to spread” (Acts 6:7).

“they are choked by the cares and riches and pleasures of life, and their fruit does not mature.” (8:14)

The thorns are not great crimes but ordinary cares and comforts. They do not kill the seed at once; they slowly crowd it out.

“these are the ones who, when they hear the word, hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patient endurance.” (8:15)

Hearing, holding fast, bearing fruit with endurance: Luke gives a whole programme of discipleship in one sentence. A little later he will show Jesus’ true family as “those who hear the word of God and do it” (8:21).

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council, quoted in the Catechism, compares the word of the Lord to a seed sown in a field: those who hear it with faith and are numbered among Christ’s little flock have truly received the kingdom (Lumen Gentium 5; CCC 543). The Catechism adds that Jesus’ parables call for a decision, and that for those who stay outside, everything remains enigmatic (CCC 546). St Gregory the Great, preaching on this Gospel, admitted that his hearers might be surprised that the Lord calls riches thorns, and explained that riches wound the mind with cares and choke the word (in substance, Homilies on the Gospels 15).

Questions people ask

Jesus says he speaks in parables “so that ‘looking they may not perceive’”. Does he want some people not to understand?

Jesus is quoting Isaiah 6:9–10, where God tells the prophet that his preaching will meet hardened hearts. The words describe the result of preaching to those who refuse to listen, not God’s wish that anyone be lost. Parables reveal to those who come close and ask, like the disciples, and they remain closed to those who stand at a distance. The Catechism says that through his parables Jesus invites people to the feast of the kingdom but also asks for a radical choice (CCC 546).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says that God gives the growth. In my work for the parish or family, do I sometimes act as if the fruit depends only on me?
  • Which of the four soils best describes my hearing of God’s word this week, and what thorns need clearing?
