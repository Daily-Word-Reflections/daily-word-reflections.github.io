Word for the day

“For the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” 1 Corinthians 4:20

Reflection

The first day of June opens with a father's letter. Paul writes to a quarrelsome community he loves, and he does not hide his feelings: “For though you might have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers. Indeed, in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” The guardian in a Greek household was the slave who walked the children to school and kept an eye on them; he could be strict and useful, but he was not their father. Paul claims something deeper. He did not merely supervise the Corinthians; he begot them in faith by preaching Christ to them. That is why he can say, without pride, “I appeal to you, then, be imitators of me.” Faith is handed on not only by teaching but by a life that can be copied. Paul sends Timothy, “my beloved and faithful child in the Lord, to remind you of my ways in Christ Jesus.” This is the apostolic pattern of the whole Season of the Apostles: a father in the faith, a son trained by him, and a community that learns the ways of Christ by watching those who walk in them. In these days, when schools reopen across Kerala and children set out with new bags and new books, the Church reminds parents, godparents, teachers and priests that they are more than guardians. Children learn the faith first from the way we pray, speak and forgive at home.

Then Paul turns to those who have grown arrogant, thinking that he will never come back: “But I will come to you soon, if the Lord wills, and I will find out not the talk of these arrogant people but their power. For the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” Corinth loved clever speech. Some had made themselves important by talking well about spiritual things. Paul asks a simple question: what has your talk produced? The kingdom of God is shown by the power of the Spirit that changes lives, heals divisions and makes people holy. Words about God are cheap; a life changed by God is costly and convincing. Then the tender ending: “What would you prefer? Am I to come to you with a stick, or with love in a spirit of gentleness?” A good father can correct, but he would much rather come with gentleness. The choice, Paul says, is partly theirs.

In the Gospel Jesus speaks some of the sternest words he ever spoke, and he speaks them in defence of the little ones: “If any of you put a stumbling block before one of these little ones who believe in me, it would be better for you if a great millstone were fastened around your neck and you were drowned in the depth of the sea.” The millstone was the heavy stone turned by a donkey to grind grain. Jesus does not use such language lightly. To lead a child, or any simple believer, into sin or out of faith is a grave evil. The Church has had to hear these words with shame in our own time, when some who should have been fathers in the faith harmed the little ones entrusted to them; every parish and institution is bound to protect children and vulnerable people, to listen to them and to act. Then Jesus turns the warning inward: “If your hand or your foot causes you to stumble, cut it off and throw it away.” He is not asking for mutilation; he is asking for decisiveness. Whatever in my life leads me into sin, a habit, a company, an app on the phone, must be cut off firmly, because eternal life is worth more than anything we give up.

Today the Church remembers St Justin, one of the first great Christian thinkers. Born in Samaria early in the second century, he searched for the truth among the philosophers of his day, until an old man he met by the sea pointed him to the prophets and to Christ. He became, as he said, a philosopher of the true philosophy, opened a school in Rome, and wrote to the emperor to defend the Christians. In those writings he left the earliest full description of the Sunday Eucharist outside the New Testament: the reading of the memoirs of the apostles and the prophets, the homily, the prayers, the bread and wine brought to the presider, the great thanksgiving, the Amen of the people, and the gifts carried to the absent. Around the year 165 he was arrested with several companions and beheaded under the prefect Rusticus for refusing to sacrifice to the gods. The readings of his feast fit his life: “the message about the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God” (1 Corinthians 1:18), and “everyone who acknowledges me before others, the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God” (Luke 12:8). Justin's power was not in talk, though he talked well; it was in the witness of his blood.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Saint Paul writes like a father. He says to the Corinthians: you have many guardians, but not many fathers; I became your father in Christ through the Gospel (1 Corinthians 4:15). Faith is not only taught; it is passed on by people who love us and whose lives we can copy.

This week children go back to school. Let us ask ourselves: who are the fathers and mothers in faith for our children? Not only priests and catechism teachers, but parents, grandparents and godparents. Children learn to pray by watching us pray.

Paul says: the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power (1 Corinthians 4:20). It is easy to talk about God. The real sign of the kingdom is a life changed by the Holy Spirit: a temper calmed, a quarrel healed, a habit broken.

In the Gospel Jesus defends the little ones with strong words: it would be better to have a millstone around one's neck than to lead a little one into sin. Let us protect our children in every way. And let us be firm with ourselves: whatever leads us into sin, let us cut it off.

Today we remember Saint Justin. He searched for the truth among the philosophers, found it in Christ, and gave his life for him in Rome. He also told us how the first Christians celebrated the Eucharist on Sunday. When we say Amen at the Qurbana, we say it with Justin. May our faith, like his, be not only talk but power. Amen.

For children

Children, Saint Paul was like a father to the first Christians, because he told them about Jesus. Who told you about Jesus? Say thank you to them today!

Jesus loves children very much. If anyone ever makes you feel unsafe or asks you to do something wrong, tell your parents or a teacher you trust.

Prayer

Father of all, you gave the Corinthians a father in the faith through the Gospel, and you gave St Justin the wisdom to find in Christ the truth he was seeking. Give our children faithful fathers and mothers, teachers and priests, whose lives they can copy. Keep the little ones from every stumbling block, and give us courage to cut off whatever leads us into sin. Let your kingdom be seen among us not in talk but in the power of your Holy Spirit. Through Christ our Lord, to whom with you and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about the care owed to those who are young in faith. Paul writes as a father to the Corinthians: he begot them in Christ through the Gospel, and he asks them to imitate him, warning that the kingdom of God is a matter of power, not of talk. In Matthew, Jesus speaks with unusual severity about anyone who makes one of his little ones stumble. On the memorial of St Justin, a philosopher who found in Christ the truth he had been seeking and gave his life for it, the Church hears that faith is proved not by clever words but by a life given.

1 Corinthians 4:15–21 · I became your father through the gospel

Setting.

These verses close the first part of the letter (chapters 1 to 4), in which Paul has dealt with the parties at Corinth, some saying “I belong to Paul”, others “I belong to Apollos” (1:12). He has just spoken with sharp irony of the Corinthians who think themselves already rich and wise, while the apostles are treated as the rubbish of the world (4:8–13). Now his tone changes: “I am not writing this to make you ashamed, but to admonish you as my beloved children” (4:14).

Structure.

  • v. 15: many guardians, but only one father: Paul begot them in Christ through the Gospel.
  • vv. 16–17: be imitators of me; Timothy is sent to remind them of Paul’s ways in Christ.
  • vv. 18–20: some are arrogant, thinking he will not come; he will come and test their power, not their talk.
  • v. 21: a choice: shall he come with a stick, or with love and gentleness?

Key words.

  • paidagōgos, guardian: In a Greek household this was the slave who took a boy to school and watched over his conduct. He could be strict and useful, but he was not the father. Paul uses the same word for the Law in Galatians 3:24.
  • mimētēs, imitator: The word behind our “mimic”. Paul does not ask to be admired but copied, because he himself imitates Christ (11:1). Faith is passed on by example as much as by teaching.
  • dynamis, power: Not force of personality but the working of God’s Spirit, the same power Paul spoke of in 2:4: his preaching came “with a demonstration of the Spirit and of power”.

Verse by verse.

“For though you might have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers. Indeed, in Christ Jesus I became your father through the gospel.” (4:15)

“Ten thousand” is a deliberate exaggeration. Teachers can be many; the one who first brought them to new life in Christ stands in a unique place. On calling anyone “father”, see the question below.

“For this reason I sent you Timothy, who is my beloved and faithful child in the Lord” (4:17)

Timothy is Paul’s spiritual son too (Philippians 2:22). Paul sends someone formed by him, who will remind them of a way of life, not only of ideas.

“For the kingdom of God depends not on talk but on power.” (4:20)

Paul rarely speaks of the kingdom of God, and here he defines it by what it does. Clever speech was prized in Corinth; Paul says the kingdom is known where God changes lives.

“Am I to come to you with a stick, or with love in a spirit of gentleness?” (4:21)

The stick was the rod of correction used by a father or teacher. Paul would rather come gently, but a true father does not ignore wrongdoing. Chapter 5 will show the correction he has in mind.

How the Church reads it.

The Church sees in Paul the pattern of every shepherd who begets and forms souls in Christ. The Second Vatican Council says that priests, as fathers and teachers among the people of God, should bring up their faithful as spiritual children in Christ, and that they do so above all by the example of their lives (Presbyterorum Ordinis 6 and 9, in substance). St John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, notes that Paul did not say “I gave you teaching” but “I begot you”, the language of the deepest love (in substance, Homilies on 1 Corinthians 13).

Matthew 18:6–9 · Do not make the little ones stumble

Setting.

Matthew 18 is the fourth of the five great discourses of Jesus in this Gospel, often called the discourse on the community. It begins when the disciples ask who is the greatest, and Jesus puts a child among them: whoever welcomes such a child welcomes him (18:1–5). Today’s verses follow at once. The “little ones” are children, and also all the humble and weak believers in the community, whose faith can be easily wounded.

Structure.

  • v. 6: whoever makes one of these little ones stumble: better a millstone and the sea.
  • v. 7: woe to the world because of stumbling blocks; they will come, but woe to the one through whom they come.
  • vv. 8–9: if hand, foot or eye makes you stumble, cut it off; better to enter life maimed than be thrown into the fire.

Key words.

  • skandalon, stumbling block: Originally the stick that springs a trap, then any obstacle that makes someone trip. To “scandalise” in the Gospel is not merely to shock but to lead another into sin or out of faith.
  • mikroi, little ones: Those who are small in the world’s eyes. In Matthew the word marks the disciples who are humble and vulnerable (10:42; 18:10, 14). The Father does not want one of them to be lost.
  • geenna, hell: From the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, once a place of terrible idolatry (Jeremiah 7:31). The name became a picture of final ruin, the loss of God.

Verse by verse.

“If any of you put a stumbling block before one of these little ones who believe in me, it would be better for you if a great millstone were fastened around your neck and you were drowned in the depth of the sea.” (18:6)

The Greek speaks of a millstone turned by a donkey, far heavier than a hand mill. The picture is meant to shock: harming the faith of the weak is worse than death.

“Occasions for stumbling are bound to come, but woe to the one by whom the stumbling block comes!” (18:7)

Jesus is realistic: in a fallen world scandals will happen. That does not excuse the one who causes them.

“If your hand or your foot causes you to stumble, cut it off and throw it away” (18:8)

Jesus now turns from the scandal we give to others to the occasions of sin in ourselves. The language is a vivid way of speaking, not a command to harm the body (see the question below).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism defines scandal as an attitude or behaviour which leads another to do evil, and teaches that it is especially grave when it comes from those who have authority over others or affects the weak; it is this that prompted the Lord’s words about the millstone (CCC 2284–2285). The Church applies the warning with humility to herself, above all where the young or vulnerable have been harmed by those who should have protected them. St Augustine, explaining the cutting off of hand and eye, says they stand for anything dear to us, even a friend or a habit, which draws us away from God (in substance, On the Sermon on the Mount 1.13).

Questions people ask

Jesus said, “Call no one your father on earth” (Matthew 23:9). Why does Paul call himself a father, and why do we call priests “Father”?

Jesus is warning against seeking titles that put a teacher in the place of God, the one Father from whom all fatherhood comes. He is not forbidding the word, for he himself teaches “Honor your father and your mother” (Matthew 15:4), and Stephen addresses the elders as “brothers and fathers” (Acts 7:2). Paul, filled with the Spirit, says plainly that he became the Corinthians’ father in Christ. Christians have used the title for spiritual fathers from the earliest times, as a sign that the life of grace is passed on through those God sends.

Does Jesus really mean that we should cut off a hand or tear out an eye?

No. Jesus is using a strong figure of speech, as teachers of his time often did, to show how seriously we must deal with what leads us into sin. The Church has always understood it this way and teaches that deliberately mutilating the body is against the moral law (CCC 2297). What must be cut off is the occasion of sin: a habit, a place, a screen, a relationship that draws us away from God.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St Justin was born around the year 100 at Flavia Neapolis, the ancient Shechem in Samaria. He searched for truth among the philosophers and found it in Christ, and afterwards taught the faith in Rome while still wearing the philosopher’s cloak. He was beheaded there with several companions around 165. His First Apology gives the oldest full description of the Sunday Eucharist outside the New Testament: the reading of the memoirs of the apostles and the prophets, the homily, the prayers, the bread and wine brought, the thanksgiving and the people’s Amen (chapters 65 to 67, quoted in CCC 1345). Every Holy Qurbana still follows that shape.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says the kingdom of God “depends not on talk but on power”. Where in my life does my faith remain only talk, and where can people see God’s power at work?
  • Who are the “little ones” around me whose faith I could help or harm by my words and example?
