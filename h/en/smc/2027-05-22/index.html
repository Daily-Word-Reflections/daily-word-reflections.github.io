Readings

  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 12:12–20 · We are one body in the Spirit
  • Gospel John 6:25–29 · Believe in the one whom he has sent

Word for the day

“Jesus answered them, “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.”” John 6:29

Reflection

The first week after Pentecost ends with Paul's great image of the Church: “For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ.” Notice the last words. Paul does not say ‘so it is with the Church’, but “so it is with Christ.” The Church is not a club of people who admire Jesus; she is his Body, and he is her Head. At Pentecost the Spirit did not make the apostles a committee; he made them one living organism with the Risen Lord. Paul tells how this happened to each of us: in the one Spirit we were all baptized into one body, and we were all made to drink of one Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:13). Baptism places us in the Body, Chrismation (Confirmation) gives us the Spirit to drink, and the Holy Qurbana, the one Bread, makes us one Body (1 Corinthians 10:17). The Catechism says that the unity of the body does not do away with the diversity of its members (CCC 791).

Then Paul turns playful, and his humour hides a sharp lesson. “If the foot would say, ‘Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body,’ that would not make it any less a part of the body.” He imagines an ear sulking because it is not an eye. “If the whole body were an eye, where would the hearing be?” There are two temptations in a parish. One is envy: I am only a foot, so I am not needed; let the priest and the committee members do everything. The other is pride: I am the eye, and I see everything; the others are useless. Paul answers both with one sentence: “But as it is, God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose.” No one is in the Church by accident. The grandmother who prays the rosary at the back of the church, the altar server who rings the bell, the catechism teacher, the choir singer, the young man who carries chairs after the feast, the sick person offering pain at home: each is placed by God. In the Season of the Apostles, when we recall how the Church was born and sent, we are invited to ask not what the Church does for me, but where God has placed me in the Body.

The Gospel brings us to the morning after the multiplication of the loaves. The crowds have crossed the lake to find Jesus, and he sees through them: “you are looking for me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.” Then he gives one of the great commands of John's Gospel: “Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life, which the Son of Man will give you.” They ask the practical question: “What must we do to perform the works of God?” And Jesus answers: “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” Faith is not idleness; it is the first and greatest work, the work that God himself does in us and with us. Everything else in the Christian life, our prayer, our service, our place in the Body, flows from believing in the One whom the Father sent. On Saturdays many of our families turn with love to the Blessed Virgin Mary, the first to believe the word of the angel and the Mother whom the apostles gathered around in the upper room (Acts 1:14). She is the model member of the Body: hidden, faithful, and wholly turned towards her Son. With her we learn to seek Jesus not for the bread he gives, but for himself, the Bread that endures for eternal life.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this Saturday after Pentecost, Paul describes the Church as a body. “For just as the body is one and has many members… so it is with Christ.”

The Church is the Body of Christ, and he is the Head. In Baptism we were placed in that Body, and we drank the one Spirit.

Paul jokes about a foot that says, I am not a hand, so I do not belong. And an ear that says, I am not an eye. How foolish! Every part is needed. “God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose.”

In our parish there is no unimportant member: the one who sings, the one who teaches, the one who cleans, the one who prays in a sickbed at home. Each is placed by God.

In the Gospel the people follow Jesus because he gave them bread. Jesus says: work for the food that endures for eternal life. And the work of God is this: to believe in the One whom he has sent.

Like the Blessed Virgin Mary, who prayed with the apostles, let us seek Jesus for himself, and serve him gladly in the place he has given us. Amen.

For children

Children, can your eye say to your hand, ‘I don't need you’? No! Your body needs every part. The Church is like that: everyone is important, even the smallest child.

What is your part? Maybe singing, maybe helping, maybe praying for others. Do it with joy, because you belong to Jesus' Body!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Head of the Church, by the one Spirit you baptized us into one Body and gave us one Spirit to drink. Forgive us when we envy the gifts of others or despise our own place; help each of us to serve where the Father has placed us. Give us hunger for the food that endures for eternal life, and the faith that is the work of God. With the Blessed Virgin Mary, who prayed with the apostles in the upper room, may we seek you for yourself and remain one in you, to the glory of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the eve of the feast of the Most Holy Trinity, both readings speak of unity that does not erase difference. Paul tells the Corinthians that the body is one and has many members, and that all were baptized in one Spirit into one body. In John, the crowd that ate the loaves comes looking for Jesus, and he turns them from bread that perishes to the work God asks: to believe in the one he has sent.

1 Corinthians 12:12–20 · Many members, one body

Setting.

In chapter 12 Paul answers the Corinthians’ questions about spiritual gifts. Some boasted of showy gifts; others felt they did not belong. He has just said that there are varieties of gifts but the same Spirit, given to each “for the common good” (12:4–7). Now he takes up an image well known in the ancient world, the body and its members, which writers used to call citizens to harmony. Paul gives it a new depth: the body is Christ’s.

Structure.

  • v. 12: the body is one with many members; “so it is with Christ”.
  • v. 13: one Spirit, one Baptism, one body, across every division.
  • vv. 14–17: no member may say “I do not belong”; each is needed.
  • vv. 18–20: God arranged the members as he chose: many members, one body.

Key words.

  • sōma, body: Paul does not say “so it is with the Church” but “so it is with Christ”. The Church is not only like a body; it is the body of Christ (12:27).
  • melos, member, limb: A living part of a body, not a member of a club. Each limb has its own task and lives only by the life of the whole.
  • potizō, give to drink: “We were all made to drink of one Spirit” (v. 13). The image is of water poured out or drunk. Many commentators hear Baptism and the outpouring of the Spirit; some also think of the Eucharistic cup (10:4, 16).

Verse by verse.

“For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ.” (12:12)

The surprise is at the end of the sentence. Christ himself, head and members, is the one body.

“Jews or Greeks, slaves or free” (12:13)

The deepest divisions of the ancient world, of birth and of social standing, do not divide the body. Paul says the same in Galatians 3:28.

“If the foot would say, “Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body,” that would not make it any less a part of the body.” (12:15)

Paul answers those who feel unimportant. A sense of being less does not make one less a member.

“But as it is, God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose.” (12:18)

The variety in the Church is God’s design, not an accident to be smoothed out.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the unity of the body does not do away with the diversity of its members: in the building up of Christ’s body there is a diversity of members and functions, and one Spirit distributes his gifts for the welfare of the Church (CCC 791; Lumen Gentium 7). Pope Pius XII’s encyclical Mystici Corporis (1943) presents the Church as the Mystical Body of Christ. The image also speaks to the communion of the Churches: the Syro-Malabar, Latin, Syro-Malankara and other Churches are members of one Catholic body, each with its own gifts.

John 6:25–29 · The work of God is to believe

Setting.

Jesus has fed five thousand people with five barley loaves and two fish (6:1–15) and has walked on the sea to his disciples (6:16–21). The next day the crowd crosses the lake in boats and finds him at Capernaum (6:24, 59). There begins the long discourse on the Bread of Life. Today’s verses are its opening exchange.

Structure.

  • v. 25: “Rabbi, when did you come here?”
  • v. 26: you seek me because you ate your fill, not because you saw signs.
  • v. 27: work for the food that endures; the Father has set his seal on the Son of Man.
  • vv. 28–29: “What must we do?” “Believe in him whom he has sent.”

Key words.

  • brōsis, food: Jesus contrasts food that perishes with food that endures for eternal life. Later in the chapter he says plainly what that food is: “the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh” (6:51).
  • sphragizō, set a seal: A seal marks ownership and guarantees what is genuine. The Father has marked the Son as his own. Many of the Fathers connected this seal with the Spirit, who rested on Jesus at his baptism (1:32–33).
  • ergon / erga, work / works: The crowd asks about “works” in the plural. Jesus answers with one “work”: faith. It is called the work of God because God asks it and God makes it possible.

Verse by verse.

“you are looking for me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.” (6:26)

A sign points beyond itself. The crowd saw only full stomachs. Jesus is not scornful; he wants to lead them further.

“Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures for eternal life, which the Son of Man will give you.” (6:27)

Notice the paradox: we are to work for a food that is given. Effort and gift belong together.

“This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.” (6:29)

Faith is not a work by which we earn God’s favour. It is the work God does in us and asks of us, the trusting gift of ourselves to the One the Father has sent.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that faith is a gift of God, a supernatural virtue infused by him, and at the same time an authentically human act (CCC 153–154). St Augustine, preaching on this verse, explained that to believe in him is more than to believe what he says: it is by believing to love him and to go into him and be made one with his members (in substance, Tractates on John). Faith of this kind does not stand against good works; by grace it brings them forth.

Questions people ask

Does John 6:29 mean that faith alone matters and works do not?

No. Jesus calls faith “the work of God” because it is the root of everything else. In Catholic teaching faith, hope and charity are given together by grace, and living faith “works through love” (Galatians 5:6). The good works done in grace are themselves God’s gift and truly ours (CCC 2008).

For study and teaching

  • Which member of my parish or family do I treat as if they did not belong? What would change if I saw them as needed by the body?
  • Why do I seek Jesus: for what he gives, or for who he is?
