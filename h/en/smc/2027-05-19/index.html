Word for the day

“The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in his field;” Matthew 13:31

Reflection

Three days after Pentecost, in the first week of the Season of the Apostles, the Gospel gives us two small parables about the growth of the Church. “The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that someone took and sowed in his field; it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.” Think of the beginning: about one hundred and twenty people in an upper room, a handful of fishermen, a few women, the Mother of Jesus. Think of what came of it: on the day of Pentecost three thousand, and within a few generations communities from Spain to Persia and, as our tradition holds, to the coast of Malabar. The image of the tree in whose branches the birds nest comes from the prophets. Ezekiel saw God plant a tender sprig on the mountain of Israel, so that it would become a noble cedar: “Under it every kind of bird will live” (Ezekiel 17:23). The birds are the nations. Our Church, which began, as tradition holds, with the preaching of Mar Thoma among a few families of Malabar, is one of those branches. That is not a reason for pride but for gratitude, and for patience: God's work begins small and grows slowly, and it is God who gives the growth.

The second parable is even more hidden. “The kingdom of heaven is like yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” Three measures of flour is a great quantity, enough bread for a large household; it is what Sarah prepared when the three visitors came to Abraham (Genesis 18:6). The yeast disappears into the dough; no one sees it working, yet nothing stays as it was. So the Gospel works in the world: not usually through spectacle, but through Christians mixed into ordinary life, in offices and fields, in hospitals and classrooms, in families, quietly changing everything from within. Matthew adds that Jesus spoke in parables to fulfil the prophecy: “I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.” What was hidden from the beginning is God's plan to gather all peoples into one, and Pentecost has revealed it. The apostles were the first yeast; today it is our turn to be mixed into the dough of our society without losing our flavour.

Paul's words to the Corinthians concern the Lord's Supper, and they come right after his account of its institution. Some in Corinth were eating their own meal before the poor arrived, so that “one goes hungry and another becomes drunk.” Paul warns: “But if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged.” And he gives a simple rule: “So then, my brothers and sisters, when you come together to eat, wait for one another.” Before we receive the Body of the Lord, we examine ourselves; our Church has always taught that one who is conscious of grave sin should first approach the sacrament of Reconciliation. And the Body we receive makes us one body with the poor, the late, the one who has nothing to bring. The mustard seed and the yeast grow in a community that waits for one another. It is fitting that the Syro-Malankara calendar recalls today the tradition of the first offering of the Holy Qurbono by Mar Yaqob Shleeha in Jerusalem: from that small first breaking of bread, the Eucharist of the apostles has spread like yeast through the whole world, and it reaches our altar every day.

Full homily

Dear friends, three days after Pentecost Jesus tells us how his Church grows. “The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed” (Matthew 13:31), the smallest of seeds, that becomes a tree where the birds of the air make their nests.

The Church began with about one hundred and twenty people in an upper room. Today she is in every land, and the birds of every nation find shelter in her branches. Our own Church in Kerala began, as tradition holds, with Mar Thoma and a few families. God's work starts small and grows slowly, and it is God who gives the growth.

Then Jesus says the kingdom is like yeast mixed into three measures of flour until all is leavened (13:33). Yeast is hidden, but it changes everything. Christians are meant to be such yeast in offices, fields, schools and homes: not apart from the world, but mixed into it, without losing their strength.

Paul warns the Corinthians about their Lord's Supper: “if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged” (1 Corinthians 11:31). Before we receive the Body of the Lord we examine our conscience. And he adds: “when you come together to eat, wait for one another” (11:33). The Eucharist makes us one body with the poor and the last.

So let us not despise small beginnings, let us be yeast in the world, and let us come to the Holy Qurbana prepared and attentive to one another. Amen.

For children

Children, a mustard seed is tiny, like a speck of dust, but it grows into a big plant where birds can sit! Jesus says small good things done with love grow big with God. What small good thing will you do today?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you compared the kingdom to a mustard seed and to a little yeast. Make your Church grow from small beginnings into a tree that shelters every nation, and make us yeast hidden in the dough of our society, changing it from within. Teach us to examine ourselves before we come to your table, and to wait for one another, especially for the poor and the last. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the first week after Pentecost both readings are about small beginnings that God makes great. Paul closes his correction of the Corinthian assembly with a simple rule for the Lord’s Supper: judge yourselves, and wait for one another. In Matthew, Jesus speaks of a tiny mustard seed that becomes a tree and of a little yeast hidden in a great mass of flour. The Church of the apostles was small and divided, yet it carried the life of the whole Kingdom.

1 Corinthians 11:31–34 · When you come together to eat, wait for one another

Setting.

In 11:17–34 Paul corrects the way the Corinthians celebrate the Lord’s Supper. The Eucharist was then joined to a common meal in a private house. Those who had more arrived early and ate their own food, while the poor, who may have come late after work, were left hungry (11:21–22). Paul hands on what he received from the Lord, the words over the bread and the cup (11:23–26), and warns that whoever eats and drinks “without discerning the body” eats and drinks judgment (11:29). Today’s verses are his conclusion.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–32: judge yourselves now; the Lord’s judgment is a father’s discipline, not condemnation.
  • vv. 33–34a: two practical rules: wait for one another, and satisfy hunger at home.
  • v. 34b: other matters will wait until Paul comes.

Key words.

  • diakrinō, judge, discern: The same verb stands behind “discerning the body” (v. 29) and “if we judged ourselves” (v. 31). To discern the body is to see Christ in the bread and also in the brothers and sisters at the table; to judge oneself is to look honestly at one’s own heart before coming.
  • paideuō, discipline: The word for training a child. The Lord’s correction is meant to save, “so that we may not be condemned along with the world”.
  • ekdechomai, wait for: To wait for, and so to receive one another. Some scholars think the word here also carries the sense of welcoming, sharing what one has with those who come later.

Verse by verse.

“But if we judged ourselves, we would not be judged.” (11:31)

Paul plays on the verbs for judging. An honest look at ourselves now spares us a harder judgment later.

“we are disciplined so that we may not be condemned along with the world.” (11:32)

Even the Lord’s correction is mercy. Paul has just said that some in the community are weak and ill (11:30); he does not explain this as anger but as a call to return.

“So then, my brothers and sisters, when you come together to eat, wait for one another.” (11:33)

After the high teaching on the Body and Blood of the Lord comes a very plain rule. The Eucharist is never only a private devotion; it gathers a people, and no one may be left out.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this chapter when it teaches that we must examine our conscience before receiving Holy Communion (CCC 1385), and it adds that the Eucharist commits us to the poor: to receive the Body and Blood of Christ in truth we must recognize Christ in the poorest, his brethren (CCC 1397). St John Chrysostom often reminded his people in substance that one cannot honour Christ at the altar and despise him in the poor at the church door.

Matthew 13:31–35 · The mustard seed and the yeast

Setting.

Matthew 13 is the third of the five great discourses of Jesus in this Gospel, the chapter of parables. Jesus has told the parable of the sower and of the weeds among the wheat. Now come two short parables, one taken from a man’s work in the field and one from a woman’s work in the house. The evangelist then explains why Jesus speaks in parables, quoting Psalm 78:2.

Structure.

  • vv. 31–32: the mustard seed, smallest of seeds, becomes a tree where birds nest.
  • v. 33: the yeast mixed into three measures of flour leavens it all.
  • vv. 34–35: Jesus speaks to the crowds in parables, fulfilling the word of the psalm. ¶ The two parables make a pair: one shows growth that can be seen from outside, the other a change that works hidden from within.

Key words.

  • kokkos sinapeōs, mustard seed: The black mustard of Galilee has a very small seed, proverbial for smallness (17:20), yet the plant can grow to the height of a man. Matthew says it “becomes a tree”, a deliberate stretching of the image.
  • zymē, yeast, leaven: In the Bible leaven is often a sign of corruption (16:6; 1 Corinthians 5:6–8). Here, surprisingly, it is the image of the Kingdom. A little of it, hidden in the dough, changes everything.
  • enkryptō, hide: The Greek says the woman “hid” the yeast in the flour; the NRSV renders it “mixed in”. Verse 35 then speaks of what “has been hidden from the foundation of the world”. The Kingdom works hidden before it is seen.

Verse by verse.

“it is the smallest of all the seeds, but when it has grown it is the greatest of shrubs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.” (13:32)

The birds nesting in the branches recall the great tree of Daniel 4:12 and the cedar of Ezekiel 17:23, images of a kingdom that gives shelter to the nations. The Kingdom of God begins small but has room for all peoples.

“yeast that a woman took and mixed in with three measures of flour until all of it was leavened.” (13:33)

Three measures is a very large amount, enough bread for well over a hundred people. It is the amount Sarah prepared for the three visitors at Mamre (Genesis 18:6). The woman’s small act at home feeds a feast.

“I will open my mouth to speak in parables; I will proclaim what has been hidden from the foundation of the world.” (13:35)

Textual note: a few ancient manuscripts read “through the prophet Isaiah”, although the words come from Psalm 78:2. St Jerome already knew this reading. The psalm is ascribed to Asaph, whom Scripture calls a seer (2 Chronicles 29:30), so Matthew can call him a prophet.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council asks the lay faithful to work for the sanctification of the world from within, like leaven (Lumen Gentium 31). The Catechism says that through his parables Jesus invites people to the feast of the Kingdom, but that a radical choice is also required: to gain the Kingdom one must give everything (CCC 546). St John Chrysostom, preaching on these parables, pointed to the apostles: few, poor and weak, yet the Gospel they carried filled the world (in substance, Homilies on Matthew). A Syro-Malabar reader may think of the faith brought by one apostle to our shore, which has grown into a tree with many branches.

Questions people ask

Is the mustard seed really the smallest of all seeds?

Jesus speaks as a teacher of his own land, where the mustard seed was the proverbial smallest seed people sowed. Scripture is not giving a lesson in botany; it uses common speech to teach a truth about the Kingdom. The Church teaches that Scripture teaches without error the truth God wished to put down for the sake of our salvation (Dei Verbum 11).

For study and teaching

  • Paul says, “wait for one another.” Who in my parish or family is left out or left behind at our gatherings, and what could I do about it?
  • Where do I see the Kingdom working small and hidden, like yeast, in my home or workplace?
