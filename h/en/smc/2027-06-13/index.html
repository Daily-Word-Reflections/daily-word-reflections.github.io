Word for the day

““Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” Luke 12:32

Reflection

On this Fifth Sunday of the Apostles the readings set before us two ways of facing the future: fear and trust. The first reading takes us to Kadesh, on the southern edge of the promised land. Moses reminds the people of the day when the spies came back from Canaan with fruit in their hands and fear in their hearts. The land was good, but its cities were walled and its people tall, and Israel refused to go up. Moses had pleaded with them: the Lord your God “goes before you on the way to seek out a place for you to camp, in fire by night, and in the cloud by day, to show you the route you should take.” They had seen the sea divided and the manna fall, yet they would not trust. So the Lord said that this generation would not see the good land, “except Caleb son of Jephunneh,” because of “his complete fidelity to the Lord.” Then came the second mistake, the mirror image of the first. Having refused to go when God said go, the people now insisted on going when God said stay: “We have sinned against the Lord! We are ready to go up and fight.” The Lord warned them: “Do not go up and do not fight, for I am not in the midst of you.” They went anyway, and the Amorites “chased you as bees do.” Fear refuses to move with God; presumption rushes ahead without him. Both are failures of the same thing, which is trust. And notice the tender detail in the middle of the judgment: the little ones, whom the frightened parents expected to become booty, are the very ones who will enter the land. God's promise was never withdrawn; it passed to the next generation.

Isaiah's oracle is a lament over Jerusalem, “the faithful city,” which has become unfaithful like a wife who has betrayed her husband. Strong language, but the charge is specific. The city's silver “has become dross,” its wine “is mixed with water.” Its leaders love bribes; “they do not defend the orphan, and the widow’s cause does not come before them.” For the prophets, unfaithfulness to God shows itself first in injustice to the weak. Yet the fire Isaiah announces is the fire of a refiner, not of a destroyer: “I will smelt away your dross as with lye and remove all your alloy. And I will restore your judges as at the first, and your counselors as at the beginning. Afterward you shall be called the city of righteousness, the faithful city.” God burns away what is false in order to recover what is precious. Only those who cling to their idols, the sacred oaks and gardens of the old fertility cults, are left like “a garden without water.” In the monsoon month, when every compound in Kerala turns green and every well fills, the image speaks clearly: a soul without God is a garden in which the rain has stopped.

Paul writes to a lively and gifted Church in Corinth, where some prized the gift of tongues above everything. He does not despise any gift of the Spirit, but he gives them an order: “Pursue love and strive for the spiritual gifts, and especially that you may prophesy.” Prophecy here means speaking God's word in a way others can understand, so that they are built up, encouraged and consoled. A gift that only edifies its owner is less than a gift that edifies the Church. Paul's examples are homely: a flute or a harp that does not give distinct notes, a bugle that “gives an indistinct sound,” so that no one knows it is time to prepare for battle. “So with yourselves; since you are eager for spiritual gifts, strive to excel in them for building up the church.” In this Season of the Apostles, when we thank God for the Spirit given at Pentecost, Paul reminds every prayer group, every choir, every catechist and every preacher of the test: does what I do build up the Church in love? A clear word spoken kindly in a family is worth more than an impressive word that leaves everyone confused.

In the Gospel Jesus turns to his disciples and speaks the words that have comforted anxious people ever since: “do not worry about your life, what you will eat, or about your body, what you will wear.” He points to the ravens, birds that the Law counted unclean and that no farmer ever fed: “they neither sow nor reap, they have neither storehouse nor barn, and yet God feeds them. Of how much more value are you than the birds!” He points to the lilies, which “neither toil nor spin; yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not clothed like one of these.” Worry adds nothing: “can any of you by worrying add a single hour to your span of life?” Jesus does not forbid work or planning; the birds hunt and the lilies grow. What he forbids is the anxious grasping that makes us forget the Father. “Instead, strive for his kingdom, and these things will be given to you as well.” Then comes the tenderest sentence of the passage: “Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” The apostles were a little flock indeed, a handful of fishermen and tax collectors. The Church in India has often been a little flock too, from the first communities that tradition traces to Mar Thoma. The kingdom is not something we must seize; it is the Father's pleasure to give it. Therefore the disciple can be generous: “Sell your possessions, and give alms. Make purses for yourselves that do not wear out.” The longer form of the Gospel adds the rich man who planned bigger barns and heard, “You fool! This very night your life is being demanded of you.” The Kadesh generation, the faithless city and the rich fool all made the same mistake: they trusted in what they could see. Jesus ends with a question for every heart: “For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.”

Homily outline

Theme: “Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.”

On the Fifth Sunday of the Apostles: a people who would not trust at Kadesh, a city refined by fire, gifts given to build up the Church, and the little flock that need not fear.

  • Presumption that goes without God.

“So we see that they were unable to enter because of unbelief.” Hebrews 3:19

“so that the genuineness of your faith… being more precious than gold that, though perishable, is tested by fire… may be found to result in praise and glory and honor when Jesus Christ is revealed.” 1 Peter 1:7

Apply: Do one concrete thing this week for a widow, an orphan or an elderly person living alone.

  • Every gift is for others.
  • A bugle with a clear sound.
  • Love as the measure of every gift.
“Then afterward I will pour out my spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy,” Joel 2:28
“So with yourselves; since you are eager for spiritual gifts, strive to excel in them for building up the church.” 1 Corinthians 14:12

Apply: Before speaking in a meeting or a group this week, ask whether your words will build up.

4. Do not be afraid, little flock

  • Ravens and lilies.
  • Strive first for the kingdom.
  • Treasure in heaven, heart in heaven.
“do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my victorious right hand.” Isaiah 41:10
““Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” Luke 12:32

Apply: Each night this week, hand one worry to the Father, and give one alms you had not planned.

Fear and presumption both fail the test of trust; the Father who went before Israel goes before his little flock today.

Refined by his fire and built up in love, we can strive for the kingdom without anxiety, because our treasure and our heart are with him.

Take-home resolution: This week, take one step of trust, help one person in need, speak only to build up, and give each worry to the Father in prayer.

Full homily

Dear friends, there are two ways to face tomorrow. One is the way of fear; the other is the way of trust. Today's readings show us both.

At Kadesh the people of Israel stood at the edge of the promised land. The land was good, but they were afraid, and they refused to go in, though the Lord had gone before them “in fire by night, and in the cloud by day” (Deuteronomy 1:33). Then, when God told them to wait, they rushed ahead without him and were defeated. Fear says, I will not go even with God. Presumption says, I will go even without God. Only Caleb followed the Lord with his whole heart.

Isaiah weeps over Jerusalem, a faithful city that has forgotten the orphan and the widow. But God's fire is the fire of a refiner: he burns away the dross to find the silver. When God allows trials in our lives, he is not destroying us; he is purifying us, so that we may again be called faithful.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians, proud of their spiritual gifts: “Pursue love” (1 Corinthians 14:1). Every gift of the Spirit is for building up the Church. A trumpet that gives an unclear sound helps no one. Let our words and our gifts be clear and kind, for the good of others.

And Jesus says: do not worry. Look at the ravens; look at the lilies. Your Father knows what you need. “Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom” (Luke 12:32). The kingdom is a gift. We do not have to grab it; we have only to receive it and share it.

This June, when schools reopen and fees fall due and the rains are heavy, many of us carry worries. Let us bring them to this altar. Let us strive first for the kingdom, give generously to the poor, and keep our treasure in heaven, for where our treasure is, there our heart will be also. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus says: look at the birds. Nobody gives them a lunch box, but God feeds them. Look at the flowers. Nobody sews their clothes, but they are beautiful.

You are worth much more than birds and flowers! So when you feel worried about school or anything else, tell God your Father. He loves you and he will take care of you.

Prayer

Lord God, you went before your people in fire by night and cloud by day, and you never withdrew your promise. Forgive us when fear has kept us from following you and when pride has made us rush ahead without you. Refine us as silver is refined, and make our homes and our parish faithful and just to the orphan and the widow. Holy Spirit, give us gifts that build up your Church, and above all give us love. Lord Jesus, teach your little flock not to be afraid, to seek the kingdom first, and to keep our treasure, and so our hearts, with you. Glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s four readings are about trust. In Deuteronomy, Moses recalls the day at Kadesh when Israel would not trust the God who had carried them through the wilderness, and then, when it was too late, rushed into battle on their own strength. In Isaiah, Jerusalem, once the faithful city, has turned to bribes and forgotten the orphan and the widow; God promises to purify her like silver in the furnace until she is called faithful again. Paul tells the Corinthians to pursue love and to seek the gifts that build up the whole Church, not only the speaker. And in Luke, Jesus tells his little flock not to worry about food and clothing, but to seek the Kingdom and to keep their treasure where no thief comes near. Fear made Israel turn back, and greed made Jerusalem unfaithful; trust in the Father frees us to love and to give. In the Season of the Apostles the Church hears again the Lord’s word to his small community: “Do not be afraid, little flock.”

Deuteronomy 1:33–46 · You would not trust the Lord

Setting.

Moses is giving his first great speech in the plains of Moab, retelling the forty years of the journey. Here he comes to the saddest moment: Kadesh-barnea, on the southern edge of the promised land, about two years after the Exodus. The full story is told in Numbers 13–14. Spies went up into the land; they brought back its fruit, but most of them also brought fear: the people were strong and the cities walled. The people refused to go up.

Just before today’s verses Moses said: “in spite of this, you have no trust in the Lord your God” (1:32), the God who had carried them “just as one carries a child” (1:31). Today’s reading tells what followed: the oath that this generation would not enter the land, the exceptions of Caleb and Joshua, and the people’s second mistake, a battle God had not commanded. In Numbers the reason Moses may not enter is his own failure at Meribah (Numbers 20:12); here he speaks of the Lord being angry with him “on your account” (1:37). The two books look at the same sad story from different sides: the leader shares the fate of his people.

Structure.

  • v. 33: the God who went before them in fire and cloud, whom they would not trust.
  • vv. 34–36: the oath: this evil generation shall not see the good land, except Caleb.
  • vv. 37–38: Moses too shall not enter; Joshua shall lead Israel in.
  • v. 39: the little ones they feared for will enter and possess it.
  • vv. 40–43: the people repent too late, go up against God’s word, and are warned in vain.
  • vv. 44–46: defeat by the Amorites; weeping that is not heard; the long stay at Kadesh.

Key words.

  • he’ĕmîn, to trust, to believe: The verb behind “you have no trust” in verse 32 comes from the root of “amen”: to lean on something firm. Israel’s sin at Kadesh was not first a broken rule but a refusal to lean on God.
  • millē’ ’aḥărê YHWH, complete fidelity to the Lord: Literally “he filled after the Lord”: Caleb followed God fully, all the way. The same Hebrew phrase is used of Caleb and Joshua in Numbers 32:12.
  • zîd, to act presumptuously: Translated “presumptuously went up” in verse 43. The word suggests something boiling over: pride that will not wait for God. Unbelief and presumption look like opposites, but both put our own judgment in the place of God’s word.

Verse by verse.

“who goes before you on the way to seek out a place for you to camp, in fire by night, and in the cloud by day” (1:33)

God had been their guide and scout every day. The fire and the cloud (Exodus 13:21) were there before their eyes, and still they would not trust.

“except Caleb son of Jephunneh. He shall see it” (1:36)

One man’s faithfulness stands out against a whole generation’s fear. Caleb had said, “Let us go up at once” (Numbers 13:30).

“your children, who today do not yet know right from wrong, they shall enter there” (1:39)

The people had said their children would be taken as booty (Numbers 14:3). God turns their fear around: the very children they used as an excuse will receive the gift.

“We have sinned against the Lord! We are ready to go up and fight” (1:41)

The confession is right, but what follows is not. They wanted to undo their sin by their own strength, against God’s new command. True repentance obeys God now; it does not try to rewrite yesterday.

“Do not go up and do not fight, for I am not in the midst of you” (1:42)

Victory was never in their weapons but in God’s presence among them. Without him the same mountain that was given becomes a defeat.

“chased you as bees do” (1:44)

The Hebrew word for bees is dĕbôrîm, from which the name Deborah comes. The picture is of a swarm that will not let its victims go.

How the Church reads it.

The Letter to the Hebrews reads this story as a warning for Christians: the wilderness generation could not enter because of unbelief, so “let us therefore make every effort to enter that rest” (Hebrews 3:16–4:11). The Catechism teaches that faith is first a personal adherence of the whole person to God who reveals himself (CCC 150), and it names presumption as a sin against hope: hoping to be saved by one’s own powers, or expecting God’s gifts without conversion (CCC 2092). Kadesh shows both dangers in one day: first fear that will not trust, then pride that will not obey.

Isaiah 1:21–31 · The faithful city

Setting.

This passage continues the opening chapter of Isaiah, in which God brings a case against his people. After the call to wash and make themselves clean (1:16–20), the prophet turns to Jerusalem itself. Isaiah preached there in the eighth century before Christ; he knew its court, its judges and its Temple well.

The passage opens with a lament, the word “How” that begins a funeral song (as in Lamentations 1:1). The city that was once full of justice now has rulers who take bribes and neglect the orphan and the widow. Yet the lament turns into a promise: God will purify the city as a smith purifies silver, and it will again be called “the faithful city”. The closing verses (29–31) speak of oaks and gardens, probably places where idols were worshipped.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–23: the lament: the faithful city has become unfaithful; silver turned to dross; rulers who love bribes.
  • vv. 24–26: God’s judgment as refining: he will smelt away the dross and restore judges as at the first.
  • vv. 27–28: Zion redeemed by justice; rebels and those who forsake the Lord will perish.
  • vv. 29–31: shame for the sacred oaks and gardens; the strong become like tinder and burn.

Key words.

  • qiryâ ne’ĕmānâ, faithful city: The phrase opens the passage (v. 21) and closes the promise (v. 26). Between the two, God works to give back to Jerusalem the name she lost. The word comes from the same root as “amen”: firm, reliable.
  • mišpāṭ and ṣedeq, justice and righteousness: A pair the prophets often join. Justice is right judgment, especially for the weak; righteousness is the right order of life that God wants. Isaiah measures the city’s faith by how it treats the orphan and the widow.
  • sîg, dross: The waste that separates from silver when it is melted. God’s judgment here is not to destroy the silver but to remove what spoils it.

Verse by verse.

“How the faithful city has become a whore!” (1:21)

The prophets often describe idolatry and injustice as unfaithfulness in marriage, because God’s covenant with his people is like a marriage (Hosea 2; Jeremiah 3). The harsh word expresses the pain of a betrayed love.

“Everyone loves a bribe and runs after gifts. They do not defend the orphan, and the widow’s cause does not come before them.” (1:23)

For Isaiah, worship and justice cannot be separated. A city can be full of sacrifices (1:11) and still be unfaithful if the weakest have no one to defend them.

“I will smelt away your dross as with lye and remove all your alloy.” (1:25)

God’s hand is “turned against” the city, but in order to cleanse it. His anger serves his love.

“Afterward you shall be called the city of righteousness, the faithful city.” (1:26)

Judgment is not God’s last word. He restores what was lost, “as at the first”.

“Zion shall be redeemed by justice, and those in her who repent, by righteousness.” (1:27)

Redemption and repentance go together. The Hebrew word for “those who repent” literally means “those who turn back”.

How the Church reads it.

The Church hears in this passage both a warning and a hope. The warning is that faith is shown in justice for the poor. The Catechism quotes St John Chrysostom: not to enable the poor to share in our goods is to steal from them and deprive them of life (CCC 2446). The hope is that God purifies rather than abandons. Holy Scripture often speaks of God testing his people like silver and gold in the fire (Malachi 3:3; 1 Peter 1:7); the Church sees here the patient work of grace, which cleanses a heart and a community until they are faithful again.

1 Corinthians 14:1–12 · Pursue love, build up the Church

Setting.

Paul has just written his great hymn to love (chapter 13). Now he applies it to a problem at Corinth. Some members prized the gift of speaking in tongues, a prayer in the Spirit that others could not understand, and seem to have used it in the assemblies as a sign of being more spiritual. Paul does not forbid the gift (14:5, 39), but he sets a test for every gift: does it build up the Church?

By “prophecy” Paul does not mean chiefly foretelling the future. He means speaking God’s word in a way others can understand, so that they are strengthened, encouraged and consoled (14:3).

Structure.

  • v. 1: pursue love; strive for the spiritual gifts, especially prophecy.
  • vv. 2–5: tongues speak to God and build up the speaker; prophecy speaks to people and builds up the Church.
  • v. 6: what use is Paul to them unless he brings revelation, knowledge, prophecy or teaching?
  • vv. 7–9: three pictures: flute, harp and bugle must give clear notes.
  • vv. 10–12: a sound without meaning makes us foreigners; seek gifts that build up the Church.

Key words.

  • diōkete tēn agapēn, pursue love: The verb means to run after, to chase. Love is not only a feeling to wait for but a goal to pursue with effort. Every other gift is measured by it.
  • glōssa, tongue: In this chapter, prayer or speech in the Spirit that the hearers do not understand without an interpreter. At Pentecost the apostles were understood by people of many languages (Acts 2:6–11); at Corinth an interpreter was needed (14:13, 27).
  • oikodomē, building up: Literally “house-building”. The word comes again and again in this chapter (vv. 3, 4, 5, 12, 26). The Church is God’s house, and every gift is a tool for building it.

Verse by verse.

“Pursue love and strive for the spiritual gifts, and especially that you may prophesy.” (14:1)

The verse joins chapter 13 to chapter 14. Gifts are good, and Paul tells them to strive for them; but love is the path on which the gifts must walk.

“those who prophesy speak to other people for their upbuilding and encouragement and consolation.” (14:3)

A good description of every true word in the Church: it builds, it encourages, it consoles. Preachers, catechists and parents can test their words by these three.

“If they do not give distinct notes, how will anyone know what is being played?” (14:7)

Paul uses music to make his point. A beautiful instrument that plays no clear tune helps no one.

“since you are eager for spiritual gifts, strive to excel in them for building up the church.” (14:12)

Paul does not cool their eagerness; he turns it in the right direction, from the self to the community.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Holy Spirit gives special gifts to the faithful of every rank, which are to be received with thanksgiving, and that judgment about their genuineness and right use belongs to those who preside in the Church (Lumen Gentium 12). The Catechism adds that charity is the true measure of all charisms (CCC 800), and that charisms are for the good of the Church (CCC 799–801). St Augustine explained that at Pentecost the gift of tongues was a sign that the Church would speak in the languages of all nations (in substance, Homilies on 1 John 6).

Luke 12:22–34 · Do not worry, little flock

Setting.

Jesus is on his journey to Jerusalem, teaching his disciples along the way. Just before this passage, a man asked him to settle a quarrel about an inheritance, and Jesus told the parable of the rich fool who built bigger barns and died that same night (12:13–21). Now he turns to his disciples: the opposite of greed is not careless poverty but trust in the Father.

Matthew gives much of this teaching in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 6:25–34). Luke has some features of his own: he speaks of ravens rather than “birds of the air”, and he adds the tender words to the “little flock” and the command to sell possessions and give alms.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–23: do not worry about food and clothing; life is more than these.
  • vv. 24–26: consider the ravens; worry cannot add an hour to life.
  • vv. 27–28: consider the lilies; God clothes the grass, how much more you.
  • vv. 29–31: do not strive like the nations; your Father knows; strive for his Kingdom.
  • v. 32: do not be afraid, little flock; the Father is pleased to give you the Kingdom.
  • vv. 33–34: sell, give alms, keep treasure in heaven; where your treasure is, your heart is.

Key words.

  • merimnaō, to worry: Anxious care that divides the heart and pulls it in many directions. Jesus does not forbid work or planning; he forbids the worry that forgets the Father. Martha is “worried and distracted by many things” in the same Gospel (10:41).
  • mikron poimnion, little flock: A tender name. The disciples were few and weak, as Israel was called God’s flock (Psalm 23; Ezekiel 34). Their smallness is no reason for fear, because the Kingdom is the Father’s gift.
  • thēsauros, treasure: Both the treasure and the place where it is kept. Jesus moves the disciples’ storehouse from earth to heaven, and the heart follows it.

Verse by verse.

“Consider the ravens: they neither sow nor reap, they have neither storehouse nor barn, and yet God feeds them.” (12:24)

Ravens were counted among the unclean birds (Leviticus 11:15), yet God feeds even them (Psalm 147:9). The rich fool built barns; the ravens have none, and they live by God’s care.

“And can any of you by worrying add a single hour to your span of life?” (12:25)

The Greek can mean a cubit added to one’s height or a short measure added to one’s age; the NRSV takes it of time. The point is the same: worry adds nothing.

“even Solomon in all his glory was not clothed like one of these.” (12:27)

Solomon was remembered as the richest and most splendid of Israel’s kings (1 Kings 10). A wild flower, given its beauty by God, outshines him.

“Instead, strive for his kingdom, and these things will be given to you as well.” (12:31)

Jesus does not say that food and clothing do not matter. He puts them in their place: they come “as well”, when God comes first.

““Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” (12:32)

The Kingdom is not earned by anxious effort; it is given by a Father who delights to give. That is why the disciples can afford to give, too.

“For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” (12:34)

A simple test of the heart: where do my thoughts go when my mind wanders? What would I find hardest to lose?

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus asks for childlike abandonment to the providence of our heavenly Father, who takes care of his children’s smallest needs, and it quotes this teaching of the Lord (CCC 305). It also teaches that Jesus asks his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone (CCC 2544). Trust is not laziness: the Church teaches that God gives his creatures the dignity of acting and cooperating in his plan (CCC 306–307). Pope Francis notes that Jesus invited people to be attentive to the beauty of the world, and pointed to the lilies and the birds as signs of the Father’s care (Laudato Si’ 96–97).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus forbid us to work, save or plan for the future?

No. Paul himself worked with his hands and told the Thessalonians that anyone unwilling to work should not eat (2 Thessalonians 3:10). What Jesus forbids is worry that takes the place of trust, and greed that makes possessions our treasure. The disciple works and plans as a child of the Father, and shares with the poor.

In Deuteronomy, the people repent and weep, and the Lord does not listen. Does God refuse forgiveness?

No. In Numbers the Lord says, “I do forgive, just as you have asked” (Numbers 14:20). What God refused was the people’s plan to go up and fight against his new command. Forgiveness takes away guilt, but some consequences of sin may remain; the people had to learn trust again in the wilderness. God’s “no” protected them from a battle they could not win without him.

Does the gift of tongues still exist in the Church?

The Church teaches that the Holy Spirit continues to give charisms, ordinary and extraordinary, for the good of the Church, and that they are to be received with gratitude and tested by the Church’s pastors (Lumen Gentium 12; CCC 799–801). Paul’s rule still holds: every gift is measured by love and by whether it builds up the Church.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Season of the Apostles our Church remembers that the Gospel came to India through Mar Thoma Shleeha, and that for many centuries the St Thomas Christians lived as a small community among many neighbours. “Do not be afraid, little flock” has been their story: they trusted the Father, kept the faith and handed it on in their families. Today the Syro-Malabar Church has eparchies in India and across the world, but it remains a little flock that lives by the same trust.

Paul’s concern that prayer in the assembly be understood speaks to us too. Our Holy Qurbana was for centuries prayed in Syriac, and today it is prayed in Malayalam and in many other languages, so that all may understand and answer “Amen”. Isaiah’s words about the orphan and the widow remind every parish and family that the Qurbana we celebrate must be lived in justice and care for the weak.

For study and teaching

  • At Kadesh Israel first would not trust God, and then would not wait for him. Which is my more common temptation: fear or presumption?
  • Isaiah measures a city’s faith by how it treats the orphan and the widow. Who are the orphans and widows in my parish today, and who defends their cause?
  • Paul says every gift must build up the Church. How do I use my gifts: to be seen, or to build up others?
  • “Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” What would I find hardest to give away, and what does that tell me about my heart?
