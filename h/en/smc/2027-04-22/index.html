Word for the day

“This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me;” Matthew 15:8

Reflection

The Second Letter of Peter was written for Christians who had received the faith from the apostles and were now hearing other voices. Peter does not pretend the Church will be spared trouble from within: “But false prophets also arose among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you, who will secretly bring in destructive opinions. They will even deny the Master who bought them—bringing swift destruction on themselves.” Two things mark these false teachers. They work secretly, slipping their opinions in beside the true faith so that the change is hardly noticed. And they deny the Master who bought them. The word is the language of the slave market: Christ has paid the price for us, with his own Blood, and we belong to him. A teaching that empties the Cross, or reduces the Resurrection to a pleasant idea, or makes Jesus one teacher among many, denies the very Lord who bought us. Peter adds a sad note: “many will follow their licentious ways, and because of these teachers the way of truth will be maligned.” False doctrine and loose living travel together, and when Christians live badly, the world speaks ill of the faith itself. Finally, “in their greed they will exploit you with deceptive words.” Then as now, some made religion a business.

How do we recognise a false teacher? The Church has never answered by asking each Christian to judge alone. The same letter reminds its readers to remember “the commandment of the Lord and Savior spoken through your apostles” (2 Peter 3:2). The test is the faith of the apostles, handed down in the Scriptures, kept in the living Tradition and taught by the bishops in communion with the successor of Peter. Our own Church carries this memory deeply. For centuries the Thomas Christians of Kerala lived the faith received from the apostle with very few books; when strangers came with new teachings, the people asked one question: is this what our fathers received? The Easter season is a good time to hold fast to the apostolic faith, because it rests on the apostles' witness to the Risen Lord. They did not hand on an opinion. They handed on what they had seen and heard and touched. A sound test for any new voice, on a screen or in a meeting, is this: does it lead me to Christ crucified and risen, to the sacraments and to love of the Church, or away from them?

In the Gospel some Pharisees and scribes come from Jerusalem and ask why the disciples do not wash their hands before eating, as the tradition of the elders required. The washing was a religious rite, not a matter of hygiene, and many devout people kept it with love. Jesus does not attack washing; he exposes a misuse. Some had found a way to declare their savings dedicated to God, Korban, so that they need not support their aged parents: “So, for the sake of your tradition, you make void the word of God.” And he quotes Isaiah: “This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching human precepts as doctrines.” This is a word for religious people of every age, and first for us. Jesus is not speaking against the Jewish people, nor against tradition as such; he himself kept the feasts and prayed in the synagogue, and the Church lives by the apostolic Tradition. He is speaking against any custom, ours included, that becomes a way to avoid love. A Syro-Malabar Christian may keep every fast, every feast and every family custom, and still neglect an old father or speak bitterly of a neighbour. Then our worship is lip-service. Easter asks for a new heart: the Risen Lord does not want only our lips at the Qurbana, but hearts that go home and honour father and mother.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Peter warns us about false teachers, and Jesus warns us about worship that stays on the lips.

Peter says false teachers will come, bringing in destructive opinions secretly, and “They will even deny the Master who bought them.” Christ bought us with his own Blood. A teaching that empties the Cross or the Resurrection denies the very Lord who bought us.

How do we know true teaching? The Church answers: by the faith of the apostles, handed down in Scripture and Tradition and taught by the bishops in communion with Peter's successor. Our forefathers in Kerala kept the faith of St Thomas for centuries by asking: is this what our fathers received?

In the Gospel Jesus sees some who used a religious vow to avoid supporting their parents. He says: “for the sake of your tradition, you make void the word of God.” And he quotes Isaiah: “This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me.”

This word is for us first. We can keep every fast and feast and still neglect our parents or speak bitterly of a neighbour. Jesus wants our hearts, not only our lips.

So today, let us test the voices we listen to, look at the crucifix and remember the price, and do one real act of love for our parents. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said some people said nice prayers with their mouths, but their hearts were far from God. God wants our hearts too!

Today, show love at home with your hands and your heart: help Amma or Appa without being asked!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you bought us with your precious Blood, and you want our hearts, not only our lips. Keep us faithful to the faith of the apostles, handed down in your Church, and guard us from voices that would lead us away from you. Deliver us from customs that excuse us from love. Give us new hearts that honour our parents, care for the elderly and worship you in spirit and truth. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what makes teaching true. Second Peter warns that false teachers will arise inside the community, as false prophets arose in Israel, and that they will deny the Master who bought them. In Matthew, Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem question Jesus about washing hands, and he answers with Isaiah's word about honour given with the lips while the heart is far away. True teaching hands on what God has given and leads to a true heart. The key words are heresy, tradition and heart.

2 Peter 2:1–3 · Beware of false teachers

Setting.

Second Peter is written as Peter's farewell testament (1:13–15). Many scholars think it comes from a later generation that wrote in his name to keep his teaching alive, while others defend Peter's own authorship; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture. Chapter 2 is closely related to the short Letter of Jude.

The letter has just spoken of the prophetic word, which comes not by human will but from the Holy Spirit (1:20–21). Now it turns to the shadow side: in Israel there were false prophets, and in the Church there will be false teachers.

Structure.

  • v. 1: false teachers will arise; they bring in destructive opinions and deny the Master.
  • v. 2: many will follow them, and the way of truth will be maligned.
  • v. 3: their greed and deceptive words; their judgment is not asleep.

Key words.

  • haireseis apōleias, destructive opinions: Hairesis first meant a choice or a party (Acts 5:17 uses it of the Sadducees). Here it already means a chosen opinion that cuts a person off from the common faith, the root of our word heresy.
  • despotēs, Master: The owner of a household and its slaves. The false teachers deny the very Lord who “bought” them at the price of his blood (compare 1 Corinthians 6:20).
  • plastoi logoi, deceptive words: Literally “moulded words”, shaped like clay to fit what the hearers want. Our word plastic comes from the same root.

Verse by verse.

“just as there will be false teachers among you, who will secretly bring in destructive opinions.” (2:1)

The danger is inside, not outside, and it comes “secretly”: a gradual shift rather than open denial.

“because of these teachers the way of truth will be maligned.” (2:2)

Acts calls the Christian faith “the Way” (Acts 9:2). Bad lives among believers bring shame on the faith in the eyes of others.

“And in their greed they will exploit you with deceptive words.” (2:3)

The verb means to trade in people, to make merchandise of them. Faith turned into business is a temptation in every age.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism defines heresy as the obstinate denial, after baptism, of a truth which must be believed with divine and catholic faith (CCC 2089). It is distinct from honest questions and doubts. The Church's teaching office does not stand above the word of God but serves it, teaching only what has been handed on (CCC 86). Against “moulded words” the faithful have the Creed, which the Church professes in every Holy Qurbana.

Matthew 15:1–9 · Honour with the lips, and the heart far away

Setting.

A delegation of Pharisees and scribes comes from Jerusalem to Galilee. The Pharisees were a lay movement devoted to living the Law in daily life; many scholars think they wished to bring the holiness of the Temple to every table, which is why they washed hands before meals. Their “tradition of the elders” was the oral teaching that applied the Law to daily life.

Jesus does not attack the Law. He points to a case where a human custom was used to escape a commandment of God. Matthew's account is close to Mark 7, without Mark's explanation of the washing customs for non-Jewish readers.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the charge: your disciples break the tradition of the elders.
  • vv. 3–6: Jesus' counter-question: you break the commandment of God; the example of a gift dedicated to God.
  • vv. 7–9: Isaiah's prophecy: lips and heart.

Key words.

  • paradosis, tradition: Something handed on. The word is neutral: Paul praises the Corinthians for keeping the traditions he handed on (1 Corinthians 11:2). The question is whether a tradition serves God's word or empties it.
  • dōron, gift: Mark gives the Aramaic word korban, “an offering” (Mark 7:11). A son could declare his goods dedicated to God and then refuse them to his parents. Jewish teachers themselves discussed such vows and how they could be released.
  • hypokritēs, hypocrite: In Greek, an actor on the stage. Jesus uses it for those whose outward religion does not match the heart.

Verse by verse.

“And why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?” (15:3)
“For God said, ‘Honor your father and your mother,’” (15:4)

The fourth commandment (Exodus 20:12). Jesus defends the care of aged parents against a pious excuse.

“This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me;” (15:8)

Isaiah 29:13, quoted in the Greek form. It is a prophet's word to his own people, and like all prophecy it is addressed to the people of God in every age, the Church included.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism distinguishes the Tradition that comes from the apostles, handing on what they received from Christ, from the many theological, disciplinary, liturgical or devotional traditions that arise in local Churches; these can be kept, changed or dropped under the guidance of the Church's teaching office (CCC 83). Jesus' rebuke is therefore no reason to despise Tradition. It is a warning that any custom, ours included, can be used to avoid love. The fourth commandment asks grown children to give their parents material and moral support in old age (CCC 2218).

Questions people ask

Is Jesus saying here that the Pharisees were hypocrites in general?

No. He answers a particular dispute with a particular example, as the prophets did. Many Pharisees were devout, and some were friends of Jesus and of the early Church (Luke 13:31; Acts 5:34; 15:5). The warning about lips and heart is for every believer, and the Church reads it first as a word to herself.

For study and teaching

  • Is there a custom or excuse in my life that keeps me from caring for my parents or elders?
  • When I pray or sing in the Holy Qurbana, where is my heart?
