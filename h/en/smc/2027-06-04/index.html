Word for the day

“Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” John 19:34

Reflection

Eight days after the Feast of the Body and Blood of Christ, on the First Friday of June, the Church turns to the source of that gift: the Heart of Jesus. In very many Syro-Malabar homes a picture of the Sacred Heart hangs above the family altar, with a small lamp burning before it, and the family has been consecrated to the Sacred Heart, often on the day the house was blessed. On First Fridays parishes hold adoration, confessions are heard, and many of the faithful come to the Holy Qurbana. June is the month of the Sacred Heart. The devotion is not a sentimental extra. In the Bible the heart is the centre of the person, the place where one thinks, decides, loves and meets God. To speak of the Heart of Jesus is to speak of the whole person of the Word made flesh, who loved the Father and loved us with a human heart. The Church found this devotion in the Gospel itself, in the pierced side of the Crucified; it was deepened by medieval mystics, spread through the revelations given to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial between 1673 and 1675, extended to the whole Church by Pope Pius IX in 1856, explained by Pius XII in the encyclical Haurietis Aquas (1956), and recommended anew by Pope Francis in Dilexit Nos (2024).

The first reading may seem at first to have little to do with the feast. Abram and his nephew Lot have both grown rich, and the land cannot support their flocks; their herders quarrel. Abram is the elder and the head of the clan; by right, the first choice is his. Instead he says: “Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herders and my herders; for we are kindred. Is not the whole land before you? Separate yourself from me. If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right; or if you take the right hand, then I will go to the left.” Lot looks, and chooses with his eyes: “the plain of the Jordan was well watered everywhere like the garden of the Lord.” The text adds quietly that he moved his tent as far as Sodom, and “the people of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the Lord.” Abram, who has given up the best land for the sake of peace, is not the loser. God speaks to him: “Raise your eyes now, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward; for all the land that you see I will give to you and to your offspring forever.” Abram builds an altar at Mamre. Here is a heart that prefers peace to rights, kinship to profit, and God's promise to what the eye can grasp. The Church reads it on this feast because it is a first sketch of the Heart of Christ, who did not hold on to what was his, and who says in the fuller Gospel of the day: “learn from me; for I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29). In Kerala, where families are so often divided by quarrels over land and inheritance, Abram's words to Lot are a word for brothers and sisters today.

The second reading is the song of the suffering Servant, which the Church reads on Good Friday: “He was despised and rejected by others; a man of suffering and acquainted with infirmity… Surely he has borne our infirmities and carried our diseases… But he was wounded for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the punishment that made us whole, and by his bruises we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have all turned to our own way, and the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all.” The Heart of Jesus is not a heart that loves from a safe distance. It is a wounded heart, a heart that carries. In the Season of the Apostles we remember how the deacon Philip met an Ethiopian official on the road to Gaza who was reading this very passage, “like a sheep he was led to the slaughter”, and asked of whom the prophet spoke. “Then Philip began to speak, and starting with this scripture, he proclaimed to him the good news about Jesus” (Acts 8:35). The apostles preached the Servant song as the key to the Cross: the One who was pierced bore our sins out of love.

Paul's Epistle opens with Paul's own heart: “Brothers and sisters, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved.” He is speaking of his own people, Israel, whom he loves and for whom, in the chapter before, he says he has great sorrow and unceasing anguish. This is not the language of contempt but of love, and Christians must always read it so, honouring the Jewish people to whom belong the covenants and the promises. Then Paul places faith exactly where today's feast places it, in the heart: “The word is near you, on your lips and in your heart (that is, the word of faith that we proclaim); because if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For one believes with the heart and so is justified, and one confesses with the mouth and so is saved.” Devotion to the Sacred Heart is not a feeling to be enjoyed in private. It is faith in the risen Lord believed with the heart and confessed with the lips: at the family prayer, before meals in a restaurant, in the office, in the hospital ward.

The Gospel takes us to Calvary at the moment after death. “When Jesus had received the wine, he said, ‘It is finished.’ Then he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.” Because the next day was the great Sabbath of Passover, the bodies were not to remain on the crosses, and the soldiers came to break the legs of the crucified. “But when they came to Jesus and saw that he was already dead, they did not break his legs. Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” The evangelist, who stood there with the Mother of Jesus, solemnly testifies to what he saw, “so that you also may believe.” He sees two Scriptures fulfilled: “None of his bones shall be broken”, as with the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46), and “They will look on the one whom they have pierced” (Zechariah 12:10). The Fathers saw in the blood and water the sacraments of the Church, Baptism and the Eucharist, and they saw the Church herself born from the side of the new Adam asleep on the Cross, as Eve was formed from the side of the first Adam. And our own father in the faith comes into this mystery. Eight days after Easter the risen Lord said to Thomas, “Reach out your hand and put it in my side. Do not doubt but believe” (John 20:27). The apostle of India, invited to touch the wounded side of Christ, confessed, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). The Mar Thoma Christians received their faith from the one to whom the Lord showed his opened side. Today we look on the One whom we have pierced, not to stay in grief, but to drink from the spring of mercy, and to say with Thomas and with every First Friday pilgrim: Jesus, meek and humble of heart, make our hearts like yours.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this First Friday the Church celebrates the Feast of the Sacred Heart of Jesus. In many of our homes the picture of the Sacred Heart hangs above the family altar, with a lamp before it. In the Bible the heart is the centre of the person. The Heart of Jesus means the whole love of Jesus for the Father and for us.

In the first reading Abram and Lot quarrel over land. Abram has the right to choose first, but he says: let there be no strife between us; we are kindred; you choose. Lot takes the best-looking land and goes toward Sodom. Abram gives up for peace, and God gives him the whole land. Here is a heart like the Heart of Jesus, who says: learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart (Matthew 11:29).

Isaiah shows us the suffering Servant: he was wounded for our transgressions; by his bruises we are healed (Isaiah 53:5). The Heart of Jesus is a wounded heart. He does not love us from far away. He carries our sins and sorrows.

Saint Paul says: if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved (Romans 10:9). Devotion to the Sacred Heart is faith with the heart and courage with the lips. Let us not be ashamed to show that we belong to Jesus.

In the Gospel a soldier pierces the side of Jesus, and blood and water come out (John 19:34). The Fathers saw in them Baptism and the Eucharist, and the Church born from the side of Christ. Eight days after Easter Jesus said to our father Thomas: put your hand in my side. And Thomas said: My Lord and my God!

Today let us look on the One whom we have pierced. Let us go to confession, receive him in the Qurbana, and renew the consecration of our families. And let us pray: Jesus, meek and humble of heart, make our hearts like yours. Amen.

For children

Children, when Jesus died, a soldier opened his side with a spear, and blood and water came out. It shows how much Jesus loves us: he gave us everything!

Look at the picture of the Sacred Heart at home. Say to Jesus: “Jesus, I love you. Make my heart gentle and kind like yours.”

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, gentle and humble of heart, your side was opened by the spear, and from your Heart flowed blood and water, the fountain of our salvation. Like Abram, teach us to choose peace over our rights; like the Servant, you bore our infirmities: heal us by your wounds. Give us hearts that believe and lips that confess that you are Lord. As you showed your side to Mar Thoma, show us your mercy, that we too may say, My Lord and my God. Make our families your dwelling, bless our priests, and make our hearts like your Heart. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory and honour for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of the Sacred Heart the four readings lead us step by step into the heart of God. In Genesis, Abram shows a large and peaceful heart: rather than quarrel with his nephew Lot, he lets him choose first, and God renews the promise to the one who gave way. In Isaiah, the Servant of the Lord is wounded for our transgressions, and by his bruises we are healed. Paul, his heart aching for his own people, teaches that one believes with the heart and is justified. And in John, a soldier’s spear opens the side of the crucified Jesus, and at once blood and water flow out.

The Heart of Jesus is not a sentimental picture but the whole of his love, human and divine, given to the end. The Fathers saw the Church and her sacraments born from that opened side. Today we are invited to look on the one whom they pierced, and to let our own hearts be made like his.

Genesis 13:8–18 · Let there be no strife between you and me

Setting.

Abram has come back from Egypt to the hill country of Bethel, where he had built an altar at the start of his journey (13:3–4; 12:8). Both he and his nephew Lot have grown rich in flocks, herds and tents, and the land cannot support them both; their herdsmen quarrel (13:5–7). Abram is the elder, and the land has been promised to him (12:7). By custom he could have chosen first.

Instead he offers Lot the choice. Lot looks down on the plain of the Jordan, green and well watered, and takes it, moving towards Sodom. Only after Lot has gone does God speak to Abram, and the promise that seemed to be given away comes back to him enlarged.

Structure.

  • vv. 8–9: Abram’s proposal: no strife, for we are kindred; the whole land is before you, choose.
  • vv. 10–11: Lot looks, sees the well-watered plain, and chooses it for himself.
  • vv. 12–13: Abram stays in Canaan; Lot settles near Sodom, whose people were wicked.
  • vv. 14–17: God renews the promise: look in every direction; the land and countless descendants.
  • v. 18: Abram moves his tent to the oaks of Mamre at Hebron and builds an altar.

Key words.

  • mĕrîbâ, strife: A quarrel, a dispute. The same word names the place where Israel later quarrelled with God in the desert (Exodus 17:7). Abram refuses to let possessions divide a family.
  • ’aḥîm, kindred: Literally “brothers”. Lot is Abram’s nephew, but Hebrew uses “brother” for close relatives. The word makes Abram’s argument: family is worth more than pasture.
  • ’ēlōnê mamrē’, the oaks of Mamre: Great trees near Hebron, a place of worship. Here Abram will later welcome three visitors and receive the promise of a son (18:1). His life moves from altar to altar.

Verse by verse.

“Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herders and my herders; for we are kindred.” (13:8)

Abram does not wait for Lot to make peace. The one with the stronger claim takes the first step.

“Is not the whole land before you? Separate yourself from me.” (13:9)

He gives up his right of choice. This is not weakness but trust: Abram knows the land is God’s gift, not something to be seized.

“Lot looked about him, and saw that the plain of the Jordan was well watered everywhere like the garden of the Lord” (13:10)

Lot chooses by what his eyes see. The narrator adds a warning at once: this was before the Lord destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah.

“Raise your eyes now, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward” (13:14)

Lot lifted his eyes to choose for himself; now God tells Abram to lift his eyes and receive. What Abram let go, God gives back in every direction.

“So Abram moved his tent, and came and settled by the oaks of Mamre, which are at Hebron; and there he built an altar to the Lord.” (13:18)

Abram answers the promise with worship. Tents move, but the altar marks where God is honoured.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism describes Abraham’s heart as entirely submissive to the word of God, and notes that his prayer is expressed first by deeds: a man of silence, he builds an altar to the Lord at each stage of his journey (CCC 2570). The Letter to the Hebrews sees in his life in tents a sign that he was looking forward to a city whose builder is God (Hebrews 11:9–10). On this feast the Church hears in Abram’s generosity an echo of the Heart that is “gentle and humble” (Matthew 11:29): the one who gives way in love loses nothing that God wants to give.

Isaiah 52:13–53:9 · He was wounded for our transgressions

Setting.

This is the fourth and greatest of the Servant Songs in the second part of Isaiah (the others are 42:1–9; 49:1–6; 50:4–9). These chapters (40 to 55) speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ, announcing comfort and return. Many scholars think they come from a prophet of that time who stood in the tradition of Isaiah of Jerusalem.

Who is the Servant? Within the book he is sometimes Israel itself (41:8; 49:3), and Jewish tradition has often read this song of the people of Israel in their sufferings. Others have seen a prophet or a future figure. From the beginning Christians have recognised in this Servant Jesus, who suffered for the sins of others: the deacon Philip explains this passage to the Ethiopian official as the good news of Jesus (Acts 8:32–35).

Structure.

  • 52:13–15: God speaks: my servant shall be exalted; many were appalled at his disfigured form; kings will be silent.
  • 53:1–3: a group speaks (“we”): he grew up like a root in dry ground; despised and rejected.
  • 53:4–6: the turning point: he bore our infirmities; wounded for our transgressions; the Lord laid on him the iniquity of us all.
  • 53:7–9: he was silent like a lamb; taken away by injustice; his grave with the wicked though he had done no violence.

Key words.

  • ‘ebed, servant: A title of honour, given to Moses and David. The Servant belongs wholly to God and carries out his will, even through suffering.
  • yaśkîl, shall prosper: The verb means both to act wisely and to succeed. The song begins with the end: the one who seems a failure is the one whose work succeeds.
  • ḥabbûrâ, bruises: A singular noun, literally “his bruise” or “stripe”, the mark of a blow. 1 Peter 2:24 quotes this verse: “by his wounds you have been healed.”

Verse by verse.

“See, my servant shall prosper; he shall be exalted and lifted up, and shall be very high.” (52:13)

The same verbs are used of God himself in Isaiah’s vision (6:1). John’s Gospel speaks of Jesus being “lifted up” on the cross, which is already his glory (John 12:32–33).

“He was despised and rejected by others; a man of suffering and acquainted with infirmity” (53:3)

People turned their faces away. The Servant knows the loneliness of every sufferer.

“But he was wounded for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the punishment that made us whole, and by his bruises we are healed.” (53:5)

The heart of the song. “We” thought he was punished for his own sins (v. 4); now “we” see that he carried ours. The wounds of the Servant become the place of our healing.

“like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he did not open his mouth.” (53:7)

Jesus was silent before the high priest and before Pilate (Mark 14:61; 15:5). John the Baptist calls him “the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Scriptures foretold God’s plan of salvation through the death of “the righteous one, my servant”, as a mystery of universal redemption (CCC 601, quoting Isaiah 53:11). It explains that when John the Baptist calls Jesus the Lamb of God, he reveals him as both the suffering Servant who silently lets himself be led to the slaughter and the Paschal Lamb (CCC 608). St Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, quoted this whole song to show that the prophets had foretold the sufferings of Christ (in substance, First Apology 50–51). The Gospels apply it to Jesus’ healing (Matthew 8:17) and his Passion (Luke 22:37).

Romans 10:1–10 · One believes with the heart

Setting.

Chapters 9 to 11 of Romans are Paul’s long reflection on his own people, Israel, most of whom had not accepted Jesus as Messiah. He begins with deep grief: he would be willing to be cut off from Christ for the sake of his kindred (9:3). He insists that to them belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the law, the worship and the promises, and from them comes the Messiah (9:4–5).

In these verses Paul contrasts two ways of seeking righteousness: by one’s own achievement under the law, and by faith in what God has done in Christ. He takes words of Moses from Deuteronomy 30:12–14, which said that God’s commandment is not far away in heaven or across the sea, and applies them to the word of faith: Christ has already come down and been raised.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: Paul’s prayer for Israel’s salvation; their zeal for God, not according to knowledge.
  • v. 4: Christ is the end of the law, for righteousness to everyone who believes.
  • vv. 5–8: two kinds of righteousness; the word is near you, on your lips and in your heart.
  • vv. 9–10: confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord, believe in your heart that God raised him: you will be saved.

Key words.

  • zēlos, zeal: Ardent devotion. Paul had it himself as a Pharisee, so much that he persecuted the Church (Philippians 3:6). He speaks of his people’s zeal with respect, as one who knows it from within.
  • telos, end: Both “end” and “goal”. Christ is the goal toward which the law was leading and its fulfilment, not its cancellation (compare Matthew 5:17).
  • Kyrios Iēsous, Jesus is Lord: One of the earliest Christian confessions (1 Corinthians 12:3). In the Greek Old Testament Kyrios stands for the holy Name of God. To call Jesus Lord is to confess him as divine.

Verse by verse.

“Brothers and sisters, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved.” (10:1)

Paul’s argument begins with love and prayer. He does not write about Israel as an outsider but as one of them (11:1).

“For Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.” (10:4)

“Everyone”: the righteousness given in Christ is offered to Jew and Gentile alike (10:12).

“The word is near you, on your lips and in your heart” (10:8)

Paul quotes Deuteronomy 30:14. God does not ask us to climb to heaven; in Christ he has come close.

“because if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.” (10:9)

Lips and heart: faith is both inward and spoken. The order of verse 9 follows Deuteronomy’s words (“lips” then “heart”); verse 10 puts the heart first.

“For one believes with the heart and so is justified, and one confesses with the mouth and so is saved.” (10:10)

In the Bible the heart is not only feelings but the centre of the person, where decisions are made. On this feast we remember that Christ first gave us his own Heart.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 4 to teach that the moral law finds its fullness and unity in Christ, who is the end of the law because he alone teaches and gives the righteousness of God (CCC 1953). It explains that by giving Jesus the divine title “Lord”, the first confessions of the Church’s faith affirmed that the honour due to God the Father is due also to him (CCC 449). It calls the heart the dwelling place where I am, our hidden centre, the place of decision and of covenant (CCC 2563). And it reminds us that the gifts and calling of God to the Jewish people are irrevocable, as Paul himself says in this same section (Romans 11:29; CCC 839; Nostra Aetate 4).

John 19:30–37 · They will look on the one whom they have pierced

Setting.

Jesus has been crucified at Golgotha. He has entrusted his mother to the beloved disciple and the disciple to her (19:26–27), and has said, “I am thirsty,” receiving sour wine on a branch of hyssop (19:28–29). Now he dies.

John has told us that this was the day of Preparation for the Passover (19:14), the day the Passover lambs were slain in the Temple. The Law of Moses required that the body of an executed man not remain hanging overnight (Deuteronomy 21:22–23), and this sabbath was the great feast. The Roman practice of breaking the legs of the crucified hastened death, because they could no longer push themselves up to breathe.

Structure.

  • v. 30: Jesus receives the wine, says “It is finished,” bows his head and gives up his spirit.
  • vv. 31–33: the request to remove the bodies; the soldiers break the legs of the other two, but not of Jesus, already dead.
  • v. 34: a soldier pierces his side with a spear; at once blood and water come out.
  • v. 35: the eyewitness’s solemn testimony, so that you also may believe.
  • vv. 36–37: two Scriptures fulfilled: no bone broken; they will look on the one whom they have pierced.

Key words.

  • tetelestai, it is finished: From telos, end and goal. Not “it is over” but “it is accomplished”: the work the Father gave him is brought to completion (17:4), and he loved his own “to the end” (13:1).
  • pleura, side: The word for Jesus’ side is the same word used in the Greek Old Testament for the rib taken from the side of the sleeping Adam, from which Eve was formed (Genesis 2:21–22). The Fathers built much on this.
  • exekentēsan, they pierced: The verb John uses to render Zechariah 12:10 from the Hebrew, where God says that the inhabitants of Jerusalem will look on the one they have pierced and mourn for him. John sees in the soldier’s spear the fulfilment of that prophecy of grief and grace.

Verse by verse.

“When Jesus had received the wine, he said, “It is finished.” Then he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.” (19:30)

Literally “he handed over the spirit”. He is not overcome by death; he lays down his life of his own accord (10:18). Many have also seen here a first hint of the gift of the Holy Spirit.

“But when they came to Jesus and saw that he was already dead, they did not break his legs.” (19:33)

The detail points to the Passover lamb, whose bones were not to be broken (Exodus 12:46).

“Instead, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once blood and water came out.” (19:34)

Jesus had promised that rivers of living water would flow from within him, and John explained this of the Spirit (7:38–39). Blood and water recall the Eucharist and Baptism, by which the Church lives.

“He who saw this has testified so that you also may believe. His testimony is true” (19:35)

The evangelist steps forward to vouch for what he saw, as he does at the end of the Gospel (21:24). This is the purpose of the whole book: “that you may come to believe” (20:31).

“None of his bones shall be broken.” (19:36)

This recalls the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12) and also the righteous sufferer whom God protects (Psalm 34:20).

“And again another passage of scripture says, “They will look on the one whom they have pierced.”” (19:37)

Zechariah 12:10 continues: they will mourn for him as for an only child. Looking on the pierced Christ leads to sorrow for sin and to the fountain that cleanses (Zechariah 13:1).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus knew and loved each of us during his life, his agony and his Passion, and that his Sacred Heart, pierced by our sins and for our salvation, is rightly considered the chief sign and symbol of the love with which he loves the Father and all people (CCC 478). The Church, it says, was born primarily of Christ’s total self-giving, anticipated in the Last Supper and realised on the cross, symbolised by the blood and water flowing from his opened side (CCC 766). The blood and water are types of Baptism and the Eucharist (CCC 1225).

St John Chrysostom taught that the Church was formed from the side of Christ as Eve was formed from the side of Adam, and that water and blood are the symbols of Baptism and the holy mysteries (in substance, Baptismal Catecheses 3). St Augustine observed that the evangelist chose his word carefully: the soldier opened his side, so that the door of life might be opened, from which the sacraments of the Church flowed (in substance, Tractates on John 120.2). Pope Pius XII took the title of his encyclical on the Sacred Heart from Isaiah 12:3, “With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation” (Haurietis Aquas, 1956), and Pope Francis returned to the theme in Dilexit Nos (2024).

Questions people ask

Why does the Church honour Jesus’ heart? Is this not just a sentimental image?

In the Bible the heart means the centre of the person, where love, thought and choice meet (CCC 2563). To honour the Heart of Jesus is to honour the whole person of the incarnate Son in his love, which is both human and divine. The Catechism calls his pierced Heart the chief sign and symbol of that love (CCC 478). The devotion grew in the Middle Ages and was spread especially after the visions given to St Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial in France in the 1670s. Pope Pius IX extended the feast to the whole Latin Church in 1856.

John says that “the Jews” asked for the legs to be broken. Does the Gospel blame the Jewish people?

No. In John’s Passion “the Jews” usually means the Temple authorities who acted against Jesus, not the Jewish people as a whole. Jesus himself, his mother, the beloved disciple and all the first disciples were Jews. Here the request even comes from a concern to keep God’s Law about the dead (Deuteronomy 21:22–23) and to honour the feast. The Church teaches that Christ’s death cannot be charged against all the Jews then living, nor against the Jews of today, and that all sinners were the authors of his Passion (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597–598).

Is Isaiah 53 really about Jesus, or about Israel?

Both readings have a long history. Within Isaiah, the Servant is at times Israel, and Jewish interpreters have often read this song of the sufferings of their people, a reading Christians should respect. The New Testament, from Philip’s words to the Ethiopian (Acts 8:35) to 1 Peter 2:22–25, sees its fullest meaning in Jesus, the one innocent man who suffered for the sins of many. The Church holds that the Old Testament keeps its own value and that its full meaning is revealed in Christ (CCC 128–129).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Many Syro-Malabar families keep a picture of the Sacred Heart in the place of prayer at home and have consecrated their family to it, and the First Friday devotion, with confession and Holy Qurbana, is loved in our parishes. The feast falls in the Season of the Apostles: the Church born from the opened side is the Church sent out on mission, as Mar Thoma Shleeha was sent to our land.

Our East Syriac tradition loves to set the cross beside the tree of life. Mar Ephrem, meditating on the Passion, speaks of entering through the pierced side of Christ into the garden that had been closed by the sword since Adam’s sin (in substance, Commentary on the Diatessaron 21). The fountain that flows from the side of the second Adam reopens Paradise. Each time we receive the Body and Blood of the Lord in the Holy Qurbana, we drink from that fountain.

For study and teaching

  • Abram let Lot choose first rather than quarrel. In which relationship in my family or parish am I being asked to give way for the sake of peace?
  • Isaiah says “by his bruises we are healed”. What wound in my life do I need to bring to the wounds of Christ?
  • Paul says we believe with the heart and confess with the lips. Is there a place where my faith stays hidden in my heart and needs to be spoken?
  • John asks us to look on the one whom they pierced. How could my family spend a few minutes before the image of the Sacred Heart this week?
