Word for the day

“No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.” Luke 13:3

Reflection

Five weeks after Pentecost the readings of the Season of the Apostles put a question to us: what does it look like to live as God's people in the middle of the nations? The first reading answers from the plains of Moab. Moses, about to die, gathers Israel at the edge of the promised land: “So now, Israel, give heed to the statutes and ordinances that I am teaching you to observe, so that you may live to enter and occupy the land”. Notice the purpose: so that you may live. God's commandments are not a fence around our happiness but the shape of life itself. “You must neither add anything to what I command you nor take away anything from it”: neither the religion that piles up human rules nor the religion that quietly drops the hard ones. And then two great questions. “For what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him? And what other great nation has statutes and ordinances as just as this entire law that I am setting before you today?” The law is the sign of God's nearness. Israel's wisdom will not be its armies or its wealth; the nations will look at a people who live by God's word and say, “Surely this great nation is a wise and discerning people!” The longer reading adds a command for every Christian family: “make them known to your children and your children’s children”. June in Kerala is the month the schools reopen and the Sunday catechism classes begin a new year. Faith that is not handed on at home does not survive the second generation.

Isaiah lifts our eyes from the plains of Moab to a mountain at the end of days: “In days to come the mountain of the Lord’s house shall be established as the highest of the mountains, and shall be raised above the hills; all the nations shall stream to it.” Rivers flow down from mountains; here the nations flow up, drawn by a light. “Many peoples shall come and say, ‘Come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, to the house of the God of Jacob; that he may teach us his ways and that we may walk in his paths.’ For out of Zion shall go forth instruction, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem.” The Church has always read this as a prophecy of Pentecost. From Jerusalem the word went out with the apostles, to Samaria, to Antioch, to Rome and, as our tradition holds, to the coasts of India with Mar Thoma. St Justin Martyr, writing in Rome in the second century, quoted this very passage and said (in substance) that from Jerusalem twelve unlearned men went out into the world and proclaimed the word of God to every race. And the fruit of that word is peace: “they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” We have not yet seen that day in full; the news of every week tells us so. But wherever the Gospel is truly received, swords begin to become ploughs: enemies are reconciled, families make peace, communities divided by caste, language or politics learn to sit at one table. The prophet ends with an invitation, not a command: “O house of Jacob, come, let us walk in the light of the Lord!”

Paul brings the vision down to the dinner table. Corinth was full of temples, and much of the meat in the market had first been offered to idols. Some Christians said, “All things are lawful.” Paul quotes the slogan and corrects it: “but not all things are beneficial. ‘All things are lawful,’ but not all things build up. Do not seek your own advantage, but that of the other.” The question is not only what I am allowed to do, but what builds up my brother and sister. Paul is not anxious about the meat itself: “the earth and its fullness are the Lord’s.” But if someone at table says, “This has been offered in sacrifice,” then do not eat, “out of consideration for the one who informed you, and for the sake of conscience”, the other person's conscience. Love sometimes gives up what liberty allows. In our own country, where Christians live with Hindu and Muslim neighbours, share their festivals and receive their hospitality, Paul's principle is a wise guide: receive every good gift with thanksgiving and respect, never do anything that would be taken as worship of another god, and never wound the conscience of a weaker brother or sister. Then comes the sentence that turns ordinary life into liturgy: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” Eating, working, studying, cooking, driving the autorickshaw, answering emails: all of it can be offered. The Holy Qurbana does not end at the church door; it continues at the dinner table.

The Gospel is sharp. Jesus has been speaking about the signs of the times, and he asks: “And why do you not judge for yourselves what is right?” If you are on your way to court with an opponent, “on the way make an effort to settle the case”, before it is too late. Then some people tell him a terrible piece of news: Pilate has killed some Galileans while they were offering sacrifice, mingling their blood with the blood of their animals. Behind the report is an old assumption: those who suffer such things must have deserved them. Jesus rejects it firmly. “Do you think that because these Galileans suffered in this way they were worse sinners than all other Galileans? No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.” He adds a disaster of his own: “those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam fell on them”, an accident, not a crime. Were they worse offenders? No. Jesus will not let us turn the suffering of others into a judgment on them; he turns it into a call to ourselves. When a landslide buries a village in the monsoon, or a boat overturns in the backwaters, we must not say, ‘they were punished.’ We must weep with them, help them, and ask: am I ready to meet God? The longer reading gives the other side of the picture: a fig tree that has borne no fruit for three years, an owner who says, “Cut it down!”, and a gardener who pleads, “Sir, let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it.” Repentance is urgent because life is short; it is possible because God is patient. The Fathers saw in the gardener Christ himself, who intercedes for us and digs around our roots with his word and sacraments. Today is the year of grace. On this Sixth Sunday of the Apostles, let us walk in the light of the Lord, do everything for his glory, and turn back to him while we are still on the way.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this Sixth Sunday of the Apostles, Moses asks a beautiful question: “what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him?” (Deuteronomy 4:7). God gave his commandments not to burden us but so that we might live. And he asks us to make them known to our children and our children's children.

This month our children have gone back to school and the catechism classes have begun again. Parents, you are the first teachers of the faith. Do not leave it only to the Sunday school. Pray with your children, read the Gospel with them, and let them see you live it.

Isaiah sees all the nations streaming to the mountain of the Lord, saying, “Come, let us go up… that he may teach us his ways.” The word goes out from Jerusalem, and its fruit is peace: swords become ploughshares (Isaiah 2:3–4). This happened at Pentecost, when the apostles carried the word to the nations, and it happens wherever enemies are reconciled.

Saint Paul tells us that not everything we are allowed to do builds up others. Love thinks about the other person's conscience. And he gives a rule for every day: “whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31). Our kitchen, our office and our classroom can all become places of worship.

In the Gospel, people tell Jesus about Galileans killed by Pilate and about a tower that fell and killed eighteen people. Were they worse sinners? Jesus says, “No… but unless you repent, you will all perish” (Luke 13:3). When disaster strikes others, let us not judge them; let us help them, and look into our own hearts.

And Jesus tells of a fig tree without fruit, and a gardener who says, “let it alone for one more year.” That gardener is Jesus, who pleads for us and works on our roots. Today is our year of grace. Let us turn back to him, walk in the light of the Lord, and bear fruit for his glory. Amen.

For children

Children, Isaiah dreamed of a day when people would melt their swords and make them into tools for farming. That means: stop fighting and start helping!

Saint Paul says we can do everything for God, even eating and playing. Today, say a prayer before your meal, and if you have a fight with someone, be the first to make peace!

Prayer

Lord our God, there is no other people with a God so near as you are to us whenever we call upon you. Teach us your statutes, that we may live, and help us to hand on your word to our children and our children's children. Draw all the nations to the mountain of your house, and let the word that went out from Jerusalem with the apostles make swords into ploughs in our land and in our homes. Whether we eat or drink, or whatever we do, may we do everything for your glory. Lord Jesus, patient gardener, dig around our roots and give us one more year of grace, that we may turn back to you and bear fruit. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

This Sunday’s readings are about a word that gives life and a time that must not be wasted. In Deuteronomy, Moses tells Israel that the Lord’s statutes are their wisdom before the nations, and that no nation has a God so near. Isaiah sees the nations streaming to the mountain of the Lord to learn his ways, beating their swords into plowshares. Paul teaches the Corinthians how to use their Christian liberty: seek the good of the other, and do everything for the glory of God. And in Luke, Jesus urges his hearers to read the signs of their own time, to settle accounts while they are still on the way, and to repent.

The thread is this: God has come near and spoken. His word is not a burden but life, wisdom and peace, first for Israel and through Israel for all peoples. The right answer is to turn to him now, while there is time, and to live in a way that builds up others and gives him glory. In the Season of the Apostles the Church hears how that word went out from Zion to the ends of the earth, and to us.

Deuteronomy 4:1–8 · A God so near

Setting.

Deuteronomy presents itself as Moses’ last words to Israel on the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from the promised land. Chapters 1–3 have recalled the journey from Horeb. Now, in chapter 4, Moses turns from history to exhortation: “So now, Israel, give heed.” A new generation is about to enter the land, and Moses wants them to know that their life there depends on listening to the Lord.

The reference to the Baal of Peor recalls Numbers 25: at Shittim, on these same plains, many Israelites joined in the worship of the Baal of Peor, and a plague followed. Those who held fast to the Lord survived. Moses uses this recent memory as a lesson: faithfulness is life.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the call to hear and keep the statutes, adding nothing and taking nothing away.
  • vv. 3–4: the lesson of the Baal of Peor: those who held fast to the Lord are alive.
  • vv. 5–6: keeping the law is Israel’s wisdom in the sight of the nations.
  • vv. 7–8: two questions of wonder: what nation has a God so near, and what nation has such just laws?

Key words.

  • shema‘, hear, give heed: The same verb that opens the great prayer of Israel, “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4). In the Bible, to hear is to obey: listening that does not change life is not yet hearing.
  • ḥuqqîm u-mishpāṭîm, statutes and ordinances: A pair that runs through Deuteronomy. The first word comes from a root meaning to engrave, things fixed by God; the second means judgments, decisions about what is just. Together they name the whole of the Lord’s teaching.
  • dābaq, to hold fast: The verb used when a man “clings” to his wife (Genesis 2:24). Israel’s faithfulness to the Lord is pictured as the bond of love in marriage.

Verse by verse.

“So now, Israel, give heed to the statutes and ordinances that I am teaching you to observe, so that you may live” (4:1)

The purpose of the law is life. God does not command in order to burden but in order that his people may live.

“You must neither add anything to what I command you nor take away anything from it” (4:2)

The word belongs to God, not to us. The same warning closes the Bible (Revelation 22:18–19).

“while those of you who held fast to the Lord your God are all alive today.” (4:4)

Moses speaks to people who remember. Faith rests on what God has done in their own lifetime.

“For what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him?” (4:7)

Israel’s greatness is not its size or its army but the nearness of its God. Christians hear this verse fulfilled beyond measure in the Word made flesh, who “lived among us” (John 1:14).

How the Church reads it.

The Church receives the Law given to Israel as God’s gift and as a true revelation. The Catechism teaches that the commandments take their full meaning within the covenant: they express what it means to belong to God (CCC 2062). Jesus did not abolish the Law but fulfilled it (Matthew 5:17), and he summed it up in the love of God and neighbour, quoting Deuteronomy itself (Matthew 22:37). Moses’ question, “What other great nation has a god so near?”, has been read by Christians as a promise kept in the Incarnation and in the Eucharist, where God is nearer still.

Isaiah 2:1–5 · All the nations shall stream to it

Setting.

Isaiah son of Amoz prophesied in Jerusalem in the second half of the eighth century before Christ, in the days of kings Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (1:1). His first chapter is a harsh indictment of Judah’s sins. Then, suddenly, comes this vision of hope: not Jerusalem as it is, but as God will make it.

An almost identical oracle appears in Micah 4:1–3. Scholars do not agree whether one prophet borrowed from the other or both used an older text. Either way, the Church received it twice in her Scriptures, as if to underline its importance.

Structure.

  • v. 1: a new title: the word Isaiah “saw”.
  • v. 2: the mountain of the Lord’s house raised above all mountains; the nations stream to it.
  • v. 3: the peoples invite one another to go up and learn; instruction goes out from Zion.
  • v. 4: the Lord judges between nations; swords become plowshares; war is no longer learned.
  • v. 5: the call to Israel: walk now in the light of the Lord.

Key words.

  • be’aḥarît hayyāmîm, in days to come: Literally “at the end of the days”. Not simply some later time, but the time when God brings his plan to its goal. The New Testament says that in Christ these last days have begun (Hebrews 1:2; Acts 2:17).
  • nāhar, to stream, to flow: The verb from which the Hebrew word for river comes. The picture is strange on purpose: rivers flow down, but here the nations flow up the mountain, drawn by God.
  • tôrâ, instruction: Usually rendered “law”, but its root sense is teaching or direction. What goes out from Zion is God’s teaching for the way of life, and the nations come wanting to learn it.

Verse by verse.

“In days to come the mountain of the Lord’s house shall be established as the highest of the mountains” (2:2)

Zion was not a high mountain. Its height is the honour God gives it, because his presence dwells there.

“that he may teach us his ways and that we may walk in his paths.” (2:3)

The nations do not come to conquer Jerusalem but to be taught. Peace begins with learning God’s ways.

“they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks” (2:4)

Weapons are not merely laid down; they are reforged into tools that feed people. The iron is the same; its purpose changes.

“O house of Jacob, come, let us walk in the light of the Lord!” (2:5)

The vision ends with an appeal to Israel now. The future peace is to be lived in advance by God’s own people.

How the Church reads it.

The Fathers saw this prophecy beginning to be fulfilled when the Gospel went out from Jerusalem. St Justin Martyr, quoting the words about the law going forth from Zion (“out of Zion shall go forth instruction”), tells the Roman emperor that twelve men went out from Jerusalem into the world, unlearned and unable to speak well, and by the power of God proclaimed Christ to every race (in substance, First Apology 39). The Second Vatican Council quotes the words about swords and plowshares in its teaching on peace, and calls Christians to work for a peace that is the fruit of justice and love (Gaudium et Spes 78).

1 Corinthians 10:23–31 · Do everything for the glory of God

Setting.

Since chapter 8 Paul has been answering a practical question from Corinth: may Christians eat meat that has been offered to idols? In a Greek city much of the meat sold in the market (Greek makellon) came from animals sacrificed in temples, and invitations to meals with pagan friends were part of ordinary life.

Paul has already forbidden taking part in meals in idol temples (10:14–22): one cannot share the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons. Now he deals with the ordinary market and the private home, and gives a principle that reaches far beyond food.

Structure.

  • vv. 23–24: the Corinthian slogan corrected: not everything builds up; seek the other’s good.
  • vv. 25–26: market meat may be eaten, for the earth is the Lord’s.
  • vv. 27–29a: at a host’s table eat without scruple, unless someone points out that the food was sacrificed; then abstain for his conscience.
  • vv. 29b–30: Paul defends his liberty and his thanksgiving.
  • v. 31: the rule for all of life: do everything for the glory of God.

Key words.

  • oikodomeō, to build up: A building word. For Paul the measure of an action is not only whether it is allowed but whether it builds up the community, the temple of God (3:16).
  • syneidēsis, conscience: The inner judgment about right and wrong. Paul respects the conscience of the weaker brother even when it is mistaken about the facts, because to make him act against it would harm him.
  • eucharisteō, to give thanks: The word from which “Eucharist” comes. Paul gives thanks over his food, as Jews blessed God at meals and as Jesus gave thanks at the Last Supper (11:24).

Verse by verse.

““All things are lawful,” but not all things are beneficial.” (10:23)

Paul seems to quote a slogan used at Corinth (as in 6:12) and corrects it. Liberty is real, but it is for love.

“Do not seek your own advantage, but that of the other.” (10:24)

This is the heart of Paul’s ethic, learned from Christ, who did not please himself (Romans 15:3).

“for “the earth and its fullness are the Lord’s.”” (10:26)

Paul quotes Psalm 24:1. Later Jewish teachers used the same verse to teach that every food should be received with a blessing; Paul uses it to show that no food is unclean in itself.

“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” (10:31)

Nothing is too ordinary to be offered to God. A meal, a day’s work, a conversation can all give him glory.

How the Church reads it.

The Church teaches that a person must always obey the certain judgment of conscience, and also has the duty to form it well (CCC 1790, 1783). Paul adds that charity respects the conscience of others: we should not lead a brother to act against his conscience (CCC 1789, which cites this letter). The motto of St Ignatius of Loyola and the Society of Jesus, Ad maiorem Dei gloriam, for the greater glory of God, echoes the words “do everything for the glory of God”.

Luke 12:57–13:5 · Unless you repent

Setting.

Jesus is on his way to Jerusalem (9:51), teaching the crowds about readiness for the coming of the Son of Man (12:35–48). Just before this reading he rebuked the crowds for being able to read the weather but not “the present time” (12:56). These verses continue that warning.

Luke alone records the two events in 13:1–5. Nothing else is known of Galileans killed by Pilate while offering sacrifice, but the Jewish historian Josephus tells of other harsh acts by Pilate, so the report fits his character. The tower of Siloam stood near the pool of Siloam, in the south of Jerusalem; its fall is also known only from Luke.

Structure.

  • 12:57: judge for yourselves what is right.
  • 12:58–59: a parable from the courts: settle with your accuser on the way, before it is too late.
  • 13:1: news of the Galileans killed by Pilate.
  • 13:2–3: were they worse sinners? No; but unless you repent, you will all perish.
  • 13:4–5: the eighteen killed at Siloam: the same question and the same answer.

Key words.

  • kairos, time, the present time: In 12:56 Jesus speaks of “the present time”: not clock time but the decisive moment of God’s visit. The crowds can read the sky but not the hour of salvation standing before them.
  • metanoeō, to repent: Literally to change one’s mind, to turn round. In the Gospels it means a turning of the whole person back to God, not only sorrow for a single act.
  • lepton, penny: The smallest coin, the same as the widow’s two coins in Mark 12:42. Nothing will be left unpaid.

Verse by verse.

“Thus, when you go with your accuser before a magistrate, on the way make an effort to settle the case” (12:58)

The road to the court is the time of grace. We are all on that road; the moment to be reconciled with God and neighbour is now.

“Do you think that because these Galileans suffered in this way they were worse sinners than all other Galileans?” (13:2)

Many people then, as now, assumed that disaster proves guilt. Jesus rejects that assumption, as the Book of Job had done.

“No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.” (13:3)

Jesus turns a question about others into a question about us. The tragedy is a call, not a verdict on the victims.

“Or those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam fell on them” (13:4)

The first case was a crime by a ruler, the second an accident. Whether evil comes from human hands or from chance, the lesson is the same.

How the Church reads it.

The Church does not teach that every suffering is a punishment for the sufferer’s own sins. Jesus says the same of the man born blind (John 9:3), and St John Paul II, reflecting on Job, wrote that not all suffering is a consequence of a fault (in substance, Salvifici Doloris 11). What Jesus asks is conversion. The Catechism explains that his call to conversion is first of all a call to interior conversion, a radical reorientation of the whole life, a return to God with all our heart (CCC 1430–1431). The parable that follows in Luke, the barren fig tree given one more year (13:6–9), shows that God’s patience gives us time to turn.

Questions people ask

When a disaster strikes, should we say it is God’s punishment?

Jesus forbids us to conclude that those who suffer are worse sinners than others (Luke 13:2–4). God permits evil only to draw good from it, in ways we may not see in this life (CCC 312–314). The right response to tragedy is compassion for the victims, prayer, and our own conversion, not judgment of others.

Paul says we may eat meat sold in the market. Do Christians still have rules about food?

Paul teaches that no food is unclean in itself, since the earth is the Lord’s (10:26; see Mark 7:19). The Church’s days of fasting and abstinence are not about clean and unclean food. They are acts of penance and love, willingly embraced in union with Christ, and each Church sets its own discipline (CCC 1438, 2043). Syro-Malabar Catholics follow the fasts and abstinences laid down by their own Church.

Does “You must neither add anything to what I command you nor take away anything from it” mean the Church may not develop doctrine?

Moses forbids changing God’s word to suit ourselves. The Church does not add new revelation; she guards the deposit she received and, with the help of the Holy Spirit, comes to understand it more deeply (Dei Verbum 8, 10). Development of doctrine is growth in understanding, not a change of the word.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Season of the Apostles our Church celebrates the Church’s mission: the word going out from Zion, as Isaiah saw, carried by the apostles to the nations and by Mar Thoma Shleeha to India. Isaiah’s picture of the nations coming to learn God’s ways is fulfilled every time a catechism class meets in a parish or a family reads Scripture together at evening prayer. Every Holy Qurbana proclaims the word and then gives us the One who is nearer to us than Moses could have imagined.

Paul’s words “do everything for the glory of God” fit the Syro-Malabar home well: the blessing before meals, the evening family prayer, and the work of farmers, teachers and nurses offered to God. Our Church’s rich tradition of fasting is lived not to make some foods unclean but to turn the heart to God.

For study and teaching

  • Moses asks what other great nation has a god so near to it. Where have I experienced God’s nearness this week?
  • Isaiah sees swords turned into plowshares. Is there a “sword” in my words or relationships that God is asking me to reforge into something that builds up?
  • Paul says, “Do not seek your own advantage, but that of the other.” Where in my family or parish could I give up a right for someone else’s good?
  • Jesus says, “unless you repent”. What turning is he asking of me now, while I am still on the way?
