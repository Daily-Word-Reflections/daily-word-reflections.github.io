Word for the day

“No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.” Luke 13:3

Reflection

Five weeks after Pentecost the readings of the Season of the Apostles put a question to us: what does it look like to live as God's people in the middle of the nations? The first reading answers from the plains of Moab. Moses, about to die, gathers Israel at the edge of the promised land: “So now, Israel, give heed to the statutes and ordinances that I am teaching you to observe, so that you may live to enter and occupy the land”. Notice the purpose: so that you may live. God's commandments are not a fence around our happiness but the shape of life itself. “You must neither add anything to what I command you nor take away anything from it”: neither the religion that piles up human rules nor the religion that quietly drops the hard ones. And then two great questions. “For what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him? And what other great nation has statutes and ordinances as just as this entire law that I am setting before you today?” The law is the sign of God's nearness. Israel's wisdom will not be its armies or its wealth; the nations will look at a people who live by God's word and say, “Surely this great nation is a wise and discerning people!” The longer reading adds a command for every Christian family: “make them known to your children and your children’s children”. June in Kerala is the month the schools reopen and the Sunday catechism classes begin a new year. Faith that is not handed on at home does not survive the second generation.

Isaiah lifts our eyes from the plains of Moab to a mountain at the end of days: “In days to come the mountain of the Lord’s house shall be established as the highest of the mountains, and shall be raised above the hills; all the nations shall stream to it.” Rivers flow down from mountains; here the nations flow up, drawn by a light. “Many peoples shall come and say, ‘Come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, to the house of the God of Jacob; that he may teach us his ways and that we may walk in his paths.’ For out of Zion shall go forth instruction, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem.” The Church has always read this as a prophecy of Pentecost. From Jerusalem the word went out with the apostles, to Samaria, to Antioch, to Rome and, as our tradition holds, to the coasts of India with Mar Thoma. St Justin Martyr, writing in Rome in the second century, quoted this very passage and said (in substance) that from Jerusalem twelve unlearned men went out into the world and proclaimed the word of God to every race. And the fruit of that word is peace: “they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” We have not yet seen that day in full; the news of every week tells us so. But wherever the Gospel is truly received, swords begin to become ploughs: enemies are reconciled, families make peace, communities divided by caste, language or politics learn to sit at one table. The prophet ends with an invitation, not a command: “O house of Jacob, come, let us walk in the light of the Lord!”

Paul brings the vision down to the dinner table. Corinth was full of temples, and much of the meat in the market had first been offered to idols. Some Christians said, “All things are lawful.” Paul quotes the slogan and corrects it: “but not all things are beneficial. ‘All things are lawful,’ but not all things build up. Do not seek your own advantage, but that of the other.” The question is not only what I am allowed to do, but what builds up my brother and sister. Paul is not anxious about the meat itself: “the earth and its fullness are the Lord’s.” But if someone at table says, “This has been offered in sacrifice,” then do not eat, “out of consideration for the one who informed you, and for the sake of conscience”, the other person's conscience. Love sometimes gives up what liberty allows. In our own country, where Christians live with Hindu and Muslim neighbours, share their festivals and receive their hospitality, Paul's principle is a wise guide: receive every good gift with thanksgiving and respect, never do anything that would be taken as worship of another god, and never wound the conscience of a weaker brother or sister. Then comes the sentence that turns ordinary life into liturgy: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” Eating, working, studying, cooking, driving the autorickshaw, answering emails: all of it can be offered. The Holy Qurbana does not end at the church door; it continues at the dinner table.

The Gospel is sharp. Jesus has been speaking about the signs of the times, and he asks: “And why do you not judge for yourselves what is right?” If you are on your way to court with an opponent, “on the way make an effort to settle the case”, before it is too late. Then some people tell him a terrible piece of news: Pilate has killed some Galileans while they were offering sacrifice, mingling their blood with the blood of their animals. Behind the report is an old assumption: those who suffer such things must have deserved them. Jesus rejects it firmly. “Do you think that because these Galileans suffered in this way they were worse sinners than all other Galileans? No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.” He adds a disaster of his own: “those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam fell on them”, an accident, not a crime. Were they worse offenders? No. Jesus will not let us turn the suffering of others into a judgment on them; he turns it into a call to ourselves. When a landslide buries a village in the monsoon, or a boat overturns in the backwaters, we must not say, ‘they were punished.’ We must weep with them, help them, and ask: am I ready to meet God? The longer reading gives the other side of the picture: a fig tree that has borne no fruit for three years, an owner who says, “Cut it down!”, and a gardener who pleads, “Sir, let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it.” Repentance is urgent because life is short; it is possible because God is patient. The Fathers saw in the gardener Christ himself, who intercedes for us and digs around our roots with his word and sacraments. Today is the year of grace. On this Sixth Sunday of the Apostles, let us walk in the light of the Lord, do everything for his glory, and turn back to him while we are still on the way.

Homily outline

Theme: “No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all perish as they did.”

On the Sixth Sunday of the Apostles: the God who is near, the nations streaming to his mountain, a life lived for his glory, and the urgent, patient call to repentance.

1. A god so near

  • The commandments: so that you may live.
  • Neither adding nor taking away.
  • Make them known to your children.
“For what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him?” Deuteronomy 4:7
“And the Word became flesh and lived among us,” John 1:14

Apply: Read one Gospel passage with your children or grandchildren this week.

2. All the nations shall stream to it

  • The mountain of the Lord's house.
  • The word goes out from Jerusalem with the apostles.
  • Swords become ploughs.
“they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” Isaiah 2:4
“For he is our peace; in his flesh he has made both groups into one and has broken down the dividing wall, that is, the hostility between us.” Ephesians 2:14

Apply: Make peace this week with one person, or help two others to be reconciled.

3. Do everything for the glory of God

  • Lawful, but not building up.
  • The conscience of the other.
  • Ordinary life as worship.
“The earth is the Lord’s and all that is in it, the world, and those who live in it;” Psalm 24:1
“So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.” 1 Corinthians 10:31

Apply: Pray before every meal this week, and offer your daily work to God each morning.

4. Unless you repent

  • Suffering is not a verdict on the sufferer.
  • A call to my own conversion.
  • The gardener who asks for one more year.
“For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone, says the Lord God. Turn, then, and live.” Ezekiel 18:32
“He replied, ‘Sir, let it alone for one more year, until I dig around it and put manure on it.” Luke 13:8

Apply: Make a good confession before the feast of Sts Peter and Paul.

God is near; his word goes out to the nations and makes peace; every act of our day can give him glory.

Do not judge those who suffer; turn back to God yourself, for the gardener has asked for one more year.

Take-home resolution: This week, hand on the faith to a child, make one peace, pray before every meal, and go to confession before 29 June.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this Sixth Sunday of the Apostles, Moses asks a beautiful question: “what other great nation has a god so near to it as the Lord our God is whenever we call to him?” (Deuteronomy 4:7). God gave his commandments not to burden us but so that we might live. And he asks us to make them known to our children and our children's children.

This month our children have gone back to school and the catechism classes have begun again. Parents, you are the first teachers of the faith. Do not leave it only to the Sunday school. Pray with your children, read the Gospel with them, and let them see you live it.

Isaiah sees all the nations streaming to the mountain of the Lord, saying, “Come, let us go up… that he may teach us his ways.” The word goes out from Jerusalem, and its fruit is peace: swords become ploughshares (Isaiah 2:3–4). This happened at Pentecost, when the apostles carried the word to the nations, and it happens wherever enemies are reconciled.

Saint Paul tells us that not everything we are allowed to do builds up others. Love thinks about the other person's conscience. And he gives a rule for every day: “whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31). Our kitchen, our office and our classroom can all become places of worship.

In the Gospel, people tell Jesus about Galileans killed by Pilate and about a tower that fell and killed eighteen people. Were they worse sinners? Jesus says, “No… but unless you repent, you will all perish” (Luke 13:3). When disaster strikes others, let us not judge them; let us help them, and look into our own hearts.

And Jesus tells of a fig tree without fruit, and a gardener who says, “let it alone for one more year.” That gardener is Jesus, who pleads for us and works on our roots. Today is our year of grace. Let us turn back to him, walk in the light of the Lord, and bear fruit for his glory. Amen.

For children

Children, Isaiah dreamed of a day when people would melt their swords and make them into tools for farming. That means: stop fighting and start helping!

Saint Paul says we can do everything for God, even eating and playing. Today, say a prayer before your meal, and if you have a fight with someone, be the first to make peace!

Prayer

Lord our God, there is no other people with a God so near as you are to us whenever we call upon you. Teach us your statutes, that we may live, and help us to hand on your word to our children and our children's children. Draw all the nations to the mountain of your house, and let the word that went out from Jerusalem with the apostles make swords into ploughs in our land and in our homes. Whether we eat or drink, or whatever we do, may we do everything for your glory. Lord Jesus, patient gardener, dig around our roots and give us one more year of grace, that we may turn back to you and bear fruit. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.
