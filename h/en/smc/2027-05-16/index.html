Word for the day

“All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.” Acts 2:4

Reflection

Fifty days ago we sang the Resurrection; today the fifty days reach their goal. “When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place.” Pentecost was already a great feast for the Jewish people, the Feast of Weeks, the harvest festival kept fifty days after Passover, and in later Jewish tradition it was also linked with the giving of the Law on Sinai. That is why our Church opens the feast with Exodus 19. “On the third new moon after the Israelites had gone out of the land of Egypt, on that very day, they came into the wilderness of Sinai.” God speaks to Moses from the mountain: “You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to myself.” Then the promise: “you shall be for me a priestly kingdom and a holy nation.” God comes in a dense cloud, and a few verses later the mountain is wrapped in smoke “because the LORD had descended upon it in fire” (Exodus 19:18). At Sinai, fire on the mountain gave Israel the Law on tablets of stone; in the upper room, fire on each head writes the new law of love on human hearts, as the prophet had promised: “I will put my spirit within you” (Ezekiel 36:27). The people at Sinai answered, “Everything that the LORD has spoken we will do”; at Pentecost the Spirit gives the power to do it. Peter would later write to the baptised the very words of Sinai: “you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God’s own people” (1 Peter 2:9).

Luke describes the event with signs that every reader of the Scriptures would recognise. “Suddenly from heaven there came a sound like the rush of a violent wind, and it filled the entire house where they were sitting.” Wind and breath are one word in Hebrew and in our Syriac tradition: the breath God breathed into Adam (Genesis 2:7), the wind Ezekiel summoned over the dry bones, the breath of the Risen Lord who said, “Receive the Holy Spirit” (John 20:22). “Divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them. All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.” The Spirit does not come as a general mood over a crowd; a tongue rests on each one. And the first thing the Spirit does is make them speak. The Church is born speaking, born on mission. The disciples who had hidden behind locked doors now go out into the street. Mary is among them; the Spirit who overshadowed her at Nazareth to form the body of Christ now comes down to form his Body, the Church.

The crowd that gathers is made of “devout Jews from every nation under heaven living in Jerusalem”, and each one hears the apostles “speaking in the native language of each.” Then Luke lists the nations, and we Syro-Malabar Christians should listen closely: “Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and residents of Mesopotamia”. These are the lands of the East where the Gospel soon took root, the lands of the Church of the East, whose faith, liturgy and Syriac language came to the Thomas Christians of Malabar. At Pentecost our own spiritual ancestors were, in a sense, already standing in the crowd. At Babel human pride had confused the languages and scattered the peoples (Genesis 11:9); at Pentecost the Spirit does not abolish the languages but makes the one Gospel heard in each of them. That is why the Church can pray in Syriac and Malayalam, in Latin and Tamil, in English and Hindi, and still be one. The reaction is mixed: “All were amazed and perplexed, saying to one another, ‘What does this mean?’ But others sneered and said, ‘They are filled with new wine.’” The Spirit's work always provokes both wonder and mockery. Joel had promised, and Peter will soon proclaim, that God would pour out his Spirit on all flesh (Acts 2:16–17). It has happened.

Paul tells the Corinthians how to recognise the Spirit. Not by spectacle, but by a confession: “no one can say ‘Jesus is Lord’ except by the Holy Spirit.” Every time a child in our homes lisps the name of Jesus, every time a dying person whispers, Eeshoye, the Spirit is at work. Then Paul describes the Church as a body with many gifts: “Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of services, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of activities, but it is the same God who activates all of them in everyone.” Notice the Holy Trinity in those three lines: Spirit, Lord, God. The gifts differ: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, prophecy, discernment, tongues. But the purpose is one: “To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good.” No gift is for showing off, and no one is without a gift. The catechist who teaches on Sunday morning, the choir member, the parent who prays with the children, the youth who visits the sick, the elderly woman who prays the rosary for the parish: all of these “are activated by one and the same Spirit, who allots to each one individually just as the Spirit chooses.” We received the seal of this Spirit in our Thailabhishekam, the anointing with holy Myron given in our Church together with Baptism.

In the Gospel Jesus prepares his disciples for this day. They are sad that he is leaving, and he says something astonishing: “it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you.” The Ascension was not a loss; it opened the way for the gift. The Spirit will “prove the world wrong about sin and righteousness and judgment”: he shows the world its sin of unbelief, reveals the righteousness of Christ who returns to the Father, and announces that “the ruler of this world has been condemned.” And for the disciples: “When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth”. He does not invent a new Gospel: “he will take what is mine and declare it to you. All that the Father has is mine.” Here, on the threshold of next Sunday's feast of the Holy Trinity, we glimpse the life of God: the Father who gives all to the Son, the Son who sends the Spirit, the Spirit who glorifies the Son. Today the Season of the Apostles begins, the seven weeks in which our Church contemplates how the Spirit sent the apostles to the ends of the earth, and how one of them, Mar Thoma, came to our shores. The Spirit who came down in the upper room is the same Spirit who keeps the faith alive in Kerala homes and parishes. Come, Holy Spirit, fill the hearts of your faithful.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, today is Pentecost, the fiftieth day, the Feast of Our Lord that crowns the Season of the Resurrection and opens the Season of the Apostles. Pentecost was already a Jewish feast, the harvest festival, linked by tradition with the giving of the Law on Sinai. So our first reading takes us to Sinai, where God told Israel: “you shall be for me a priestly kingdom and a holy nation” (Exodus 19:6).

At Sinai God came in cloud and fire and gave his Law on stone. In the upper room he comes in wind and fire and writes his law of love on hearts. “All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit” (Acts 2:4). A tongue of fire rested on each one. Mary was there with them. The Spirit who formed Christ in her womb now forms his Body, the Church.

The first thing the Spirit does is make the apostles speak. Frightened men go out into the street. And people from many nations hear them each “in the native language of each” (Acts 2:6): Parthians, Medes, Elamites and residents of Mesopotamia. Those are the lands of the Church of the East, from which our own liturgy and our Syriac heritage came. Babel scattered the peoples; Pentecost gathers them into one Church with many tongues.

Paul says: “no one can say ‘Jesus is Lord’ except by the Holy Spirit” (1 Corinthians 12:3). There are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit, and each gift is given “for the common good” (12:7). No one in this church today is without a gift. We received the Spirit's seal in our Baptism and Thailabhishekam. The question is whether we use our gift for others.

Jesus told the sad disciples: “it is to your advantage that I go away” (John 16:7). He went to the Father so that the Advocate could come. “When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth” (16:13). The Spirit takes what belongs to Christ and gives it to us; and next Sunday we shall celebrate the Holy Trinity whom he reveals.

Today the Season of the Apostles begins. The Spirit who sent the apostles to the ends of the earth brought Mar Thoma to our shores. Let us not keep the fire to ourselves. Let us say every day, Come, Holy Spirit, and let us go out, each with our gift, to tell the world that Jesus is Lord. Alleluia! Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, on the night before he died Jesus saw that his disciples were full of sorrow. He told them: it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you (John 16:7). Today, at Pentecost, the Advocate has come.

The word Advocate means one who is called to stand beside you, a defender, a counsellor. Isaiah foretold that the spirit of the LORD would rest on the Messiah: the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might (Isaiah 11:2). That same Spirit is now given to the Church and to each of us. We are never alone.

But Jesus also said: when he comes, he will prove the world wrong about sin and righteousness and judgment (John 16:8). The Spirit shows us our sin, not to crush us but to heal us. On Easter evening the risen Lord breathed on the apostles and said: receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them (John 20:22–23). Every confession is a little Pentecost. God promised through Ezekiel: a new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you (Ezekiel 36:26).

And Jesus said of the Spirit: he will glorify me, because he will take what is mine and declare it to you (John 16:14). The Spirit does not draw attention to himself. He makes Jesus known. Joel had promised: I will pour out my spirit on all flesh (Joel 2:28). Today that promise is kept, and every baptised person is called to make Christ known.

So let us pray each morning: Come, Holy Spirit, stand beside me today. Let us make a good confession in this Season of the Apostles. And let us ask the Spirit to give us the courage to speak of Christ, and to receive him with burning hearts at the Holy Qurbana. Alleluia! Amen.

Dear friends, before the great day of Pentecost, the apostles did not organise a campaign. They prayed. Luke says: all these were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14). And when the day came, they were all together in one place (Acts 2:1).

The Psalm says: how very good and pleasant it is when kindred live together in unity (Psalm 133:1). The Spirit came upon a Church that was united and praying. If our families and parishes want the fire of the Spirit, we must first be together, forgiving one another and praying with one heart.

Mary was there. Long before the apostles, the Spirit had come upon her. At Nazareth the angel had said to her: the Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you (Luke 1:35). The Spirit who formed Christ in her womb now forms the Body of Christ, the Church. That is why we call her Mother of the Church. Where Mary prays, the Spirit is near.

At Mount Carmel, when Elijah prayed, the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the offering (1 Kings 18:38). Paul speaks of an offering made acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit (Romans 15:16). In every Holy Qurbana our Church calls upon the Holy Spirit to come down upon the bread and wine. The fire of Pentecost falls again on our altar, the gifts become the Body and Blood of Christ, and we who receive them are made one.

So in this Season of the Apostles let us make peace with someone before next Sunday. Let us pray the Rosary, asking Mary to obtain the Spirit for our families. And at the next Holy Qurbana, when the Spirit is invoked, let us bow our hearts and say: Come, Holy Spirit. Alleluia! Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Pentecost opens the Season of the Apostles, and its four readings show how God makes a people his own. At Sinai, in the third month after the Exodus, God offers Israel a covenant: they will be his treasured possession, a priestly kingdom and a holy nation. In Jerusalem, on the feast that remembered the harvest and, for many Jews, the gift of the Law, the Spirit comes with wind and fire, and the apostles speak so that every nation hears in its own language. Paul tells the Corinthians that the one Spirit gives many gifts for the common good. In John, Jesus promises that the Spirit of truth will guide his disciples into all the truth. The words to watch are covenant, fire and truth. What began at Sinai with one people is opened to all.

Exodus 19:1–9 · A priestly kingdom and a holy nation

Setting.

Israel has crossed the sea, been fed with manna and given water from the rock. Now, in the third month, they reach the mountain where God first spoke to Moses from the bush (3:12). Here the covenant will be made and the Law given (chapters 19–24).

Later Jewish tradition came to celebrate the giving of the Law at Sinai on the Feast of Weeks, the harvest festival kept fifty days after Passover. That is why this reading stands beside Acts 2 today: the Church’s Pentecost is the new Sinai, where the law is written not on stone but on hearts (Jeremiah 31:33).

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: arrival at Sinai, in the third month; Israel camps before the mountain.
  • vv. 3–4: God calls Moses: “I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to myself.”
  • vv. 5–6: the offer: if you keep my covenant, you shall be my treasured possession, a priestly kingdom, a holy nation.
  • vv. 7–8: Moses sets the words before the elders; the people answer as one.
  • v. 9: God will come in a dense cloud, so that the people may hear and believe.

Key words.

  • sĕgullâ, treasured possession: A king’s private treasure, the jewels he keeps for himself. The whole earth is God’s, but Israel is his own jewel, chosen not to be proud but to serve.
  • mamleket kōhănîm, priestly kingdom: A people set between God and the nations, as priests stand between God and the people. Israel’s calling is for the sake of the world.
  • gôy qādôš, holy nation: Holy means set apart for God. Israel is to be different from the nations so as to show them who God is.

Verse by verse.

“You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to myself.” (19:4)

The covenant begins with grace, not with a command. God first reminds Israel of what he has done; only then does he ask for their answer. The goal of the Exodus is not a place but a person: “to myself”.

“you shall be my treasured possession out of all the peoples. Indeed, the whole earth is mine” (19:5)

Election is not favouritism. The God of the whole earth chooses one people so that through them he may bless all (Genesis 12:3).

“but you shall be for me a priestly kingdom and a holy nation.” (19:6)

St Peter applies these words to the baptized: “you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God’s own people” (1 Peter 2:9). The Church does not take Israel’s place; she is brought into Israel’s calling.

“The people all answered as one: “Everything that the Lord has spoken we will do.”” (19:8)

A single voice, a single answer. At Pentecost the many languages will again make one confession of faith.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the chosen people was constituted by God as “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation”, and that the whole Church, the new people of God, shares in Christ’s priesthood (CCC 1539, 1546). The Second Vatican Council quotes 1 Peter 2:9 when it teaches the common priesthood of the faithful, by which the baptized offer spiritual sacrifices and share in offering the Eucharist (Lumen Gentium 10). Many Fathers saw in Sinai, with its fire, cloud and voice, a figure of Pentecost: there the Law was given on tablets of stone; here the Spirit writes it in hearts (in substance, St Augustine, On the Spirit and the Letter 17).

Acts 2:1–13 · Tongues as of fire

Setting.

The disciples have been waiting in Jerusalem, as the Risen Lord told them, for the promise of the Father (1:4–5). The Twelve are complete again (1:26). Now comes the Feast of Weeks, called in Greek Pentecost, one of the three pilgrim feasts when Jews came from far and wide to the Temple. That is why the city is full of “devout Jews from every nation under heaven”.

Luke tells the event with signs from Israel’s Scriptures. Wind and fire recall Sinai, where the Lord descended in fire and the whole mountain shook (Exodus 19:18). The many languages recall Babel, where human pride ended in confusion and scattering (Genesis 11:1–9).

Structure.

  • v. 1: the day of Pentecost; all together in one place.
  • vv. 2–3: a sound like wind; tongues as of fire resting on each.
  • v. 4: filled with the Holy Spirit, they speak in other languages.
  • vv. 5–11: the crowd from every nation hears God’s deeds of power in its own language.
  • vv. 12–13: amazement and mockery: “What does this mean?” “They are filled with new wine.”

Key words.

  • symplērousthai, had come: Literally “was being fulfilled”. Luke uses the word for the coming of a promised time (as in Luke 9:51). The feast itself reaches its fulfilment.
  • pnoē, pneuma, wind, Spirit: Luke uses pnoē, a blast of wind, beside pneuma, Spirit. In Hebrew one word, rûaḥ, means both wind and spirit, as in the breath that moved over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2).
  • dialektos, native language: The word of verses 6 and 8, the language one learned at home. The Gospel does not erase the languages and cultures of the nations; it speaks in each of them.

Verse by verse.

“When the day of Pentecost had come, they were all together in one place.” (2:1)

“All together”: the one hundred twenty, with the apostles and Mary (1:14–15). The Spirit comes to a community at prayer.

“Divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them.” (2:3)

One fire, divided so that each receives it. The Spirit is given to the Church and to each member personally.

“Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and residents of Mesopotamia” (2:9)

The list begins in the east, beyond the Roman Empire, in the lands where the Church of the East would grow. Our own Church’s tradition traces its family to these very regions.

“in our own languages we hear them speaking about God’s deeds of power.” (2:11)

The content of their speech is praise: the mighty works of God. Babel tried to make a name for itself (Genesis 11:4); Pentecost proclaims the name of God.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that on the day of Pentecost, when the seven weeks of Easter had come to an end, Christ’s Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, and that on that day the Holy Trinity is fully revealed (CCC 731–732). The Church, it says, was made manifest to the world on the day of Pentecost by the outpouring of the Spirit (CCC 1076). St Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching on Pentecost, contrasts the confusion of tongues at Babel, which scattered the proud, with the gift of tongues at Pentecost, which gathers all into one (in substance, Oration 41).

1 Corinthians 12:1–11 · The same Spirit

Setting.

The Corinthians were rich in spiritual gifts and proud of them. Some prized speaking in tongues above all and looked down on those without it (see chapters 12–14). Paul does not reject the gifts; he puts them in order. They come from one Spirit, they serve the one body, and the greatest of all, as chapter 13 will show, is love.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: the test of the Spirit: no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit.
  • vv. 4–6: varieties of gifts, services and activities; the same Spirit, the same Lord, the same God.
  • v. 7: each gift is a manifestation of the Spirit for the common good.
  • vv. 8–10: a list of nine gifts.
  • v. 11: one Spirit gives to each as he chooses.

Key words.

  • charismata, gifts: Gifts of grace, from charis, grace. A charism is not a reward for holiness but a gift for service.
  • Kyrios Iēsous, Jesus is Lord: Perhaps the oldest Christian creed, spoken at baptism (Romans 10:9). In the Greek Old Testament Kyrios stands for the divine name. To call Jesus Lord is to confess him as God.
  • to sympheron, the common good: What benefits all together. A gift kept for oneself has missed its purpose.

Verse by verse.

“no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit.” (12:3)

Faith itself is the first gift of the Spirit. Every Christian who confesses Christ already shows that the Spirit is at work.

“Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of services, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of activities, but it is the same God” (12:4–6)

Spirit, Lord, God: Paul’s pattern is already Trinitarian. The unity of the Church reflects the unity of the one God in three persons.

“To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good.” (12:7)

“To each”: no one in the Church is without a gift, and no gift is for private glory.

“All these are activated by one and the same Spirit, who allots to each one individually just as the Spirit chooses.” (12:11)

The Spirit chooses, as the Lord chose Matthias. We do not choose our gifts; we receive them and put them to use.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that charisms, whether extraordinary or simple and humble, are graces of the Holy Spirit which directly or indirectly benefit the Church, ordered to her building up, to the good of all and to the needs of the world (CCC 799). Charity is the true measure of them (CCC 800), and discernment of charisms belongs to those who lead the Church, who must not extinguish the Spirit but test everything (CCC 801; Lumen Gentium 12).

John 16:5–15 · He will guide you into all the truth

Setting.

These words are spoken at the Last Supper, on the night before Jesus died (John 13–17). Jesus is preparing his friends for his departure. They are full of sorrow. He tells them that his going is for their good, because only then will the Advocate come. Here come the last two of the five sayings about the Spirit in the Supper discourse (14:16–17; 14:26; 15:26; 16:7–11; 16:13–15).

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: sorrow at his going; it is to your advantage, for the Advocate will come.
  • vv. 8–11: the Spirit will prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment.
  • vv. 12–13: the Spirit of truth will guide you into all the truth.
  • vv. 14–15: he will glorify me; all that the Father has is mine.

Key words.

  • paraklētos, Advocate: One called to stand beside another: a defender in court, a helper, a comforter. Jesus has been their Advocate; he will send “another” (14:16).
  • elenchō, prove wrong, convict: A word from the law court: to expose, to bring to the light. The Spirit reverses the world’s verdict on Jesus. The one it condemned is shown to be righteous.
  • hodēgeō, guide: To lead along a road, as a guide leads travellers. The Spirit does not give a new Gospel; he leads the disciples deeper into the truth that is Christ (14:6).

Verse by verse.

“Nevertheless I tell you the truth: it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Advocate will not come to you” (16:7)

The disciples wanted Jesus to stay as he was. He gives them something greater: his presence within them through the Spirit.

“about sin, because they do not believe in me; about righteousness, because I am going to the Father” (16:9–10)

The deepest sin is unbelief, refusing the light. Jesus’ going to the Father, through the Cross and Resurrection, is the Father’s verdict that he is righteous.

“When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth; for he will not speak on his own, but will speak whatever he hears” (16:13)

The Spirit speaks what he hears, as the Son speaks what he hears from the Father (8:26). The Trinity is a communion of listening and giving.

“All that the Father has is mine. For this reason I said that he will take what is mine and declare it to you.” (16:15)

The Spirit’s work is to make Christ known. Wherever the Spirit is truly at work, Christ is glorified.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism gathers these promises: the Spirit of truth, the other Paraclete, will teach the disciples everything, remind them of all that Christ said, bear witness to him, lead them into all truth, glorify Christ, and prove the world wrong about sin, righteousness and judgment (CCC 729). Pope St John Paul II devoted a large part of his encyclical on the Holy Spirit, Dominum et Vivificantem (1986), to the words “he will convince the world concerning sin”. The Second Vatican Council teaches that through the Spirit the understanding of the apostolic Tradition grows in the Church, as she advances towards the fullness of divine truth (Dei Verbum 8).

Questions people ask

Did the apostles speak foreign languages, or did the crowd hear a miracle?

Luke stresses both: they “began to speak in other languages” (2:4), and each one “heard them speaking in the native language of each” (2:6). Whether the wonder was in the speaking or in the hearing, the sign is the same: the Gospel is for every people, in its own tongue. This is different from the gift of tongues at Corinth, which needed an interpreter (1 Corinthians 14:27).

Does the Spirit bring new revelations beyond Christ?

No. Jesus says the Spirit “will take what is mine and declare it to you” (16:14). The Church teaches that the Christian economy will never pass away and that no new public revelation is to be expected before the glorious coming of Christ (Dei Verbum 4; CCC 66). The Spirit leads the Church deeper into what Christ has revealed, so that its meaning is grasped more fully over the centuries.

Why is Pentecost linked to Sinai?

In Exodus, Israel reaches Sinai in the third month after leaving Egypt, about the time of the harvest festival. Later Jewish tradition remembered the giving of the Law on the Feast of Weeks. Luke tells the coming of the Spirit with signs of Sinai: a sound from heaven, fire, a people gathered. For Christians, the Spirit fulfils what Jeremiah promised, a new covenant written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

With Pentecost the Syro-Malabar Church begins the Season of the Apostles (Shleeha), which remembers the Church’s first mission under the Spirit. It suits our Church that the list of nations at Pentecost begins with “Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and residents of Mesopotamia”: the Church of the East grew in those lands, and from that Church our ancestors in Malabar received their liturgy and their bishops for many centuries, while they kept the faith first preached, by our tradition, by Mar Thoma Shleeha, who was in the upper room (Acts 1:13).

At every Holy Qurbana, in the Anaphora of Mar Addai and Mar Mari, the priest asks that the Holy Spirit come and rest upon the offering, bless it and sanctify it, for the pardon of sins and the hope of resurrection. Pentecost is not only a memory; it happens again on our altars and in us. This is a good day to renew our confirmation, the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit, and to ask what gift the Spirit has given each of us for the common good.

For study and teaching

  • God reminded Israel, “I bore you on eagles’ wings,” before asking for obedience. What has God done for me that I need to remember before I answer him?
  • At Pentecost each one heard the Gospel in his own language. How can my family and parish speak the faith in the language of young people, the poor and those far away?
  • Paul says each one is given a gift for the common good. What gift has the Spirit given me, and how am I using it?
  • Jesus said his going away was for his disciples’ good. Where in my life has a loss opened the way for the Spirit?
