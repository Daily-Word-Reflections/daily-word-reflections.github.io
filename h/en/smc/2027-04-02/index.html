Word for the day

“Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven.” Matthew 10:32

Reflection

On the first Friday after Easter the Church of the East keeps the feast of all the saints, and above all of the martyrs. The placing is deliberate. The martyrs are those who went with Christ through death into life, and so their feast belongs in the week of the Resurrection, not in the gloom of a funeral. The East Syriac tradition explains the day by its own history. In the fourth century the Persian king Shapur II launched a long persecution against the Christians of his empire. Mar Shemun bar Sabbae, Catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, refused to betray his flock or to worship the sun, and by tradition he was put to death on Good Friday, about the year 341 or 344, together with a great company of bishops, priests and faithful. Since Good Friday could not become a feast, the martyrs came to be remembered on the Friday after Easter: so the tradition tells it. The Church of St Thomas in India received this calendar and this memory, and the Syro-Malabar Church still keeps the day as the Feast of Martyrs. The witnesses have not stopped. Blessed Rani Maria Vattalil, a Franciscan Clarist sister from Pulluvazhy in Kerala, was stabbed to death on a bus near Indore in 1995, killed in hatred of the faith, stirred up by her work among the poor, and was beatified in 2017. Her family later forgave the man who killed her, and he came to their home to ask their pardon. A martyr's blood, and a martyr's family's forgiveness, preach the Resurrection more loudly than any sermon.

The first reading takes us into a furnace. Three young Jews in Babylon, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, have refused to worship the golden statue. Before they are thrown into the fire they give the king an answer that every martyr has repeated: “If our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the furnace of blazing fire and out of your hand, O king, let him deliver us. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods and we will not worship the golden statue that you have set up.” (Daniel 3:17–18). Then the astonishing scene: “They walked around in the midst of the flames, singing hymns to God and blessing the Lord.” Azariah stands still in the fire and prays, and his prayer is not a complaint but a confession: “Blessed are you, O Lord, God of our ancestors, and worthy of praise; and glorious is your name forever! For you are just in all you have done.” He does not accuse God of injustice; he accepts that his people have sinned and still trusts in God's mercy. The king, looking in, sees four men walking unbound in the fire, and the fourth looks like a son of the gods. Many Fathers saw in that fourth figure the Son of God, who does not take his witnesses out of the fire but walks with them in it. This is how the martyrs have died: singing, forgiving, and not alone.

In Acts we meet the first Christian martyr. “Stephen, full of grace and power, did great wonders and signs among the people.” Some members of one synagogue in Jerusalem argue with him, “But they could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke.” False witnesses are found to accuse him of speaking against the Temple and the law. Stephen was himself a Jew, as were the apostles and the whole first community; the quarrel was within one family, and the Church, following the Second Vatican Council (Nostra Aetate 4), never reads such passages as a charge against the Jewish people. What Luke wants us to see is the face of the witness: “And all who sat in the council looked intently at him, and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.” Like Moses coming down from Sinai, Stephen's face shines because he has been with God. Soon he will see heaven opened, pray for his killers and die (Acts 7:56–60). The Letter to the Hebrews then opens the great roll of the witnesses of faith. “By faith Abel offered to God a more acceptable sacrifice than Cain's... he died, but through his faith he still speaks.” By faith Noah built the ark. “By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to set out for a place that he was to receive as an inheritance; and he set out, not knowing where he was going.” These men and women did not see the promise fulfilled, “For he looked forward to the city that has foundations, whose architect and builder is God.” The martyrs are the ones who believed the city was real enough to die for.

The Gospel is Jesus' instruction to witnesses. Three times he says, Do not be afraid. “So have no fear of them; for nothing is covered up that will not be uncovered.” “Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul.” “So do not be afraid; you are of more value than many sparrows.” The reason is the tenderness of the Father: “Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father. And even the hairs of your head are all counted.” Then the promise and the warning: “Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven; but whoever denies me before others, I also will deny before my Father in heaven.” The Syriac word for martyr, sahda, like the Greek martys, means simply a witness. Few of us will be asked to die for Christ, but all of us are asked to acknowledge him: at the office table, in the hostel, in the family group chat, in the way we do business and the way we speak of the poor. Today is also the First Friday, the day many families give to the Sacred Heart. The Heart that was pierced on the Cross and rose glorified is the source of every martyr's courage. To keep this feast well is to ask for a little of that courage, and to make one small act of acknowledgement before the day ends.

Full homily · Homily of the day

Dear friends, on the first Friday after Easter the Church of the East remembers all the saints and above all the martyrs. They belong in Easter week, because they went with Christ through death into life. Our tradition remembers Mar Shemun bar Sabbae and the martyrs of Persia, killed, the story says, on a Good Friday long ago. And our own Church has given witnesses in our own time, like Blessed Rani Maria, whose family forgave the man who killed her.

In Daniel three young men face the furnace and say: our God is able to save us; but if not, we will still not bow down. That ‘but if not’ is the heart of faith. We do not love God only for what he gives. And in the fire they walk and sing, and a fourth man walks with them. Christ does not always take us out of the fire, but he never leaves us alone in it.

Stephen, full of grace and power, is dragged before the council on false charges. Stephen was a Jew, like the apostles; this is not a story against any people. It is the story of a witness whose face shines like an angel's, who sees heaven open and prays for those who stone him. He died as his Master died: forgiving.

Hebrews reminds us of the long line of witnesses. Abel, of whom it is written, “he died, but through his faith he still speaks.” Abraham, who “set out, not knowing where he was going.” They lived for a city they did not yet see. So did the martyrs. So must we.

Jesus says three times: Do not be afraid. Your Father sees every sparrow, and he has counted the hairs of your head. And then: “Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven.” Most of us will not be asked to die. But every day we are asked to acknowledge him: at work, in college, in the family chat, in how we treat the poor.

Today is also First Friday. Let us go to the Heart of Jesus, pierced and risen, and ask for a little of the martyrs' courage. And before tonight let us make one small act of witness. Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

Dear brothers and sisters, in the joy of Easter our Church keeps the feast of the martyrs and of all the saints. Why now? Because the saints are the fruit of Easter. Christ's victory over death is seen first in those who followed him through death into life.

The Book of Wisdom says: the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them (Wisdom 3:1). Hebrews tells us that we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses (Hebrews 12:1). We do not run the race alone. The saints are not far from us. They are our elder brothers and sisters, cheering us on and praying for us.

Sirach says: let us now sing the praises of famous men, our ancestors in their generations (Sirach 44:1). Our Church has its own family of saints: St Alphonsa, St Kuriakose Elias Chavara, St Euphrasia, St Mariam Thresia, Blessed Rani Maria and many others. Hebrews says: remember your leaders, those who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith (Hebrews 13:7).

But this feast is not only about them. God said to Israel: you shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy (Leviticus 19:2). Paul says God chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love (Ephesians 1:4). Holiness is not for a few special people. It is the vocation of every baptised person, in the kitchen, the office and the field.

So let us thank God today for the saints, especially the martyrs. Let us learn the life of one saint this season. And let us take one practical step toward holiness, so that one day we may join them in the great feast of heaven, of which every Holy Qurbana is a foretaste. Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

Dear friends, in today's reading Stephen, full of grace and power, is accused by false witnesses who say he speaks against the holy place and the law (Acts 6:13). The Psalm had prayed: false witnesses have risen against me (Psalm 27:12). The world has often answered the truth with lies.

The word martyr means witness. Stephen bore witness not only with his words but with his face: all who sat in the council saw that his face was like the face of an angel (Acts 6:15). Later, as they were stoning him, he knelt down and cried out: Lord, do not hold this sin against them (Acts 7:60).

In the Old Testament, the priest Zechariah was also stoned in the court of the Lord's house, and as he died he said: may the LORD see and avenge! (2 Chronicles 24:22). Stephen prays differently, because he has learned from Jesus on the Cross. The martyr's greatest victory is not to die bravely, but to die forgiving.

And here is the surprise. The witnesses laid their coats at the feet of a young man named Saul, and Saul approved of their killing him (Acts 7:58; 8:1). Stephen's prayer was answered in that very man, who became the Apostle Paul. Jesus had said: unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24). That day the Church was scattered, and the word spread everywhere.

Today many Christians still suffer for their faith. Let us pray for them and help them. Let us refuse to join in false accusations. And let us pray every day this week for someone who has hurt us, as Stephen did. Then we too will be witnesses of the risen Lord. Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the Friday of the week of the Resurrection the Church of the East keeps the feast of all the martyrs. The day joins the Lord's victory over death to the courage of those who died for him. Azariah blesses God from inside the furnace. Stephen stands before the council with a face like an angel's. Hebrews calls up the ancestors who “received approval” by faith, and the Greek word there comes from the same root as martyr. In the Gospel Jesus tells his disciples three times not to be afraid and promises to acknowledge before his Father those who acknowledge him. The key words are witness, fear not and acknowledge.

Daniel 3:25–31 · Blessed in the furnace

Setting.

King Nebuchadnezzar has set up a golden statue and commanded all to worship it. Three young Jews, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, refuse. Even if God does not rescue them, they say, they will not serve the king's gods (3:18). They are bound and thrown into the furnace (3:23).

At this point Catholic Bibles contain a long passage, numbered 3:24–90, that is found in the ancient Greek translations of Daniel but not in the Aramaic text: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men. The Catholic and Orthodox Churches receive it as inspired Scripture; many Protestant Bibles place it among the Apocrypha. Azariah is the Hebrew name of the one the Babylonians called Abednego (1:7).

The prayer is spoken in the fire, but it is not a cry for personal rescue. It is a confession for the whole people, like the prayers in Daniel 9 and Baruch 1–3: God is just, and Israel has sinned.

Structure.

  • v. 25: Azariah stands in the fire and prays aloud.
  • v. 26: the opening blessing of God and his name.
  • vv. 27–28: all God's works and judgments are true.
  • vv. 29–30: the confession: we have sinned and not obeyed.
  • v. 31: all that has come upon us was a true judgment. The prayer goes on to plead for mercy (vv. 32–45), and the angel of the Lord comes down into the furnace (vv. 49–50).

Key words.

  • ‘Ăzaryāh, Azariah: The name means “the Lord has helped”. The Babylonians gave him a new name, but in the furnace he prays under the name his parents gave him, the name that confesses Israel's God.
  • eulogētos, blessed: The prayer opens with the Jewish form of blessing: “Blessed are you, O Lord”. To bless God is to give him thanks and praise for who he is. Azariah does this before there is any sign of help.
  • “God of our ancestors”, the God of the fathers: Azariah prays to the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. In exile, with no Temple, no king and no sacrifice, the memory of the ancestors and their covenant is what holds the people together.

Verse by verse.

“Then Azariah stood still in the fire and prayed aloud:” (3:25)

Fire is the last place one expects to hear a prayer of praise. Early Christian writers, St Cyprian among them, held up the three young men as models for those facing martyrdom (in substance).

“Blessed are you, O Lord, God of our ancestors, and worthy of praise; and glorious is your name forever!” (3:26)
“For you are just in all you have done; all your works are true and your ways right,” (3:27)

Azariah does not accuse God. Suffering has not made him doubt God's justice; it has made him look at his people's sins.

“For we have sinned and broken your law in turning away from you;” (3:29)

The three young men had not bowed to the statue, yet Azariah says “we”. The righteous do not separate themselves from the people when they pray.

“So all that you have brought upon us, and all that you have done to us, you have done by a true judgment.” (3:31)

This humble confession prepares for the plea that follows: that a contrite heart and a humble spirit may be accepted in place of the Temple sacrifices (3:39).

How the Church reads it.

Hebrews 11:34 praises those who “quenched raging fire”, and the Church has always counted the three young men among the witnesses of faith. Some of the earliest Christian paintings in the catacombs of Rome show them standing unharmed in the furnace with arms raised in prayer. Many early Christian writers saw in the fourth figure in the fire (3:92) a sign of the Son of God present with those who suffer for him (in substance). The Song of the Three that follows the prayer, with its call to all creation to bless the Lord, is sung in the morning prayer of many Churches to this day.

Acts 6:8–15 · A face like an angel's

Setting.

Stephen was the first named among the seven chosen to serve (6:5). Now Luke shows him doing more than serving tables: he works signs and argues with wisdom. His opponents come from a Greek-speaking synagogue (or synagogues) in Jerusalem made up of Jews from abroad: from Cyrene and Alexandria in Africa, and from Cilicia and Asia in Asia Minor. Saul of Tarsus came from Cilicia, and he will appear at Stephen's death (7:58).

Luke tells Stephen's trial so that it echoes the trial of Jesus: false witnesses, a charge about the Temple, the council of the elders. Stephen is Jewish, as are his accusers, his judges and the first believers. The conflict is among Jews about Jesus, and it is not a story of “the Jews” against Christians.

Structure.

  • v. 8: Stephen full of grace and power.
  • vv. 9–10: debate; no one can withstand his wisdom.
  • vv. 11–12: men are put up to accuse him; he is seized and brought to the council.
  • vv. 13–14: false witnesses: the Temple and the law.
  • v. 15: his face like an angel's.

Key words.

  • charis kai dynamis, grace and power: Stephen is “full of grace and power”, just as he was “full of faith and the Holy Spirit” (6:5). Luke shows that the gifts of the risen Lord fill the servant as much as the apostles.
  • synedrion, council: The supreme council in Jerusalem, made up of chief priests, elders and scribes. Jesus stood before the same council (Luke 22:66).
  • martys, witness: Paul later calls Stephen “your witness” (22:20). The Greek word for witness came to mean one who bears witness with his blood, a martyr. Stephen is honoured as the first martyr, the protomartyr.

Verse by verse.

“Stephen, full of grace and power, did great wonders and signs among the people.” (6:8)
“But they could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke.” (6:10)

Jesus had promised this: “I will give you words and a wisdom that none of your opponents will be able to withstand” (Luke 21:15).

“They set up false witnesses who said, “This man never stops saying things against this holy place and the law;” (6:13)

The words echo the false witness against Jesus about destroying the Temple (Mark 14:57–58). Luke wants us to see the disciple walking the road of his Master.

“And all who sat in the council looked intently at him, and they saw that his face was like the face of an angel.” (6:15)

Stephen is accused of speaking against Moses, and his face shines as Moses' face shone when he came down from the mountain (Exodus 34:29–35). The accused man carries the very glory he is said to despise.

How the Church reads it.

Stephen's death in the next chapter completes the likeness to Christ: he sees the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God (7:56), commends his spirit to the Lord Jesus (7:59) and prays for those who stone him (7:60). The Church has always honoured him as the first martyr. In him she sees what a martyr is: one who is not only killed for Christ but who forgives as Christ forgave.

Hebrews 11:3–10 · By faith they received approval

Setting.

The Letter to the Hebrews is a long sermon to Christians who were growing weary under pressure and loss (10:32–39). Its author is not named. The ancient Church debated who wrote it; Origen said that only God knows who wrote it (in substance, as Eusebius reports). The Church receives it as inspired Scripture.

Chapter 11 opens with a description of faith (11:1) and then walks through the great figures of Israel's story, each introduced by the words “by faith”. The list ends with those who suffered, were tortured and killed (11:35–38), and the next chapter calls them “so great a cloud of witnesses” (12:1).

Structure.

  • v. 3: faith understands creation by the word of God.
  • v. 4: Abel.
  • vv. 5–6: Enoch; without faith it is impossible to please God.
  • v. 7: Noah.
  • vv. 8–10: Abraham the traveller, looking for the city whose builder is God.

Key words.

  • pistis, faith: In Hebrews faith is trust that holds on to what is promised and not yet seen. It looks forward, and it acts: Abel offers, Noah builds, Abraham sets out.
  • emartyrēthē, received approval: Literally “was witnessed to”. The verb comes from the same root as martys, witness. God bears witness to those who trust him (vv. 2, 4, 5). On the feast of martyrs we see the two sides: they bore witness to God, and God bears witness to them.
  • themelioi, foundations: Abraham lived in tents, which have no foundations. He looked for a city that has them (v. 10). The life of faith is lived in tents while it waits for the lasting city.

Verse by verse.

“By faith we understand that the worlds were prepared by the word of God,” (11:3)

The list begins before Abel, with creation itself. To believe in the Creator is the first act of faith.

“he died, but through his faith he still speaks.” (11:4)

Abel is the first righteous man to die at the hands of another. Jesus names him first among the innocent whose blood was shed (Matthew 23:35). His death was not the end of his witness.

“And without faith it is impossible to please God, for whoever would approach him must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him.” (11:6)
“and he set out, not knowing where he was going.” (11:8)

Abraham's faith is a road, not a map. He obeys first and learns the way as he walks.

“For he looked forward to the city that has foundations, whose architect and builder is God.” (11:10)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism points to this chapter as the great eulogy of the faith of Israel's ancestors, laying special stress on Abraham (CCC 145). It quotes verse 6 when it teaches that faith is necessary for salvation (CCC 161). The martyrs stand at the end of this long line. Their faith, like Abraham's, set out without seeing the end of the road, and they now belong to the cloud of witnesses who surround the Church as she runs her race (12:1).

Matthew 10:26–33 · Do not be afraid

Setting.

This passage comes from the missionary discourse of Matthew 10. Jesus has just warned the Twelve that they will be handed over to councils, flogged and hated (10:17–22), and that the disciple will be treated as the teacher was (10:24–25). Now he tells them three times not to fear (vv. 26, 28, 31). Matthew wrote for a Church that already knew persecution.

Structure.

  • vv. 26–27: what is hidden will be revealed; proclaim it from the housetops.
  • v. 28: whom to fear and whom not to fear.
  • vv. 29–31: the sparrows and the hairs of your head.
  • vv. 32–33: acknowledging and denying.

Key words.

  • homologeō, acknowledge, confess: To declare openly that one belongs to Jesus. The Church calls those who suffered for this public confession confessors, and those who died for it martyrs.
  • assarion, penny: A small copper coin. Luke has five sparrows sold for two pennies (Luke 12:6). The cheapest thing in the market does not fall without the Father.
  • geenna, hell: From the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, once a place of terrible idolatry (Jeremiah 7:31). In the Gospels it names final loss. Jesus speaks of it not to frighten his disciples into silence, but so that they will fear nothing else.

Verse by verse.

“So have no fear of them; for nothing is covered up that will not be uncovered,” (10:26)
“Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell.” (10:28)

The one to fear is God, the judge, not the persecutor. The persecutor's power ends at the body; God holds the whole person. Fear of God, rightly understood, sets a person above every other fear.

“Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father.” (10:29)

Jesus does not promise that the sparrow will not fall. He promises that it will not fall without the Father.

“And even the hairs of your head are all counted.” (10:30)
“Everyone therefore who acknowledges me before others, I also will acknowledge before my Father in heaven;” (10:32)

The martyr's confession on earth is answered by Christ's confession of him in heaven.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls martyrdom “the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith”, a witness even unto death (CCC 2473). It recalls that the Church has carefully gathered the records of those who persevered to the end, and quotes St Ignatius of Antioch on his way to death in Rome: it is better for him to die in Christ Jesus than to reign over the ends of the earth (CCC 2474). Tertullian wrote that the blood of Christians is a seed (Apology 50, in substance): the more they were cut down, the more they grew.

Questions people ask

Why is the feast of the martyrs kept in the week of the Resurrection?

The martyrs share most closely in the death of Christ, and so they share in his victory. In the Church of the East this Friday recalls above all the great persecution in Persia in the fourth century, when the Catholicos Mar Shimun bar Sabba'e and many companions were put to death, according to tradition on a Good Friday. Placing their feast in Easter week says that their death was not a defeat.

Is the Prayer of Azariah really part of the Bible?

Yes, for Catholics. It belongs to the Greek form of Daniel, which the Church received from the beginning, and the Council of Trent listed Daniel among the sacred books in the form read in the Church. It is not found in the Aramaic text, so many Protestant Bibles print it separately or leave it out.

In Matthew 10:28, who is the one we should fear?

Most interpreters, with the main Catholic tradition, understand it to be God, the judge of soul and body. Jesus does not mean a slavish dread. He means the holy fear that puts God first, and he goes straight on to speak of the Father who counts the hairs of our head (vv. 29–31).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the East Syriac tradition this is the Friday of the Confessors, the feast of all the sahde, the martyrs. The Church of the East, to which our Church was joined for centuries, suffered terribly under the Persian king Shapur II. Mar Shimun bar Sabba'e, Catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, refused to deny Christ and was put to death with a great company of bishops, priests and faithful. Our own Church remembers that its father in faith, Mar Thoma Shliha, gave his life for Christ in India. Today we pray for Christians who suffer for their faith in many lands, and for those who wrong them, as Stephen prayed for his.

For study and teaching

  • Azariah blessed God from inside the fire. What would it mean for me to praise God in the middle of a trial, before it ends?
  • Stephen's face shone when he was falsely accused. How do I respond when I am misjudged?
  • Abraham “set out, not knowing where he was going”. Where is God asking me to take a step without seeing the whole road?
  • Jesus says three times, “Do not be afraid.” What fear keeps me from acknowledging him before others?
