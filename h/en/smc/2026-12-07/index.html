Word for the day

“If in my name you ask me for anything, I will do it.” John 14:14

Reflection

Paul's goal for the Church is maturity: “until all of us come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to maturity, to the measure of the full stature of Christ.” No longer “children, tossed to and fro”, but “speaking the truth in love, we must grow up in every way into him who is the head.”

Jesus promises that the one who believes in him will do the works he does, and even greater, and that whatever we ask in his name he will do. St Ambrose, bishop of Milan, lived this: chosen bishop while still a catechumen, he grew into a pastor of great faith who stood firm before emperors and led St Augustine to baptism.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul has a beautiful goal for the Church: that all of us may come to the unity of the faith and the knowledge of the Son of God, to maturity, to the measure of the full stature of Christ (Ephesians 4:13). God wants us to grow up.

Paul describes spiritual childishness: being tossed to and fro and blown about by every wind of doctrine, by people's trickery (Ephesians 4:14). Today the winds blow through our phones: forwarded messages, half-truths, aggressive opinions, new teachings every day. An immature faith is pushed around by every wave.

What is the alternative? Speaking the truth in love, we must grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ (Ephesians 4:15). Mature faith is rooted in Christ and the Church's teaching, and it speaks truth always with love: not harsh, not timid.

In the Gospel Jesus promises: whoever believes in me will do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these; if in my name you ask me for anything, I will do it (John 14:12–14). Asking in Jesus' name means asking as he would ask: for the Father's glory and our true good.

Today we remember St Ambrose, bishop of Milan. He was a mature Christian leader: deeply rooted in Scripture, brave enough to correct the emperor Theodosius after a massacre, gentle enough to welcome his repentance, and wise enough to lead the young Augustine to faith.

How do we grow to maturity? By regular prayer, by knowing the Scriptures, by learning the Catechism, by receiving the sacraments, and by belonging to a community. Faith that is only emotion or only custom stays childish.

This Nombu, let us choose one way to grow: read one chapter of a Gospel each day, join a Bible study, or read the Catechism of the Catholic Church on Christmas. And let us always speak the truth in love. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the day of St Ambrose, the readings speak of growing up and of doing greater things. Ephesians wants the Church to stop being tossed about like children by “every wind of doctrine” and to grow up into Christ. In John, Jesus promises that those who believe will do even greater works than his, and that he will do what they ask in his name. Ambrose, a bishop who stood firm in a storm of false teaching and who helped to bring Augustine to Christ, shows what both mean.

Ephesians 4:13–16 · Grow up into him who is the head

Setting.

The letter comes from prison in Paul’s name (4:1); many scholars think it was written by a close disciple. Its first half (chapters 1–3) sings of God’s plan to unite all things in Christ. Its second half (chapters 4–6) describes the life that follows. Just before today’s verses, the author lists the gifts Christ has given to the Church: apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers (4:11), who are given to equip the saints “until all of us come to the unity of the faith”.

Structure.

  • v. 13: the goal: unity, knowledge, maturity.
  • v. 14: the opposite: children tossed by every wind.
  • v. 15: speaking the truth in love, we grow into the head.
  • v. 16: the whole body grows, as each part does its work.

Key words.

  • andra teleion, maturity (literally, a mature man): The word is singular. The goal is not many mature individuals but one whole person, the Church grown into Christ.
  • kybeia, trickery: The word for playing with dice. False teachers treat the truth like a dice game, with a skilful throw of the hand.
  • alētheuontes, speaking the truth: Literally “truthing”, doing and living the truth, not only speaking it. In the Greek the words “truth” and “love” are side by side, and the Christian needs both at once.

Verse by verse.

“until all of us come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to maturity, to the measure of the full stature of Christ.” (4:13)

“All of us”: maturity is a goal for the whole Church. No one reaches it alone, and it is measured by Christ himself, not by comparison with others.

“We must no longer be children, tossed to and fro and blown about by every wind of doctrine” (4:14)

This is a sailor’s picture: a small boat with no steering. In the fourth century, Ambrose faced such a wind in Arianism, which denied the full divinity of the Son and had powerful friends at court.

“But speaking the truth in love, we must grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ,” (4:15)

Truth without love wounds, and love without truth deceives. The growth is “into him”: the Church is not growing toward some idea, but toward a person.

“from whom the whole body, joined and knit together by every ligament with which it is equipped, as each part is working properly, promotes the body’s growth in building itself up in love.” (4:16)

The sentence is long and tangled, as a body is. Every part matters, down to the least. The body’s growth comes from the head and is built up by what each member gives.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws on this passage to teach that Christ, the head, provides in his Body, the Church, the gifts and helps by which we support one another on the way of salvation (CCC 794). The Council speaks of the same growth of the whole body toward its head (Lumen Gentium 7).

John 14:11–14 · Greater works than these

Setting.

This is the Last Supper in John’s Gospel. Jesus has said that he is going away, and the disciples are troubled. Philip has just said, “Lord, show us the Father”, and Jesus has replied, “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (14:8–9). Today’s verses continue that reply and turn it into a promise for the time after he has gone.

Structure.

  • v. 11: believe in the unity of Son and Father, or at least believe because of the works.
  • v. 12: the promise: greater works, because he is going to the Father.
  • vv. 13–14: the promise about prayer in his name.

Key words.

  • erga, works: John’s word for what Jesus does, including his signs. They are the Father’s works in him (14:10), and they show who he is.
  • meizona, greater: Greater not in power but in reach. Jesus ministered in one small land for a few years; after his return to the Father, his Church will carry the word to the nations.
  • en tō onomati mou, in my name: In the Bible a person’s name stands for the person. To ask “in his name” is not a formula added at the end of prayer. It means asking as one united with him, wanting what he wants.

Verse by verse.

“Believe me that I am in the Father and the Father is in me; but if you do not, then believe me because of the works themselves.” (14:11)

Jesus accepts a beginning of faith. Those who cannot yet trust his word may at least look at what he has done.

“Very truly, I tell you, the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do and, in fact, will do greater works than these, because I am going to the Father.” (14:12)

The reason given is striking: “because I am going”. His departure is not the end of his work but its widening, through the Spirit he will send (14:16).

“I will do whatever you ask in my name, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son.” (14:13)

The goal of the promise is the Father’s glory. This sets the limit of the prayer, and its joy.

“If in my name you ask me for anything, I will do it.” (14:14)

A note on the text: Many ancient manuscripts have “ask me”, as the NRSV and the Malayalam POC do, and some leave out “me”. With “me”, Jesus himself receives the prayer. The Church has prayed to him from the beginning.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine, commenting on this Gospel, says that the justification of the wicked is a greater work than the creation of heaven and earth; the Catechism quotes him (CCC 1994). The Catechism also teaches that prayer in Jesus’ name is the heart of the new prayer he gives (CCC 2614–2615), and that “There is no other way of Christian prayer than Christ” (CCC 2664).

Questions people ask

Jesus says that he will do “whatever” we ask. Why are some prayers not answered?

The promise is for prayer “in my name”, that is, in union with him, and “so that the Father may be glorified”. It is not a way of getting what we want. The Catechism speaks of this difficulty directly: we should ask whether our prayer is that of a child who trusts, and we should remember that God’s gift is greater than what we ask (CCC 2735–2737). Jesus’ own prayer in Gethsemane is the model.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

St Ambrose (c. 340–397) was a Roman governor at Milan and still a catechumen when the people acclaimed him bishop in 374. His feast falls on the anniversary of his ordination. He stood firm against Arianism, taught the people through sermons and hymns, and did not hesitate to make the Emperor Theodosius do penance after the massacre at Thessalonica. St Augustine tells in his Confessions how Ambrose’s preaching opened the Old Testament to him; Ambrose baptised him at Easter in 387. In that one baptism the “greater works” of John 14 can be seen, and every parish priest who preaches and baptises shares that work.

For study and teaching

  • Ephesians says that we must speak the truth “in love”. In my family or parish, do I find it harder to speak truth or to speak love?
  • What “greater work” might the Lord want to do through me, my family or my parish in this season?
