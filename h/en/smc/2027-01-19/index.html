Word for the day

“But the tax collector, standing far off, would not even look up to heaven, but was beating his breast and saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’” Luke 18:13

Reflection

On the second day of the fast the readings show what a true fast looks like. At Mizpah Samuel tells Israel: “If you are returning to the LORD with all your heart, then put away the foreign gods”. The people draw water and pour it out before the LORD, fast all day and confess: “We have sinned against the LORD.” When the Philistines attack, they beg Samuel: “Do not cease to cry out to the LORD our God for us”, and Samuel prays, “and the LORD answered him.” Isaiah explains why prayer can seem unheard: “See, the LORD’s hand is not too short to save, nor his ear too dull to hear. Rather, your iniquities have been barriers between you and your God”. Fasting is the work of taking those barriers down.

Then Jesus shows two men at prayer. The Pharisee is a serious religious man: “I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of all my income.” But he prays “standing by himself”, comparing himself with “this tax collector”. The tax collector stands far off and says only: “God, be merciful to me, a sinner!” It is he who goes home justified, “for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted.” Paul shows the fruit of such humility: we offer our bodies “as a living sacrifice”, we are “transformed by the renewing of your minds”, and we learn “not to think of yourself more highly than you ought to think”, because “we, who are many, are one body in Christ”. A fast that makes us proud is the Pharisee's fast; a fast that makes us humble and one with others is the fast God accepts. In this Unity Octave, the tax collector's prayer is also the prayer of divided Christians.

Full homily

Dear friends, on this second day of the fast, the readings ask: what kind of fast pleases God? At Mizpah Samuel tells Israel: if you are returning to the LORD with all your heart, put away the foreign gods (1 Samuel 7:3). The people pour out water before the LORD, fast and confess: we have sinned against the LORD. Samuel prays for them, and the LORD answers.

Isaiah answers those who complain that God does not hear: the LORD's hand is not too short to save, nor his ear too dull to hear. Rather, your iniquities have been barriers between you and your God (Isaiah 59:1–2). Fasting is the work of taking down those barriers, stone by stone.

Then Jesus tells of two men at prayer. The Pharisee fasts twice a week and gives a tenth of all his income. But his prayer is about himself, and he despises the tax collector. The tax collector stands far off, beats his breast and says: God, be merciful to me, a sinner (Luke 18:13). Jesus says this man went home justified.

Notice: the Pharisee fasted. Fasting is not the problem. Pride is the problem. Paul tells us not to think of ourselves more highly than we ought, because we, who are many, are one body in Christ (Romans 12:3, 5). A fast that makes us look down on others has missed its goal.

In this Unity Octave, let us pray the tax collector's prayer for ourselves and for all Christians. God, be merciful to us, sinners, and make us one. Amen.

For children

Children, one man said to God: I am so good! Another man said: God, be merciful to me, a sinner. Jesus liked the second prayer. Let us pray it today: Jesus, have mercy on me.

Prayer

Lord God, your hand is not too short to save. Take away the barriers our sins have raised between us and you. Keep us from the pride that fasts and despises others; give us the heart of the tax collector, and transform us by the renewing of our minds, one body in Christ. God, be merciful to us, sinners. Amen.
