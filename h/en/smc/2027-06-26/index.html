Readings

  • Epistle 2 Corinthians 11:1–6 · I promised you in marriage to one husband, Christ
  • Gospel John 7:14–24 · Do not judge by appearances

Word for the day

“Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” John 7:24

Reflection

Paul writes to the Corinthians like a father who has arranged his daughter's marriage and now fears for her. “I feel a divine jealousy for you, for I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” In the customs of the time, the friend of the bridegroom arranged the betrothal and watched over the bride until the wedding day. Paul sees himself in that role: by his preaching he betrothed the Church of Corinth to Christ, and he longs to present her pure on the day of the Lord's coming. His jealousy is “divine”, the jealousy of God himself, who in the prophets calls Israel his bride: “I will take you for my wife forever; I will take you for my wife in righteousness and in justice, in steadfast love, and in mercy” (Hosea 2:19). The Church is not a club of the like-minded; she is a bride, loved and promised to one Husband. Our Syro-Malabar liturgy speaks often of the Church as the bride of Christ, and the crowning of bride and groom at a wedding is a living picture of that mystery.

Then comes the fear: “But I am afraid that as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning, your thoughts will be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ.” The danger is not that the Corinthians will abandon religion; it is that they will accept “another Jesus”, “a different spirit”, “a different gospel”, and hardly notice. Certain preachers, whom Paul calls with irony “these super-apostles”, had arrived with fine speech and impressive letters. They offered a Jesus without the Cross, a spirit without humility, a gospel of success. Paul admits, “I may be untrained in speech, but not in knowledge.” The question is never how eloquent the preacher is, but whether the Jesus he preaches is the Jesus of the apostles. In this last week of the Season of the Apostles the warning is timely. Our phones bring us a new preacher every hour; some promise wealth and healing as a reward for money, some mock the Church and the sacraments, some offer a Jesus who never asks for repentance. The bride keeps her devotion sincere and pure by staying with the faith of the apostles, handed down in the Church.

The Gospel takes us to Jerusalem at the Festival of Booths. Jesus goes up to the Temple and teaches, and those who hear him are astonished: “How does this man have such learning, when he has never been taught?” He had not studied under a famous rabbi. In John's Gospel the phrase “the Jews” often refers to the religious authorities of Jerusalem who opposed Jesus; it never condemns the Jewish people as a whole, to whom Jesus, his Mother and all the apostles belonged. Jesus answers: “My teaching is not mine but his who sent me.” Here is the test of every true teacher. “Those who speak on their own seek their own glory; but the one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true.” Jesus himself, the eternal Word, does not present his teaching as his own invention; it is the Father's. How much more must every preacher, priest and catechist in the Church hand on what was received, and not his own opinions.

Then Jesus speaks of the healing he had done on a sabbath (John 5), which had caused such anger. He reasons with them from their own practice: if a boy may be circumcised on the sabbath so that the law of Moses is kept, why be angry that he made a man's whole body well on the sabbath? And he ends with the line that is today's word: “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” The crowd judged by appearances: the man had no degree, so he could not be a teacher; the healing happened on the wrong day, so it could not be from God. We do the same when we judge people by colour, caste, accent, dress, bank balance or the size of their house, or when we decide about a marriage proposal by a photograph. The Lord said to Samuel, “they look on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). On this Saturday we look to Mary, the Virgin who was wholly devoted to Christ, whose humble appearance in Nazareth hid the purest heart of any creature. She teaches the Church to be the chaste bride Paul longed for, and to see others as God sees them.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul writes to the Corinthians with the love of a father who has arranged his daughter's marriage: “I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ” (2 Corinthians 11:2). The Church is the bride of Christ, and each of us belongs to that bride.

Paul is afraid. Clever preachers have come with fine words and a different Jesus: a Jesus without the Cross, a gospel of success. The Corinthians accepted them easily. We too hear many voices today, on our phones and in new groups. Let us test every voice by the faith of the apostles that the Church has kept.

In the Gospel, people are surprised: how can Jesus teach so well when he has not studied? Jesus says: “My teaching is not mine but his who sent me” (John 7:16). A true teacher does not seek his own glory but God's.

Then he says: “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment” (John 7:24). We often judge by appearance: by colour, caste, dress, money, or a photograph. But God looks at the heart.

On this Saturday let us look to Mary, the humble Virgin of Nazareth, wholly devoted to Christ. May she keep our love for her Son sincere and pure, and give us eyes that see others as God sees them. Amen.

For children

Children, some people did not believe Jesus because he had not gone to a famous school. They looked only at the outside. Jesus said: do not judge by appearances! God looks at the heart. This week, be kind to someone others laugh at because of how they look.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Bridegroom of the Church, you loved her and gave yourself up for her. Keep us, your bride, in a sincere and pure devotion to you. Guard us from every other Jesus, every other spirit and every other gospel, and keep us faithful to the teaching of your apostles. You did not seek your own glory but the glory of the Father who sent you: make us seek his glory, not ours. Give us eyes that do not judge by appearances but see the heart as you see it. Through the prayers of the Blessed Virgin Mary, wholly devoted to you, make us holy and blameless for the day of your coming. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about true and false teachers. Paul fears that the Corinthians, betrothed to Christ, will be led astray by smooth preachers who bring “another Jesus”. In John, Jesus teaches in the Temple, and the people wonder where his learning comes from; he answers that his teaching is not his own but the Father’s, and asks them to judge rightly, not by appearances. The test of a teacher is not eloquence or credentials but whether he seeks the glory of the One who sent him.

2 Corinthians 11:1–6 · Betrothed to one husband

Setting.

Chapters 10–13 of 2 Corinthians are Paul’s sharpest words to this Church. Since his last letter, preachers have arrived at Corinth who boast of their speaking skill and of letters of recommendation, and who look down on Paul as weak and unimpressive in person (10:10). Paul calls them, with irony, “super-apostles”. To defend the Gospel he must now speak about himself, which he calls a kind of foolishness.

Structure.

  • v. 1: Paul asks them to bear with a little foolishness.
  • vv. 2–3: his divine jealousy: he has betrothed them to Christ and fears they will be seduced as Eve was.
  • v. 4: they accept another Jesus, another spirit, another gospel all too easily.
  • vv. 5–6: he is not inferior to the super-apostles; untrained in speech, but not in knowledge.

Key words.

  • zēlos theou, divine jealousy: The jealousy of God himself, who loves his people as a husband loves his bride and will not share them with idols (Hosea 2:19–20). Paul shares God’s own protective love for the Church.
  • parthenos hagnē, chaste virgin: In Jewish custom the betrothal was already binding; the bride was then led to the bridegroom’s house. Paul sees himself as the friend who arranged the betrothal and must present the bride faithful on the wedding day, the Lord’s coming.
  • hyperlian apostoloi, super-apostles: Literally “the over-much apostles”. Paul mocks their self-importance. True apostleship is measured by faithfulness to Christ crucified, not by impressive speech.

Verse by verse.

“I feel a divine jealousy for you, for I promised you in marriage to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” (11:2)

The whole Church, and each soul, is the bride of Christ. Faithfulness to the Gospel is faithfulness in love.

“as the serpent deceived Eve by its cunning, your thoughts will be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ.” (11:3)

Error rarely comes as plain evil. Like the serpent, it comes with clever words and makes the truth seem small.

“I may be untrained in speech, but not in knowledge” (11:6)

Paul may have lacked the polished style that the Greeks admired, but he did not lack the knowledge of Christ. The Gospel does not depend on rhetoric.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism draws on this verse when it teaches that the Church is the bride of Christ: the Apostle speaks of the whole Church and of each of the faithful as a bride betrothed to Christ the Lord, to become one spirit with him (CCC 796). The Church has always heard in Paul’s fear a warning against changing the Gospel: there is not “another Jesus”, but the one handed on by the apostles. St John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter, notes that Paul calls his concern jealousy because it burns with love for them, not for himself (in substance, Homilies on 2 Corinthians).

John 7:14–24 · My teaching is not mine

Setting.

Jesus has gone up to Jerusalem for the Feast of Tabernacles (7:2, 10), the joyful autumn harvest feast when people lived in shelters made of branches. Opinions about him are divided, and the authorities are seeking to kill him (7:1). In the middle of the feast he teaches openly in the Temple. The “one work” he speaks of is the healing of the man at the pool of Bethesda on a sabbath (5:1–18), which had first stirred the authorities against him.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: Jesus teaches in the Temple; they wonder at his learning.
  • vv. 16–18: his teaching comes from the Father; whoever wants to do God’s will will recognize it.
  • vv. 19–20: none of you keeps the law; why seek to kill me? The crowd: “You have a demon!”
  • vv. 21–24: the argument from circumcision on the sabbath; judge with right judgment.

Key words.

  • grammata, learning: Literally “letters”: the learning of one trained under a teacher of the Law. Jesus had not studied in a rabbinic school, yet he taught with authority.
  • didachē, teaching: Jesus does not claim a teaching of his own invention. As the Son he speaks what he has received from the Father (12:49).
  • kat’ opsin, by appearances: Literally “according to the face”, by what is seen on the surface. Right judgment looks to the purpose of the Law and the heart of God.

Verse by verse.

“My teaching is not mine but his who sent me.” (7:16)

This is not modesty but a statement of who he is. The Son’s teaching is the Father’s teaching, because the Son is one with the Father (10:30).

“Anyone who resolves to do the will of God will know whether the teaching is from God” (7:17)

Knowing the truth about Christ is not only a matter of study. Those who set themselves to obey God come to recognize his voice.

“If a man receives circumcision on the sabbath in order that the law of Moses may not be broken, are you angry with me because I healed a man’s whole body on the sabbath?” (7:23)

A boy is circumcised on the eighth day (Leviticus 12:3) even if it falls on a sabbath; later Jewish teachers also recorded this rule. Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater: if one rite of the covenant may be done on the sabbath, how much more may a whole person be made well.

“Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” (7:24)

The words are for every age. We easily judge by outward signs; God looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7).

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine asks how the teaching can be both “mine” and “not mine”, and answers that the Son is himself the Word of the Father: what is the Father’s teaching but the Word? He also takes verse 17 as a rule for faith: understanding is the reward of faith; do not seek to understand in order to believe, but believe so that you may understand (in substance, Tractates on John 29). In John, “the Jews” in verse 15 means the Temple authorities and their circle in Jerusalem, not the Jewish people as such; Jesus, his disciples and the crowd were all Jews, and the Church warns against any reading that blames the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

For study and teaching

  • Paul fears that the Corinthians may accept “another Jesus”. What images of Jesus in today’s culture differ from the Jesus of the Gospel and the Church?
  • Jesus says, “Do not judge by appearances.” Whom have I judged by appearances this week, and how can I look again with right judgment?
