Readings

  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 14:20–25 · God is really among you
  • Gospel Luke 19:11–27 · Be faithful with what the Lord has entrusted

Word for the day

“Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much; and whoever is dishonest in a very little is dishonest also in much.” Luke 16:10

Reflection

Paul's reading is about the Church at worship, and about what an outsider sees when he walks in. The Corinthians loved the spectacular gifts of the Spirit, above all speaking in tongues, and some of their meetings had become a contest of display. Paul begins with a sentence that is full of tenderness and good sense: “Brothers and sisters, do not be children in your thinking; rather, be infants in evil, but in thinking be adults.” Be as innocent as a baby about evil, and as grown up as an adult in your judgement. Then he thinks of a visitor, a neighbour or a stranger, who comes in on the day of worship. “If, therefore, the whole church comes together and all speak in tongues, and outsiders or unbelievers enter, will they not say that you are out of your mind? But if all prophesy, an unbeliever or outsider who enters is reproved by all and called to account by all. After the secrets of the unbeliever’s heart are disclosed, that person will bow down before God and worship him, declaring, ‘God is really among you.’” By prophecy Paul means the word of God spoken so that it can be understood and can touch the heart. The test of a gathering is whether a stranger who comes in can say that God is really there. Gifts are given “for building up the church”; the Church teaches that every charism, simple or striking, is a grace of the Holy Spirit for the good of all (CCC 799). The Second Vatican Council asked in the same spirit that the rites should be “within the people's powers of comprehension” (Sacrosanctum Concilium 34). In Kerala, neighbours of all faiths come to our weddings, funerals and feasts. What do they see and hear? Do they see reverence, a word they can understand, and people who love each other?

Why does the Church put “infants in evil” beside a parable about business? Because both are about what we do with what God has given. In the Gospel Jesus is near Jerusalem, and his hearers “supposed that the kingdom of God was to appear immediately”, so he tells them a parable about the time between. “A nobleman went to a distant country to get royal power for himself and then return. He summoned ten of his slaves, and gave them ten pounds, and said to them, ‘Do business with these until I come back.’ But the citizens of his country hated him and sent a delegation after him, saying, ‘We do not want this man to rule over us.’” Scholars often hear in this an echo of the journey of Archelaus, a son of King Herod, to Rome to have his kingship confirmed, while a delegation of Jews and others opposed him; Jesus' hearers knew such stories. When the nobleman returns, the first says, “Lord, your pound has made ten more pounds”, and is told, “Well done, good slave! Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing, take charge of ten cities.” The second has made five and receives five cities. “Then the other came, saying, ‘Lord, here is your pound. I wrapped it up in a piece of cloth, for I was afraid of you, because you are a harsh man; you take what you did not deposit, and reap what you did not sow.’” The master answers, “I will judge you by your own words”: if he really thought him so demanding, he should at least have put the money in the bank. And the principle: “I tell you, to all those who have, more will be given; but from those who have nothing, even what they have will be taken away.”

The heart of the parable is the third servant's picture of God. He did nothing wrong, he stole nothing and spent nothing; he buried the gift. But he thought the master was harsh, and fear made him idle. Many people live their faith in this way: keeping everything safe and clean, wrapped in a cloth, and giving nothing to anyone. The Lord is not a harsh man reaping where he did not sow; he is the generous giver of every gift, and he wants it to be used. “Like good stewards of the manifold grace of God, serve one another with whatever gift each of you has received.” The Catechism teaches that God gives different gifts to people so that each may receive what he needs from others, and that those endowed with talents share their benefits with those who need them (CCC 1937). The first servants are praised not for the amount, because one made ten and the other five, but for being “trustworthy in a very small thing”: “Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much.” The ending of the parable is hard: the nobleman says of the citizens who rejected him, “bring them here and slaughter them in my presence.” This is the king of the story speaking; Jesus himself, as he goes up to Jerusalem, will not strike anyone but will give his own life. The verse is a picture of the seriousness of rejecting the king, and the judgement belongs to God alone, never to us. It does not speak of any one people; it speaks of every heart that says, “We do not want this man to rule over us.” In Kaitha, the season of fruit, the Lord asks each of us: what have you done with my gift? Trade with it until I come back.

Today's calendar also lists Saint Maria Goretti, and she belongs naturally beside Paul's first sentence, “be infants in evil, but in thinking be adults.” Maria was born in 1890 into a poor farming family in Italy and grew up in the marshlands south of Rome, where her father died of malaria when she was nine. She worked from morning to night, looking after her small brothers and sisters while her mother worked in the fields, and she prepared with great care for her First Communion. On 5 July 1902, when she was eleven, a young man from the neighbouring family tried to assault her, and she refused him: she knew that what he asked was a sin against God. He stabbed her many times. She died the next day, 6 July, in hospital, and before she died she forgave him and said that she wanted him to be with her in paradise. Years later the man, Alessandro, who had been in prison, repented, asked her mother's forgiveness and was received by her, and he lived a quiet life of penance. Maria was canonised by Pope Pius XII in 1950. In her we see what it means to be “an infant in evil” and an adult in judgement: she was a child in age, but she was clear and strong in her choice. “Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God”, and “Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good”: she overcame it with a forgiveness that echoes the words of Jesus from the cross, “Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing” (CCC 2844). The Church holds her up to young people today as a friend and companion in the struggle for purity, and to everyone who has suffered violence as a protector.

Full homily

Dear friends, today is the first Tuesday of Kaitha, and two Bible readings speak to us about gifts: what God has given and what we do with it.

Saint Paul tells the Corinthians: “Be infants in evil, but in thinking be adults” (1 Corinthians 14:20). Be innocent about evil, and clear and wise in your judgement. He also asks what a stranger sees when he walks into our church. If all is noise and show, he will say, “They are out of their mind.” But if the word of God is spoken so that it can be understood, the stranger will fall on his knees and say, “God is really among you” (1 Corinthians 14:25). That is what our worship is for.

In the Gospel a nobleman goes away and gives ten servants ten pounds each: “Do business with these until I come back” (Luke 19:13). When he returns, two servants have used the money and are praised: “Well done, good slave! Because you have been trustworthy in a very small thing...” (Luke 19:17). The third buried it in a cloth. Why? “I was afraid of you.” He thought his master was harsh, and fear made him do nothing.

Many of us have buried something: a talent for singing, for teaching, for kindness, for prayer; time we could give to the parish or to a sick neighbour. God is not a harsh master. He is the giver of every gift, and he wants it to be used. “Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10). It is not the size of the gift that counts but the faithfulness.

Today the Church also remembers Saint Maria Goretti. She was a poor girl of eleven who refused to do wrong and was badly hurt for it. Before she died she forgave the one who hurt her. Later he repented. She was a child in age, but in her choices she was strong and clear: an “infant in evil” and an adult in judgement. Let us ask her to pray for our children and young people. And let us not bury our gifts, but use them, until the Lord comes. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus tells a story about a master who gave his servants money and went away. Two servants used it well. One was afraid and hid it. Jesus wants us to use the gifts God has given us: singing, helping, kind words, drawing, praying.

Today we also remember Saint Maria Goretti. She was a girl who loved Jesus very much and always chose what was right, and when someone hurt her badly, she forgave him. Ask her to help you to be brave and kind, and to forgive.

Prayer

Lord God, you give to each of us different gifts and ask that we trade with them until your Son comes again. Keep us from burying them in fear. Make us childlike in evil and mature in judgement; and make our worship so true that the stranger who enters may say, “God is really among you.” Through the prayers of Saint Maria Goretti, who chose purity and forgave her attacker, give young people strength to say Yes to you, and give us all the grace to forgive and to overcome evil with good. Through Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.
