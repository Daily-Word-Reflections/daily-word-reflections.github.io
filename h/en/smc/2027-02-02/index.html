Word for the day

“for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel.” Luke 2:30–32

Reflection

Forty days after his birth the Lord enters his Temple. The feast is also called the Entrance: the child carried in Mary's arms is the Lord of the Temple coming into his own house. The first reading shows the law the Holy Family obeyed. After the birth of a son a mother waited forty days for her purification, and then brought “a lamb in its first year for a burnt offering, and a pigeon or a turtledove for a sin offering.” The law made room for the poor: “If she cannot afford a sheep, she shall take two turtledoves or two pigeons.” Luke tells us that Mary and Joseph offered “a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons.” They were poor. They also fulfilled another law, that every firstborn son belongs to God: “Every firstborn male shall be designated as holy to the Lord.” The true Lamb was carried into the Temple, and the poor man's offering was made for him.

Two old people were waiting. Simeon was “righteous and devout, looking forward to the consolation of Israel, and the Holy Spirit rested on him.” Guided by the Spirit he came into the Temple at the very moment the parents brought the child in. He took the child in his arms and praised God: “Master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace, according to your word; for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel.” Then he turned to the mother with a harder word: “This child is destined for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and to be a sign that will be opposed”, and a sword would pierce her own soul too. Anna, a widow of eighty-four, “never left the temple but worshiped there with fasting and prayer night and day.” She too began to praise God and to speak about the child “to all who were looking for the redemption of Jerusalem.” The prophet Malachi had promised that “the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple.” He came quietly, as a baby, and only those who were waiting recognized him.

Isaiah describes the one Simeon held: “Here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights.” He will not shout or crush the weak: “a bruised reed he will not break, and a dimly burning wick he will not quench.” God gives him “as a covenant to the people, a light to the nations, to open the eyes that are blind.” Simeon's song repeats this promise: a light for the Gentiles, and so for us in India too. Paul, in the Epistle, carries the sorrow of the sword. He grieves that many of his own people stumbled over “the stumbling stone”, and he prays that they may be saved. Yet he states the meaning of this feast in one line: “For Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.” The child who was presented according to the law is the goal toward which the whole law was moving.

The weekday readings keep the Denha light turned toward the end of time. Paul proclaims that “as all die in Adam, so all will be made alive in Christ”, and Jesus warns, “Keep awake therefore, for you do not know on what day your Lord is coming.” Simeon and Anna kept awake for a lifetime, and their eyes saw salvation. Today is also the World Day for Consecrated Life. Religious sisters, brothers and priests, so many of them from our own families, are presented to God like the child in the Temple, poor, chaste and obedient, so that the light of Christ may reach every people.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, today the Lord enters his Temple. Forty days after his birth, Mary and Joseph bring him to Jerusalem to present him to the Lord, as the law commanded, and they offer a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons (Luke 2:24). It was the offering of the poor.

The law of Leviticus said that a mother who could not afford a lamb could bring two birds. But on that day the true Lamb was in his mother's arms. The poor offering was made for the One who would offer himself for the whole world.

Simeon, righteous and devout, has waited all his life. Guided by the Spirit he comes into the Temple, takes the child in his arms and says: master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace; for my eyes have seen your salvation (Luke 2:29–30).

Then he tells Mary that this child will be a sign that will be opposed, and that a sword will pierce her soul. Paul, in the Epistle, grieves that many stumbled over this stone. Yet Christ is the end of the law, so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes (Romans 10:4).

Anna, eighty-four years old, worships night and day with fasting and prayer, and speaks about the child to all who are waiting. Isaiah had promised a Servant who would be a light to the nations. Simeon and Anna see that light in a baby.

On this World Day for Consecrated Life let us thank God for our religious sisters, brothers and priests, presented to God like the child in the Temple. And let us keep awake, like Simeon and Anna, so that when the Lord comes we may recognize him and say: my eyes have seen your salvation. Amen.

For children

Children, when Jesus was a tiny baby, Mary and Joseph took him to the Temple to give him to God. Old Simeon held him and was so happy!

Each morning you can also say: Jesus, I give you my day!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, firstborn Son of the Father, you were carried into your Temple and offered with the gift of the poor. Let us, like Simeon, take you into our arms with faith, and like Anna, wait for you with fasting and prayer. Shine on all the nations as the light of revelation, strengthen your mother's children when the sword pierces their hearts, and bless all who have consecrated their lives to you. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Forty days after his birth the Lord is carried into his Temple, and all four readings meet there. Leviticus gives the law of a mother’s offering, with its concession to the poor, and Luke shows Mary and Joseph keeping that law with the poor person’s gift. Isaiah presents the Servant as “a light to the nations”, and old Simeon, holding the child, says the same words over him. Paul, grieving for his own people, calls Christ the goal to which the law was always moving. The readings fit together around three words: law, light and waiting. The law is kept, the light is seen, and those who waited are at last able to depart in peace.

Leviticus 12:1–8 · The offering of a mother

Setting.

Leviticus 11–15 sets out the laws of ritual purity. These laws do not speak of moral guilt. “Unclean” describes a state that kept a person from the sanctuary for a time. Birth, blood and death stand at the edge of life, where human beings touch the mystery of God who gives life, and the Law surrounds them with care and reverence.

Chapter 12 is short and practical. After bearing a son a mother waits seven days and then thirty-three, forty in all. After bearing a daughter the periods are doubled, eighty in all. The text gives no reason for the difference, and the explanations offered by commentators remain guesses. The boy’s circumcision on the eighth day (v. 3) is mentioned in passing.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–4: after the birth of a son: seven days, then thirty-three.
  • v. 5: after the birth of a daughter: two weeks, then sixty-six days.
  • vv. 6–7: the offering: a lamb and a pigeon or turtledove.
  • v. 8: the provision for the poor.

Key words.

  • ṭāmē’, unclean: A term of worship, not of ethics. The mother has not sinned by giving birth. In the first creation account fruitfulness is God’s own blessing (Genesis 1:28). She is simply kept for a time from contact with what is holy.
  • ḥaṭṭā’t, sin offering: The traditional translation, and the Malayalam POC’s too. Many scholars prefer “purification offering” here, since no sin is in question. The offering restores the woman to the worship of the community.
  • ‘ōlâ, burnt offering: From a verb meaning “to go up”. The whole animal is burned and rises to God as a gift. It speaks of a life given back to the One who gave it.

Verse by verse.

“she shall not touch any holy thing, or come into the sanctuary, until the days of her purification are completed.” (12:4)

The rule looks ahead to a return. The period ends, and the mother comes back to the sanctuary with her offering.

“When the days of her purification are completed, whether for a son or for a daughter, she shall bring to the priest at the entrance of the tent of meeting a lamb in its first year for a burnt offering, and a pigeon or a turtledove for a sin offering.” (12:6)

On the feast the Church notices the words “a lamb in its first year”. Mary brings no lamb, and yet she carries one in her arms.

“If she cannot afford a sheep, she shall take two turtledoves or two pigeons, one for a burnt offering and the other for a sin offering; and the priest shall make atonement on her behalf, and she shall be clean.” (12:8)

The Law makes room for the poor, and the poor woman’s offering is fully valid. Luke quotes this very provision (2:24), and in doing so he tells us quietly how poor the Holy Family was.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council notes that Mary presented her Son to the Lord “making the offering of the poor” (Lumen Gentium 57). The Catechism sees the Presentation as showing Jesus to be the firstborn Son who belongs to the Lord (CCC 529), and his circumcision as the sign of his submission to the Law (CCC 527). The purity laws have been fulfilled in Christ and no longer bind Christians (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15). What remains is reverence for the giving of life, and gratitude when a child is born.

Isaiah 42:1–7 · A light to the nations

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the Jews in exile in Babylon, promising return. Within them are four passages commonly called the Servant Songs, and this is the first (42:1–4); vv. 5–7 add God’s word addressed to the Servant.

Who is the Servant? Elsewhere in these chapters Israel itself is called God’s servant (41:8; 49:3), and that is how Jewish tradition has generally read it. The Songs also speak of an individual with a mission to Israel (49:5–6). The New Testament sees the Songs fulfilled in Jesus. Matthew quotes this very passage of him (Matthew 12:18–21), and the voice at the baptism echoes its opening line (Matthew 3:17).

Structure.

  • v. 1: God presents the Servant: chosen, upheld, given the Spirit.
  • vv. 2–3a: his gentle way: no shouting, no breaking.
  • vv. 3b–4: his steady persistence until justice is established.
  • v. 5: the Creator speaks.
  • vv. 6–7: the Servant’s mission: covenant, light, sight, release.

Key words.

  • ‘ebed, servant: Not a menial, but one who acts with his master’s authority. Moses and David have the same title. The Servant’s greatness lies in whom he serves.
  • mišpāṭ, justice: It comes three times in vv. 1–4. It means more than court verdicts. It is the right order of things that God wills for the world. The Servant’s mission reaches all the nations.
  • ’ôr gôyim, a light to the nations: The phrase returns in 49:6. Simeon takes it up in the Temple (Luke 2:32). The title of the Council’s great document on the Church, Lumen Gentium, is the same phrase in Latin, and it opens by saying that this light is Christ.

Verse by verse.

“Here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations.” (42:1)

“Chosen” and “in whom my soul delights”: at the baptism the Father’s voice says, “with whom I am well pleased” (Matthew 3:17), and the Spirit comes down.

“a bruised reed he will not break, and a dimly burning wick he will not quench;” (42:3)

A cracked reed is useless, and a smoking wick is about to go out. Anyone else would throw them away. This Servant does not. Matthew reads these words as a portrait of Jesus’ gentleness toward the weak (Matthew 12:20).

“I have given you as a covenant to the people, a light to the nations,” (42:6)

The Servant does not merely bring a covenant and a light. In some way he is the covenant and the light. On this feast the Church hears these words being fulfilled in a child forty days old.

“to open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon, from the prison those who sit in darkness.” (42:7)

In the first place this points to the exiles coming home from Babylon. Jesus’ healing of the blind gives the words a further fullness (Luke 4:18; 7:22).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the Messiah’s characteristics are revealed above all in the Servant Songs (CCC 713), and that Jesus’ baptism is his acceptance and inauguration of his mission as God’s suffering Servant (CCC 536). The Second Vatican Council begins its constitution on the Church with these words: Christ is the light of the nations, and the Church’s task is to let that light shine on all people (Lumen Gentium 1).

Romans 9:30–10:4 · Christ, the end of the law

Setting.

Romans 9–11 is Paul’s anguished reflection on his own people, the people of the covenant, most of whom had not accepted Jesus as Messiah. He begins by saying that he has “great sorrow and unceasing anguish” in his heart (9:2), and he lists Israel’s gifts with love (9:4–5). These chapters end not in condemnation but in hope and praise: “all Israel will be saved” (11:26), “for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29).

Today’s passage belongs to the middle of the argument and must be read inside that whole. Paul, himself a Jew, is speaking of a question within his own family. It must never be used to speak against the Jewish people (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597, 839).

Structure.

  • 9:30–31: the surprise: Gentiles have found what they did not seek.
  • 9:32–33: the stumbling stone, from Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16.
  • 10:1–2: Paul’s prayer for his people, and his witness to their zeal.
  • 10:3: God’s righteousness and one’s own.
  • 10:4: Christ is the end of the law.

Key words.

  • dikaiosynē, righteousness: In Paul, right relationship with God, which God gives and which we receive in faith. It is not first of all a human achievement, though it bears fruit in good deeds.
  • zēlos, zeal: Paul knows this word from inside: he describes himself as a persecutor “as to zeal” (Philippians 3:6). He speaks of his people’s zeal as a witness and with respect.
  • telos, end: The word means both “termination” and “goal”, as when a road reaches the place it was built to reach. The NRSV has “end”, and the Malayalam POC translates it as fulfilment. Many exegetes think Paul’s main sense is the goal: the law was always moving toward Christ.

Verse by verse.

“They have stumbled over the stumbling stone,” (9:32)

In Isaiah the stone is the Lord himself (8:14) and the sure foundation he lays in Zion (28:16). Paul reads it of Christ. The same stone that causes stumbling also saves: “whoever believes in him will not be put to shame” (9:33).

“Brothers and sisters, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved. I can testify that they have a zeal for God, but it is not enlightened.” (10:1–2)

Paul’s first response is prayer. He writes as a brother and with no contempt.

“For Christ is the end of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes.” (10:4)

On this feast the verse has a particular force. In the Temple the law is carefully kept (Luke 2:22–24) for the very child toward whom it was moving.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says, “Christ is the end of the law” and that only he teaches and bestows the justice of God (CCC 1977). It also teaches that Christ did not abolish the Law but fulfilled it (CCC 577–582). St Augustine, explaining the word “end”, distinguishes between an end that finishes something off and an end that brings it to perfection, and he says that Christ is the second kind (in substance). The Church honours the covenant with Israel, which God never revoked (CCC 121, 839).

Luke 2:22–38 · My eyes have seen your salvation

Setting.

Luke’s account of the infancy begins in the Temple with Zechariah (1:8–9) and ends in the Temple with the boy Jesus (2:41–51). This scene stands in the middle. The “law” is mentioned four times in vv. 22–27, and the Holy Spirit three times in vv. 25–27. Luke wants us to see that the old order of the Law and the new breath of the Spirit move together here.

Two laws lie behind v. 22: the mother’s purification (Leviticus 12) and the consecration of the firstborn (Exodus 13:2, 12). Luke does not mention the redemption price of Numbers 18:15–16, and he seems to have in mind the story of Hannah bringing Samuel to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:24–28). Behind it all is Malachi’s promise: “the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple” (Malachi 3:1). He comes in the arms of his mother, and two old people are the only ones who notice.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–24: the parents keep the law.
  • vv. 25–28: Simeon, led by the Spirit, takes the child.
  • vv. 29–32: the canticle of Simeon.
  • vv. 33–35: the word to Mary: the sign and the sword.
  • vv. 36–38: Anna the prophet.

Key words.

  • paraklēsis, consolation: The word for what Israel was waiting for, as in Isaiah 40:1: “Comfort, O comfort my people”. Simeon’s whole life is described by what he was looking toward.
  • despota / apolyeis, Master / you are dismissing: A slave’s word for his owner, and the verb for releasing a slave from duty; it is also used of dying. Simeon is a watchman, told at last that he may go off duty.
  • sēmeion antilegomenon, a sign that will be opposed: Literally “a sign spoken against”. The joy of the canticle is immediately followed by the shadow of the Cross. Jesus will be a sign before which people have to decide.
  • lytrōsis, redemption: “The redemption of Jerusalem” (v. 38): the release of a captive or the buying back of what was lost. Anna’s hearers shared Simeon’s hope.

Verse by verse.

“and they offered a sacrifice according to what is stated in the law of the Lord, “a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons.”” (2:24)

This is the offering Leviticus 12:8 allows to those who cannot afford a lamb. Luke makes no comment.

“Simeon took him in his arms and praised God, saying,” (2:28)

A priest might be expected at this moment, but Luke names no priest, only an old man guided by the Spirit. He holds in his arms the One he has waited for all his life.

“Master, now you are dismissing your servant in peace, according to your word; for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples,” (2:29–31)

Simeon is looking at a baby and he calls him salvation. The Church has long prayed this canticle at the close of the day, so that each evening is a small rehearsal for the last one.

“a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for glory to your people Israel.” (2:32)

Isaiah’s two horizons meet (42:6; 46:13; 49:6). The Gentiles are named first, but it is Israel’s own glory that shines for them. The salvation of the nations comes through Israel, and it does not replace Israel.

“This child is destined for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and to be a sign that will be opposed” (2:34)

Falling and rising recall Isaiah’s stone (8:14), the same image Paul uses in the third reading. Luke does not say that Israel as a whole falls. “Many” fall and rise, and the words apply to every hearer of the Gospel.

“a sword will pierce your own soul too.” (2:35)

The only word in the infancy account spoken directly to Mary about her own pain. The Church reads it in the light of the Cross (CCC 618; Lumen Gentium 58).

“She never left the temple but worshiped there with fasting and prayer night and day.” (2:37)

The Greek can mean that she was eighty-four years old, as the NRSV has it, or that she had been a widow for eighty-four years. Either way, she stands here as the picture of a long and faithful waiting. A man and a woman together greet the Lord, as so often in Luke.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism gathers up the scene: with Simeon and Anna all Israel awaits its encounter with the Savior, the name given to this event in the Byzantine tradition; the sword foretold for Mary announces Christ’s perfect and unique offering on the Cross (CCC 529). It also says that it was in the Temple that Joseph and Mary presented him, forty days after his birth (CCC 583). In a homily for this feast St Sophronius of Jerusalem invites all to hasten to meet Christ carrying lights, and to take him up in our arms as Simeon did (in substance). St Ambrose says that whoever wishes to be released in peace must come to the Temple and take the Word of God into the arms of faith (in substance).

Questions people ask

Does the Bible teach that childbirth is sinful or that women are unclean?

No. The purity laws deal with worship, not with guilt. Many things that are good and necessary made a person ritually unclean for a time, including burying the dead, which was a work of mercy. The Scriptures treat children as a blessing from the Lord (Psalm 127:3). In the Temple today, a mother and her child are at the centre of God’s saving work.

Did Mary need to be purified?

The Church teaches that Mary was preserved from all sin (CCC 491–493). St Thomas Aquinas taught that the law of purification did not strictly bind her, since her child-bearing was unique, but that she kept it willingly, in humility and obedience, just as her Son was subject to the Law (Summa Theologiae III, q. 37, a. 4; in substance).

Does Romans say that God has rejected the Jewish people?

No. Paul asks that very question and answers: “By no means!” (Romans 11:1). “The gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29). The Second Vatican Council rests its teaching on these chapters: the Jews remain most dear to God, and they must not be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

This feast falls in Denha, the season of the Lord’s dawning, and its theme is the same: the Light shows itself. The feast is also known as the Entrance, for the Lord enters his own house. At each Holy Qurbana we too come to the Temple, and in the Mysteries we receive the same Lord whom Simeon held in his arms. Parents who bring a newborn child to the church for baptism are walking the road of Mary and Joseph. St John Paul II set this day aside in 1997 as the World Day for Consecrated Life, so today the Church also gives thanks for those who, like Anna, have given their lives to God in prayer and service.

For study and teaching

  • Mary and Joseph offered what the poor could offer. What small and honest offering can I bring to God today, without being ashamed that it is small?
  • Isaiah’s Servant does not break the bruised reed. Who around me is a bruised reed, and how do I treat that person?
  • Paul’s first response to those who did not accept his message was prayer. How do I respond to those who do not share my faith?
  • Simeon and Anna waited for a lifetime. What helps me to keep hoping when God seems slow?
