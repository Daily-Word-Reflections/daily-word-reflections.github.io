Word for the day

“By the tender mercy of our God, the dawn from on high will break upon us, to give light to those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death, to guide our feet into the way of peace.” Luke 1:78–79

Reflection

Filled with the Holy Spirit, Zechariah sings: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favourably on his people and redeemed them.” He sees God's ancient promises, to David, to Abraham, being fulfilled. Then he turns to his child: “And you, child, will be called the prophet of the Most High.” The song ends with an image of dawn: “the dawn from on high will break upon us.”

Paul tells the Galatians: “As many of you as were baptised into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ… you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise.” The promises Zechariah sang about are ours through baptism. The Church sings his Benedictus every morning at dawn.

Full homily

Dear friends, St Paul tells the Galatians: in Christ Jesus you are all children of God through faith; as many of you as were baptised into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ (Galatians 3:26–27). Baptism is like putting on new clothes: Christ himself.

Then he says something revolutionary for his time: there is no longer Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female; for all of you are one in Christ Jesus (Galatians 3:28). In Christ the walls fall: caste, class, nationality, wealth. In Kerala, where divisions of caste and community have run deep, even in the Church, this word challenges us.

In the Gospel Zechariah, his tongue freed, sings the Benedictus: blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favourably on his people and redeemed them (Luke 1:68). The man who was silent for nine months now sings the history of salvation.

He sings of the oath sworn to Abraham, of deliverance from our enemies, so that we might serve him without fear, in holiness and righteousness all our days (Luke 1:73–75). Salvation frees us to serve without fear.

Then he turns to his tiny son: and you, child, will be called the prophet of the Most High, for you will go before the Lord to prepare his ways. And he ends with the dawn: by the tender mercy of our God, the dawn from on high will break upon us, to give light to those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death (Luke 1:76–79).

Christmas is near: the dawn from on high. Let us pray the Benedictus each morning this week, perhaps as a family. And let us ask: whom do I treat as less than a child of God? In Christ, all are one. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's Gospel continues directly from last Sunday's, where John was born and named. Zechariah, silent for months, opens his mouth and sings. Paul's few verses to the Galatians tell us what the song has come to mean for us: all those clothed with Christ have become the heirs of the promise made to Abraham. God has remembered his promise, and now it reaches everyone.

Galatians 3:26–29 · Clothed with Christ

Setting.

Paul is writing to Gentile Christians in Galatia who were being told that they had to accept circumcision and the Law of Moses to be true children of Abraham. Chapter 3 is his answer: the promise to Abraham came before the Law, and it is fulfilled in Christ. Today's verses are the high point. Many scholars think v. 28 echoes words already used at baptisms in the early Church.

Structure.

  • v. 26: all are children of God through faith.
  • v. 27: baptism has clothed you with Christ.
  • v. 28: the old dividing lines no longer decide who belongs.
  • v. 29: therefore you are Abraham's offspring and heirs.

Key words.

  • enedysasthe, you have clothed yourselves: The ordinary word for putting on a garment. Clothes in the Bible stand for what a person is and how he is seen (compare Isaiah 61:10). The baptized now go about clothed in Christ, and the Father sees his Son in them.
  • heis, one: Paul does not say that you are all one thing, in the neuter, but uses the masculine: one person, one new man, in Christ Jesus.
  • klēronomoi, heirs: Heirs do not earn an inheritance. They receive it because of who they are. This is what Paul has been defending all through the chapter.

Verse by verse.

“As many of you as were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ.” (3:27)

Paul does not offer an idea here, but points to something that has happened to them, in water, on a day they can remember.

“There is no longer Jew or Greek, there is no longer slave or free, there is no longer male and female; for all of you are one in Christ Jesus.” (3:28)

Paul does not mean that these differences have disappeared. He still writes as a Jew, and to men and women. He means that none of them gives anyone a better place before God. The phrase "male and female" is the wording of Genesis 1:27.

“And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham’s offspring, heirs according to the promise.” (3:29)

Gentile Christians are brought into the promise made to Israel, and they do not take it away from Israel. Compare the olive tree in Romans 11:17–18.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the baptized have "put on Christ" (CCC 1227), and that the white garment of baptism is the sign of it (CCC 1243). The Second Vatican Council draws the conclusion from this verse: in Christ and in the Church there is no inequality on the basis of race or nationality, social condition or sex (Lumen Gentium 32).

Luke 1:67–79 · The dawn from on high

Setting.

In the Temple, Zechariah did not believe the angel and was struck dumb (1:20). When he writes the name "John" at the child's circumcision, his tongue is freed (1:63–64). Luke says he was "filled with the Holy Spirit". The first words of the old priest, after his silence, are not a complaint or an explanation but a blessing of God. The song is full of the language of the Psalms and the prophets.

Structure.

  • vv. 68–75: God is blessed for what he has done: he has visited his people, raised up a savior from David's house, and kept his oath to Abraham.
  • vv. 76–77: the song turns to the child: he will go ahead of the Lord.
  • vv. 78–79: the promised dawn.

Key words.

  • epeskepsato, he has looked favorably: Literally, he has visited. The same verb comes back at v. 78, where the dawn "will break upon us", so the visit of God frames the song. In v. 78 the manuscripts differ between "will visit" and "has visited"; the promise holds either way.
  • keras sōtērias, a mighty savior: Literally, "a horn of salvation", as in Psalm 18:2. An animal's horn stands for strength. Here it is the strength of a king from David's line.
  • anatolē, dawn: The rising of the sun or a star. The Greek Old Testament uses the same word for the messianic "Branch" (Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 3:8), so it may carry both pictures: a shoot rising from the ground and a light rising in the sky.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has looked favorably on his people and redeemed them.” (1:68)

The verbs are in the past tense, although Jesus is not yet born. For the prophet, what God has begun is as good as done. Names are at work too: Zechariah means "the Lord has remembered", and the song is about God remembering (v. 72).

“And you, child, will be called the prophet of the Most High; for you will go before the Lord to prepare his ways,” (1:76)

An old man speaks to an eight-day-old baby. The child is great, but only as one who goes ahead. The song is not finally about John; it soon moves past him to the dawn.

“By the tender mercy of our God, the dawn from on high will break upon us, to give light to those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death, to guide our feet into the way of peace.” (1:78–79)

The picture comes from Isaiah 9:2. The last word of the song is "peace", which is also what the angels will sing at the birth of Christ (Luke 2:14). "Tender mercy" is, literally, mercy felt in the depths of one's being.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sees John as the last of the prophets and the one who goes before the Lord in the Spirit (CCC 523, 717–719). The Latin Church sings this canticle at Morning Prayer every day, as the sun comes up. For us it is a good prayer of the last days before the feast of the Lord's birth.

For study and teaching

  • "Clothed with Christ." In what part of my daily life do I most easily forget what I am wearing?
  • Zechariah's long silence ended in praise. What has God been teaching me in my own silences this season?
