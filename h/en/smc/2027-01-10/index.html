Word for the day

“And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father's only son, full of grace and truth.” John 1:14

Reflection

Denha celebrates the manifestation of the Lord, and today's Gospel puts its heart into one sentence: “And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory.” The Greek verb means that the Word pitched his tent among us. Israel already knew a God who travels with his people. In the Book of Numbers the tribes leave Sinai “with the ark of the covenant of the LORD going before them three days' journey, to seek out a resting place for them”, and the cloud of the Lord is over them by day. Whenever the ark set out, Moses prayed: “Arise, O LORD, let your enemies be scattered.” God did not stay on the mountain; he went ahead of his people on the road.

Isaiah confesses the same mystery from the other side: “Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior.” The Creator whose hands stretched out the heavens is beyond our sight, and yet he saves: “Israel is saved by the LORD with everlasting salvation.” John answers this longing of every human heart: “No one has ever seen God. It is God the only Son, who is close to the Father's heart, who has made him known.” The hidden God has shown his face in Jesus. That is Denha: the God who hides himself becomes visible in a child of Nazareth, in a man baptised in the Jordan, in the Lord who walks the road with us.

The Letter to the Hebrews compares Moses and Jesus. Moses was faithful in God's house as a servant; “Christ, however, was faithful over God's house as a son, and we are his house if we hold firm the confidence and the pride that belong to hope.” John says the same: “The law indeed was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.” The law was a gift; Christ is the Giver himself. “From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace.” In the Holy Qurbana that fullness is offered to us again: the Word who pitched his tent among us dwells on our altar and in our hearts, and we become his house, his tent among the nations.

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, the season of Denha keeps one question before us: how has God shown himself to us? Today's Gospel answers in a single line: the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory (John 1:14).

Long before Bethlehem, God was already a travelling God. In the Book of Numbers Israel leaves Sinai with the ark of the covenant going before them to seek out a resting place for them, and the cloud of the Lord over them by day (Numbers 10:33–34). Moses even begs his relative Hobab to come along: come with us, and we will treat you well. And whenever the ark set out, Moses prayed: Arise, O Lord! God did not stay on the mountain; he went ahead of his people.

Yet Isaiah also knows the pain of faith: truly, you are a God who hides himself (Isaiah 45:15). Many of us know that feeling. We pray, and God seems silent. John gives the answer: no one has ever seen God; it is God the only Son, close to the Father's heart, who has made him known (John 1:18). If you want to know what God is like, look at Jesus.

The Letter to the Hebrews tells us that Moses was faithful in God's house as a servant, but Christ is faithful over God's house as a son, and we are his house (Hebrews 3:5–6). John says: the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ (John 1:17). The law showed the way; Jesus is the Way himself.

From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace (John 1:16). Every Holy Qurbana is that fullness given again. The Word who pitched his tent among us comes to dwell in us, and we leave the church as his house, carrying him into our streets and homes.

So this week, before you leave home each morning, pray the prayer of Moses: Arise, O Lord, go before me today. And each night count one grace you received. We are his house; let us let him live in it. Amen.

For children

Children, long ago God's people carried a special golden box called the ark, and it went in front of them on the road to show that God was with them. Then God did something even better: his Son Jesus became a baby and came to live with us!

When you walk to school, say: Jesus, walk in front of me today. He will!

Prayer

O Lord our God, you went before your people in the ark and in the cloud, and in the fullness of time your eternal Word became flesh and pitched his tent among us. Grant that we who have received from his fullness grace upon grace may hold firm our hope, become your living house, and walk with him until we see your glory face to face. To you, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, be glory, now and always and for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The name Denha means dawning: the Lord shows himself. Today's four readings ask how God can be with us and still be God. In Numbers the ark goes ahead of a people on the road, under the cloud. In Isaiah the Creator who stretched out the heavens is confessed as “a God who hides himself”. Hebrews sets Moses the faithful servant beside Jesus the Son. And John gathers all these threads in one sentence: the Word became flesh and pitched his tent among us. The hidden God travels with us, and at last he has a face. The key words are tent, house and glory.

Numbers 10:29–36 · The ark goes before them

Setting.

Israel has been at Sinai since Exodus 19. At Numbers 10:11, in the second year after leaving Egypt, the cloud lifts and the people set out at last. This reading is the moment of departure: the book's turn from receiving God's law at the mountain to following him through the desert. The rest of Numbers tells how hard that road will be.

Structure.

  • vv. 29–32: Moses asks Hobab to come with them as a guide.
  • vv. 33–34: three days' march, with the ark going before and the cloud above.
  • vv. 35–36: the two short prayers of Moses, as the ark sets out and as it rests.
  • ¶ In the Hebrew manuscripts vv. 35–36 are marked off by special signs (inverted letters), and an old rabbinic tradition treats them almost as a book of their own.

Key words.

  • ’ărôn bĕrît YHWH, ark of the covenant of the Lord: The chest holding the tablets of the covenant (Deuteronomy 10:1–5), the sign of God's presence with his people. In Numbers it travels; God is not fixed to one mountain.
  • mĕnûḥâ, resting place: The goal of the journey (v. 33). The ark itself looks for rest for the people. Psalm 95:11 and Hebrews 3–4 will take up this word: the promised rest is more than a piece of land.
  • qûmâ YHWH, Arise, O Lord: A battle cry that became a prayer. It returns in Psalm 68:1 and Psalm 132:8.

Verse by verse.

“Do not leave us, for you know where we should camp in the wilderness, and you will serve as eyes for us.” (10:31)

There is a lovely tension here. The ark goes ahead and the cloud gives direction, and yet Moses asks an experienced desert man for help. God's guidance does not make human skill and friendship unnecessary. Hobab is a Midianite; the people of God, from the beginning, travel with outsiders' help. Judges 1:16 suggests that his descendants did come.

“So they set out from the mount of the Lord three days’ journey with the ark of the covenant of the Lord going before them three days’ journey, to seek out a resting place for them,” (10:33)

God scouts the way for his people. The picture is of a God who goes ahead, not one who waits at the end.

“Arise, O Lord, let your enemies be scattered, and your foes flee before you.” (10:35)

Moses prays this each time the ark sets out. It is not a prayer of confidence in Israel's strength; the march depends on God going first.

“Return, O Lord of the ten thousand thousands of Israel.” (10:36)

And this each time it rests. The people's day begins and ends with a prayer about God's presence.

How the Church reads it.

The Second Vatican Council teaches that Israel on its way through the desert was already called the Church of God, and that the Church too is a people on pilgrimage (Lumen Gentium 9). The Catechism sees the cloud of the Old Testament as a sign of the Spirit, who both reveals and hides God (CCC 697). Christian tradition also sees in Mary a new ark of the covenant, “the place where the glory of the Lord dwells” (CCC 2676), for in her the Word took flesh.

Isaiah 45:11–17 · A God who hides himself

Setting.

We are again in the Book of Consolation, among the exiles of Babylon. This chapter opens with a startling claim: the Persian king Cyrus, a foreigner who does not know the Lord, is called God's “anointed” (45:1). It seems some of the exiles objected, for vv. 9–10 scold anyone who questions the potter. Our reading answers them: the Creator has the right to use whom he chooses.

Structure.

  • v. 11: will you question me about my children?
  • vv. 12–13: I made the earth, and I have raised up this man to let the exiles go.
  • v. 14: the nations will come and confess that God is with Zion.
  • v. 15: the confession: you are a God who hides himself.
  • vv. 16–17: idol makers are shamed; Israel is saved for ever.

Key words.

  • bārā’, to create: The verb used in the Old Testament only with God as subject (Genesis 1:1). In v. 12 he himself says it: “created humankind”.
  • ’ēl mistattēr, a God who hides himself: The verb form suggests a God who actively keeps himself hidden. He is not absent; his ways cannot be read off from the surface of events. Who would have looked for salvation in a Persian conqueror?
  • tešû‘at ‘ôlāmîm, everlasting salvation: Literally “salvation of ages”. The name Yeshua, Jesus, comes from the same root.

Verse by verse.

“I have aroused Cyrus in righteousness, and I will make all his paths straight; he shall build my city and set my exiles free, not for price or reward, says the Lord of hosts.” (45:13)

The Hebrew says only “him”; the NRSV supplies the name Cyrus from the context, whereas the POC says “one”. Cyrus's decree allowed the exiles to return (Ezra 1:1–4). God can use even those who do not know him.

“God is with you alone, and there is no other; there is no god besides him.” (45:14)

The nations, once captors, confess it. The picture of chains is the poetry of its time, and the point is the confession. St Paul echoes these words when he says that an outsider will cry, “God is really among you” (1 Corinthians 14:25).

“Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior.” (45:15)

The verse joins two words we would keep apart: hidden and Savior. God saves in ways we fail to recognise at first.

“But Israel is saved by the Lord with everlasting salvation; you shall not be put to shame or confounded to all eternity.” (45:17)

The promise stands. The Church hears in it God's lasting fidelity to Israel, and the hope of all the nations.

How the Church reads it.

“Even when he reveals himself, God remains a mystery beyond words”, says the Catechism, and it quotes St Augustine: if you understood him, it would not be God (CCC 230). Blaise Pascal returned to this verse: he saw that God hides himself as well as shows himself, and thought any teaching that does not say so is untrue. Denha does not remove the mystery. Even in Jesus God is seen by faith, in a child, a man in the waters of the Jordan, the Holy Qurbana under the form of bread.

Hebrews 3:1–6 · Servant in the house, Son over the house

Setting.

Hebrews calls itself a “word of exhortation” (13:22). It is a sermon to Christians, probably of Jewish background, who were growing tired; the author is not named. Having shown the Son above the angels and our brother in suffering (chapters 1–2), the preacher now turns to Moses, the greatest figure of Israel. He uses Numbers 12:7, where God himself praises Moses as the one entrusted with all his house. This is not a put-down of Moses. Moses is the standard, the highest comparison there is.

Structure.

  • v. 1: consider Jesus.
  • v. 2: both he and Moses were faithful.
  • vv. 3–4: the builder has more honour than the house, and God is the builder of all.
  • vv. 5–6a: Moses as a servant in the house, Christ as a son over it.
  • v. 6b: we are that house, if we hold firm.

Key words.

  • apostolos kai archiereus, apostle and high priest: The only place in the New Testament where Jesus is called “apostle”, the one sent. Sent from God to us, as high priest he goes from us to God.
  • oikos, house: The word returns again and again in these verses. It means both a building and a household. The people themselves are God's home.
  • therapōn, servant: Not the common word for a slave, but an honoured attendant, the word used of Moses in the Greek of Numbers 12:7.

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, brothers and sisters, holy partners in a heavenly calling, consider that Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession,” (3:1)

“Consider” is the whole aim of the sermon. Tired Christians are told to look hard and long at Jesus.

“Yet Jesus is worthy of more glory than Moses, just as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself.” (3:3)

Moses belongs to the house; Christ belongs to its builder's side. The next verse says plainly that the builder of all things is God.

“Now Moses was faithful in all God’s house as a servant, to testify to the things that would be spoken later.” (3:5)

Moses points forward. His whole service is a witness to what God would say at last in his Son (compare 1:1–2).

“Christ, however, was faithful over God’s house as a son, and we are his house if we hold firm the confidence and the pride that belong to hope.” (3:6)

A note on the text. Some ancient manuscripts add “to the end”, and the POC has these words; the NRSV follows the older witnesses that lack them. In either form the warning is the same. “Confidence” translates parrēsia, the boldness of those who have nothing to hide.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism calls Jesus the Father's Emissary, the one on whose mission all the Church's mission depends (CCC 858). It also lists “the house of God in which his family dwells” among the images of the Church (CCC 756). Hebrews gives this image a condition: we are that house “if we hold firm”. Faith is a long fidelity, like that of Moses on the road.

John 1:14–18 · The Word pitched his tent among us

Setting.

These verses are the climax of John's Prologue (1:1–18), the opening of the Gospel, written as a kind of hymn. Until now the Word has been spoken of as being with God, as the maker of all things and as the light. At v. 14 comes the sentence for which the Prologue was written. The Word's coming among us is the true manifestation that Denha celebrates. The Old Testament background is the Exodus: tent, glory, Moses, law, and God's refusal to let Moses see his face (Exodus 33:18–23; 34:6).

Structure.

  • v. 14: the Word became flesh, and we have seen his glory.
  • v. 15: John's witness.
  • v. 16: from his fullness, grace upon grace.
  • v. 17: Moses and Jesus Christ.
  • v. 18: no one has seen God; the Son has made him known.

Key words.

  • sarx, flesh: Not a pious word for “a person”, but the human condition in its weakness and mortality. John chooses the strongest word.
  • eskēnōsen, lived, pitched his tent: From skēnē, tent, the word for the tent of meeting in the Greek Old Testament. The sound also recalls the Hebrew for dwelling. What the ark and the tent stood for, the flesh of Jesus now is.
  • charis kai alētheia, grace and truth: Many scholars hear in this the Hebrew ḥesed we’emet, “steadfast love and faithfulness”, by which God describes himself to Moses in Exodus 34:6.
  • exēgēsato, has made him known: Literally “he led out, he explained”. Our word “exegesis” comes from this verb. Jesus himself is the Father's exegesis.

Verse by verse.

“And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father’s only son, full of grace and truth.” (1:14)

“We” is the voice of those who saw: the community of witnesses. The verb “became” is strong, yet the Word does not cease to be God. The glory Moses asked to see is now seen, above all in the Cross (compare 12:23–24).

“From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace.” (1:16)

Literally “grace in place of grace”. Scholars debate the sense: an unending supply of grace, or the grace of Christ following the grace of the Law. Both agree on abundance.

“The law indeed was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.” (1:17)

The Greek has no “but”. The Law is God's gift; in Christ the gift reaches its fullness. This is the first time the Gospel uses the name Jesus Christ.

“No one has ever seen God. It is God the only Son, who is close to the Father’s heart, who has made him known.” (1:18)

A note on the text. The oldest Greek manuscripts have “only-begotten God”, which the NRSV and the POC follow in their own ways. Many others read “only-begotten Son”. Both are firmly Catholic faith. The literal image is of the Son “in the Father's bosom”, the place of closest love, the place the Beloved Disciple has at the Supper (13:23).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that the Church calls the Son's assuming a human nature the Incarnation, and it quotes this verse (CCC 461); Jesus is true God and true man, not part God and part man (CCC 464). It calls the Son the Father's “one, perfect and unsurpassable Word” (CCC 65), and repeats that only he has seen the Father and made him known (CCC 151). St Athanasius says that the Son of God became man so that we might become God (quoted in CCC 460). St John Chrysostom notes that the word “lived among us” guards the mystery: the Word did not change into something else, but truly dwelt in our flesh (in substance).

Questions people ask

If “no one has ever seen God”, what of Moses and Isaiah?

They experienced God through signs: the cloud, the fire, the vision. Exodus 33:11 speaks of friendship, and 33:20 denies that Moses saw God's face. John says that all this looked toward Jesus: “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (14:9).

Did the Word turn into a human being and stop being God?

No. The Word remained what he was and took up what he was not. The Church confesses one person in two natures, truly divine and truly human (CCC 464–469).

Is Isaiah's “hidden God” the same as a God who does not care?

No. The verse says hidden and Savior in one breath. The Psalms and the prophets, and the Cross itself, teach that God is at work even when we do not see him.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

At Denha the Syro-Malabar Church celebrates the baptism of the Lord and his manifestation as the Son. In many Kerala parishes the feast is kept with lights, in the custom of Pindikuthi, a reminder that the Light has come into our dark. Today's Gospel gives the reason. On this Sunday, as we stand before the altar in the Holy Qurbana, we receive the same flesh of which John says, “we have seen his glory”. The hidden God still comes under signs, and those who receive him are his house.

For study and teaching

  • Moses asked Hobab, “you will serve as eyes for us”. Who has God sent to be eyes for me on my road? For whom can I be eyes?
  • Isaiah calls God hidden and Savior at once. When has God been at work in my life in a way I recognised only later?
  • Hebrews tells tired Christians to “consider” Jesus. How could I give more time to looking at him?
  • John says Jesus is the one who has explained God. What in the Gospel has changed my picture of who God is?
