Word for the day

“Without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever.” Hebrews 7:3

Reflection

The Letter to the Hebrews reaches back to one of the most mysterious figures of the Old Testament. In Genesis 14 Abram returns from rescuing his nephew Lot, and a stranger comes out to meet him: “And King Melchizedek of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was priest of God Most High.” He blesses Abram, and Abram gives him a tenth of everything. Then Melchizedek vanishes from the story. Centuries later Psalm 110 speaks of a king whom God has sworn to make “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” Hebrews brings these two passages together and sees in them a portrait of Christ. Melchizedek's name means “king of righteousness”; he is king of Salem, that is, “king of peace”. Scripture gives him no father or mother, no genealogy, no birth or death: “resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever.” The writer does not say Melchizedek was an angel or a ghost. He says that Scripture's silence about his origin and end makes him a living picture of the true Priest, who has no beginning as the eternal Son and no end as the Risen One.

Then the letter argues, in the careful way of a rabbi, that this priesthood is greater than the priesthood of Levi. Abraham, the father of the whole people and the one who “had received the promises”, received a blessing from Melchizedek and gave him tithes: “It is beyond dispute that the inferior is blessed by the superior.” Levi, still unborn, was in a sense in Abraham and paid tithes through him. Then comes a line that rings with Easter: “In the one case, tithes are received by those who are mortal; in the other, by one of whom it is testified that he lives.” The Levitical priests died one after another, and their office passed from father to son. Christ's priesthood does not pass to anyone, for, as the same chapter says a little later, “he holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever. Consequently he is able for all time to save those who approach God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them.” The Resurrection is not only the end of Christ's suffering; it is the beginning of his eternal priesthood. At this moment the Risen Lord stands before the Father, interceding for us. The Fathers of the Church loved to point out that Melchizedek brought out bread and wine. In every Holy Qurbana the eternal Priest offers himself under those same signs; our priests stand at the altar not in their own name but in his.

In the Gospel Jesus answers those who challenged him for healing on the sabbath. He speaks of witnesses, as in a court of law: the Law required two or three witnesses before a charge could stand. “If I testify about myself, my testimony is not true.” So he calls his witnesses. First John the Baptist: “He was a burning and shining lamp, and you were willing to rejoice for a while in his light.” Then a greater witness: “The works that the Father has given me to complete, the very works that I am doing, testify on my behalf that the Father has sent me.” And behind the works, the Father himself: “And the Father who sent me has himself testified on my behalf.” Jesus is not seeking his own glory: “I seek to do not my own will but the will of him who sent me.” The Church reads this passage in Easter time because the Resurrection is the Father's great testimony to his Son. When the Father raised Jesus from the dead, he declared before the whole world that this was his Son. The apostles then became witnesses in their turn, and so must we. The world will not believe because we argue well; it believes when it sees the works that the Risen Lord still does through his Church.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Letter to the Hebrews takes us back to a mysterious king who met Abraham with bread and wine.

Melchizedek, king of Salem, “brought out bread and wine; he was priest of God Most High.” His name means king of righteousness; Salem means peace. Scripture tells us nothing of his birth or death, so Hebrews says he resembles the Son of God and remains a priest forever. He is a picture of Christ.

Hebrews then says something full of Easter joy: the old priests were mortal, but Christ is “one of whom it is testified that he lives.” He holds his priesthood for ever, and “he always lives to make intercession” for us. Right now, the Risen Lord is praying for you before the Father.

And in every Holy Qurbana, the eternal Priest offers himself under the signs of bread and wine, as Melchizedek once foreshadowed. Our priests stand at the altar in his name.

In the Gospel Jesus calls his witnesses: John the Baptist, “a burning and shining lamp”; the works the Father gave him to do; and the Father himself. At Easter the Father gave his greatest testimony: he raised his Son from the dead.

Now it is our turn to be witnesses. Let us pray today for our priests, lean on Christ's intercession when we cannot pray, and let our works point to him. Alleluia! Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Today's readings are joined by the word witness. Hebrews looks at Melchizedek, the priest-king who blessed Abraham, and draws its conclusion from a short phrase: he is one “of whom it is testified that he lives”. In John, Jesus names the witnesses that stand behind him: John the Baptist, the works the Father gave him to do, and the Father himself. In the Easter season both point to the same truth: the priest who lives for ever is the risen Son. The key words are priest, testimony and works.

Hebrews 7:1–10 · A priest according to the order of Melchizedek

Setting.

Hebrews is a long sermon in written form, whose author is not named. Its great theme is that Christ is our high priest. Jesus did not belong to the priestly tribe of Levi, so the author turns to Psalm 110:4, “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”, and to the only story about Melchizedek, in Genesis 14:18–20.

In Genesis, Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, meets Abraham after a battle, brings out bread and wine, and blesses him; Abraham gives him a tenth of everything. Hebrews reads the passage closely, paying attention even to what it does not say.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: who Melchizedek is: his names, and the silence about his origin and end.
  • vv. 4–7: his greatness: he received a tenth from Abraham and blessed him.
  • vv. 8–10: he “lives”; even Levi paid tithes through Abraham.

Key words.

  • malkî-ṣedeq, Melchizedek: In Hebrew the name means “my king is righteousness”; Hebrews renders it “king of righteousness”. Salem sounds like shālôm, peace. Righteousness and peace are the gifts of the Messiah (Psalm 85:10).
  • apatōr, amētōr, without father, without mother: Genesis gives Melchizedek no parents and no genealogy, unlike the priests of Levi, whose descent had to be proven. Hebrews reads the silence as a sign pointing to Christ, whose priesthood does not come by descent.
  • aphōmoiōmenos, resembling: “Made like” the Son of God. Melchizedek is the copy; Christ is the original. The figure points forward to the one who fulfils it.

Verse by verse.

“Without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever.” (7:3)

The author reasons from what Scripture leaves unsaid, a known way of reading in his time. He is not saying Melchizedek was an angel or a heavenly being; he is saying the text has been shaped to point beyond him.

“It is beyond dispute that the inferior is blessed by the superior.” (7:7)

Abraham, the father of the people of the promise, bows to receive a blessing. The priesthood of Christ is greater than any priesthood before it.

“In the one case, tithes are received by those who are mortal; in the other, by one of whom it is testified that he lives.” (7:8)

In Easter light the phrase points to Christ, who “always lives to make intercession” for us (7:25).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism says that Christian tradition sees Melchizedek, “priest of God Most High”, as a figure of the priesthood of Christ (CCC 1544), and that the Church sees in his offering of bread and wine a prefiguring of her own offering (CCC 1333). St Cyprian, writing on the cup of the Lord, saw in Melchizedek's bread and wine an image of the Eucharistic sacrifice (in substance). Every priest who offers the Holy Qurbana stands within the one priesthood of Christ, which is not passed on by birth but given by the Lord.

John 5:30–38 · The witnesses to the Son

Setting.

Jesus has healed a man at the pool of Bethzatha on a sabbath and has called God his own Father. In John's language “the Jews” who start to persecute him (5:16–18) are the religious authorities in Jerusalem, not the Jewish people; the healed man, the disciples and Jesus himself were Jews.

Jesus now speaks as one on trial. Under the Law a matter was settled by the evidence of two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15), so he brings his witnesses.

Structure.

  • v. 30: the Son does nothing on his own; he seeks the Father's will.
  • vv. 31–32: self-testimony is not enough; there is another witness.
  • vv. 33–35: John the Baptist, the burning and shining lamp.
  • v. 36: the works that the Father gave him.
  • vv. 37–38: the Father himself, whose word they do not keep.

Key words.

  • martyria, testimony: A key word of John's Gospel. The whole Gospel is presented as testimony so that the reader may believe (19:35; 20:31).
  • lychnos, lamp: John the Baptist is a lamp, lit from another flame. The Prologue says “He himself was not the light, but he came to testify to the light” (1:8). Jesus is the light itself.
  • erga, works: John's word for the signs and deeds of Jesus. They are works the Father has given him “to complete”, a verb from the same root as “It is finished” on the Cross (19:30).

Verse by verse.

“I can do nothing on my own. As I hear, I judge; and my judgment is just, because I seek to do not my own will but the will of him who sent me.” (5:30)

The Son is never independent of the Father. This is not weakness but the deepest unity.

“He was a burning and shining lamp, and you were willing to rejoice for a while in his light.” (5:35)

“For a while”: interest in a good preacher can fade. A lamp is meant to lead us to the light.

“The works that the Father has given me to complete, the very works that I am doing, testify on my behalf that the Father has sent me.” (5:36)

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the signs worked by Jesus attest that the Father has sent him and invite belief in him (CCC 548). The greatest of the Father's testimonies is the Resurrection, by which he raised his Son. The Church in her turn is a witness: the apostles bore testimony to the Risen One, and every baptized person is sent to do the same.

Questions people ask

Was Melchizedek a real person, or Christ himself appearing in the Old Testament?

Genesis presents him as a real king of Salem, which tradition identifies with Jerusalem. A few ancient writers thought he was a heavenly figure, but the mainstream of the Church's tradition reads him as a man whom Scripture describes in a way that makes him a figure of Christ. Hebrews says he “resembles” the Son of God; it does not say he is the Son.

For study and teaching

  • John the Baptist was a lamp that pointed beyond himself. Whom does my life point to?
  • Which “works” of God in my own life bear witness to me that the Father has sent his Son?
