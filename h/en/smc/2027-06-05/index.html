Third Saturday of the Apostles: the Feast of the Immaculate Heart of Mary

Readings

  • First Reading Genesis 17:15–22 · Sarah shall give rise to nations
  • Second Reading Isaiah 7:10–16 · The Lord himself will give you a sign: Immanuel
  • Epistle Ephesians 1:2–8 · Chosen in Christ to be holy and blameless
  • Gospel Luke 2:41–51 · His mother treasured all these things in her heart

Word for the day

“Then he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them. His mother treasured all these things in her heart.” Luke 2:51

Reflection

Yesterday the Church looked on the pierced Heart of Jesus; today she turns to the Heart of his Mother. The two belong together. When the infant Jesus was presented in the Temple, old Simeon said to Mary, “and a sword will pierce your own soul too” (Luke 2:35), and at the foot of the Cross that sword went through her heart as the spear went through his. The devotion to the Heart of Mary is old in the Church. St John Eudes in the seventeenth century gave it a liturgical form; at Fatima in 1917 the Mother of God spoke to three shepherd children of her Immaculate Heart; Pope Pius XII consecrated the world to the Immaculate Heart in 1942 and extended the feast to the whole Church in 1944, and Pope Francis renewed the consecration of humanity, and in particular of Russia and Ukraine, on 25 March 2022. The word Immaculate reminds us that this heart was never stained by sin. By a singular grace and in view of the merits of Christ, Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception. The word Heart reminds us of her inner life: the place where she received the word of God, kept it, pondered it and answered it with her whole self. In the Syro-Malabar calendar this is an important Marian feast, and it falls fittingly in the Season of the Apostles, for after the Ascension the apostles “were constantly devoting themselves to prayer, together with certain women, including Mary the mother of Jesus” (Acts 1:14). The first Church was gathered around the heart of the Mother.

The first reading is God's promise to Abraham about his wife: “As for Sarai your wife, you shall not call her Sarai, but Sarah shall be her name. I will bless her, and moreover I will give you a son by her. I will bless her, and she shall give rise to nations; kings of peoples shall come from her.” Abraham falls on his face and laughs: “Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? Can Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?” God holds to his word: Sarah will bear Isaac, and the everlasting covenant will pass through him. God does not forget Ishmael either: “As for Ishmael, I have heard you; I will bless him.” The Church reads this passage today because Sarah, the barren woman who becomes a mother of nations by God's power, is a figure of the Mother of the Messiah. What God began with Sarah's laughter he completed in Mary's faith. To her the angel said what was said to Abraham long before: “For nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37). Mary became the mother not of one nation but of all, for from the Cross Jesus gave her to the beloved disciple and, through him, to the whole Church: “Here is your mother” (John 19:27).

In the second reading King Ahaz of Judah is terrified because two neighbouring kings have joined against him. The prophet Isaiah invites him to trust God and even to ask for a sign, as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven. Ahaz refuses with pious words, “I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test”, because he has already decided to trust in a foreign empire rather than in God. Isaiah answers: “Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel.” The Hebrew word speaks of a young woman of marriageable age; the ancient Greek translation made by Jewish scholars before Christ rendered it “virgin”; and St Matthew, quoting that translation, sees the sign fulfilled in Mary: “Look, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall name him Emmanuel,” which means, “God is with us” (Matthew 1:23). The Church professes that Mary conceived by the Holy Spirit without the seed of man and remained a virgin for ever. And notice the contrast between two hearts. Ahaz's heart was closed by fear and calculation; he would not even ask. Mary's heart was open; she asked, “How can this be?”, and then said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (Luke 1:38).

The Epistle is the great blessing that opens the Letter to the Ephesians: “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” This is God's plan for the whole Church: to be holy and without blemish. What is promised to all of us, and will be completed only at the end, has already been accomplished in one person. Mary was chosen before the foundation of the world, and from the first moment of her existence she was holy and blameless before God in love. She too was redeemed; Paul's next words apply to her first of all: “In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace that he lavished on us.” She was redeemed, as the Church says, in a more exalted way: not by being washed from sin but by being preserved from it, through the blood her Son would shed. Her Immaculate Heart is the masterpiece of grace, “to the praise of his glorious grace.” That is why honouring Mary never takes anything from Christ; it praises what his grace can do.

The Gospel is the only story from the hidden years of Jesus. At twelve he goes up with Mary and Joseph to Jerusalem for the Passover and stays behind. For three days they search for him. When they find him in the Temple among the teachers, his mother speaks: “Child, why have you treated us like this? Look, your father and I have been searching for you in great anxiety.” Mary does not hide her pain, and she names Joseph first: your father and I. Jesus answers, “Why were you searching for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father’s house?” And Luke tells us honestly: “But they did not understand what he said to them.” The Immaculate Heart is not a heart that understood everything at once, nor a heart without anxiety. It is a heart that did not close itself in the dark. “Then he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them. His mother treasured all these things in her heart.” She kept what she did not yet understand, and turned it over in prayer until God gave the light. Parents who worry about children growing up and going their own way, mothers who have searched through a long night for a son or daughter, can bring their anxiety to her. And all of us can learn from her the way to hold God's word: not to throw it away when it is hard, but to treasure it, ponder it and wait.

Full homily

Dear friends, yesterday we looked at the Heart of Jesus; today we look at the Heart of his Mother. Simeon said to Mary: a sword will pierce your own soul too (Luke 2:35). The Heart of the Son and the heart of the Mother are joined in love and in suffering.

Her heart is called Immaculate because it was never stained by sin. God chose her before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in love (Ephesians 1:4), and preserved her from all sin from her conception, through the merits of Jesus, her Son and Saviour.

In the first reading God promises old Sarah a son, and Abraham laughs. But nothing is impossible with God. Sarah becomes the mother of a people; Mary becomes the Mother of all peoples, for Jesus gave her to us from the Cross: here is your mother.

Isaiah tells frightened King Ahaz: the Lord himself will give you a sign; the young woman shall bear a son, Immanuel. Saint Matthew sees it fulfilled in the Virgin Mary. Ahaz's heart was closed by fear; Mary's heart was open: here am I, the servant of the Lord (Luke 1:38).

In the Gospel Mary and Joseph search for the boy Jesus for three days in great anxiety. When they find him, they do not understand his words. But his mother treasured all these things in her heart (Luke 2:51). Mary did not understand everything, but she kept God's word and waited.

Parents, when you are worried about your children, bring them to Mary's heart. All of us, when God's ways are hard, let us keep his word as she did. And let us pray the rosary each day for peace, as she asked. Immaculate Heart of Mary, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Mary and Joseph lost Jesus for three days, and they were very worried. When they found him, Mary kept everything in her heart.

Mary is our Mother too. When you are worried or sad, tell her: “Mother Mary, keep me in your heart.” And pray a Hail Mary for peace in the world!

Prayer

God our Father, you prepared a worthy dwelling for your Son in the Immaculate Heart of the Virgin Mary, chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in love. As you gave Sarah a son and gave Ahaz a sign, you gave us Immanuel through the faith of Mary. Give us hearts like hers, open to your word, faithful in anxiety, and treasuring what we do not yet understand. Mother of God and Mother of the Church, as you prayed with the apostles in the Upper Room, pray with us now: keep our families in your heart, bring peace to the nations, and lead us to your Son. Through Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The day after the feast of the Sacred Heart, the Church turns to the Heart of the Mother. The four readings trace how God prepared and filled that heart. In Genesis, God gives Sarai a new name and promises that the barren woman will become the mother of nations, the bearer of the covenant child. In Isaiah, when King Ahaz refuses to trust, God himself gives a sign: a young woman will bear a son called Immanuel. Ephesians blesses the Father who chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love. And in Luke, after three days of searching, Mary finds her Son in the Temple, does not fully understand his words, and treasures all these things in her heart.

Mary’s heart is called immaculate because God preserved it from every stain of sin from the first moment of her existence, by the merits of her Son. But Luke shows us that this pure heart was not spared sorrow, searching or the effort of faith. It is a heart that keeps God’s word and ponders it, and so it is the model for ours.

Genesis 17:15–22 · Sarah shall give rise to nations

Setting.

Genesis 17 tells of God’s covenant with Abraham when he was ninety-nine years old (17:1). God changes his name from Abram to Abraham, “ancestor of a multitude” (17:5), and gives circumcision as the sign of the covenant. Abraham already has a son, Ishmael, born to Sarai’s servant Hagar, now thirteen years old (16:15–16; 17:25). Many years have passed since the first promise, and Sarai is far past the age of childbearing.

In today’s verses the promise becomes precise: the covenant child will be born of Sarah herself, and he will be called Isaac.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: Sarai is renamed Sarah; God will bless her and give Abraham a son by her; she will become nations.
  • v. 17: Abraham falls on his face and laughs: can a man of a hundred and a woman of ninety have a child?
  • v. 18: Abraham’s plea for Ishmael.
  • vv. 19–21: God’s answer: Sarah will bear a son, Isaac, heir of the everlasting covenant; Ishmael too will be blessed.
  • v. 22: God goes up from Abraham.

Key words.

  • śārâ, Sarah, princess: Both Sarai and Sarah are usually understood as forms of the word for “princess”. The new name marks a new mission: she will be the mother of kings.
  • yiṣḥāq, Isaac, “he laughs”: Abraham laughs in disbelief (v. 17), and Sarah will laugh too (18:12). God gives the child a name that turns their laughter into joy: “God has brought laughter for me” (21:6).
  • bĕrît ‘ôlām, everlasting covenant: A covenant without end, binding God to Abraham’s descendants. God’s faithfulness does not depend on human possibility.

Verse by verse.

“As for Sarai your wife, you shall not call her Sarai, but Sarah shall be her name.” (17:15)

God names Sarah himself. In this chapter she is no longer only Abraham’s wife; she has her own place in the covenant.

“I will bless her, and she shall give rise to nations; kings of peoples shall come from her.” (17:16)

Twice God says “I will bless her”. The woman who seemed to have no future becomes a mother of peoples.

“Can Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?” (17:17)

Abraham’s laughter is not mockery but astonishment. The question will be answered at Mamre: “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” (18:14), words echoed by the angel to Mary: “nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37).

“your wife Sarah shall bear you a son, and you shall name him Isaac. I will establish my covenant with him as an everlasting covenant for his offspring after him.” (17:19)

The covenant passes through a child born not by human strength but by God’s promise. Paul will say that believers in Christ are, like Isaac, “children of the promise” (Galatians 4:28).

“As for Ishmael, I have heard you; I will bless him and make him fruitful and exceedingly numerous” (17:20)

God hears the father’s prayer for his other son. Ishmael is not rejected; he too receives a blessing and will become a great nation.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that throughout the Old Covenant the mission of many holy women prepared for that of Mary, and names Sarah among them: by virtue of God’s promise, she conceives a son in spite of her old age (CCC 489). God chooses those who seem powerless to show his faithfulness. Sarah’s late motherhood and Mary’s virginal motherhood are both signs that the child of the covenant is God’s gift. Mary in her Magnificat praises the God who keeps the promise “to Abraham and to his descendants forever” (Luke 1:55).

Isaiah 7:10–16 · The Lord himself will give you a sign

Setting.

The year is about 735 BC. Rezin king of Aram (Damascus) and Pekah king of Israel (the northern kingdom) have marched against Jerusalem, wanting to force Judah into their alliance against Assyria. “The heart of Ahaz and the heart of his people shook as the trees of the forest shake before the wind” (7:2). Isaiah went to meet the young king and told him not to fear: “If you do not stand firm in faith, you shall not stand at all” (7:9).

Ahaz had other plans. He would soon send silver and gold to the king of Assyria and ask for his help (2 Kings 16:7–8), making Judah a vassal. When Isaiah offers him a sign from God, he refuses, hiding behind pious words.

Structure.

  • vv. 10–11: God offers Ahaz a sign, as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven.
  • v. 12: Ahaz refuses: I will not put the Lord to the test.
  • v. 13: Isaiah’s rebuke to the house of David: you weary my God.
  • v. 14: the Lord himself will give the sign: the young woman will bear a son, Immanuel.
  • vv. 15–16: before the child can choose good from evil, the lands of the two kings will be deserted.

Key words.

  • ’ôt, sign: A visible pledge of God’s word. God offers it to strengthen faith, not to satisfy curiosity.
  • ‘almâ, young woman: A young woman of marriageable age. The Greek translation made in Egypt before Christ, the Septuagint, rendered it parthenos, “virgin”, and Matthew quotes it in that form (Matthew 1:23). See the question below.
  • ‘immānû ’ēl, Immanuel: “God with us”. Isaiah repeats the name in 8:8 and 8:10. Matthew begins his Gospel with Immanuel (1:23) and ends it with Jesus’ promise, “I am with you always” (28:20).

Verse by verse.

“Ask a sign of the Lord your God; let it be deep as Sheol or high as heaven.” (7:11)

God is generous: he lets Ahaz choose any sign at all. Sheol is the world of the dead; heaven the highest place.

“But Ahaz said, I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test.” (7:12)

The words sound devout (Deuteronomy 6:16), but they hide a refusal to trust. Ahaz has already decided to rely on Assyria.

“Hear then, O house of David! Is it too little for you to weary mortals, that you weary my God also?” (7:13)

Isaiah now addresses not Ahaz alone but the whole royal house, the line to which God had promised a lasting throne (2 Samuel 7:16).

“Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel.” (7:14)

When the king refuses, God gives the sign anyway. Many scholars think the first meaning was a child soon to be born in the royal house, perhaps Hezekiah, as a pledge that God had not abandoned David’s line. The Church, following Matthew, sees the full meaning in the virgin birth of Jesus, who is God with us in person.

“He shall eat curds and honey by the time he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good.” (7:15)

Curds and honey may mean simple food in a land laid waste, or the food of plenty. Either way, within a few years the threat from the two kings will be gone.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Church sees in the virginal conception of Jesus the fulfilment of the divine promise given through the prophet Isaiah: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son” (CCC 497). The Second Vatican Council names Isaiah 7:14 among the Old Testament texts that, read in the light of later revelation, bring the figure of the Mother of the Redeemer into ever clearer light (Lumen Gentium 55). St Justin Martyr discussed this verse at length with the Jewish teacher Trypho, and St Irenaeus defended the reading “virgin” against those who preferred “young woman” (in substance, Justin, Dialogue with Trypho 66–67; Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.21).

Ephesians 1:2–8 · Chosen to be holy and blameless

Setting.

The Letter to the Ephesians opens, after the greeting, with one of the longest sentences in the New Testament: in Greek, verses 3 to 14 are a single sentence of praise. It takes the form of a Jewish blessing, “Blessed be God”, like the prayers that begin “Blessed are you, Lord”. It praises the Father for his plan, carried out in the Son and sealed by the Holy Spirit.

The letter presents itself as written by Paul from prison (3:1). Many scholars think it was written after his death by a disciple who gathered up his teaching; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. Either way, the Church receives it as inspired Scripture.

Structure.

  • v. 2: greeting: grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.
  • v. 3: blessed be God, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing.
  • v. 4: he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.
  • vv. 5–6: he destined us for adoption as his children, to the praise of his glorious grace in the Beloved.
  • vv. 7–8: in him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of trespasses, the riches of grace lavished on us.

Key words.

  • eulogētos, blessed: The word that opens Jewish blessings. God has blessed us (eulogēsas); we bless him in return. Praise is the right response to grace.
  • amōmos, blameless: Literally “without blemish”, the word used for sacrificial animals offered to God without defect. Here it describes the holiness God wants for his people (5:27).
  • echaritōsen, he bestowed grace: A rare verb, “to fill with grace”. Its only other use in the New Testament is the angel’s greeting to Mary: kecharitōmenē, “favoured one”, full of grace (Luke 1:28).

Verse by verse.

“who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places” (1:3)

Every gift comes “in Christ”, a phrase that runs through this whole passage like a refrain.

“just as he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love.” (1:4)

Before anything existed, God had us in mind. Holiness is not our invention but his first purpose for us, and it is lived “in love”.

“He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ” (1:5)

Adoption was a well-known legal act in the Roman world, giving the adopted child full rights as heir. In Christ we become sons and daughters of God.

“to the praise of his glorious grace that he freely bestowed on us in the Beloved.” (1:6)

“The Beloved” recalls the Father’s voice at the Baptism of Jesus (Mark 1:11). All grace reaches us through the beloved Son.

“In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses” (1:7)

Redemption means a ransom that releases captives. The price is Christ’s own blood, the blood that flowed from his side.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism applies these very verses to Mary: the Father blessed Mary more than any other created person “in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places” and chose her “in Christ before the foundation of the world, to be holy and blameless before him in love” (CCC 492). It teaches, with Blessed Pius IX’s definition of 1854, that Mary was preserved from all stain of original sin from the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ (CCC 491). She too is redeemed, but in a more exalted way, by the blood of her Son (CCC 492; Lumen Gentium 53). What God did fully in her at the start, he wants to do in all of us by the end.

Luke 2:41–51 · His mother treasured all these things in her heart

Setting.

This is the only story in the Gospels from the years between Jesus’ infancy and his public ministry. The Law asked every Israelite man to go up to the sanctuary for the three great feasts (Deuteronomy 16:16), and devout families went to Jerusalem at least for Passover. Pilgrims from Galilee travelled in large groups of relatives and neighbours, about three or four days’ journey each way.

Jesus is twelve, close to the age at which a Jewish boy took on the duties of the Law. Luke has already told us that Mary treasured and pondered what happened at the birth (2:19); now he ends the infancy stories with the same note.

Structure.

  • vv. 41–42: the yearly Passover pilgrimage; Jesus is twelve.
  • vv. 43–45: Jesus stays behind; his parents travel a day, search among relatives, return to Jerusalem.
  • vv. 46–47: after three days they find him in the Temple among the teachers; all are amazed.
  • vv. 48–50: Mary’s question and Jesus’ answer about his Father; they do not understand.
  • v. 51: he goes down to Nazareth and is obedient to them; his mother treasures all these things in her heart.

Key words.

  • odynōmenoi, in great anxiety: A strong word for pain and anguish. Luke uses it again of the elders of Ephesus weeping at Paul’s farewell (Acts 20:38). Mary’s anxiety is the sorrow of love, not of sin. Simeon had foretold that a sword would pierce her soul (2:35).
  • en tois tou patros mou, in my Father’s house: Literally “in the things of my Father”. It can mean “in my Father’s house” (NRSV) or “about my Father’s affairs”, as the POC renders it. Both are true: Jesus belongs wholly to his Father.
  • dietērei, treasured: To keep carefully, to hold on to. In 2:19 Luke used a related verb (synetērei), adding that she pondered these things. Mary does not throw away what she cannot yet understand; she keeps it until its meaning is revealed.

Verse by verse.

“After three days they found him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions.” (2:46)

Many have seen in the three days a foreshadowing of the three days between the cross and the Resurrection, when Jesus was again lost to those who loved him and found again.

“Child, why have you treated us like this? Look, your father and I have been searching for you in great anxiety.” (2:48)

Mary speaks first, as a mother would. She calls Joseph “your father”: he is truly the father who raises and protects Jesus, as the Church honours him.

“Why were you searching for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father’s house?” (2:49)

These are Jesus’ first words in Luke’s Gospel. Mary said “your father”; Jesus speaks of “my Father”. The word “must” will return in his Passion: the Son of Man must suffer (9:22).

“But they did not understand what he said to them.” (2:50)

Luke is honest: even Mary and Joseph did not grasp everything at once. Faith is a journey.

“Then he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them. His mother treasured all these things in her heart.” (2:51)

The Son of God lives in obedience in an ordinary home for many years. Mary’s heart becomes the place where the mysteries of her Son are kept and slowly understood.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the finding in the Temple is the only event that breaks the silence of the Gospels about the hidden years of Jesus. Mary and Joseph did not understand his words, but they accepted them in faith, and Mary “kept all these things in her heart” during the years Jesus remained hidden in an ordinary life (CCC 534). Jesus’ obedience to his mother and legal father fulfils the fourth commandment perfectly (CCC 532). The Second Vatican Council says that Mary advanced in her pilgrimage of faith and faithfully kept her union with her Son even to the cross (Lumen Gentium 58). Pope St Paul VI, at Nazareth in 1964, spoke of the lessons of silence, family life and work taught by this hidden home.

Questions people ask

Does Isaiah 7:14 say “virgin” or “young woman”?

The Hebrew word ‘almâ means a young woman of marriageable age; the usual Hebrew word for virgin is another one. But the Greek translation made by Jewish scholars before Christ, the Septuagint, rendered it parthenos, virgin, and Matthew quotes this form to show its fulfilment in Mary (Matthew 1:23). The Church does not rest the virgin birth on one word of Isaiah, but on the testimony of Matthew and Luke, and reads the prophecy in its light: the sign promised to David’s house is fulfilled beyond all expectation when the Son of God is born of a virgin (CCC 497).

If Mary’s heart was immaculate, why did she not understand Jesus’ words?

Freedom from sin is not the same as knowing everything. Mary was full of grace, but she still lived by faith, not by sight. Luke shows her searching, asking, not understanding at once, and keeping what she did not grasp until God made it clear. The Council says that she advanced in her pilgrimage of faith (Lumen Gentium 58). That makes her close to us: a pure heart can still be a searching heart.

What does devotion to the Immaculate Heart of Mary mean?

It honours Mary’s inner life: her love for God and her Son, her faith, her sorrow and her care for us, all of it untouched by sin and wholly turned to God. St John Eudes in the seventeenth century promoted the liturgical honour of the Hearts of Jesus and Mary. After the apparitions at Fatima in 1917, Pope Pius XII consecrated the world to the Immaculate Heart in 1942 and extended the feast to the whole Latin Church in 1944. True devotion always leads to the Heart of her Son, as Mary’s own words at Cana lead to him: “Do whatever he tells you” (John 2:5).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Mar Ephrem, the harp of the Spirit honoured by all the Syriac Churches, praised Mary’s purity in words the Church has often recalled: Christ and his Mother alone are beautiful in every way, for in him there is no blemish and in his Mother no stain (in substance, Nisibene Hymns 27). In the Season of the Apostles we remember that Mary was with the apostles in prayer before Pentecost (Acts 1:14). In many Syro-Malabar homes the family gathers each evening for the rosary; on this feast it is a fitting way to entrust our families to her heart, which kept the word of God and pondered it.

For study and teaching

  • Sarah received a new name and a mission when all hope seemed gone. Where in my life do I need to trust that nothing is impossible with God?
  • Ahaz refused a sign because he had already decided to rely on his own plans. Where am I tempted to trust my own strategies rather than God?
  • Ephesians says God chose us to be holy and blameless in love. How does Mary help me believe that holiness is God’s plan for me too?
  • Mary did not understand at once but kept everything in her heart. What event in my life do I need to keep and ponder before God rather than judge too quickly?
