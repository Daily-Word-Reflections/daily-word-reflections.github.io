Word for the day

“You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” Matthew 5:14

Reflection

Today our Church keeps the feast of Mar Aprem, St Ephrem the Syrian, whom the Syriac tradition calls “the Harp of the Spirit”. For the Syro-Malabar Church he is not a distant figure. He is one of the fathers of our own Syriac heritage: his hymns and his way of praying shaped the liturgy of the Church of the East, from which our Holy Qurbana, our Hours and our feasts have come down to us. Ephrem was born around the year 306 in Nisibis, a frontier city between the Roman and Persian empires, in what is now south-eastern Turkey. He grew up in the Christian community of that city, was formed by its bishop, Mar Jacob of Nisibis, and served the Church as a deacon and a teacher. When Nisibis was handed over to Persia in 363, Ephrem went with the Christians who left the city and settled in Edessa (Urhai), where he taught and wrote for the last ten years of his life. Tradition says that he died in 373, after spending himself in caring for the hungry and the sick during a famine. In 1920 Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the universal Church.

Ephrem was a theologian who sang. He wrote verse homilies (memre), commentaries on Scripture, and above all hundreds of madrashe, teaching hymns set to melodies that the people knew. He trained choirs of women to sing them in the church, so that the faith of Nicaea, the mystery of the Incarnation, the virginity of Mary and the gift of the sacraments would be carried in the memory and the heart of ordinary believers. He did not reduce God to definitions. He knew that God is beyond our grasp, and that the best way to speak of him is often through the symbols that God himself has placed in Scripture and in nature: the pearl, the light, the fire, the bread, the robe, the womb, the river. His hymns teach us to stand before the mystery with wonder rather than curiosity, with love rather than argument. That is why the Church calls him a Doctor: not only because he taught truly, but because he taught us how to adore.

The readings of his feast are well chosen. Sirach praises Moses, “whose memory is blessed”, whom the Lord “allowed to hear his voice, and led him into the dark cloud, and gave him the commandments face to face, the law of life and knowledge, so that he might teach Jacob the covenant, and Israel his decrees.” Ephrem too entered the cloud of the divine mystery and came back to teach. Deuteronomy calls Israel “a people holy to the Lord your God”, a treasured people set high “in praise and in fame and in honor”. Ephrem's hymns are one long effort to make the Church that holy, praising people. And Paul gives the source of it all: “hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” In this Season of the Apostles, after Pentecost, we see in Ephrem what the Spirit does when he is poured into a heart: he turns it into an instrument of praise, a harp on which the Spirit himself plays.

The Gospel is from the Sermon on the Mount: “You are the salt of the earth… You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” Jesus adds a word that fits a teacher of the Church: “whoever does them and teaches them will be called great in the kingdom of heaven.” Ephrem did not only teach; he lived what he taught. He remained a deacon, a servant, all his life, and his last work was not a hymn but the care of the hungry in a time of famine. His light shone through good works, “so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” His love for Mary was tender and strong; he sang of her as the one who carried the Fire that does not burn, and he could say to Christ that he and his Mother alone are entirely beautiful, without any stain (in substance). In his hymns on the Eucharist he marvels that the Spirit and the fire dwell in the bread and in the cup, and that we eat and drink the Lord himself.

The weekday readings add their own note. Paul says, “I die every day!”, and asks the Corinthians to come “to a sober and right mind”, because the dead will be raised (1 Corinthians 15:31–34). In the Gospel Jesus walks on the water, and Peter, sinking, cries out, “Lord, save me!” (Matthew 14:30). Ephrem's life, lived on a troubled frontier, through sieges, exile and famine, was a long walk on the water, held by the hand of Christ. He invites us today to sing our faith, to love the liturgy we have received, to teach it to our children, and to let the Holy Spirit make each of us a harp in the hands of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate Mar Aprem, St Ephrem the Syrian. He was born around the year 306 in Nisibis, served as a deacon and a teacher, and spent his last years in Edessa. He died in 373, and the Church declared him a Doctor of the Church. The Syriac tradition calls him “the Harp of the Spirit”.

Ephrem taught the faith in a special way: he sang it. He wrote hundreds of hymns, set them to tunes the people knew, and trained choirs to sing them. In this way ordinary people learned the truth about Jesus, about Mary and about the sacraments, not only in their heads but in their hearts.

Saint Paul tells us where such praise comes from: “God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us” (Romans 5:5). When the Spirit fills a heart, it begins to sing. Ephrem's heart became a harp, and the Spirit played on it.

Sirach praises Moses, to whom God spoke face to face, who received “the law of life and knowledge” in order to teach the people (Sirach 45:5). Ephrem too entered the mystery of God in prayer and came back to teach. He did not try to explain God away; he stood before God with wonder and love.

In the Gospel Jesus says: “You are the salt of the earth… You are the light of the world” (Matthew 5:13–14). Ephrem's light shone not only in his hymns but in his service. At the end of his life, during a famine, he cared for the hungry and the sick.

Our Church has received the Syriac liturgy that Ephrem helped to shape. Let us love it, pray it with understanding, and teach it to our children. Let us sing our faith, and let our good works give glory to our Father in heaven. Mar Aprem, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Mar Aprem loved Jesus so much that he wrote songs about him, hundreds of songs! He taught people to sing them in church, so that they could learn about God by singing.

When you sing in church, sing with your whole heart, because singing is praying twice. And like Mar Aprem, help someone who is hungry or sad. Then your light will shine for Jesus!

Prayer

Lord God, you poured your love into the heart of Mar Aprem through the Holy Spirit and made him a harp of praise, a teacher of the true faith and a servant of the hungry. By his prayers, give us hearts that sing your wonders, minds that ponder your mysteries with humility, and hands that serve the poor. Make our families and parishes love the holy liturgy we have received from our fathers; keep us faithful to the faith of the apostles; and with the Virgin Mary, whom he praised so tenderly, let us glorify you in the Church, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.
