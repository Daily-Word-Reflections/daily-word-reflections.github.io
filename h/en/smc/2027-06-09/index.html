Word for the day

“You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” Matthew 5:14

Reflection

Today our Church keeps the feast of Mar Aprem, St Ephrem the Syrian, whom the Syriac tradition calls “the Harp of the Spirit”. For the Syro-Malabar Church he is not a distant figure. He is one of the fathers of our own Syriac heritage: his hymns and his way of praying shaped the liturgy of the Church of the East, from which our Holy Qurbana, our Hours and our feasts have come down to us. Ephrem was born around the year 306 in Nisibis, a frontier city between the Roman and Persian empires, in what is now south-eastern Turkey. He grew up in the Christian community of that city, was formed by its bishop, Mar Jacob of Nisibis, and served the Church as a deacon and a teacher. When Nisibis was handed over to Persia in 363, Ephrem went with the Christians who left the city and settled in Edessa (Urhai), where he taught and wrote for the last ten years of his life. Tradition says that he died in 373, after spending himself in caring for the hungry and the sick during a famine. In 1920 Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the universal Church.

Ephrem was a theologian who sang. He wrote verse homilies (memre), commentaries on Scripture, and above all hundreds of madrashe, teaching hymns set to melodies that the people knew. He trained choirs of women to sing them in the church, so that the faith of Nicaea, the mystery of the Incarnation, the virginity of Mary and the gift of the sacraments would be carried in the memory and the heart of ordinary believers. He did not reduce God to definitions. He knew that God is beyond our grasp, and that the best way to speak of him is often through the symbols that God himself has placed in Scripture and in nature: the pearl, the light, the fire, the bread, the robe, the womb, the river. His hymns teach us to stand before the mystery with wonder rather than curiosity, with love rather than argument. That is why the Church calls him a Doctor: not only because he taught truly, but because he taught us how to adore.

The readings of his feast are well chosen. Sirach praises Moses, “whose memory is blessed”, whom the Lord “allowed to hear his voice, and led him into the dark cloud, and gave him the commandments face to face, the law of life and knowledge, so that he might teach Jacob the covenant, and Israel his decrees.” Ephrem too entered the cloud of the divine mystery and came back to teach. Deuteronomy calls Israel “a people holy to the Lord your God”, a treasured people set high “in praise and in fame and in honor”. Ephrem's hymns are one long effort to make the Church that holy, praising people. And Paul gives the source of it all: “hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” In this Season of the Apostles, after Pentecost, we see in Ephrem what the Spirit does when he is poured into a heart: he turns it into an instrument of praise, a harp on which the Spirit himself plays.

The Gospel is from the Sermon on the Mount: “You are the salt of the earth… You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” Jesus adds a word that fits a teacher of the Church: “whoever does them and teaches them will be called great in the kingdom of heaven.” Ephrem did not only teach; he lived what he taught. He remained a deacon, a servant, all his life, and his last work was not a hymn but the care of the hungry in a time of famine. His light shone through good works, “so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” His love for Mary was tender and strong; he sang of her as the one who carried the Fire that does not burn, and he could say to Christ that he and his Mother alone are entirely beautiful, without any stain (in substance). In his hymns on the Eucharist he marvels that the Spirit and the fire dwell in the bread and in the cup, and that we eat and drink the Lord himself.

The weekday readings add their own note. Paul says, “I die every day!”, and asks the Corinthians to come “to a sober and right mind”, because the dead will be raised (1 Corinthians 15:31–34). In the Gospel Jesus walks on the water, and Peter, sinking, cries out, “Lord, save me!” (Matthew 14:30). Ephrem's life, lived on a troubled frontier, through sieges, exile and famine, was a long walk on the water, held by the hand of Christ. He invites us today to sing our faith, to love the liturgy we have received, to teach it to our children, and to let the Holy Spirit make each of us a harp in the hands of God.

Full homily

Dear friends, today we celebrate Mar Aprem, St Ephrem the Syrian. He was born around the year 306 in Nisibis, served as a deacon and a teacher, and spent his last years in Edessa. He died in 373, and the Church declared him a Doctor of the Church. The Syriac tradition calls him “the Harp of the Spirit”.

Ephrem taught the faith in a special way: he sang it. He wrote hundreds of hymns, set them to tunes the people knew, and trained choirs to sing them. In this way ordinary people learned the truth about Jesus, about Mary and about the sacraments, not only in their heads but in their hearts.

Saint Paul tells us where such praise comes from: “God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us” (Romans 5:5). When the Spirit fills a heart, it begins to sing. Ephrem's heart became a harp, and the Spirit played on it.

Sirach praises Moses, to whom God spoke face to face, who received “the law of life and knowledge” in order to teach the people (Sirach 45:5). Ephrem too entered the mystery of God in prayer and came back to teach. He did not try to explain God away; he stood before God with wonder and love.

In the Gospel Jesus says: “You are the salt of the earth… You are the light of the world” (Matthew 5:13–14). Ephrem's light shone not only in his hymns but in his service. At the end of his life, during a famine, he cared for the hungry and the sick.

Our Church has received the Syriac liturgy that Ephrem helped to shape. Let us love it, pray it with understanding, and teach it to our children. Let us sing our faith, and let our good works give glory to our Father in heaven. Mar Aprem, pray for us. Amen.

For children

Children, Mar Aprem loved Jesus so much that he wrote songs about him, hundreds of songs! He taught people to sing them in church, so that they could learn about God by singing.

When you sing in church, sing with your whole heart, because singing is praying twice. And like Mar Aprem, help someone who is hungry or sad. Then your light will shine for Jesus!

Prayer

Lord God, you poured your love into the heart of Mar Aprem through the Holy Spirit and made him a harp of praise, a teacher of the true faith and a servant of the hungry. By his prayers, give us hearts that sing your wonders, minds that ponder your mysteries with humility, and hands that serve the poor. Make our families and parishes love the holy liturgy we have received from our fathers; keep us faithful to the faith of the apostles; and with the Virgin Mary, whom he praised so tenderly, let us glorify you in the Church, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

On the feast of Mar Aprem, deacon, teacher and poet of our Syriac tradition, the readings speak of God’s word received, kept and handed on. In Deuteronomy, Moses sets before Israel the covenant: the Lord will be their God, and they will be his treasured people, holy to him. Sirach praises Moses himself, the meek and faithful man to whom God gave “the law of life and knowledge” so that he might teach it. Paul tells what the new covenant gives: peace with God, hope that does not disappoint, and the love of God poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit. And in Matthew, Jesus tells his disciples that they are salt and light, and that he has come not to abolish the Law and the prophets but to fulfil them. A teacher of the Church like Mar Aprem stands in this line: he received the word, let it fill his heart, and made it shine for others.

Deuteronomy 26:16–19 · A people holy to the Lord

Setting.

Deuteronomy is presented as Moses’ farewell speeches on the plains of Moab, before Israel crosses the Jordan. Its long central section of laws (chapters 12–26) ends with these verses. They are like the signing of a covenant: what God has said and what the people have promised are summed up in two solemn declarations.

The word “today” is repeated. Deuteronomy speaks to every generation as if it stood at the Jordan on that day, hearing the covenant for the first time.

Structure.

  • v. 16: this very day: keep these laws with all your heart and soul.
  • v. 17: today you have declared that the Lord is your God and that you will walk in his ways.
  • vv. 18–19: today the Lord has declared that you are his treasured people, to be set high in praise, fame and honour, and holy to him.

Key words.

  • he’ĕmartā, you have obtained agreement, you have declared: A rare form of the verb “to say”, found only here and in verse 18. Literally “you have caused (the Lord) to say”. Each partner in the covenant leads the other to speak a binding word.
  • sĕgullâ, treasured people, special possession: A word for a king’s private treasure. God used it at Sinai: “you shall be my treasured possession out of all the peoples” (Exodus 19:5). Israel is precious to God, not because it is great, but because he loves it (Deuteronomy 7:6–8).
  • ‘am qādôš, a holy people: Holy means set apart for God. Israel’s holiness is first God’s choice, and then a way of life that answers it.

Verse by verse.

“This very day the Lord your God is commanding you to observe these statutes and ordinances; so observe them diligently with all your heart and with all your soul.” (26:16)

“With all your heart and with all your soul” echoes the Shema (6:5). The commandments are to be kept from within, not only outwardly.

“Today you have obtained the Lord’s agreement: to be your God; and for you to walk in his ways” (26:17)

The covenant is a bond of two parties. God binds himself to be Israel’s God; Israel binds itself to walk with him.

“Today the Lord has obtained your agreement: to be his treasured people, as he promised you” (26:18)

“As he promised” points back to the promises to Abraham. The covenant at Moab fulfils what God had sworn to the fathers.

“and for you to be a people holy to the Lord your God, as he promised.” (26:19)

Israel is lifted high not for its own glory but so that the nations may see the holiness of its God.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads Israel’s covenant with reverence: the Old Covenant has never been revoked (CCC 121), and the people of Israel remain the people to whom God first spoke (CCC 839, quoting the Roman Missal). The New Testament applies the language of this passage to the Church, called in Christ “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God’s own people” (1 Peter 2:9; CCC 803). The Church does not take Israel’s place; through Christ the Gentiles are grafted into the one olive tree (Romans 11:17–18).

Sirach 45:1–5 · Moses, whose memory is blessed

Setting.

Chapters 44 to 50 of Sirach are a hymn in praise of the ancestors of Israel: “Let us now sing the praises of famous men” (44:1). Ben Sira walks through the history of Israel from Enoch and Noah to the high priest Simon, his own contemporary. These verses are given to Moses, after the patriarchs and before Aaron, whose praise is much longer (45:6–22).

Structure.

  • v. 1: Moses, raised from Jacob’s descendants, beloved by God and people, whose memory is blessed.
  • v. 2: God made him glorious, like the holy ones, and great before his enemies.
  • v. 3: signs done at his word; glory before kings; commandments for his people; a vision of God’s glory.
  • v. 4: chosen for his faithfulness and meekness.
  • v. 5: God’s voice, the dark cloud, the commandments face to face, the law of life and knowledge, to teach Jacob and Israel.

Key words.

  • prautēs, meekness: Gentleness that does not insist on itself. Numbers 12:3 says that Moses was very humble, more than anyone on earth. Jesus will say, “Blessed are the meek” (Matthew 5:5), and “I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29).
  • gnophos, the dark cloud: The thick darkness into which Moses entered on Sinai, where God was (Exodus 20:21). The Fathers saw in it a sign that God is greater than all our ideas of him.
  • nomos zōēs kai epistēmēs, the law of life and knowledge: The Law is not a burden but a gift that gives life and understanding (compare Deuteronomy 30:15–20). It was given to Moses so that he might teach it.

Verse by verse.

“Moses, whose memory is blessed.” (45:1)

In Jewish custom one still adds “of blessed memory” to the name of a revered person who has died. Sirach gives Moses this honour.

“He gave him commandments for his people, and revealed to him his glory.” (45:3)

On Sinai Moses asked, “Show me your glory” (Exodus 33:18), and was allowed to see God’s back, not his face.

“For his faithfulness and meekness he consecrated him, choosing him out of all humankind.” (45:4)

God chooses the faithful and gentle. Moses’ authority came from God, not from force.

“and gave him the commandments face to face, the law of life and knowledge, so that he might teach Jacob the covenant, and Israel his decrees.” (45:5)

“Face to face” as with a friend (Exodus 33:11). The goal of revelation is teaching: what Moses received, he handed on.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God revealed himself to Moses as the God of the fathers and as “I AM”, and that through Moses he gave Israel the Law as a preparation for the Gospel (CCC 204–205, 1961–1964). Moses is the great intercessor of the Old Testament, and his prayer foreshadows the prayer of Christ (CCC 2574–2577). St Gregory of Nyssa, in his Life of Moses, reads Moses’ entry into the dark cloud as an image of the soul that comes ever closer to God, who is beyond understanding (in substance).

Romans 5:1–5 · Hope does not disappoint

Setting.

In the first four chapters of Romans Paul has shown that all, Jews and Gentiles, need God’s mercy, and that God makes sinners righteous through faith in Christ, as he credited faith to Abraham (chapter 4). Now he begins to describe the new life of those who have been justified. Chapter 5 begins with peace and ends with grace overflowing where sin had abounded (5:20).

Structure.

  • v. 1: justified by faith, we have peace with God through Christ.
  • v. 2: through him we have access to grace, and we boast in hope of God’s glory.
  • vv. 3–4: we boast even in sufferings: suffering, endurance, character, hope.
  • v. 5: hope does not disappoint, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit.

Key words.

  • eirēnē, peace: Not only a calm feeling but a restored relationship: enemies have become friends (5:10). It is the Hebrew shalom, wholeness given by God.
  • prosagōgē, access: The word for being brought into the presence of a king. Through Christ the door to God’s throne is open (Ephesians 2:18).
  • ekkechytai, has been poured out: A perfect tense: poured once and still overflowing. It echoes the prophecy of the Spirit poured out (Joel 2:28) and Pentecost (Acts 2:33).

Verse by verse.

“Therefore, since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,” (5:1)

Some ancient manuscripts read “let us have peace”. The difference in Greek is one vowel. Either way, peace is a gift received in Christ and then lived.

“And not only that, but we also boast in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance,” (5:3)

Paul does not praise pain for its own sake. He sees what God makes of it in a believer: a chain that leads to hope.

“and hope does not disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us.” (5:5)

“God’s love” here is God’s love for us, given as a gift to live in us. Christian hope rests not on our feelings but on this love already given.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes verse 5 to teach that love is God’s first gift, which contains all the others, and that this love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit (CCC 733). It teaches that hope keeps us from discouragement and sustains us in times of abandonment (CCC 1817–1818), and that justification has been merited for us by the Passion of Christ and is granted through Baptism (CCC 1992). St Augustine often returned to verse 5: the love of God is poured out not from ourselves but by the Holy Spirit (in substance, On the Spirit and the Letter).

Matthew 5:13–20 · Salt, light and the fulfilled Law

Setting.

Jesus has just spoken the Beatitudes on the mountain (5:1–12). Now he tells the disciples what they are in the world, and how his teaching stands toward the Law of Moses. Like Moses on Sinai, Jesus teaches from a mountain, but he speaks with his own authority.

Verses 17–20 introduce the rest of chapter 5, where Jesus takes six commandments and leads them to their full depth: “You have heard that it was said… But I say to you…”.

Structure.

  • v. 13: you are the salt of the earth; salt that loses its taste is thrown out.
  • vv. 14–16: you are the light of the world; let your light shine so that others glorify the Father.
  • vv. 17–19: I have come to fulfil the Law and the prophets; not one letter will pass away.
  • v. 20: your righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees.

Key words.

  • mōranthē, has lost its taste: The verb literally means “to become foolish”. In the Bible, wisdom is the salt of life; a disciple without it is like tasteless salt.
  • plērōsai, to fulfil: To fill full, to bring to completion. Jesus does not cancel the Law; he brings it to its goal, in his teaching and in his own life and death.
  • iōta hen ē mia keraia, one letter, one stroke of a letter: The iota (Hebrew yod) is the smallest letter; the “stroke” is the small mark that tells one Hebrew letter from another.

Verse by verse.

““You are the salt of the earth; but if salt has lost its taste, how can its saltiness be restored?” (5:13)

Salt preserves, gives taste, and was used with sacrifices (Leviticus 2:13). Jesus does not say “try to be salt”; he says “you are”. The task follows from what grace has made us.

““You are the light of the world. A city built on a hill cannot be hid.” (5:14)

Jesus calls himself the light of the world (John 8:12). The disciples shine with light that is his.

“let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father in heaven.” (5:16)

The aim is not our praise but the Father’s glory. Good works shown in humility point beyond themselves.

“I have come not to abolish but to fulfill.” (5:17)

Jesus honours the Law and the prophets as God’s word. Everything in them points to him and finds its meaning in him.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus did not abolish the Law of Sinai but fulfilled it with such perfection that he revealed its ultimate meaning (CCC 577–582, quoting this passage), and that the New Law is the perfection of the Old (CCC 1965–1968). The Second Vatican Council teaches that lay people are to make Christ known to others above all by the witness of a life shining with faith, hope and charity (Lumen Gentium 31). On verse 20, the Church explains that “the scribes and Pharisees” here stand for a righteousness measured by outward observance; Jesus asks for a righteousness of the heart, and many Pharisees too sought that (CCC 579).

Questions people ask

Who was Mar Aprem?

St Ephrem the Syrian, Mar Aprem, was born about 306 at Nisibis in Mesopotamia and served there as a deacon and teacher. When Nisibis was handed over to Persia in 363, he moved to Edessa, where he taught until his death in 373. He wrote biblical commentaries and hundreds of hymns, often sung by choirs he trained. He is called “the Harp of the Spirit”, and Pope Benedict XV declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1920.

Did Jesus abolish the Law of Moses?

No. He says plainly, “I have come not to abolish but to fulfill.” The moral law of the Ten Commandments remains and is deepened by his teaching (CCC 2052–2068). The ritual and ceremonial laws prepared for Christ and find their fulfilment in him, as the Church teaches from the Letter to the Hebrews and the Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15). The Law is not set aside; it is brought to its goal.

Paul says we are justified by faith. Then why does Jesus speak of commandments and good works?

Both are true together. We are made righteous by God’s grace, received in faith and Baptism; we do not earn the first grace (CCC 1996–2003). That grace then lives and acts in us, and the commandments are the shape of the new life. Jesus asks for a righteousness greater than outward observance: the love poured into our hearts by the Spirit (Romans 5:5).

For the Syro-Malabar reader

Mar Aprem is a teacher of our own Syriac tradition. His hymns, written in Syriac, have shaped the prayer of the Syriac Churches, East and West, and some of the poetry of our liturgical prayer goes back to him or to his school. He taught theology through poetry: he loved to say that God has clothed himself in our words and our names so that we might come to him, and he warned against prying into God with proud questions instead of praising him (in substance, Hymns on Faith).

Like Moses in Sirach, he received the word and handed it on as a teacher. Like the salt and light of the Gospel, he gave his life in service: according to an old account, at the end of his life he organised help for the sick and hungry during a famine in Edessa. On his feast our deacons, catechists and choirs can see in him a model: Scripture loved, prayed, sung and lived.

For study and teaching

  • Deuteronomy says “today” again and again. How can I hear God’s word in the Qurbana today as if it were spoken for the first time to me?
  • Moses was chosen for faithfulness and meekness. Where do I need more gentleness in the way I teach or correct others?
  • Paul says hope does not disappoint because God’s love has been poured into our hearts. What helps me to remember that love when I suffer?
  • Mar Aprem made Scripture into song. Which verse from today’s readings could I learn by heart and pray this week?
