Word for the day

“Cure the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out demons. You received without payment; give without payment.” Matthew 10:8

Reflection

Five days after Easter the Church hears a song addressed to a woman who has no children. “Sing, O barren one who did not bear; burst into song and shout, you who have not been in labor! For the children of the desolate woman will be more than the children of her that is married, says the LORD.” Isaiah was speaking to Jerusalem in exile, a city that looked finished, a mother with an empty house. And God tells her to start building extensions: “Enlarge the site of your tent, and let the curtains of your habitations be stretched out; do not hold back; lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes.” Children are coming whom she never expected. Paul reads this song as a word about the Church (Galatians 4:26–27), and the Easter liturgy reads it this week for a reason. On the night of the Resurrection the font gave birth to new sons and daughters; the Church that seemed so small on Good Friday, a handful of women and one disciple at the Cross, now has to widen her tent. The heart of the passage is the tenderness of God: “For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you.” And he binds himself with the oath he swore to Noah: “For the mountains may depart and the hills be removed, but my steadfast love shall not depart from you, and my covenant of peace shall not be removed, says the LORD, who has compassion on you.” Whatever moment of abandonment we have passed through, Easter says it was a brief moment. The steadfast love remains.

The tent grows, and growth brings trouble. In Acts the community in Jerusalem has become large and mixed: Greek-speaking believers and Aramaic-speaking believers, all of them Jews who believe in Jesus. “The Hellenists complained against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution of food.” The apostles do not deny the problem or tell the complainers to be quiet. They gather the whole community and make a decision that will shape the Church for ever. “It is not right that we should neglect the word of God in order to wait on tables. Therefore, friends, select from among yourselves seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom we may appoint to this task, while we, for our part, will devote ourselves to prayer and to serving the word.” The seven are chosen, all with Greek names, from the very group that felt neglected; the apostles pray and lay hands on them. The Church has always seen here the beginning of the diaconate. And the result? “The word of God continued to spread; the number of the disciples increased greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith.” Care for widows and the preaching of the word are not rivals; when the poor are served, the word runs faster. Paul tells the Colossians where this new life comes from: “when you were buried with him in baptism, you were also raised with him through faith in the power of God, who raised him from the dead.” God has cancelled our debt, “erasing the record that stood against us with its legal demands. He set this aside, nailing it to the cross.” On the Cross Christ “disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them, triumphing over them in it.” The newly baptised do not have to fight their old masters in their own strength. The battle has already been won.

The Gospel takes us back to the beginning of the mission. “Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” Matthew lists them by name, and among them, in the pair “Thomas and Matthew the tax collector”, is the apostle whom the Christians of Kerala call their father in faith. Jesus gives them a message and a manner. The message: “As you go, proclaim the good news, ‘The kingdom of heaven has come near.’” The manner: “You received without payment; give without payment.” They are to travel without gold, without a bag, without a spare tunic, depending on the welcome of those who receive them. They bring peace into houses: “As you enter the house, greet it. If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it.” Read in Easter week, this Gospel reminds us that the mission did not begin after the Resurrection; it was rehearsed in Galilee, and the Risen Lord now gives it its full meaning. What the apostles received without payment was, in the end, life from the dead. Every Christian has received the same: forgiveness, Baptism, the Body and Blood of the Lord, none of it earned. So everything we give, our time to a lonely widow, our money to the poor, our word of faith to a doubting friend, is only the overflow of what was given to us without payment. A Church that charges for grace has forgotten Easter; a Church that gives freely is preaching the Resurrection without saying a word.

Full homily

Dear friends, Isaiah sings to a woman with an empty house: “Sing, O barren one… Enlarge the site of your tent.” This week the Church is that woman. On Good Friday she was a few women and one disciple at the Cross. On Easter night the font gave her new children. Now God says: make room, there are more coming.

And God adds a promise: “For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you… my steadfast love shall not depart from you.” If you have been through a dark time, hear this: it was a brief moment. His love has not moved.

But a bigger family brings bigger problems. In Acts the Greek-speaking widows are being forgotten at the daily meal. The apostles listen. They do not say, ‘Stop complaining.’ They ask the community to choose seven men full of the Spirit and of wisdom, and they lay hands on them. These are the first deacons. And the word of God spreads even faster.

Paul tells us where the strength comes from. In Baptism we were buried with Christ and raised with him. God erased the record that stood against us, “nailing it to the cross.” The powers that held us are disarmed. We do not serve out of fear. We serve as children of God, no longer slaves.

Then Jesus sends the Twelve, Thomas among them, with nothing in their bags. “You received without payment; give without payment.” Everything we have from God is a gift: our life, our faith, the Holy Qurbana we receive every Sunday. Nobody paid for it.

So this week let us give something without payment. Visit one widow. Do one job with no thanks. Pray for our deacons and seminarians. A Church that gives freely is the best sermon on the Resurrection. Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told his friends: ‘You got it as a gift, so give it as a gift!’ God gives us so many presents: our family, our food, his love.

This week, give a present of kindness: help someone without being asked, and do not ask for anything back.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, you widen the tent of your Church and give her children she never expected. Thank you for the grace we received without payment: our Baptism, your forgiveness, your Body and Blood. Make us generous with what we have received. Bless the deacons of your Church and all who serve at the tables of the poor; let no widow be neglected among us. Send us, as you sent the Twelve, to bring your peace into every house we enter. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

In the week of the Resurrection the readings show what the risen Lord does with his people: he enlarges them and sends them. Isaiah calls the barren city to sing because her children will be many. In Acts the Church has grown so fast that it must organize its care for the poor, and seven men are chosen to serve. Colossians tells the newly baptized that they were buried with Christ and raised with him, and that the record of their debts was nailed to the Cross. In the Gospel Jesus calls the Twelve by name and sends them out with his own authority. The key words are enlarge, serve and send.

Isaiah 54:1–10 · Sing, O barren one

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the people of Judah in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC. Many scholars call this part of the book Second Isaiah. Its message is consolation: God has not forgotten Jerusalem, and the exile will end.

Chapter 54 follows at once the fourth Song of the Servant (52:13–53:12), the Servant who bears the sins of many. After his suffering comes this song of joy. Jerusalem is pictured as a woman who has no children, whose husband has left her, and who is ashamed. God speaks to her as husband and redeemer and promises her more children than she can count.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the barren woman is told to sing.
  • vv. 2–3: enlarge the tent; your children will fill the land.
  • vv. 4–6: do not fear; your Maker is your husband.
  • vv. 7–8: a brief moment of anger, everlasting compassion.
  • vv. 9–10: an oath like the oath after the flood: my steadfast love shall not depart from you.

Key words.

  • ‘ăqārāh, barren: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and Hannah were all barren before God gave them a child. In Scripture barrenness is the place where human hope ends and God's gift begins. Zion's joy does not come from her own strength.
  • gō’ēl, Redeemer: In Israel's law the gō’ēl was the next of kin who bought back a relative's land or bought them back from slavery (Leviticus 25:25, 47–49; Ruth 4). God names himself the nearest relative of his people, the one who pays to bring them home.
  • ḥesed, steadfast love: The faithful, loyal love of the covenant. Mountains may move, but this love will not (v. 10). It is the word Israel sings again and again in the Psalms: his steadfast love endures forever.

Verse by verse.

“Sing, O barren one who did not bear; burst into song and shout, you who have not been in labor!” (54:1)

The song comes before the children. Zion is told to rejoice over a future she cannot yet see, which is what faith in the Resurrection asks of us too.

“Enlarge the site of your tent, and let the curtains of your habitations be stretched out; do not hold back; lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes.” (54:2)

A tent is enlarged when the family grows. The Church read this in Easter week, when the newly baptized had just joined her family at the font.

“For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you.” (54:7)

The two halves of the verse are not equal. The anger is a moment; the compassion is great. The Hebrew word for compassion is related to the word for a mother's womb.

“For the mountains may depart and the hills be removed, but my steadfast love shall not depart from you, and my covenant of peace shall not be removed,” (54:10)

Nothing in creation is as firm as God's promise. The mountains are the most stable thing a person can see, and even they are less sure than his love.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul quotes the first verse of this chapter in Galatians 4:27 and applies it to “the Jerusalem above”, which he calls “our mother” (Galatians 4:26). The Church has always read the barren woman who becomes a mother of many as a figure of herself, the Bride of Christ who bears children in Baptism (CCC 796). God's oath in the days of Noah (v. 9) points the same way: the Catechism notes that the Church has seen in Noah's ark a figure of salvation by Baptism (CCC 1219; compare 1 Peter 3:20–21).

Acts 6:1–7 · Seven men to serve

Setting.

The Jerusalem community is growing, and with growth comes a quarrel. All the believers are Jewish. The “Hellenists” are Jewish believers whose language was Greek, many of them born outside the land of Israel and settled in Jerusalem. The “Hebrews” are those who spoke Aramaic and had their roots in the land. Widows without family depended on the community for their daily food, and those of the Greek-speaking group were being overlooked.

Luke does not hide the problem. He shows how the apostles dealt with it: they called the whole community together, proposed a solution and let the community choose.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the complaint about the daily distribution.
  • vv. 2–4: the proposal of the Twelve: seven men for this task, while the apostles keep to prayer and the word.
  • vv. 5–6: the choice of the seven; prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • v. 7: the word spreads; even many priests believe.

Key words.

  • diakonia, service, distribution: The same Greek word is used for the daily distribution of food (v. 1) and for the “serving” of the word (v. 4); the verb for waiting on tables (v. 2) is from the same root. Both are service. The apostles do not say that the tables are beneath them, only that each task needs its own servants.
  • Hellēnistai, Hellenists: Here the word means Greek-speaking Jews. All seven chosen men have Greek names, which suggests that the community gave the task to people from the very group that had complained.
  • epethēkan tas cheiras, they laid their hands: Moses laid his hands on Joshua to hand on his task (Numbers 27:18–23). The gesture with prayer shows that the seven receive their work from God through the apostles, not merely from a vote.

Verse by verse.

“It is not right that we should neglect the word of God in order to wait on tables.” (6:2)

This is not contempt for practical work. The apostles see that if they do everything, the preaching of the word will suffer and the widows will still be overlooked.

“select from among yourselves seven men of good standing, full of the Spirit and of wisdom,” (6:3)

Even the care of tables needs men full of the Spirit. In the Church, practical service is a spiritual task.

“They had these men stand before the apostles, who prayed and laid their hands on them.” (6:6)
“The word of God continued to spread; the number of the disciples increased greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith.” (6:7)

Luke closes with a summary of growth. The note about the priests is striking: men who served in the Temple came to believe in the risen Jesus.

How the Church reads it.

The text does not use the noun “deacon”, but from early times the Church saw in these seven the beginning of the diaconate. St Irenaeus already speaks of Nicolaus as one of the seven first ordained to the diaconate by the apostles (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that deacons receive the laying on of hands “not unto the priesthood, but unto a ministry of service” (Lumen Gentium 29). Two of the seven, Stephen and Philip, are soon seen preaching (Acts 6–8), so their service was never only at tables.

Colossians 2:12–15 · Nailed to the Cross

Setting.

Colossae was a small city in the Lycus valley in Asia Minor, in present-day Turkey. The letter opposes teachers who were urging special observances and fear of spiritual powers (2:8, 16–18). Its answer is that in Christ the believers already have everything: “in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily” (2:9).

The letter comes under Paul's name. Many scholars think a disciple wrote it in his name after his death; others hold that Paul wrote it himself. Either way the Church receives it as inspired Scripture.

Structure.

  • v. 12: buried with him in baptism, raised with him through faith.
  • v. 13: you were dead; God made you alive and forgave all your trespasses.
  • v. 14: the record against us is erased and nailed to the Cross.
  • v. 15: the powers are disarmed and led in triumph.

Key words.

  • synetaphentes, buried with: The same picture Paul uses in Romans 6:4. Going down into the water is like going into the tomb with Christ, and coming up is rising with him.
  • cheirographon, record: Literally “something written by hand”: a signed note of debt, an IOU. Our debts before God are pictured as a bond in our own handwriting. God does not only cancel it; he nails it to the Cross, where the charge against Jesus was also nailed.
  • thriambeuō, to lead in triumph: A Roman general who won a great victory led his captives through the streets in a triumphal procession. Here the Cross, which looked like Christ's defeat, is the procession in which the powers are shown to be beaten.

Verse by verse.

“when you were buried with him in baptism, you were also raised with him through faith in the power of God, who raised him from the dead.” (2:12)

Baptism is not only a washing. It is a burial and a rising, and faith is how we receive what God does in it.

“God made you alive together with him, when he forgave us all our trespasses,” (2:13)

The writer moves from “you” to “us”: he too needed forgiveness.

“erasing the record that stood against us with its legal demands. He set this aside, nailing it to the cross.” (2:14)

Every debt is cancelled at once. The nails that held Christ's hands are the nails that hold our cancelled bond.

“He disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them, triumphing over them in it.” (2:15)

The believers at Colossae feared spiritual powers. The letter says that these powers have nothing left to fight with.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that Baptism, whose original and full sign is immersion, signifies the Christian's going down into the tomb with Christ to rise to new life, and it cites this verse (CCC 628, in substance). The Church of the East has always venerated the Cross as the sign of Christ's victory. The Cross here is not only the place of suffering; it is where the debt was cancelled and the powers were overcome.

Matthew 10:1–15 · He sent out the Twelve

Setting.

This is the beginning of the second of the five great discourses in Matthew's Gospel, the missionary discourse. Just before it, Jesus saw the crowds “like sheep without a shepherd” and told the disciples to pray for labourers for the harvest (9:36–38). Now he sends those very disciples.

Only here does Matthew call them “apostles”. The command to go only to Israel will be widened after the Resurrection, when the risen Lord sends the eleven to all nations (28:19). That is one reason the Church reads this passage in the week of the Resurrection.

Structure.

  • v. 1: the Twelve receive authority.
  • vv. 2–4: their names.
  • vv. 5–6: go to the lost sheep of Israel.
  • vv. 7–8: preach and heal; give as you received.
  • vv. 9–10: travel light.
  • vv. 11–13: the worthy house; the greeting of peace.
  • vv. 14–15: when a town will not listen.

Key words.

  • apostolos, apostle: One who is sent with the authority of the sender. The Syriac word for apostle is šlīḥā, from a root meaning to send, which is why Syro-Malabar Christians speak of the Shliha, Mar Thoma Shliha.
  • exousia, authority: Jesus gives the Twelve a share of his own authority over unclean spirits and every sickness. Their power is his, not their own.
  • eirēnē, peace: The Hebrew greeting šālôm. It is more than good wishes: it is the blessing of God's order and wholeness, and the disciples carry it into every house they enter.

Verse by verse.

“Then Jesus summoned his twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to cure every disease and every sickness.” (10:1)

The same words were used of Jesus' own work just before (9:35). The disciples are to do what he does.

“first, Simon, also known as Peter,” (10:2)

Peter is named “first”, not only because he was called first. Matthew gives him a place at the head of the Twelve (CCC 552).

“Thomas and Matthew the tax collector;” (10:3)

Our father in faith, Thomas, stands in the list beside a former tax collector. The Twelve were not chosen for their likeness to one another. Simon “the Cananaean” (v. 4) probably means “the zealous one”; Luke calls him Simon the Zealot (Luke 6:15).

“You received without payment; give without payment.” (10:8)

The whole mission rests on this sentence. What the disciples have was a gift; they may not sell it.

“If the house is worthy, let your peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you.” (10:13)

Peace is never wasted. If it is refused, it comes back to the one who gave it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Jesus chose the Twelve to be with him and to share in his mission, and gave them a share in his authority (CCC 551). The apostles were sent as he was sent by the Father, and the Church is apostolic because she is still founded on them and still sent by Christ (CCC 857–858). The instruction to take nothing for the road has been read through the ages as a call to trust God's providence and to preach the Gospel without seeking gain.

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus tell the Twelve to go only to Israel?

Matthew follows the order of God's plan: the promises were given to Israel first, and Jesus came first to the lost sheep of the house of Israel (15:24). This is not a rejection of the nations. After the Resurrection the risen Lord sends the same apostles to “all nations” (28:19). Paul keeps the same order: “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” (Romans 1:16).

Were the seven men in Acts 6 the first deacons?

Acts does not call them deacons, and their work soon included preaching. But the laying on of hands, the prayer and the task of service led the early Church to see them as the first deacons, and this has remained the common Catholic reading.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the Syro-Malabar Church the deacon is called shemmashan, from the Syriac word for one who serves. In the Holy Qurbana he serves at the altar, proclaims the Gospel in many places and leads the people's responses. Today's reading from Acts shows where his ministry began: in care for widows and in prayer. The Gospel too is close to us. Thomas is named among the Twelve, and our Church keeps the tradition that he came to the land of Kerala as one sent by the Lord.

For study and teaching

  • Isaiah tells Zion to sing before her children are born. What promise of God am I asked to rejoice in before I see it fulfilled?
  • In Acts the Church solved a quarrel by listening, sharing tasks and praying. What can my parish or family learn from this?
  • What does it mean for me that the record of my sins was nailed to the Cross?
  • “You received without payment; give without payment.” What have I received that I am keeping to myself?
