Word for the day

“So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” Romans 10:17

Reflection

In the synagogue of Capernaum the people are “astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.” The scribes quoted the teachers before them; Jesus speaks as the one whose word does what it says. A man with an unclean spirit cries out: “What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are, the Holy One of God.” Jesus rebukes the spirit: “Be silent, and come out of him!” and the man is set free. The crowd asks: “What is this? A new teaching, with authority!” In Denha we see that the word of Jesus is not mere advice but power: it drives out evil and makes people whole.

Paul explains how this word reaches every nation: “For one believes with the heart and so is justified, and one confesses with the mouth and so is saved.” And “Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” But no one can call on a Lord of whom they have never heard. Paul asks: “And how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent?” and answers with Isaiah: “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!” Then he sums it up: “So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” Our own faith came by hearing, through apostles, missionaries, parents and catechists who were sent. Now it is our turn: to open our mouths for Christ, to support those who are sent, and to let our feet carry the good news.

Full homily

Dear friends, in the synagogue at Capernaum the people were astounded: Jesus taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes (Mark 1:22). His word does not only explain; it acts.

A man with an unclean spirit cries out: I know who you are, the Holy One of God. Jesus says: be silent, and come out of him! And the man is freed (Mark 1:24–25). Evil recognises its Master. The people ask: what is this? A new teaching, with authority!

St Paul tells us how this word reaches us: everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved. But how can they call on one of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone to proclaim him? So faith comes from what is heard (Romans 10:13–14, 17).

Our faith came to us because someone was sent: an apostle, a missionary, a grandmother, a catechist. Now we are the ones sent. Let us believe with the heart, confess with the mouth, and let our feet carry the good news. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus' words are so strong that even bad spirits obey him! And somebody once told you about Jesus. This week you can tell a friend: Jesus loves you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Holy One of God, you teach with authority and your word drives out every evil. Let your word live in my heart and on my lips; free me from all that binds me, and make my feet beautiful in carrying your good news to others. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about a word that does something. Paul traces the chain by which faith reaches a person: someone is sent, someone proclaims, someone hears, someone believes and calls on the Lord. In Capernaum people hear that word for the first time from the mouth of Jesus, and even an unclean spirit has to obey it. In this season of Denha, the Lord is shown forth by what he says.

Romans 10:10–20 · Faith comes from what is heard

Setting.

Romans 9–11 is Paul's long and painful reflection on his own people: how can the Gospel be for all, when many in Israel have not received it? He wrote about their “zeal for God” with love (10:1–2). This reading stands in the middle of that argument. It ends with questions that are carried on into chapter 11, where Paul insists that God has not rejected his people (11:1) and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (11:29).

Structure.

  • vv. 10–13: believing and confessing; no distinction; all who call will be saved.
  • vv. 14–15: the chain: call, believe, hear, proclaim, be sent.
  • vv. 16–17: faith comes from hearing.
  • vv. 18–20: the word has gone out; Moses and Isaiah on those who were not seeking.

Key words.

  • homologeō, to confess: To say the same thing, to declare openly. What the heart believes, the mouth says aloud: “Jesus is Lord” (10:9). Faith is more than private opinion.
  • akoē, what is heard: The same Greek word means both the act of hearing and the message heard. Faith begins by receiving something from outside ourselves.
  • kēryssō, to proclaim: The work of a herald, who announces the words of another. The herald does not make up the message, and he has to be sent.

Verse by verse.

“For there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; the same Lord is Lord of all and is generous to all who call on him.” (10:12)

Paul has used the same phrase about sin (3:22–23). The levelling is complete: all need mercy, and it is offered to all.

“And how are they to proclaim him unless they are sent? As it is written, “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!”” (10:15)

He quotes Isaiah 52:7, the runner bringing news of God's victory to Jerusalem. Feet, usually dusty and overlooked, are here called beautiful because they have made the journey.

“So faith comes from what is heard, and what is heard comes through the word of Christ.” (10:17)

This is the verse's plain teaching: faith has a history. Somebody told us. Each of us stands at the end of a long chain of people who were sent and who spoke.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes these verses to explain why the Church has an ordained ministry: no one can give himself the mandate to preach the Gospel, for he must be sent (CCC 875). It also teaches that the Church's mission flows from God's will that all be saved (CCC 851), and that God's covenant with the Jewish people has not been revoked (CCC 839).

Mark 1:21–28 · A new teaching, with authority

Setting.

This is the first public act of Jesus in Mark's Gospel, straight after the call of the four fishermen (1:16–20). Capernaum was a fishing town on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee and became his home base (2:1). The synagogue was the place where the community met on the sabbath to hear the Law and the Prophets, and a visiting teacher could be invited to speak.

Structure.

  • vv. 21–22: he teaches, and they are astounded.
  • vv. 23–24: the unclean spirit cries out.
  • vv. 25–26: the rebuke and the expulsion.
  • vv. 27–28: amazement, and the news spreads.

Key words.

  • exousia, authority: The word means power that is rightly one's own. A scribe taught by handing on what the teachers before him had said, which was honest and valuable work. Jesus speaks in his own name.
  • ho hagios tou theou, the Holy One of God: The spirit knows what the crowd does not yet know. Peter uses the same title in John 6:69, but there as a confession of faith. Here it is a cry of fear (compare James 2:19).
  • phimōthēti, be silent: Literally “be muzzled”, the word used for an ox in 1 Corinthians 9:9. Jesus does not accept testimony from this source.

Verse by verse.

“They were astounded at his teaching, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.” (1:22)

Mark does not yet tell us what he taught. The first thing we learn about the word of Jesus is its effect.

“and he cried out, “What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are, the Holy One of God.”” (1:24)

The “us” is telling. Evil recognizes what this Teacher's coming means. The first to name Jesus in public in this Gospel is his enemy.

“But Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be silent, and come out of him!”” (1:25)

There is no ritual here and no long formula. Only a word. The man himself is not condemned; he is set loose.

“He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him.” (1:27)

The crowd joins teaching and exorcism: the word that instructs is the same word that releases people. The question “What is this?” will drive the rest of Mark's Gospel.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that the people were struck by Jesus teaching not as their scribes did (CCC 581), and that his expulsions of demons announce the coming of the Kingdom, the defeat of “the ruler of this world” (CCC 550). The Church continues this work, in the name of Jesus and by his authority, in the prayers of baptism (CCC 1673).

Questions people ask

Does “not as the scribes” mean that Jesus looked down on the teachers of Israel?

No. Mark is contrasting two ways of teaching, not judging a whole class of people. Jesus praises a scribe in Mark 12:34 and Matthew speaks of scribes trained for the Kingdom (13:52). The difference lies in who is speaking: the one whose own word has authority.

For study and teaching

  • Who first told me about Christ? Have I ever thanked God for that person, or thanked that person?
  • Paul asks how anyone can hear without someone to proclaim. Is there someone who will hear about Christ only if they hear it from me?
