Readings

  • Epistle James 1:9–11 · The lowly boast in being raised up; the rich will fade like the flower
  • Gospel Matthew 22:34–40 · The greatest commandment

Word for the day

“He said to him, “‘You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind.’ This is the greatest and first commandment. And a second is like it: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’” Matthew 22:37–39

Reflection

On the Friday that closes the fourth week of Kaitha, the Church gives us the shortest reading of the week and one of the most demanding. James writes only three verses, and in them turns the world's scale upside down: “Let the believer who is lowly boast in being raised up, and the rich in being brought low, because the rich will disappear like a flower in the field.” The lowly believer, the one whom the world overlooks, is told to boast, because in Christ he has been raised to the dignity of a child of God. The rich believer is told to boast too, but in being brought low, in the humility of seeing that wealth does not last. James is not praising poverty or condemning money; he is changing the place where we stand to measure our worth. Elsewhere he asks: “Has not God chosen the poor in the world to be rich in faith and to be heirs of the kingdom that he has promised to those who love him?”

The picture he uses is one that every Malayali knows from July: “For the sun rises with its scorching heat and withers the field; its flower falls, and its beauty perishes. It is the same way with the rich; in the midst of a busy life, they will wither away.” After weeks of rain the grass is thick and green, and the flowers are everywhere; then a few days of hot sun in a break of the monsoon, and the tender growth dries and turns brown. So it is with human strength and success. Isaiah says it in the same way: “All people are grass, their constancy is like the flower of the field. The grass withers, the flower fades, when the breath of the Lord blows upon it.” The point is not despair, but relief: what fades should not be the foundation of a life. Jesus puts it simply: “Take care! Be on your guard against all kinds of greed; for one's life does not consist in the abundance of possessions.”

The Gospel gives the foundation that does not fade. Pharisees gather after Jesus has silenced the Sadducees, and one of them, a lawyer, asks him a question to test him: “Teacher, which commandment in the law is the greatest?” Jesus answers from the Scriptures that every devout Jew knew by heart. The first words come from the Shema, the prayer of Israel: “Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord alone. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your might.” The second comes from Leviticus: “you shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Jesus is not setting aside the law of Israel; he is showing its centre. This answer belongs to the heart of Jewish faith and is shared with the Church, and it should make us look with respect at the people to whom we owe it.

“On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.” A door hangs on its hinges, and a garment hangs on its peg; the whole of Scripture hangs on love. The two commandments are one love: the second is “like” the first, and the First Letter of John joins them with an edge: “Those who say, ‘I love God,’ and hate their brothers or sisters, are liars; for those who do not love a brother or sister whom they have seen, cannot love God whom they have not seen.” Paul adds: “Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore, love is the fulfilling of the law.” The rich and the lowly of James's letter are both neighbours to each other, and the love of neighbour is what makes the rich man's fading wealth useful: shared, it becomes a treasure in heaven. Alphonsa, Martha and Joachim and Anne, whom we have remembered this week, each lived these two commandments in their own way.

As Kaitha continues, we are at the heart of the monsoon, and the great rivers are rising. A river does not ask whose compound it is; the water goes where it goes and shows that no human house is the foundation of anything. The only foundation that does not wash away is the love of God and neighbour. The prayer of the Holy Qurbana puts us in this love: we stand before God with the whole heart, and then we turn to give the sign of peace to our neighbour. Let us ask that, on the day when the flower fades and the grass dries, the Lord may find in us what he asked of the lawyer: the love that is the fulfilling of the law.

Homily outline

Theme: “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart… and your neighbor as yourself.”

On the Fourth Friday of Kaitha: boasting in the right place, the foundation that does not fade, and the one love of God and neighbour.

1. Boast in the right place

  • The lowly are raised; the rich are brought low.
  • Wealth fades like the flower of the field.
  • Our worth is measured by God, not by possessions.

“Let the believer who is lowly boast in being raised up,” James 1:9

Apply: Hold loosely one thing you rely on, and share a part of it.

  • The lawyer's question.
  • The Shema and Leviticus: the heart of Israel's law.
  • On these two hang all the law and the prophets.

“On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.” Matthew 22:40

Apply: Say the Shema each morning: “Lord, I love you with all my heart.”

3. One love, two commandments

  • No love of God without love of neighbour.
  • Love is the fulfilling of the law.
  • Shared wealth becomes a lasting treasure.

Apply: Do one concrete act of love for a neighbour you find hard.

Everything that is gathered in our hands fades like grass; the love of God and neighbour is the one thing that stays.

Take-home resolution: This week, say the Shema each morning, share one possession, and love one hard neighbour in a concrete way.

Full homily

Dear friends, the Gospel today has a simple question and a simple answer. A lawyer asks Jesus, “Teacher, which commandment in the law is the greatest?” (Matthew 22:36). Jesus answers with two sentences from the Bible that every Jewish child knew: “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind. This is the greatest and first commandment. And a second is like it: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself’” (Matthew 22:37–39).

“On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets” (Matthew 22:40). Think of a door: it hangs on its hinge. Think of a garment: it hangs on a peg. The whole Bible hangs on love. If we have love, we have the whole of what God asks. If we have no love, all the rest is empty. And these two commandments cannot be separated. Saint John says: “Those who say, ‘I love God,’ and hate their brothers or sisters, are liars” (1 John 4:20).

The reading from the letter of James is only three verses. Saint James says: “Let the believer who is lowly boast in being raised up, and the rich in being brought low, because the rich will disappear like a flower in the field” (James 1:9–10). The poor person, whom the world forgets, has the dignity of a child of God. The rich person must remember that money is like grass: green for a season, and then dried by the sun.

We see this in July. After the heavy rain the grass is green and thick, and then the sun comes out in a break of the monsoon, and it begins to dry. Our strength, our money, our position are like that. They are gifts, but they do not last. What lasts is love. And love is not only a feeling; it is a decision to give: time to an old person, food to a hungry neighbour, patience to someone difficult.

In these days we have remembered Saint Alphonsa, Saint Martha, and the grandparents of Jesus. Each of them lived this one commandment in their own way. This week, let us build on the one foundation that does not wash away: love of God and love of neighbour. Amen.

For children

Children, someone asked Jesus: what is the most important thing God wants? And Jesus said: love God with all your heart, and love other people like yourself. If you remember only this, you remember everything! This week, say to God every morning: ‘I love you.’ And share something with a friend or your brother or sister. That is how we keep God's greatest commandment!

Prayer

Lord God, you alone are God, and you ask us to love you with our whole heart and our neighbour as ourselves. Teach us to measure our lives by your love and not by what fades. Raise up those who are lowly and teach the rich to be humble, so that all may boast in you. When the sun scorches and the flower falls, let us stand on the one foundation that does not wash away. Make our homes and our parishes places where the love of God and neighbour is one love. Through the prayers of your martyrs and saints, whom we remember in these days of Kaitha, keep us in your love. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings turn the world’s values upside down in order to teach what lasts. James says that the lowly believer should boast in being raised up, and the rich in being brought low, because wealth passes like the flower of the grass. In Matthew, when a lawyer asks which commandment in the law is the greatest, Jesus answers with two: love God with all one’s heart, soul and mind, and love one’s neighbour as oneself. The two readings are close: what lasts is not what we own but whom we love.

James 1:9–11 · The lowly and the rich

Setting.

James begins his letter with a section on trials (1:2–18), in which he says that tests produce endurance and that God gives wisdom generously to those who ask. In today’s verses he applies this to rich and poor in the community. The person of low standing is told to be proud of the high position God has given him, and the person of wealth to be proud of being brought low. Scholars discuss whether the “rich” here is a rich Christian, who is called to humility, or a rich outsider, as in 5:1–6. Either way, the logic is the same: wealth is not a lasting foundation.

The picture of the flower in the field comes from Isaiah 40:6–8 and Psalm 103:15–16, where human life is like grass that withers in the scorching wind, while the word of God stands for ever.

Structure.

  • v. 9: the lowly believer should boast in being raised up.
  • v. 10: the rich in being brought low, because the rich will disappear like a flower in the field.
  • v. 11: the sun rises with scorching heat, the grass withers, the flower falls; so the rich wither in the midst of a busy life.

Key words.

  • tapeinos, lowly, humble: In the Bible the word often means those who are poor and without power, whom God favours (Luke 1:52). It also means the humble in heart.
  • kauchasthō, let him boast: The same word as in 2 Corinthians 10:17. The only boast worth having is in what God gives, which includes dignity as his child.

Verse by verse.

“Let the believer who is lowly boast in being raised up, and the rich in being brought low” (1:9–10)

The ranks of the world are reversed in God’s eyes. The poor believer is raised by God’s adoption; the rich believer learns that wealth cannot save.

“For the sun rises with its scorching heat and withers the field; its flower falls, and its beauty perishes.” (1:11)

A vivid picture of how quickly beauty and wealth fade. It teaches that our lasting treasure is not on earth.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God has a special love for the poor and that poverty of heart is a condition for entering the kingdom (CCC 2443–2446, 2544–2546). The Church’s teaching has always been that riches in themselves are not evil, but that attachment to them can close the heart to God and neighbour. The Second Vatican Council recalls that the Church is called to be a Church of the poor and to see in them the image of her poor and suffering Lord (Lumen Gentium 8).

Matthew 22:34–40 · The greatest commandment

Setting.

Jesus is in the Temple in the last days before his Passion. After the Sadducees’ question about the resurrection (22:23–33), the Pharisees gather, and one of them, a lawyer, asks which commandment of the law is the greatest. The question was a real one among Jewish teachers, who counted 613 commandments and sought the principle that held them together. Matthew says the lawyer asked “to test him”, but in the parallel in Mark the scribe agrees warmly with Jesus’ answer (Mark 12:28–34).

Jesus answers from within the Jewish tradition, quoting two verses of the Torah: the Shema, “Hear, O Israel”, which every Jew recites (Deuteronomy 6:5), and Leviticus 19:18, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” His answer is not new in its parts; what is new is how he joins them and makes them the key to everything.

Structure.

  • vv. 34–36: the Pharisees gather; a lawyer asks which commandment is greatest.
  • vv. 37–38: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul and mind: the greatest and first.
  • v. 39: a second is like it: love your neighbour as yourself.
  • v. 40: on these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.

Key words.

  • agapēseis, you shall love: A verb of committed, practical love that seeks the good of the other, not only a feeling.
  • holē kardia, psychē, dianoia, whole heart, soul, mind: The whole person: will and affections, life itself, and understanding. Deuteronomy has “heart, soul, might”; Matthew adds “mind”, perhaps for Greek readers.
  • kremannytai, hang: As a door hangs on its hinges, the whole Law and the Prophets turn on these two commandments.

Verse by verse.

“Teacher, which commandment in the law is the greatest?” (22:36)

A real and long-debated question. Jesus does not set aside the other commandments but shows where they come from.

“‘You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind.’” (22:37)

Love of God asks everything. No part of the person is left out.

“And a second is like it: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’” (22:39)

The second is “like” the first. Love of neighbour is how love of God is made real, as 1 John 4:20 says.

“On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.” (22:40)

Everything in Scripture, from Genesis to Malachi, is explained by love.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Ten Commandments are summed up in the two commandments of love (CCC 2055, 2083, 2196), and that charity is the greatest of the virtues, the form of all the others (CCC 1822–1829). St Augustine’s famous saying, “Love, and do what you will”, is to be read in this light: if a person truly loves, what he does will be right (in substance, Homilies on the First Letter of John 7.8). The Church, honouring the Jewish roots of this teaching, remembers that the first commandment is the prayer that Jesus himself learned as a Jewish child, and that Christians and Jews share this heritage (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 839).

For study and teaching

  • James says that wealth is like the flower of the grass. What do I trust in more than God?
  • Jesus joins love of God and love of neighbour. Is there someone nearby whom I find it hard to love, and what small act of love can I do for that person this week?
