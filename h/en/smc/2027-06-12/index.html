Word for the day

“Moved with pity, Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him, and said to him, “I do choose. Be made clean!”” Mark 1:41

Reflection

Today's Gospel brings us very close to Jesus, close enough to see his hand. “A leper came to him begging him, and kneeling he said to him, ‘If you choose, you can make me clean.’” To understand the courage of this man we must remember what leprosy meant in the world of the Bible. The law said that the person with a leprous disease must wear torn clothes, cover his upper lip, and cry out, “Unclean, unclean”; “He shall live alone; his dwelling shall be outside the camp” (Leviticus 13:45–46). He was cut off from his family, from worship and from every human touch. The man in the Gospel breaks that distance. He comes near, he kneels, and he makes a prayer that is at once humble and full of faith: “If you choose, you can make me clean.” He does not doubt the power of Jesus; he only leaves the decision to his will.

Mark tells us what happened in the heart of Jesus before he speaks: “Moved with pity, Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him.” The word Mark uses for pity speaks of being moved in one's very depths, a compassion felt in the body. And then comes the gesture that no one expected: he touched him. Jesus could have healed with a word, as he did at other times. He chooses to touch first. For a man who had perhaps not been touched for years, that touch was already a healing. By the law, touching a leper made a person unclean; here the opposite happens. The uncleanness does not pass to Jesus; the holiness of Jesus passes to the man. “I do choose. Be made clean!” The prayer “if you choose” receives its answer: Jesus wills our healing. “Immediately the leprosy left him, and he was made clean.”

Jesus then sends the man to the priest, to offer what Moses commanded “as a testimony to them”, so that he may be received back into the community and into worship. Healing in the Gospel is never only private; it restores people to the people of God. But the man cannot keep silent. He goes out and spreads the word everywhere, so that Jesus can no longer enter a town openly and stays out in the country. There is a quiet exchange here: the man who lived outside the town can now go in, and Jesus, who touched him, must now stay outside. It is a small picture of the whole Gospel. Christ takes our place outside the camp so that we may come home. The Letter to the Hebrews will say that Jesus “suffered outside the city gate” (Hebrews 13:12) to sanctify the people by his own blood.

Paul's letter adds a lovely image for the life of an apostle. He has come to Troas, where “a door was opened for me in the Lord”, but he is restless because he cannot find Titus. Then he breaks into thanksgiving: “But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads in every place the fragrance that comes from knowing him. For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing.” In a Roman triumph, incense was burned along the route. Paul says that the apostles are like that incense: wherever they go, the fragrance of Christ goes with them. We know this from experience. Some people carry the scent of Christ into a room: patience, kindness, purity, joy. Others carry the smell of gossip, anger or greed. Paul adds that the apostles are not “peddlers of God's word”, selling a diluted message for profit, but persons of sincerity, “sent from God and standing in his presence.”

On this Saturday at the end of the fourth week of the Season of the Apostles, the two readings meet. The leper, touched by Jesus, becomes a messenger who cannot stop speaking of what was done for him. The apostle, led in Christ's procession, spreads his fragrance everywhere. Our country has known the long work of doctors, sisters and lay volunteers among people with leprosy, many of them inspired by Christ who touched the leper. There are still people around us whom society keeps outside the camp: those with stigmatised illnesses, those with disabilities, those of low caste or no status, those whose families have abandoned them. Let us ask the Lord for his pity that moves the heart and for hands that are not afraid to touch, so that our lives may be the aroma of Christ in the places where we live.

Full homily

Dear friends, today a man with leprosy comes to Jesus. In those days such a person had to live alone, outside the town, and cry out “Unclean, unclean” so that no one would come near. Yet this man comes near. He kneels and says: “If you choose, you can make me clean” (Mark 1:40).

Mark tells us that Jesus was “moved with pity”. His heart was touched deep inside. And then he does something nobody expected: he stretches out his hand and touches the man. Perhaps no one had touched him for years. Before the healing comes the touch.

Jesus says: “I do choose. Be made clean!” And immediately he is clean. The man's prayer was “if you choose”; the answer of Jesus is “I do choose”. Jesus wants to heal us. Whatever makes us feel unclean or ashamed, he is willing to touch it and make it clean, especially in the sacrament of Reconciliation.

The healed man cannot keep quiet; he tells everyone. Now he can go into the town, and Jesus has to stay outside. Jesus takes the place of the outcast so that the outcast can come home. That is what he did for all of us on the Cross.

Saint Paul says that the apostles are “the aroma of Christ” (2 Corinthians 2:15): wherever they go, they spread the fragrance of knowing him. Let us ask Jesus for his pity and his courage, to touch those whom others avoid, and to carry the fragrance of Christ into our homes and our town. Amen.

For children

Children, a man who was very sick with leprosy had no friends, because everyone was afraid to touch him. But Jesus touched him and healed him! Jesus is never afraid of us, even when we have done wrong. And Saint Paul says we can be like a sweet perfume of Jesus. When you are kind, people can smell Jesus' love!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, moved with pity you stretched out your hand and touched the leper, and said, I do choose; be made clean. Touch us where we are unclean and ashamed, and heal us by your mercy in the sacrament of Reconciliation. Give us your compassion for those whom the world keeps outside the camp, and hands that are not afraid to touch them. Lead us in your triumph, and make us the aroma of Christ in our homes, schools and workplaces, sincere and sent from God, standing in his presence. To you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings show how Christ spreads through the world. Paul says that God leads the apostles in Christ’s triumphal procession and through them spreads everywhere “the fragrance that comes from knowing him”. In Mark, Jesus, moved with pity, touches a leper and makes him clean, and the healed man cannot stop telling the news, until people come to Jesus from every quarter. The touch of Christ, received with faith, becomes a fragrance that cannot be hidden.

2 Corinthians 2:12–17 · The aroma of Christ

Setting.

Paul has been explaining why he changed his travel plans and wrote a painful letter instead of visiting Corinth (1:15–2:4). He had sent Titus to Corinth and waited anxiously for news. At Troas, on the coast of Asia, a door opened for the Gospel, yet he was so restless about Titus and the Corinthians that he moved on to Macedonia (2:12–13). The story of his meeting with Titus resumes only at 7:5.

In between, from 2:14, Paul breaks into thanksgiving and begins a long reflection on the apostolic ministry. Even his anxieties and changes of plan are part of God’s victory.

Structure.

  • vv. 12–13: an open door at Troas, but no rest without Titus; on to Macedonia.
  • v. 14: thanks be to God, who leads us in triumph in Christ and spreads the fragrance of knowing him.
  • vv. 15–16a: we are the aroma of Christ: to some a fragrance from death to death, to others from life to life.
  • vv. 16b–17: who is sufficient? We are not peddlers of God’s word; we speak in sincerity, sent from God.

Key words.

  • thriambeuonti, leading in triumphal procession: In a Roman triumph a victorious general led his army and his captives through the city, while incense was burned along the way. Paul pictures himself in Christ’s procession, a captive of Christ’s love who now shares his victory.
  • osmē, euōdia, fragrance, aroma: Euōdia is the “pleasing odour” of sacrifices in the Greek Old Testament (Genesis 8:21). Paul’s life, offered with Christ, rises to God like the fragrance of an offering (compare Ephesians 5:2).
  • kapēleuontes, peddling: Used of small traders who watered down wine to sell it. Paul will not water down the Gospel for gain or popularity.

Verse by verse.

“When I came to Troas to proclaim the good news of Christ, a door was opened for me in the Lord;” (2:12)

An “open door” is Paul’s image for an opportunity for the Gospel (1 Corinthians 16:9). Even so, love for the Corinthians weighed more on him than a successful mission.

“But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads in every place the fragrance that comes from knowing him.” (2:14)

The victory is Christ’s, not Paul’s. A fragrance spreads silently and reaches where words cannot.

“For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing;” (2:15)

The aroma is offered first “to God”. A Christian life is worship before it is a message.

“but in Christ we speak as persons of sincerity, as persons sent from God and standing in his presence.” (2:17)

Three marks of a true minister: sincerity, being sent by God, and living before God’s face.

How the Church reads it.

The Church has long linked this passage with the anointing of Christians. St Cyril of Jerusalem, explaining the anointing with chrism to the newly baptised, tells them that they have become a fragrance of Christ (in substance, Mystagogical Catecheses 3). The Catechism teaches that the chrism of Confirmation gives the baptised a share in the mission of Christ and the fullness of the Holy Spirit, so that their lives may give off “the aroma of Christ” (CCC 1294, quoting 2:15). Verse 17 is also a lasting warning to all who preach: the word of God must never be sold or diluted.

Mark 1:40–45 · I do choose. Be made clean!

Setting.

This is the last healing in Mark’s first chapter. Jesus has been preaching and healing throughout Galilee (1:39). Under the Law, a person with a skin disease called “leprosy” (a broader term than modern leprosy) was unclean and had to live apart, crying out “Unclean, unclean” (Leviticus 13:45–46). Only a priest could declare someone clean again, with the offerings described in Leviticus 14.

The leper breaks the distance by coming to Jesus. Jesus breaks it further by touching him.

Structure.

  • v. 40: the leper kneels and begs: if you choose, you can make me clean.
  • v. 41: Jesus, moved with pity, stretches out his hand, touches him, and says: I do choose; be made clean.
  • v. 42: the leprosy leaves him at once.
  • vv. 43–44: Jesus sends him to the priest, as Moses commanded, as a testimony to them, and tells him to say nothing.
  • v. 45: he spreads the news; Jesus can no longer enter a town openly; people come from everywhere.

Key words.

  • splanchnistheis, moved with pity: From splanchna, the inner organs, the seat of deep feeling. It is a gut-wrenching compassion. Some ancient manuscripts, among them Codex Bezae, read “moved with anger” instead. Most editions keep “pity”; if “anger” is original, it is anger at the disease and the suffering it caused.
  • thelō, I choose, I will: The leper doubts not Jesus’ power but his willingness. Jesus answers with the same word: “I do choose.” His will is to heal.
  • katharisthēti, be made clean: Not only cured but restored: able to return to worship, family and village. Jesus heals the body and the isolation.

Verse by verse.

“A leper came to him begging him, and kneeling he said to him, “If you choose, you can make me clean.”” (1:40)

A perfect prayer: humble, trusting, leaving everything to the Lord’s will.

“Moved with pity, Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him” (1:41)

Under the Law, touching a leper made one unclean. Jesus’ touch reverses the flow: his holiness passes to the leper. The touch comes before the word, and it was perhaps the first touch the man had felt in years.

“go, show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing what Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.” (1:44)

Jesus respects the Law of Moses and the priests. The healed man’s return to the community must be recognised in the proper way.

“But he went out and began to proclaim it freely, and to spread the word” (1:45)

Mark uses for the healed man the words he uses for preaching: “proclaim” and “the word”. A healed sinner becomes a herald, even if, here, against the Lord’s command.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ’s compassion toward the sick and his many healings are a sign that God has visited his people, and that he often uses physical signs such as the laying on of hands; the sick try to touch him, and in the sacraments Christ continues to touch us to heal us (CCC 1503–1504). St John Chrysostom, commenting on the parallel account in Matthew, notes that Jesus touched the leper to show that he is not under the Law but above it, and that for the clean nothing is unclean (in substance, Homilies on Matthew 25). The Church has always seen in the healing of lepers a sign of the forgiveness of sins: in the sacrament of Penance Christ still says, “I do choose. Be made clean!”

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus tell the man to say nothing?

Mark often shows Jesus asking for silence about his miracles. He did not want to be followed only as a wonder-worker, or misunderstood as a political Messiah, before his Passion revealed what kind of Messiah he is (Mark 8:29–31). Here the result proves the wisdom of the command: crowds made it impossible for him to enter towns openly.

For study and teaching

  • Paul says Christians are the aroma of Christ. What fragrance does my daily life leave in my home and workplace?
  • The leper prayed, “If you choose, you can make me clean.” What do I need to bring to Christ with that humble trust, perhaps in the sacrament of Reconciliation?
