Word for the day

“but in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord. Always be ready to make your defense to anyone who demands from you an accounting for the hope that is in you;” 1 Peter 3:15

Reflection

The First Letter of Peter was written to small Christian communities scattered across Asia Minor, people who were misunderstood, slandered and sometimes mistreated by their neighbours. Peter does not tell them to hit back, nor to hide. He writes: “But even if you do suffer for doing what is right, you are blessed. Do not fear what they fear, and do not be intimidated, but in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord.” The answer to fear is to enthrone Christ in the heart. Then comes the sentence that has shaped Christian witness ever since: “Always be ready to make your defense to anyone who demands from you an accounting for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and reverence.” The word for defense gives us the word apologetics, but Peter's apologetics is not a debate to be won. It is an account of hope, given with gentleness toward the questioner and reverence toward God. Christians in India know this situation well. We are a small flock among neighbours of many faiths, and most of our neighbours are friends; yet sometimes we are asked hard questions, and in some places Christians are misunderstood or falsely accused. Peter's counsel holds: know why you hope, answer when asked, and answer kindly. “Keep your conscience clear, so that, when you are maligned, those who abuse you for your good conduct in Christ may be put to shame.” A good life is the most persuasive defense.

Peter grounds all this in the Cross, which we remember on every Friday: “For it is better to suffer for doing good, if suffering should be God’s will, than to suffer for doing evil. For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, in order to bring you to God. He was put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit.” Once for all: the sacrifice of Calvary needs no repetition, and the Holy Qurbana does not repeat it; it makes that one sacrifice present, so that we may enter into it. The righteous for the unrighteous: the innocent One suffered in place of the guilty. In order to bring you to God: the whole purpose of the Passion is reconciliation. When a Christian suffers unjustly and does not return evil for evil, he is drawn into the same pattern, and his patience becomes a quiet preaching of the Cross.

The Gospel shows us the same patience from God's side. A householder sows good seed in his field, “but while everybody was asleep, an enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat, and then went away.” The weed Jesus means was probably darnel, which in its early growth looks almost exactly like wheat; farmers in Kuttanad know such weeds in the young paddy. Only at heading time can the difference be seen. The servants are scandalised: “Master, did you not sow good seed in your field? Where, then, did these weeds come from?” The answer is plain: “An enemy has done this.” Evil is not God's sowing. But when they offer to pull up the weeds, the master says: “No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them. Let both of them grow together until the harvest.” Later, in the house, Jesus explains: the field is the world, the good seed the children of the kingdom, the weeds the children of the evil one, the harvest the end of the age, the reapers the angels. The judgement is real, and it is serious; but it belongs to God and to his time. Until then the Church is a field where wheat and weeds grow side by side, and the line between them runs through every human heart, including our own. The parable forbids two things: despair when we see evil in the Church and the world, and violence in the name of purity, whether by persecuting those we judge to be weeds or by cutting off brothers and sisters we have given up on. As the Fathers loved to say (in substance), today's weed may by grace become tomorrow's wheat. And it gives one great promise: “Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. Let anyone with ears listen!”

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Peter wrote to Christians who were misunderstood and mistreated. He told them: “even if you do suffer for doing what is right, you are blessed. Do not fear what they fear… but in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord” (1 Peter 3:14–15). When Christ is Lord in our hearts, fear loses its power.

Peter adds: “Always be ready to make your defense to anyone who demands from you an accounting for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and reverence.” Many people around us, friends of other faiths, colleagues, even our own children, may ask us why we believe. Let us know our faith, and let us answer with gentleness and respect, never with pride or anger.

Today is Friday. Peter reminds us that “Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, in order to bring you to God” (1 Peter 3:18). The innocent One suffered for the guilty. When we suffer unjustly and do not return evil, we walk with him.

In the Gospel, an enemy sows weeds among the wheat. The servants want to pull them out at once, but the master says: wait until the harvest, or you will pull up the wheat too. God is patient. He gives everyone time to change. The Church is a field where good and bad grow together, and the weeds grow in our own hearts too.

So let us not despair when we see evil, and let us never use violence or contempt in the name of God. Let us pray for those we find hard to love, let us pull out the weeds in our own hearts, and let us trust the Lord of the harvest, who promises that “the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father” (Matthew 13:43). Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus tells a story about a farmer. An enemy planted weeds among his wheat. The farmer did not pull them out at once, because he did not want to hurt the good wheat. God is patient like that farmer. He gives everyone time to become good. So be patient with others, and be kind, even to people who are not kind to you!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God. When we are misunderstood or mistreated for doing what is right, keep us from fear, and let us sanctify you as Lord in our hearts. Make us ready to give an account of our hope to anyone who asks, always with gentleness and reverence. Lord of the harvest, you are patient with the field of the world and with the field of our hearts: keep us from despair and from violence, let your grace turn weeds into wheat, and on the last day let us shine like the sun in the kingdom of your Father. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask how Christians live in a world where good and evil grow side by side. First Peter tells believers who suffer for doing right not to be afraid, but to honour Christ in their hearts and to give a reason for their hope with gentleness, because Christ the righteous suffered for the unrighteous. In Matthew, Jesus explains why the master lets the weeds grow with the wheat until the harvest: judgment belongs to God at the end, and now is the time of patience. The Christian answer to evil is not fear or force, but hope, gentleness and trust in the Lord of the harvest.

1 Peter 3:14–18 · A reason for your hope

Setting.

First Peter is written to Christians scattered through five Roman provinces of Asia Minor, whom it calls “exiles” (1:1). Most were not facing a state persecution yet, but they were slandered and pushed aside by their neighbours because they no longer joined in the old ways of life (4:3–4). The letter teaches them how to live as Christ’s people in such a world: with good conduct, without fear, and with hope.

Today’s verses take up words from Isaiah 8:12–13, where the prophet is told not to fear what the people fear but to regard the Lord of hosts as holy. Peter applies them to Christ: in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord.

Structure.

  • v. 14: blessed if you suffer for what is right; do not fear their threats.
  • v. 15: sanctify Christ as Lord; always be ready to give a reason for your hope.
  • v. 16: with gentleness and reverence and a clear conscience.
  • v. 17: better to suffer for doing good than for doing evil.
  • v. 18: Christ suffered once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God.

Key words.

  • apologia, defense: A reasoned answer, as in a court. From this word comes “apologetics”. Every Christian, not only scholars, is to be ready to explain why he or she hopes.
  • praütēs kai phobos, gentleness and reverence: The manner matters as much as the message. Gentleness toward the questioner, and reverence (literally “fear”) before God. A true answer given with contempt is not a Christian witness.
  • hapax, once for all: Christ’s suffering is not repeated; it is complete and sufficient. Hebrews uses the same word (Hebrews 9:26–28). The Eucharist does not add to it but makes that one sacrifice present.

Verse by verse.

“But even if you do suffer for doing what is right, you are blessed.” (3:14)

An echo of Jesus’ beatitude: “Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness’ sake” (Matthew 5:10).

“but in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord. Always be ready to make your defense to anyone who demands from you an accounting for the hope that is in you” (3:15)

Notice the order: first Christ honoured in the heart, then the answer on the lips. Peter speaks of “the hope that is in you”: people notice hope, and ask about it.

“yet do it with gentleness and reverence.” (3:16)

The best defense of the faith is a life that matches it, so that those who speak evil are put to shame by good conduct.

“For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, in order to bring you to God.” (3:18)

Here is the reason for all the rest. Christ’s innocent suffering brought us to God; our suffering for good, joined to his, becomes a share in his work. “Put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit” speaks of his death and Resurrection; the next verse goes on to his proclamation to “the spirits in prison”.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the disciple of Christ must not only keep the faith and live by it, but also profess it, confidently bear witness to it and spread it (CCC 1816). The Church connects the verses that follow this reading (3:18–19) with Christ’s descent to the dead (CCC 632). Today many Christians in many lands suffer for their faith; the Church prays for them, and also for those who wrong them, as Christ prayed for those who crucified him.

Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43 · Let both grow together until the harvest

Setting.

Matthew 13 gathers seven parables of the Kingdom. After the sower (13:1–23), Jesus tells this parable to the crowds; later, in the house, he explains it to his disciples (vv. 36–43). Between the two parts come the mustard seed and the yeast (vv. 31–35), which today’s reading leaves out.

The “weeds” were very likely darnel, a grass that grows in wheat fields and looks much like wheat until the ears appear. Its grains can make people ill, so farmers separated them carefully at harvest. Pulling darnel out early would tear up the wheat, whose roots were tangled with it.

Structure.

  • vv. 24–26: good seed sown; an enemy sows weeds at night; both appear together.
  • vv. 27–28: the servants’ question: where did the weeds come from? An enemy has done this.
  • vv. 29–30: do not gather them now; let both grow until the harvest.
  • v. 36: the disciples ask for the meaning.
  • vv. 37–39: the sower is the Son of Man, the field the world, the enemy the devil, the harvest the end of the age.
  • vv. 40–43: the judgment at the end; the righteous will shine like the sun.

Key words.

  • zizania, weeds: Probably darnel, a weed that looks like wheat while it grows. The resemblance is the point: in this world it is not always easy to tell good from evil, and we are not the final judges.
  • aphete, let: “Let both of them grow together.” The same Greek verb also means to forgive. The master’s patience gives time for growth and, in human terms, time for conversion.
  • synteleia tou aiōnos, the end of the age: A phrase found in Matthew alone among the Gospels (13:39, 40, 49; 24:3; 28:20). The final separation belongs to that day and to God’s angels, not to the servants now.

Verse by verse.

“but while everybody was asleep, an enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat, and then went away.” (13:25)

Evil in the world does not come from God, who sowed only good seed. It has an enemy behind it, working secretly.

“No; for in gathering the weeds you would uproot the wheat along with them.” (13:29)

The master cares more about saving the wheat than about destroying the weeds quickly. Impatient zeal can do great harm to the good.

“the field is the world” (13:38)

Jesus says the field is the world. The parable is about God’s patience with the whole human family, and about how his people live within it.

“Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father.” (13:43)

An echo of Daniel 12:3: the wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky. The end of the story is not burning but glory for those who belong to the Father.

How the Church reads it.

St Augustine used this parable against the Donatists, who wanted a Church made only of the pure: in this age the Church is a mixed field, and the final separation belongs to the Lord (in substance). The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church, embracing sinners in her bosom, is at once holy and always in need of purification (Lumen Gentium 8; CCC 827). The Catechism also quotes verses 41–42 among Jesus’ solemn warnings about hell, which are a call to responsibility and conversion (CCC 1034, 1036).

Questions people ask

If the weeds must be left until the harvest, should the Church never correct sin?

The parable forbids us to take God’s place as final judge, and to try to root out evil people by force. It does not forbid correction. Jesus himself teaches how to correct a brother or sister (Matthew 18:15–17), and the Church calls sinners to conversion through the sacrament of Reconciliation. In the parable “the field is the world”; the patience of the master is the patience God shows to every sinner, ourselves first.

For study and teaching

  • Peter says, “Always be ready to make your defense… for the hope that is in you.” If someone asked me today why I hope, what would I say?
  • The master tells his servants, “Let both of them grow together.” Where am I tempted to judge or uproot others, when God is asking me to be patient and to grow as wheat?
