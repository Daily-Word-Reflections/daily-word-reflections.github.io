Readings

  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 7:32–35 · Unhindered devotion to the Lord
  • Gospel Matthew 3:7–12 · He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire

Word for the day

“I baptize you with water for repentance, but one who is more powerful than I is coming after me; I am not worthy to carry his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” Matthew 3:11

Reflection

Between the Ascension and Pentecost the Church takes us back to the banks of the Jordan, to the first promise of the Spirit in the Gospel. John the Baptist sees many Pharisees and Sadducees coming for baptism, and he speaks with the bluntness of a prophet: “Bear fruit worthy of repentance.” He is not condemning a whole people; he is speaking to religious leaders of his own nation, as the prophets of Israel always did, and his words strike every religious person in every age, ourselves first. He warns them against a comfortable security: “Do not presume to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our ancestor’; for I tell you, God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham.” A Christian can say the same kind of thing: I was baptised as a child, my family has been Catholic since the time of St Thomas, our parish is ancient. All true, and all precious. But none of it replaces the fruit of a converted life. “Even now the ax is lying at the root of the trees.” The time is short, and God looks for fruit, not for family trees.

Then John points away from himself: “I baptize you with water for repentance, but one who is more powerful than I is coming after me; I am not worthy to carry his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” Water washes; fire transforms. John's baptism prepared the heart; the baptism of Jesus gives the Spirit himself. That promise is fulfilled at Pentecost, when “divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them. All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit” (Acts 2:3–4). In these nine days we pray for that fire. It is not a fire that destroys the person; it burns away the chaff and leaves the wheat. “His winnowing fork is in his hand, and he will clear his threshing floor and will gather his wheat into the granary.” A Kerala farmer who has seen paddy winnowed in the wind knows the picture: the light husk flies away, the heavy grain falls at his feet. The Spirit does in us what the wind does on the threshing floor. What is empty in us is blown away; what is good is gathered into God's granary.

Paul speaks to the Corinthians about another kind of fruit: a heart given entirely to the Lord. “The unmarried man is anxious about the affairs of the Lord, how to please the Lord; but the married man is anxious about the affairs of the world, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided.” Paul is not despising marriage; in the same letter he calls marriage a gift from God (1 Corinthians 7:7), and the Church honours it as a sacrament. He is speaking of a particular call, celibacy for the sake of the kingdom, which lets a person belong to the Lord with an undivided heart. His purpose is clear: “I say this for your own benefit, not to put any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and unhindered devotion to the Lord.” Every Christian, married or not, is called to that undivided devotion in his or her own state of life. On this Saturday of May we look to the Virgin Mary, whose heart was never divided: she belonged wholly to God, and she was also the most devoted of mothers. In the upper room she prayed with the apostles for the Spirit who had come upon her at Nazareth (Luke 1:35). Let us ask her to obtain for us, in this novena, the fire that makes the heart whole.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Gospel takes us to the river Jordan, where John the Baptist is preaching. Many religious leaders come to him, and he tells them: “Bear fruit worthy of repentance.”

He warns them not to say, ‘We have Abraham as our father.’ We can say similar things: I was baptised as a baby, my family is old Catholic, my parish is ancient. Thank God for all that! But God looks for fruit. What fruit is in my life today?

Then John says: one more powerful than I is coming; “He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” This promise came true at Pentecost, when tongues of fire rested on the disciples. In these nine days we pray for that fire. It does not destroy us; it burns away the chaff and keeps the wheat, as the wind does on the threshing floor.

Paul speaks of an undivided heart. Some are called to give themselves wholly to the Lord in priesthood or religious life. Others are called to marriage, which is also holy. Every one of us is called to “unhindered devotion to the Lord” in our own state of life.

On this Saturday of May let us look to Mary. Her heart was never divided; she belonged wholly to God, and she was a loving mother. She prayed with the apostles for the Spirit. Let us ask her today: Mother, pray with us, that the fire of the Spirit may come on us at Pentecost. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, John the Baptist said that Jesus would give us the Holy Spirit and fire. Fire gives light and warmth.

When the Holy Spirit comes into your heart, he makes it warm with love and bright with goodness. Ask him today: ‘Holy Spirit, make my heart warm and bright!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, more powerful than John, you baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire. In these days of waiting, burn away the chaff in our hearts and gather the wheat into your granary. Keep us from trusting in our name or our history; make us bear fruit worthy of repentance. Give to your priests and religious undivided hearts, and to our families a devotion to you that nothing can divide. Through the prayers of the Virgin Mary, whose heart was wholly yours, send us your Spirit at Pentecost. Amen. Alleluia!
