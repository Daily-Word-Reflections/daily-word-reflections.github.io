Readings

  • Epistle 1 Corinthians 7:32–35 · Unhindered devotion to the Lord

Word for the day

“I baptize you with water for repentance, but one who is more powerful than I is coming after me; I am not worthy to carry his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” Matthew 3:11

Reflection

Between the Ascension and Pentecost the Church takes us back to the banks of the Jordan, to the first promise of the Spirit in the Gospel. John the Baptist sees many Pharisees and Sadducees coming for baptism, and he speaks with the bluntness of a prophet: “Bear fruit worthy of repentance.” He is not condemning a whole people; he is speaking to religious leaders of his own nation, as the prophets of Israel always did, and his words strike every religious person in every age, ourselves first. He warns them against a comfortable security: “Do not presume to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our ancestor’; for I tell you, God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham.” A Christian can say the same kind of thing: I was baptised as a child, my family has been Catholic since the time of St Thomas, our parish is ancient. All true, and all precious. But none of it replaces the fruit of a converted life. “Even now the ax is lying at the root of the trees.” The time is short, and God looks for fruit, not for family trees.

Then John points away from himself: “I baptize you with water for repentance, but one who is more powerful than I is coming after me; I am not worthy to carry his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” Water washes; fire transforms. John's baptism prepared the heart; the baptism of Jesus gives the Spirit himself. That promise is fulfilled at Pentecost, when “divided tongues, as of fire, appeared among them, and a tongue rested on each of them. All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit” (Acts 2:3–4). In these nine days we pray for that fire. It is not a fire that destroys the person; it burns away the chaff and leaves the wheat. “His winnowing fork is in his hand, and he will clear his threshing floor and will gather his wheat into the granary.” A Kerala farmer who has seen paddy winnowed in the wind knows the picture: the light husk flies away, the heavy grain falls at his feet. The Spirit does in us what the wind does on the threshing floor. What is empty in us is blown away; what is good is gathered into God's granary.

Paul speaks to the Corinthians about another kind of fruit: a heart given entirely to the Lord. “The unmarried man is anxious about the affairs of the Lord, how to please the Lord; but the married man is anxious about the affairs of the world, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided.” Paul is not despising marriage; in the same letter he calls marriage a gift from God (1 Corinthians 7:7), and the Church honours it as a sacrament. He is speaking of a particular call, celibacy for the sake of the kingdom, which lets a person belong to the Lord with an undivided heart. His purpose is clear: “I say this for your own benefit, not to put any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and unhindered devotion to the Lord.” Every Christian, married or not, is called to that undivided devotion in his or her own state of life. On this Saturday of May we look to the Virgin Mary, whose heart was never divided: she belonged wholly to God, and she was also the most devoted of mothers. In the upper room she prayed with the apostles for the Spirit who had come upon her at Nazareth (Luke 1:35). Let us ask her to obtain for us, in this novena, the fire that makes the heart whole.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Gospel takes us to the river Jordan, where John the Baptist is preaching. Many religious leaders come to him, and he tells them: “Bear fruit worthy of repentance.”

He warns them not to say, ‘We have Abraham as our father.’ We can say similar things: I was baptised as a baby, my family is old Catholic, my parish is ancient. Thank God for all that! But God looks for fruit. What fruit is in my life today?

Then John says: one more powerful than I is coming; “He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” This promise came true at Pentecost, when tongues of fire rested on the disciples. In these nine days we pray for that fire. It does not destroy us; it burns away the chaff and keeps the wheat, as the wind does on the threshing floor.

Paul speaks of an undivided heart. Some are called to give themselves wholly to the Lord in priesthood or religious life. Others are called to marriage, which is also holy. Every one of us is called to “unhindered devotion to the Lord” in our own state of life.

On this Saturday of May let us look to Mary. Her heart was never divided; she belonged wholly to God, and she was a loving mother. She prayed with the apostles for the Spirit. Let us ask her today: Mother, pray with us, that the fire of the Spirit may come on us at Pentecost. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, John the Baptist said that Jesus would give us the Holy Spirit and fire. Fire gives light and warmth.

When the Holy Spirit comes into your heart, he makes it warm with love and bright with goodness. Ask him today: ‘Holy Spirit, make my heart warm and bright!’

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, more powerful than John, you baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire. In these days of waiting, burn away the chaff in our hearts and gather the wheat into your granary. Keep us from trusting in our name or our history; make us bear fruit worthy of repentance. Give to your priests and religious undivided hearts, and to our families a devotion to you that nothing can divide. Through the prayers of the Virgin Mary, whose heart was wholly yours, send us your Spirit at Pentecost. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask for an undivided heart. Paul wants the Corinthians to serve the Lord without distraction, whether in marriage or in a life given wholly to God. John the Baptist tells those who come to the Jordan that repentance must bear fruit, and he promises one who will baptize “with the Holy Spirit and fire”. Pentecost is eight days away. The Spirit who is coming will make our hearts whole and set them on fire.

1 Corinthians 7:32–35 · Unhindered devotion to the Lord

Setting.

In chapter 7 Paul answers questions the Corinthians had sent him (7:1). Some there seem to have thought that married life was unworthy of a spiritual person. Paul defends marriage firmly (7:2–5) and calls it a gift, as celibacy is a gift (7:7). On the question of the unmarried he says he has “no command of the Lord” but gives his own opinion (7:25).

He writes with “the impending crisis” in view (7:26) and the sense that “the appointed time has grown short” (7:29). Whatever the timing, the point holds for every age: this world in its present form is passing away (7:31), and every Christian life must be lived toward the Lord who is coming.

Structure.

  • v. 32a: I want you without anxieties.
  • vv. 32b–34: the unmarried and the married: one set of cares, and divided interests.
  • v. 35: not a restraint, but good order and undivided devotion to the Lord.

Key words.

  • amerimnos, without anxiety: From merimna, anxiety. Jesus used the related verb: “do not worry about your life” (Matthew 6:25). Paul’s concern is pastoral: he wants hearts at peace.
  • memeristai, divided: Literally “has been divided into parts” (v. 34). Paul is not blaming married people. He is describing honestly that a husband or wife has two sets of real duties, both holy.
  • aperispastōs, without distraction: Translated “unhindered” (v. 35). A related verb describes Martha, who “was distracted by her many tasks” (Luke 10:40). Paul wants every Christian, married or not, to have something of Mary’s place at the Lord’s feet.

Verse by verse.

“I want you to be free from anxieties.” (7:32)

Before he speaks of states of life, Paul speaks of the heart. The goal is not a particular way of life for its own sake, but a heart given to the Lord.

“and his interests are divided.” (7:34)

This is realism, not complaint. Earlier in the same chapter Paul has praised married love and asked couples not to deprive one another (7:3–5). A married person serves the Lord through spouse and children; that service has its own cares.

“I say this for your own benefit, not to put any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and unhindered devotion to the Lord.” (7:35)

The word translated “restraint” is literally a noose or halter (brochos). Paul refuses to tie anyone. A vocation is an invitation, never a trap.

How the Church reads it.

The Church honours both callings. “Both the sacrament of Matrimony and virginity for the Kingdom of God come from the Lord himself” (CCC 1620). St John Paul II taught that virginity for the Kingdom does not contradict the dignity of marriage but presupposes and confirms it (Familiaris Consortio 16). Consecrated life is a sign of the world to come; Christian marriage is a sign of Christ’s love for his Church. Each needs the other.

Matthew 3:7–12 · He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire

Setting.

John the Baptist preaches in the wilderness of Judea, and people come to him from Jerusalem, all Judea and the region of the Jordan (3:1, 5). Matthew names Pharisees and Sadducees among them, two groups who disagreed on much, for example on the resurrection (Acts 23:8). In Luke the same warning is spoken to the crowds in general (Luke 3:7).

John speaks as the prophets of Israel spoke, calling his own people to conversion. His words are strong because the time is urgent: the one he has been sent to announce is near.

Structure.

  • vv. 7–8: the warning, and the call to bear fruit.
  • v. 9: do not rely on descent from Abraham.
  • v. 10: the ax at the root.
  • vv. 11–12: the one who is coming: Spirit and fire, the winnowing fork.

Key words.

  • metanoia, repentance: A change of mind and heart, a turning around of the whole person. John asks that it be visible: repentance has fruit.
  • en pneumati hagiō kai pyri, with the Holy Spirit and fire: Fire both purifies and judges. Christians hear in these words the day of Pentecost, when “divided tongues, as of fire” rested on each disciple (Acts 2:3).
  • ptyon, winnowing fork: The tool for throwing threshed grain into the wind, so that the light chaff blows away and the grain falls. The coming one sorts what is real from what is empty.

Verse by verse.

“Bear fruit worthy of repentance.” (3:8)

Tears alone are not enough, and words alone are not enough. Conversion shows in changed lives: in Luke, John goes on to tell people to share, to be honest and not to extort (Luke 3:10–14).

“God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham.” (3:9)

Many scholars hear a play on words in Hebrew and Aramaic, between “stones” (’ăbānîm) and “children” (bānîm). Being Abraham’s child is a gift of God, never a possession. Paul says that God’s calling of Israel is irrevocable (Romans 11:29); John’s warning is against presumption, and it reaches every Christian who trusts in a name rather than a life.

“He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” (3:11)

John’s baptism prepares; the baptism Jesus gives makes new. The one who seemed too great even to have his sandals carried will come down into the water himself (3:13–17).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism explains that while water signifies birth and fruitfulness in the Spirit, fire symbolizes the transforming energy of the Holy Spirit’s actions, and it cites the Baptist’s words (CCC 696). In the Sacraments of Initiation the Church gives both: the water of Baptism and the anointing of the Spirit. This second day of the novena is a good time to ask that the fire be kindled again in us.

Questions people ask

Is John the Baptist condemning the Pharisees and Sadducees as a group?

John speaks to those in front of him, as a prophet calls his own people to conversion. The Gospels themselves show Pharisees who were open to Jesus, such as Nicodemus (John 3:1) and Gamaliel (Acts 5:34–39), and Paul was proud to have been a Pharisee (Philippians 3:5). The Church teaches that the Jewish people may not be presented as rejected or accursed (Nostra Aetate 4). The warning against empty religion is meant for us.

For study and teaching

  • What distracts me most from the Lord in my present state of life, and what “good order” would help me?
  • What fruit of repentance can others see in my life this week?
