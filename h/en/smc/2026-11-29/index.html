Word for the day

“But the angel said to him, “Do not be afraid, Zechariah, for your prayer has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you will name him John.”” Luke 1:13

Reflection

A new liturgical year begins, and the Syro-Malabar Church begins it not with the end of the world but with an announcement: good news is coming. The season of Suvara (Annunciation) leads us through the great announcements of salvation, to Zechariah, to Mary, to Joseph, until the Nativity of the Lord.

The first announcement is made to old people who had given up hope. Abraham is ninety-nine and Sarah ninety; Abraham falls on his face and laughs: “Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old?” Centuries later Zechariah and Elizabeth are “righteous before God”, yet childless and “getting on in years”. In both stories God does the impossible and gives a son through whom the whole plan of salvation moves forward.

Zechariah is a priest serving in the Temple. As he offers incense, the angel Gabriel says: “Do not be afraid… your prayer has been heard.” Perhaps Zechariah had long stopped praying for a child; God had not stopped listening. His son John will go before the Lord “to turn the hearts of parents to their children… to make ready a people prepared for the Lord.” The preparation for Christ begins in a family.

That is why the Epistle speaks of the family: “Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.” Husbands, wives, children and parents are the first place where salvation is lived. Isaiah's words are spoken over each family at the start of this year: “Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.”

Full homily

Dear brothers and sisters, a new liturgical year begins today. Our Church does not begin with a warning but with good news: an announcement. For four weeks we will listen to the angel's messages to Zechariah, to Mary and to Joseph, and prepare for the birth of the Lord.

The first two announcements are made to old people. Abraham is almost a hundred and Sarah ninety; when God promises a son, Abraham falls on his face and laughs. Centuries later Zechariah and Elizabeth are righteous, faithful, and childless, and they are old. Humanly, the story was over. But God is the God of the impossible.

Zechariah is offering incense in the Temple when the angel says: “Do not be afraid, Zechariah, your prayer has been heard.” Perhaps he had stopped praying for a child long ago. God had not stopped listening. Is there a prayer you have stopped praying? This season is the time to take it up again.

And notice where salvation begins: in a family. John will turn the hearts of parents to their children. That is why St Paul speaks today of husbands and wives, parents and children: “Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.” Christ comes into the world through families.

So let us begin this year with hope. Let us keep the 25 Days' Fast as families, pray together, forgive one another, and make our homes ready for Jesus. God says to each of us what he said through Isaiah: “Do not fear; I have called you by name, you are mine.” Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

A new liturgical year begins with Suvara, the season of the Annunciation, and its first Sunday is full of announcements. God tells Abraham that Sarah, at ninety, will bear a son. Isaiah hears God say to his people in exile, “I have called you by name, you are mine.” In the Temple, Gabriel tells the old priest Zechariah that Elizabeth will bear John. Between these births stands Ephesians, which speaks of the family as the place where Christ’s love is lived. The East Syriac year begins not with a command but with a promise: God comes to barren places and makes them fruitful.

Genesis 17:15–22 · Sarah shall be her name

Setting.

Abram is ninety-nine years old. God appears to him, makes an everlasting covenant, changes his name to Abraham, “father of a multitude”, and gives circumcision as its sign (17:1–14). Today’s passage continues the same speech: now God turns to Sarai.

Abraham already has a son, Ishmael, born of Hagar (Genesis 16). It seems natural to him that the promise should pass through Ishmael. God has another plan.

Structure.

  • vv. 15–16: Sarai’s new name and the promise of a son through her.
  • v. 17: Abraham falls on his face and laughs.
  • v. 18: his prayer for Ishmael.
  • vv. 19–21: God’s answer: a son named Isaac, the covenant with him; a blessing also for Ishmael.
  • v. 22: God goes up from Abraham.

Key words.

  • śārâ, Sarah: Both Sarai and Sarah are forms meaning “princess”. The new name marks a new mission: “kings of peoples shall come from her” (17:16).
  • yiṣḥāq, Isaac: The name means “he laughs” or “may God laugh”. Abraham laughs here, Sarah will laugh in 18:12, and at the birth Sarah says, “God has brought laughter for me” (21:6). Doubt becomes joy.
  • bərît ‘ôlām, everlasting covenant: A covenant that does not end. God binds himself to Abraham’s descendants for ever.

Verse by verse.

“Then Abraham fell on his face and laughed, and said to himself, “Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? Can Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?”” (17:17)

He bows in worship and laughs in disbelief at the same time. Scripture does not hide the struggle of the father of faith.

“O that Ishmael might live in your sight!” (17:18)
“As for Ishmael, I have heard you; I will bless him and make him fruitful and exceedingly numerous; he shall be the father of twelve princes, and I will make him a great nation.” (17:20)

God hears the father’s prayer. Ishmael is not rejected; he receives his own blessing, fulfilled in Genesis 25:12–16.

“But my covenant I will establish with Isaac, whom Sarah shall bear to you at this season next year.” (17:21)

The promise is given a date. God’s word enters time and history.

How the Church reads it.

St Paul presents Abraham as the model of faith who “did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was already as good as dead” (Romans 4:19). The Catechism calls Abraham the model of obedience in faith, and Sarah the one who conceived the son of the promise (CCC 144–146). The birth of a son from a barren mother by God’s power prepares the births of John and of Jesus, where the angel says, “nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37; compare Genesis 18:14).

Isaiah 43:1–7, 10–11 · I have called you by name, you are mine

Setting.

Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak to the Jews in exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC. They are discouraged and feel abandoned. The prophet brings words of comfort: the God who created Israel will redeem them and bring them home. The lectionary adds vv. 10–11, where Israel is called to be God’s witness.

Structure.

  • v. 1: “Do not fear”: God creates, redeems and calls by name.
  • v. 2: through waters and fire, God is with them.
  • vv. 3–4: “you are precious in my sight”: the ransom.
  • vv. 5–7: the gathering of sons and daughters from the ends of the earth.
  • vv. 10–11: “You are my witnesses”; “besides me there is no savior”.

Key words.

  • gā’al, to redeem: The gō’ēl was the next of kin who bought back a relative from slavery or recovered his land. God acts as Israel’s nearest kinsman.
  • kōper, ransom: The price paid for a life. The language is poetic: God values Israel more than great nations.
  • ‘ēdîm, witnesses: In a court scene, the nations’ gods cannot produce witnesses; Israel’s very existence is God’s testimony.

Verse by verse.

“Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.” (43:1)

To be called by name is to be known personally. These words are often read at baptisms: each child is called by name and belongs to God.

“When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and through the rivers, they shall not overwhelm you” (43:2)

The waters recall the Red Sea and the Jordan. God does not promise a life without waters and fire, but his presence in them.

“Because you are precious in my sight, and honored, and I love you” (43:4)
“You are my witnesses, says the Lord, and my servant whom I have chosen, so that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he.” (43:10)

“I am he” (’ănî hû’) echoes the divine name. Jesus will use the same words: “then you will realize that I am he” (John 8:28).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God calls each one by name, that everyone’s name is sacred, and that the name is given in baptism (CCC 2156–2158). Isaiah’s words show that this is not a formality: the God who made the stars calls each person as his own.

Ephesians 5:21–6:4 · Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ

Setting.

Ephesians 5:21–6:9 is a “household code”, a common form in the ancient world that set out duties within the family: husbands and wives, parents and children, masters and slaves. Similar lists appear in Colossians 3:18–4:1 and 1 Peter 2–3. Paul uses the familiar form but transforms it from within: everything is placed under Christ, and the first word is mutual submission.

Structure.

  • 5:21: the principle for all: be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.
  • 5:22–24: wives and husbands, as the Church and Christ.
  • 5:25–30: husbands are to love as Christ loved the Church and gave himself up for her.
  • 5:31–33: “the two will become one flesh”: a great mystery about Christ and the Church.
  • 6:1–4: children and parents.

Key words.

  • hypotassomenoi allēlois, being subject to one another: The participle governs the whole passage. No one stands outside this mutual service; it is the attitude of all who fear Christ.
  • agapate, love: The husband’s duty is not to rule but to love with the love of Christ, a love measured by the Cross: he “gave himself up for her”.
  • mystērion mega, great mystery: The Latin Vulgate translated this as sacramentum hoc magnum est. The union of husband and wife reflects the union of Christ and the Church.

Verse by verse.

“Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.” (5:21)

This verse is the key that opens the rest. Whatever is said to wives, husbands, children and fathers is said within mutual submission.

“Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her,” (5:25)

In the ancient world this was the startling part. The husband is given the hardest command: to lay down his life.

“This is a great mystery, and I am applying it to Christ and the church.” (5:32)
“And, fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.” (6:4)

Parental authority is also service. Fathers are to form their children patiently in the Lord, not crush them.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this passage to show that “the entire Christian life bears the mark of the spousal love of Christ and the Church”, and that Christian marriage is an efficacious sign of the covenant between Christ and the Church (CCC 1616–1617). The family is “the domestic church” (CCC 1656; Lumen Gentium 11). St John Paul II taught that the “mutual subjection out of reverence for Christ” of 5:21 is the Gospel’s newness in this passage: the subjection is not one-sided but mutual (Mulieris Dignitatem 24, in substance).

Luke 1:5–25 · Your prayer has been heard

Setting.

After a short preface (1:1–4), Luke opens his Gospel in the Temple of Jerusalem, where it will also end (24:53). He builds the first chapter as a diptych: the announcement of John’s birth and the announcement of Jesus’ birth, side by side, so that John always points to someone greater.

Background: the priests were divided into twenty-four divisions, each serving in the Temple for a week twice a year; Abijah was the eighth (1 Chronicles 24:10). There were so many priests that the privilege of offering incense in the Holy Place, chosen by lot, might come only once in a lifetime. Incense was offered at the morning and evening sacrifice, while the people prayed outside.

Structure.

  • vv. 5–7: Zechariah and Elizabeth: righteous, blameless, and childless.
  • vv. 8–10: the lot, the incense, the people praying outside.
  • vv. 11–17: the angel’s appearance and message about John.
  • vv. 18–20: Zechariah’s doubt and the sign of silence.
  • vv. 21–23: he comes out unable to speak.
  • vv. 24–25: Elizabeth conceives and praises God.

Key words.

  • deēsis, prayer: A personal petition. “Your prayer has been heard” may refer both to the couple’s long prayer for a child and to the priest’s prayer for Israel’s redemption.
  • Yôḥānān, John: The name means “the Lord has been gracious”. Zechariah means “the Lord has remembered”. The names tell the story: God remembers and shows grace.
  • Gabriēl, Gabriel: “God is my strength” or “mighty one of God”. He appeared to Daniel “at the time of the evening sacrifice” (Daniel 9:21); now he comes again at the hour of the incense offering.

Verse by verse.

“Do not be afraid, Zechariah, for your prayer has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you will name him John.” (1:13)

The first words of the Gospel’s first angel are “Do not be afraid”. They will be heard again by Mary (1:30) and by the shepherds (2:10).

“With the spirit and power of Elijah he will go before him, to turn the hearts of parents to their children” (1:17)

The angel quotes Malachi 4:5–6, the last words of the prophets in the Christian Old Testament. The long silence of prophecy is about to end.

“But now, because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled in their time, you will become mute, unable to speak, until the day these things occur.” (1:20)

The silence is both a sign and a discipline. The priest who could not believe must now listen. When he speaks again, it will be to bless God (1:64).

“This is what the Lord has done for me when he looked favorably on me and took away the disgrace I have endured among my people.” (1:25)

Elizabeth’s words anticipate Mary’s: God “has looked with favor” (1:48).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that John was “filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother’s womb”, and that he is the Elijah who was to come, sent to “make ready a people prepared for the Lord” (CCC 717–718). St Ambrose, commenting on Luke, notes that Zechariah lost his voice through doubt and recovered it through faith when he named his son (in substance).

Questions people ask

Why was Zechariah punished for his question when Mary was not?

Zechariah asked for proof: “How will I know that this is so?” (1:18). Mary asked how it would happen, since she was a virgin (1:34), and then gave her full consent (1:38). The Fathers saw in Zechariah a doubt about God’s word, and in Mary a faith that seeks understanding. Even so, his silence was a mercy that prepared him to praise.

Does Ephesians 5 make wives inferior to husbands?

No. The passage begins with mutual submission (5:21), and it asks of husbands the self-giving love of Christ on the Cross. Paul speaks in the language of his time, but he fills it with a new spirit. The Church teaches the equal dignity of man and woman (CCC 369–372; Mulieris Dignitatem 24).

What does the Church say about Ishmael?

Genesis shows God hearing Abraham’s prayer and blessing Ishmael. The Church regards with esteem the Muslims, who link their faith to Abraham (Nostra Aetate 3). The covenant line passes through Isaac, but God’s care reaches Ishmael too.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In the East Syriac tradition the liturgical year begins with Suvara, the Annunciation, rather than with the end of the world. The Syro-Malabar Church starts the year by listening to God’s promises: to Abraham, to Zechariah and, in the coming weeks, to Mary and Joseph. Zechariah offering incense in the sanctuary while the people pray outside is close to our own experience: in the Holy Qurbana the priest stands at the altar in the sanctuary on behalf of the people, and the prayers of all rise together like incense. Suvara is also a season for families, and today’s epistle invites every home to become a place where God’s announcements are heard.

For study and teaching

  • Abraham laughed at God’s promise, and later Sarah laughed with joy. What promise of God do I find hard to believe?
  • God says, “I have called you by name, you are mine.” How can I help a child or a young person in my family to know this?
  • Ephesians begins with “Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ.” What would mutual submission look like in my home this week?
  • Zechariah was silent for months and then spoke to bless God. What might God want to teach me through silence in this season of Suvara?
