Word for the day

“For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.” Colossians 3:3

Reflection

On the last day of Easter week the Church goes back once more to the tomb, this time with Mark. “When the sabbath was over, Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James, and Salome bought spices, so that they might go and anoint him.” They are going to do the last thing love can do for the dead. On the way they have one worry: “Who will roll away the stone for us from the entrance to the tomb?” It is a real problem; Mark tells us the stone “was very large”. But “When they looked up, they saw that the stone, which was very large, had already been rolled back.” How much of our life is spent walking towards a stone that God has already moved. We lie awake over a conversation we fear, a sin we cannot confess, a reconciliation we think impossible, and when we finally arrive, the stone is gone. Inside the tomb a young man in a white robe gives the Easter announcement: “Do not be alarmed; you are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has been raised; he is not here. Look, there is the place they laid him.” The angel points to an empty place. The Church has never preached a Resurrection of the spirit only, or a Christ who lives on merely in the memory of his friends. The body that was laid there is gone because it has been raised, the same body that bore the nails, now glorified. The Gospels do not tell us that the Risen Lord appeared first to his Mother, but an old and loving tradition of the Church, among Eastern and Western writers alike, has believed that he did, and that Mary, who kept faith through the long Saturday, was the first to be filled with the joy of her Son's Resurrection.

Then the commission: “But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going ahead of you to Galilee; there you will see him, just as he told you.” Two words have consoled sinners for twenty centuries: and Peter. Peter, who had sworn he did not know the man; Peter, who had wept in the courtyard. He is named on purpose, so that he will not think he has been struck off the list. The Risen Lord does not send a message to the faithful disciples and leave out the one who failed. And he sends them back to Galilee, where it all began, by the lake where he first called them. He is “going ahead of you”: wherever we go, he is there first. Mark's Gospel then ends in a strange way: “So they went out and fled from the tomb, for terror and amazement had seized them; and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.” The oldest manuscripts of Mark stop here; the Church receives the longer ending (Mark 16:9–20) as inspired Scripture, and it tells of the appearances that followed. But the silence of verse 8 is itself a message. The news was too great for them at first; fear held them. Yet we know the end of the story: the women did speak, the disciples did see him in Galilee, and the news has reached Kerala and every continent. Fear was not the last word. Every Christian who hears the Easter Gospel is asked to finish the sentence the women left unfinished, by telling someone.

Paul tells the newly baptised what to do with the Easter news in daily life. “So if you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God. Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on earth.” This is not an escape from the world; Paul goes on in the same chapter to speak of family life, work and forgiveness. It is a question of where the heart is fixed. Every Holy Qurbana, before the great prayer of the anaphora, the priest calls us to lift up our minds on high, and we answer that they are turned towards the God of Abraham, Isaac and Israel. Then the deepest line: “for you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ who is your life is revealed, then you also will be revealed with him in glory.” Tomorrow, on New Sunday, the newly baptised of the ancient Church laid aside the white garments they had worn all week. The white robe was put away, but the new life was not; it went on, hidden, like a seed under the soil, like a treasure in a locked box, like the life of the Lord himself for thirty years at Nazareth. Most of the holiness in the world is hidden: a mother's prayers for a son abroad, a nurse's patience on a night shift, an old man's rosary in an empty church. None of it is lost. It is hidden with Christ in God, and on the last day it will be revealed with him in glory.

Full homily

Dear friends, three women walk to the tomb at sunrise with spices and one worry: “Who will roll away the stone for us?” It was a real worry; the stone was very large. But when they looked up, it had already been rolled back.

How often we are like them. We worry for weeks about a conversation, a confession, a reconciliation, and when we finally go, God has already gone ahead and moved the stone. Easter teaches us to walk towards the stone.

The angel says: “He has been raised; he is not here. Look, there is the place they laid him.” The tomb is empty because the Lord is risen in his body. He is not a memory. He is alive, and we meet him at this altar.

Then: “go, tell his disciples and Peter.” Why Peter? Because Peter had denied him. The Lord did not want Peter to think he was finished. If you have failed, put your own name there: tell the disciples, and tell me.

Paul adds: “seek the things that are above… for you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.” The newly baptised will take off their white garments, but their new life goes on, hidden. So does ours: in quiet prayers, faithful work, patient love. None of it is lost; it will be revealed with him in glory.

Tomorrow is New Sunday, the Sunday of Mar Thoma. Today let us take one step towards our stone, and lift up our minds to the Lord. Christ is risen! Alleluia! Amen.

For children

When you are worried, tell Jesus. He is already there to help you.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, you rolled away the stone that we feared, and you left the tomb empty because you are risen in the body. You sent word to your disciples and to Peter, so that no one who has failed would think himself forgotten. Raise our minds to the things that are above, where you are seated at the right hand of the Father. Keep our life hidden with you in God, and when you appear, let us appear with you in glory. Through the prayers of your holy Mother, who kept faith through the long Saturday, fill us with Easter joy. Glory to you, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

The Saturday of the week of the Resurrection brings us back to the empty tomb, and then lifts our eyes upward. Mark tells of the women who came with spices on the first day of the week and found a young man in white where they expected a body. Colossians draws the conclusion for the baptized: if you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, for your life is hidden with him. One reading tells what happened at the tomb; the other tells what it means for the way we live. The key words are raised, seek and hidden.

Colossians 3:1–4 · Seek the things that are above

Setting.

These four verses are the hinge of the Letter to the Colossians. Chapters 1–2 have shown who Christ is and what Baptism has done: the believers were buried with him and raised with him (2:12). From 3:5 the letter turns to daily life: what to put to death, what to put on, how to live at home. The bridge between them is this short passage. Christian conduct grows out of what has already happened to us in Christ.

The teachers at Colossae urged rules about food and festivals and the worship of angels (2:16–23). The answer is not a new set of rules but a new direction: look to where Christ is.

Structure.

  • v. 1: if raised with Christ, seek what is above, where Christ is seated.
  • v. 2: set your minds there.
  • v. 3: you have died; your life is hidden with Christ in God.
  • v. 4: when Christ appears, you will appear with him in glory.

Key words.

  • ta anō, the things above: Not a place among the stars but the realm where Christ reigns with the Father. To seek it is to take one's measure from Christ rather than from what passes away.
  • kekryptai, is hidden: The perfect tense: it has been hidden and remains so. Our true life is real now, but it is not yet visible. It is kept safe, as treasure is hidden to protect it.
  • phanerōthē, is revealed: The word for Christ's coming at the end. What is hidden now will be shown, and we will be shown with him.

Verse by verse.

“So if you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God.” (3:1)

“If” here means “since”. The writer does not doubt that they were raised; he builds on it. “Seated at the right hand” comes from Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament verse most often echoed in the New.

“Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on earth,” (3:2)

This is not contempt for creation, which the same letter says was made in Christ and for him (1:16). “Things on earth” here means the list that follows in v. 5: what belongs to the old life.

“for you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.” (3:3)
“When Christ who is your life is revealed, then you also will be revealed with him in glory.” (3:4)

Christ is not only the giver of our life; he is our life.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that, united with Christ by Baptism, believers already truly share in the heavenly life of the risen Christ, but that this life remains hidden with Christ in God until he appears (CCC 1003, quoting this passage). The Church reads these verses at the feast of the Resurrection in many traditions. They are the Easter programme for the Christian life: we live on earth with our hearts where Christ is, and so we work on earth with hope.

Mark 16:1–8 · He has been raised; he is not here

Setting.

Mark has told us that some women watched the crucifixion from a distance (15:40–41) and saw where the body was laid (15:47). Now three of them return when the sabbath is over, with spices to anoint the body. They expect a corpse and a heavy stone. They find neither.

In the oldest Greek manuscripts the Gospel of Mark ends at v. 8. The verses that follow in our Bibles (16:9–20) were added early and are received by the Church as inspired and canonical Scripture. Some manuscripts also add a short ending after v. 8, which the NRSV prints in brackets.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–2: the women come at sunrise on the first day of the week.
  • v. 3: their worry about the stone.
  • v. 4: the stone already rolled back.
  • vv. 5–7: the young man in white and his message.
  • v. 8: flight, fear and silence.

Key words.

  • neaniskos, young man: Mark does not call him an angel, but his white robe and his message show that he comes from God. Mark used the same word for the young man who ran away naked when Jesus was arrested (14:51–52). Many readers have noticed the contrast: one young man fled in shame, and this one sits robed in white.
  • ēgerthē, he has been raised: A passive verb: God has raised him. The New Testament speaks both of Jesus rising and of the Father raising him (CCC 648–649).
  • ephobounto gar, for they were afraid: In Greek the Gospel's last words are literally “they were afraid, for”. It is a startling ending. The fear is the holy fear of those who have met the work of God.

Verse by verse.

“They had been saying to one another, “Who will roll away the stone for us from the entrance to the tomb?”” (16:3)

They came anyway. Love set out before it knew how the obstacle would be removed.

“Do not be alarmed; you are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has been raised; he is not here.” (16:6)

The risen one is the same Jesus of Nazareth who was crucified. The Resurrection does not erase the Cross; the crucified one is the one who lives.

“But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going ahead of you to Galilee;” (16:7)

And Peter: the one who denied him three times is named. The message of Easter begins with mercy to the one who fell furthest.

“So they went out and fled from the tomb, for terror and amazement had seized them; and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.” (16:8)

The silence cannot be the end, since we are reading the Gospel. The women's message did reach the disciples, and so it reached us.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that Mary Magdalene and the holy women were the first to encounter the Risen One, so that the women were the first messengers of the Resurrection, even to the apostles (CCC 641). St Thomas Aquinas, commenting on John, called Mary Magdalene the apostle of the apostles (in substance). The day of the women's visit, “the first day of the week”, became the Lord's Day, the day on which the Church gathers for the Holy Qurbana (CCC 2174).

Questions people ask

Why does Mark end with fear and silence?

Many scholars think Mark ended deliberately at v. 8, leaving the reader to answer the message the women first received in trembling: he is going ahead of you. Others think the original ending was lost. Either way the Church has received the longer ending (16:9–20) as part of Scripture, and Mark himself has already told us, through Jesus' own predictions, that the disciples will see him (14:28; 16:7).

Mark says the women came “when the sun had risen”. Do the Gospels disagree about the time?

The Gospels describe the same morning from different angles: “at early dawn” (Luke 24:1), “while it was still dark” (John 20:1), “very early … when the sun had risen” (Mark 16:2). The women set out in the dark and arrived as the day broke. The details differ; the testimony is one.

For the Syro-Malabar reader

In Syriac the first day of the week is called ḥad b-šabbā, “the first in the week”. Every Sunday is for us a little feast of the Resurrection. Our tradition loves the image of the East: the rising sun is a sign of the risen Lord, whose coming in glory will be like the lightning that “comes from the east” (Matthew 24:27). Tomorrow, New Sunday, closes the first week of the Resurrection with the confession of Mar Thoma. Today's epistle gives the attitude for that feast: hearts set where Christ is.

For study and teaching

  • The women set out not knowing who would roll away the stone. What act of love am I putting off because I cannot see how the obstacle will be removed?
  • “And Peter”: whom do I need to name and include in the good news, even after a failure?
  • What would it mean, in the week ahead, to “seek the things that are above” in my work and family?
