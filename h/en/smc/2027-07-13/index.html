Word for the day

““Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God.” Luke 6:20

Reflection

On the second Tuesday of Kaitha the readings begin with a question that every family, parish and village has asked at some time: “Those conflicts and disputes among you, where do they come from?” James does not blame the weather, the neighbours or the committee. He points inward: “Do they not come from your cravings that are at war within you?” The word he uses for cravings is the one from which we get our word hedonism, the pull toward pleasure. The war outside begins with a war inside: “You want something and do not have it; so you commit murder. And you covet something and cannot obtain it; so you engage in disputes and conflicts.” James uses the strongest words on purpose, because envy, left to grow, walks the road of Cain. Even prayer can be infected by it: “You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, in order to spend what you get on your pleasures.” A prayer that only wants to add to our pile has not yet become a prayer.

Then the language grows sharper: “Adulterers! Do you not know that friendship with the world is enmity with God?” The prophets called Israel an unfaithful wife when she ran after other lovers, and James uses the same voice for a Church that wants Christ and also every prize the world hands out. “The world” here is not creation, which God made good and loved so much that he gave his Son (John 3:16). It is the order of life that organises itself without God and measures a person by what he owns and what others say of him. Against it James sets the jealous love of God: “God yearns jealously for the spirit that he has made to dwell in us.” God is not the rival of our happiness; he is a lover who wants us whole. And his answer to our cravings is a gift, not a rule: “But he gives all the more grace; therefore it says, ‘God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.’” Then comes the short road home: “Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.”

The Gospel shows what the grace given to the humble looks like. In Luke's Sermon on the Plain, Jesus “looked up at his disciples” and speaks four blessings and four woes. “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God.” The verb is in the present: the kingdom is theirs now. “Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh.” Jesus is not praising misery, and he is not saying that hunger is good. He is saying that God has taken the side of the one whose only possession is God, and that the reversal is certain. The fourth blessing is the one the martyrs of the Church lived: “Blessed are you when people hate you, and when they exclude you, revile you, and defame you on account of the Son of Man. Rejoice in that day and leap for joy, for surely your reward is great in heaven.” The Church in Kaitha looks at her martyrs and sees this joy in a human face.

Then come the four woes: “But woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation.” A woe is a lament, like the cry over a house about to fall, and not a curse. Jesus grieves for those whose comfort is already complete, who have nothing left to hope for. The last woe stings the preacher and the politician most: “Woe to you when all speak well of you, for that is what their ancestors did to the false prophets.” Put James and Luke side by side. The one who owns the kingdom does not need to go to war for a plot of land, a seat on a committee or a good name; the one who is ruled by cravings is never full. In Kerala, where a quarrel over a boundary or an inheritance can run for years in the courts and can even divide a family at the church door, the reading puts a gentle and hard question: what are we really fighting for, and did we first ask God for it in the right way?

Full homily

Dear friends, James begins today with a question we all know: where do our quarrels come from? He answers like a good doctor. They come from cravings that make war inside us (James 4:1). We want something, we do not get it, and we turn on those nearest to us.

Think of the quarrels we have seen: over a boundary, a share of the land, a seat in a committee, a word that was never forgiven. James says that friendship with the world is enmity with God. By the world he means the way of living in which everything is measured by what I have and what people say of me.

But God is not a rival to our joy. He loves us with a jealous love, and he gives more grace to the humble (James 4:6). So James gives us the way back: submit to God, resist the devil, and the devil will flee.

Jesus shows us what that grace looks like. “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God” (Luke 6:20). Not will be: is. The one who has God has everything, and has no need to fight over scraps. And he warns us: “Woe to you when all speak well of you” (Luke 6:26). It is easy to live for applause.

This week let us make one peace. Let us ask God for what we truly need, share a little of what we have, and be glad that the kingdom belongs to the poor and the humble. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus said: blessed are the poor, for the kingdom of God is theirs. That means God loves people who have little, and he wants us to share with them. When you quarrel with your brother or sister, say sorry first. That is how people become friends of God!

Prayer

Lord God, you love us with a jealous love and give grace to the humble. Quiet the cravings that make war within us and in our homes. Teach us to ask for what is good, to share what we have, and to count ourselves blessed when we have you alone. Turn our quarrels into peace, our envy into thanksgiving, and our fear of what others say into joy in your kingdom. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings unmask the wrong kind of wanting. James asks where quarrels come from and answers that they come from cravings at war inside us; he calls his readers back from friendship with the world to humble submission to God. In Luke, Jesus turns the world’s ranking upside down: blessed are the poor, the hungry, the weeping and the hated, and woe to those who have already received their comfort. Together they ask what we really want, and who we are trusting to give it.

James 4:1–7a · A friend of the world, an enemy of God

Setting.

The Letter of James is addressed to Jewish Christians “in the Dispersion” (1:1) and reads like a sermon full of practical wisdom, much like the Wisdom books of the Old Testament. Tradition ascribes it to James, “the brother of the Lord”, the leader of the Church in Jerusalem; some scholars think it was written by a later disciple in his name. Either way, the Church has received it as Scripture.

Just before today’s verses James has spoken of peace and wisdom from above (3:13–18). Now he asks the hard question: if that wisdom is missing, where do the wars among you come from? His answer looks inside the heart before it looks at any outside enemy.

Structure.

  • vv. 1–3: quarrels come from cravings; you want, you cannot get, you ask wrongly.
  • v. 4: divided loyalty: friendship with the world is enmity with God.
  • vv. 5–6: God’s jealous love for the spirit he has given us, and his greater grace for the humble.
  • v. 7: the answer in two commands: submit to God; resist the devil.

Key words.

  • hēdonai, cravings, pleasures: The word from which we have “hedonism”. James sees these desires as an army encamped inside us, fighting for control. The trouble is not that we want, but that our wants have taken the place of God.
  • philia tou kosmou, friendship with the world: “The world” here is not creation, which God made good, but the order of life that lives without God and measures everything by gain and pride. It is the opposite of the world God so loved (John 3:16). To be its friend is to share its loyalty.
  • hypotagēte, submit yourselves: A word of order: to place oneself under another willingly. James uses it for the believer’s relation to God, not for slavery but for trust, the opposite of the proud self-rule that causes quarrels.

Verse by verse.

“You do not have, because you do not ask.” (4:2)

James is not against desire. He is against desire that never turns to God. A prayer that begins with God changes what we ask for.

“You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, in order to spend what you get on your pleasures.” (4:3)

A good test of any prayer: whom is it for? A request made to serve our own cravings is not refused out of cruelty; it simply cannot be what a loving Father gives.

“God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” (4:6)

James quotes Proverbs 3:34 in its Greek form. The proud close their hands to grace; the humble receive it. The gift comes before our effort and makes it possible.

“Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.” (4:7)

The command to resist follows the command to submit. We resist the enemy not by our own strength but by standing under God’s.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes James 4:3 when it explains why some prayers seem unanswered: when we ask wrongly, we do not receive; God wants to bring our desires into harmony with his own (CCC 2737). It also teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer: only when we recognise that we are beggars before God do we receive his gift (CCC 2559). The Church hears in “Submit yourselves therefore to God” the same trust that Mary showed in her “Let it be” (Luke 1:38).

Luke 6:20–26 · Blessed are you who are poor

Setting.

This is the start of Luke’s “Sermon on the Plain”, spoken on level ground after Jesus has chosen the Twelve and healed the crowds (6:12–19). Matthew’s longer Sermon on the Mount has eight beatitudes; Luke has four, each followed by a matching “woe”. Jesus speaks to “his disciples” with the crowd listening.

Luke’s Beatitudes are direct and concrete: the poor, the hungry, the weeping, the hated. Matthew adds “in spirit” and “for righteousness” (Matthew 5:3, 6). The two Gospels do not contradict each other; Luke names the people, and Matthew names the heart that Jesus wants to form in them.

Structure.

  • v. 20: the poor: the kingdom of God is theirs now.
  • v. 21: the hungry and the weeping: they will be filled and will laugh.
  • vv. 22–23: the hated for the Son of Man’s sake: rejoice, for the prophets were treated the same way.
  • vv. 24–26: four woes: the rich, the full, the laughing, the praised.

Key words.

  • makarioi, blessed, happy: Not a wish but a declaration: you are blessed, now. It is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the “happy” of Psalm 1:1 and many other psalms. Jesus tells the poor that God’s favour already rests on them.
  • ouai, woe: A cry of grief more than of anger, as in the prophets. Jesus sorrows over those who have already received their “consolation” (paraklēsis), the comfort they trusted, and so have nothing more to hope for from God.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God.” (6:20)

The kingdom is given in the present tense. The poor are not promised only a future reward; God’s reign has already come near to them in Jesus.

“Rejoice in that day and leap for joy, for surely your reward is great in heaven” (6:23)

Joy under persecution is possible because the disciple stands in the line of the prophets. Suffering for the Son of Man is a sign of belonging, not of failure.

“Woe to you when all speak well of you” (6:26)

The woe is aimed at those who need everyone’s approval, so that they never say what the truth costs. A prophet and a disciple must sometimes be unpopular.

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the Beatitudes as the portrait of Christ himself and the call to holiness: they describe the new life of those who follow him (CCC 1716–1717). Because the Lord was poor, the Church has always held that the poor are close to his heart, and her love for them is inspired by the Gospel of the Beatitudes (CCC 2444). The woes are not a curse on wealth itself, but a warning that trust in riches can shut the door against God and against the neighbour at the gate.

Questions people ask

Does Jesus say that being poor is good and being rich is bad?

No. Jesus does not praise poverty as such, and he does not hate people for owning things. Poverty can crush, and the Church works to relieve it. He says that God is on the side of those who have nothing to lean on but him, and he warns that wealth, comfort and praise can become so much a person’s whole security that there is no room left for God. The test is where our trust lies, and whether we share what we have.

For study and teaching

  • James says we do not receive because we ask wrongly. What do I usually ask for, and how would my prayer change if I began with “Your will be done”?
  • Which of the four woes in Luke is most dangerous for me: wealth, comfort, laughter that avoids the truth, or the wish to be praised? What one step can I take this week?
