Word for the day

“Blessed is anyone who endures temptation. Such a one has stood the test and will receive the crown of life that the Lord has promised to those who love him.” James 1:12

Reflection

The letter of James opens its first chapter with a blessing for those who hold on: “Blessed is anyone who endures temptation. Such a one has stood the test and will receive the crown of life that the Lord has promised to those who love him.” The Greek word means both a trial and a temptation; it is the same test seen from two sides, depending on what the person does with it. The crown of life is the wreath that is given to the athlete who has finished the race, here the life of God himself, promised to those who love him. Then James has to be careful, because people in trouble are quick to blame God. “No one, when tempted, should say, ‘I am being tempted by God’; for God cannot be tempted by evil and he himself tempts no one.” God may test, as he tested Abraham, in order to strengthen; he never draws anyone towards sin. The Lord's Prayer asks the Father, “Lead us not into temptation”, and the Catechism explains that this is a prayer that the Spirit may help us tell a trial that makes us grow from a temptation that leads to sin and death (Catechism 2846–2847).

Then James gives the anatomy of a fall, as in a pregnancy: “But one is tempted by one's own desire, being lured and enticed by it; then, when that desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin, and that sin, when it is fully grown, gives birth to death.” Desire is the bait, and the fish is hooked by what it wants. Nothing happens suddenly: the thought, the consent, the act, the habit, and finally death. The sentence is a mirror held up to a Kerala household. The glance at the phone that becomes a hidden habit, the first drink, the first small lie about money, the first bitter word that becomes silence at the table: each grows. And against this birth that ends in death, the apostle places another birth. “In fulfillment of his own purpose he gave us birth by the word of truth, so that we would become a kind of first fruits of his creatures.” Desire gives birth to death; God gives birth to us by his word, which is truth. The second birth is baptism and the faith that comes by hearing the Gospel.

Between the two births stands the great sentence on God's character: “Every generous act of giving, with every perfect gift, is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change.” The sun, the moon and the stars change, and the monsoon sky is never the same for two hours together, with its light and shadows. The Father of lights does not change. Everything good that reaches us, a child's birth, a harvest, a healing, the forgiveness of sins, comes from him, and he never takes back what he gave. And we are “first fruits”. In Israel the first sheaf of the harvest was brought to the priest and offered to God (Leviticus 23:10) as the sign that the whole harvest belongs to him. In this season of Kaitha, when the Church looks at her growth and fruit, James tells us that the baptised are that offering: the first of a harvest that will include the whole creation.

The Gospel is the end of the Sermon on the Mount, and it is a test of everything we have heard so far. “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father in heaven.” Even prophesying, casting out demons and deeds of power in his name are not a passport if there is no obedience. The words “I never knew you” are terrible, and they should be read alongside the Father's own way of knowing, which is loving. Then comes the parable known to every child in Kerala. Two houses stand through the same weather: “The rain fell, the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house.” We have lived this: the great floods of 2018 showed us what is carried away and what stands. The house on rock stands, and the house on sand falls “and great was its fall!” The only difference is that one builder “hears these words of mine and acts on them”. The rock is Christ and his word, and building on the rock means acting on it, in small things on an ordinary Monday: the honest day's work, the forgiveness offered, the prayer kept. “Now when Jesus had finished saying these things, the crowds were astounded at his teaching.” The crowd was astonished, but the wise man built.

Full homily

Dear friends, today the Apostle James says: “Blessed is anyone who endures temptation” (James 1:12). Every one of us has trials, in health, in the family, in work. If we stay faithful, we will receive “the crown of life”. But James also says something we must never forget: God does not tempt anyone. When we fall, we must not say, ‘God tempted me.’

James shows us how sin grows: first a desire, then a consent, then the act, and then the habit, and finally death. So we must stop it at the first step. When a bad thought comes, say a short prayer, and turn. And against the birth that ends in death, God gives us another birth: “he gave us birth by the word of truth” (James 1:18). We are his first fruits.

James also tells us that every good gift comes from above, from the Father of lights, who never changes (James 1:17). In these days of monsoon, when the sky changes every hour, it is good to remember that our Father does not change. Let us thank him each evening for three gifts.

In the Gospel Jesus says: “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father” (Matthew 7:21). It is not enough to say the right words. We must do what he says.

Two houses, one rain. Everyone who hears his words and acts on them is like a wise man who built his house on rock (Matthew 7:24). In 2018 Kerala saw what the flood does to a house with no foundation. Let us build on the rock this week by doing one thing that the Lord has said. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus told a story about two houses. The wise man built his house on rock, and the foolish man on sand. When the rain and the floods came, the house on the rock did not fall! When you listen to Jesus and do what he says, you are building on rock. Be kind, tell the truth, say your prayers, and help at home. Then, when something hard comes, you will stand strong, just like the house on the rock.

Prayer

Father of lights, from whom every good gift comes down and with whom there is no change, we thank you for the gift of life, of faith and of every blessing of this day. Keep us in time of trial, and let us never say that you tempt us. When desire lures us, give us the grace to turn from it at the first thought. You have given us birth by the word of truth: make us the first fruits of your creation, a harvest that is pleasing to you. Teach us not only to say, ‘Lord, Lord’, but to do your will, and set the house of our family, our parish and our heart upon the rock of your Son, so that in the rain and the flood and the wind we may stand. We ask this through Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask what makes a life stand. James says that the one who endures the test will receive the crown of life, and that every good gift, and our very birth by the word of truth, comes from the Father of lights, who never tempts anyone. In Matthew, at the end of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says that it is not calling him “Lord” but doing the will of the Father that matters, and that the wise person builds on the rock of his words. Together they say: trust God’s goodness, and let his word be done in your life.

James 1:12–18 · Every perfect gift is from above

Setting.

The Letter of James is addressed to Jewish Christians “in the Dispersion” (1:1) and is full of practical teaching, in the spirit of Israel’s wisdom writings. Tradition names its author as James, “the brother of the Lord”, the leader of the church in Jerusalem; some scholars think it was written later by a disciple in his name. The Church receives it as Scripture.

The passage follows verses on trials as occasions for joy and endurance (1:2–4). The Greek peirasmos can mean either a test from outside or an inner temptation; James uses it in both senses and shows how God stands to each.

Structure.

  • v. 12: a blessing on one who endures; the crown of life.
  • vv. 13–15: God does not tempt; temptation comes from our own desire, which leads to sin and death.
  • vv. 16–17: do not be deceived; every good gift is from the Father of lights, who does not change.
  • v. 18: by his own will God gave us birth by the word of truth, as first fruits of his creation.

Key words.

  • peirasmos, test, temptation: The same word for the testing of faith (1:2–3) and for the pull toward sin (1:14). God allows us to be tested, to strengthen us, but he never tempts us to evil.
  • stephanos tēs zōēs, crown of life: The crown here is the wreath of the winner or the guest of honour, not of a king. It is eternal life as a gift promised to those who love God (see Revelation 2:10).
  • aparchē, first fruits: The first part of a harvest offered to God. Believers are the first fruits of a new creation that is to follow.

Verse by verse.

“Blessed is anyone who endures temptation. Such a one has stood the test and will receive the crown of life that the Lord has promised to those who love him.” (1:12)

Endurance is not grim effort alone; it is love of God that holds. The crown is promised, and what is promised is given.

“No one, when tempted, should say, “I am being tempted by God”; for God cannot be tempted by evil and he himself tempts no one.” (1:13)

A clear word against blaming God for sin. God is wholly good; evil never comes from his will.

“Every generous act of giving, with every perfect gift, is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change.” (1:17)

The sun and stars change and cast shadows; the Father who made them does not. His goodness is the one steady light.

“In fulfillment of his own purpose he gave us birth by the word of truth, so that we would become a kind of first fruits of his creatures.” (1:18)

Our new birth is the Father’s gift, by the word of truth. It is first of all his work, and we are called to live it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches, with this text, that God is not the author of temptation and that in the Our Father we ask the Father not to let us enter into it, and so to keep us from consenting to evil (CCC 2846–2847). The Church’s teaching on the new birth is that Baptism is a gift of God’s grace, before any merit of ours (CCC 1996–1999, 2008). James’s phrase “the Father of lights” is taken by the Fathers as a name for God as the source of all goodness and light, from whom comes every gift, and the Church’s prayer thanks him for these gifts (in substance).

Matthew 7:21–28 · The wise builder

Setting.

These verses end the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), the first of the five great discourses in Matthew. After the Beatitudes, the teaching on the Law, prayer and trust, Jesus ends with warnings and a parable that call for decision.

The setting is a hill in Galilee. The listeners are the disciples and the crowds (5:1; 7:28). In Palestine a house built on bare sand beside a dry stream bed might stand all summer and be swept away by the first winter flood. A house built on rock remained.

Structure.

  • v. 21: not saying “Lord, Lord”, but doing the Father’s will, opens the kingdom.
  • vv. 22–23: many will point to prophecy, exorcism and miracles; Jesus will say, “I never knew you”.
  • vv. 24–25: the one who hears and acts is like a wise man who built on rock.
  • vv. 26–27: the one who hears and does not act is like a foolish man who built on sand.
  • v. 28: the crowds are astounded at his teaching.

Key words.

  • poieō to thelēma, to do the will: Here poieō (“to do”) is the key verb: it occurs again in verses 24 and 26. The Father’s will is not only to be admired but done.
  • oudepote egnōn, I never knew you: In Hebrew thought to “know” a person is to be in living relationship with him. The tragedy is not a lack of information; the relationship was never real.
  • exeplēssonto, they were astounded: A word of amazement. Matthew ends the sermon by saying that Jesus taught “as one having authority” (7:29): his word is not a commentary but a command that creates.

Verse by verse.

““Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only the one who does the will of my Father in heaven.” (7:21)

The confession of Jesus as Lord is true and necessary. But it is meant to become a life. Faith that is only on the lips is not yet the faith Jesus asks for.

“Then I will declare to them, ‘I never knew you; go away from me, you evildoers.’” (7:23)

The great deeds of the speakers are not denied here; what is missing is the relationship and the doing of the Father’s will. Gifts and signs belong to God, not to us.

““Everyone then who hears these words of mine and acts on them will be like a wise man who built his house on rock.” (7:24)

Hearing and doing belong together. The rock is Jesus’ own word, received and lived.

“The rain fell, the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house, but it did not fall, because it had been founded on rock.” (7:25)

The wise builder is not spared the storm. The house stands because of its foundation.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse in teaching on the Our Father: “Thy will be done” is the prayer that we may do the Father’s will, as Jesus did (CCC 2826). It teaches that our works are done by grace: God first gives us the power to will and do the good, and the merit of our works is due to God’s gift (CCC 2008; Philippians 2:13). So the passage does not set faith against works: it describes living faith, which is, as James says, faith that acts (James 2:17). The Church has long read the rock as Christ’s word and, in Christ, the Church founded on him (Matthew 16:18; CCC 552).

Questions people ask

If Jesus says that works of power are not enough, is faith in him not enough?

Faith is necessary and is the foundation (CCC 161). But faith that is real shows itself in obedience and love, as the same Lord teaches (John 14:15). Jesus is not saying that works earn heaven by themselves; he is saying that calling him Lord means asking him to rule our lives. The power to do the Father’s will is itself his gift (CCC 2008).
