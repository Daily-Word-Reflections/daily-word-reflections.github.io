Readings

  • Epistle Acts 9:20–25 · Saul preaches the Risen Christ
  • Gospel John 5:41–47 · Moses wrote about Christ

Word for the day

“If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me.” John 5:46

Reflection

A few days earlier Saul of Tarsus had been on the road to Damascus with letters from the high priest, ready to arrest anyone who belonged to the Way. Then a light flashed from heaven, a voice asked, ‘Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?’, and he was led blind into the city. After three days Ananias laid hands on him, the scales fell from his eyes, and he was baptised. Today's reading begins at that point, and its first word is the most surprising: “immediately he began to proclaim Jesus in the synagogues, saying, ‘He is the Son of God.’” There was no long apprenticeship, no waiting to become respectable. The man who came to Damascus to silence the name of Jesus now cannot stop speaking it. The people who hear him are astonished: “Is not this the man who made havoc in Jerusalem among those who invoked this name?” The Resurrection does not only raise a dead body in Jerusalem; it raises dead hearts on the road to Damascus. Luke adds: “Saul became increasingly more powerful and confounded the Jews who lived in Damascus by proving that Jesus was the Messiah.” Saul is himself a Jew, preaching in the synagogue, reasoning from the Scriptures of Israel. This is an argument within the one family of Abraham about the Messiah of Israel, and the Church reads it with that love, never as a charge against the Jewish people.

Then comes danger. “After some time had passed, the Jews plotted to kill him, but their plot became known to Saul. They were watching the gates day and night so that they might kill him; but his disciples took him by night and let him down through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket.” Paul himself tells the same story years later in Second Corinthians, adding that the governor under King Aretas also guarded the city. And he tells it in the middle of a list of his weaknesses, as the thing he chooses to boast of. The great apostle, who would one day stand before kings, begins his ministry squeezed into a basket and lowered down a wall at night by his new brothers. He did not save himself; he was carried by the community he had come to destroy. There is an Easter lesson here for every proud heart: the Risen Lord makes us strong, but often he does it by first making us small, and by teaching us to depend on our brothers and sisters in faith.

In the Gospel Jesus is in Jerusalem, in a long debate after healing a man on the sabbath. He speaks to some who oppose him, and his words go deep: “I do not accept glory from human beings.” Then he puts his finger on what makes faith impossible: “How can you believe when you accept glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the one who alone is God?” Faith dies where people live for the applause of others. Then Jesus names Moses, the great lawgiver whom his hearers loved: “If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me.” Moses is not set aside; he is called as a witness. The Law, the Psalms and the Prophets all speak of Christ. That is why the Church never throws away the Old Testament: it is the word of God, and in it Christ is hidden, waiting to be found. On the evening of Easter the Risen Lord himself, “beginning with Moses and all the prophets” (Luke 24:27), opened the Scriptures to the disciples on the road. In the Syro-Malabar Holy Qurbana on Sundays and feasts, a reading from the Law or the Prophets comes before the Apostle and the Gospel: Moses and the prophets still speak in our churches, and they still speak of him. The question Jesus asks is for us too: do we seek the glory that comes from God, or the glory that comes from one another?

Full homily

Dear friends, a few days earlier Saul had come to Damascus to arrest Christians. Then the Risen Lord met him, and he was baptised. Today we hear: immediately he began to proclaim Jesus in the synagogues, saying, He is the Son of God.

No long delay, no waiting to become respectable. The man who came to silence the name now cannot stop speaking it. Easter raises dead hearts as well as dead bodies.

Then danger came, and his new brothers lowered him down the city wall in a basket at night. The great apostle began his work being carried. The Risen Lord often makes us strong by first making us small, and by teaching us to lean on one another.

In the Gospel Jesus says: How can you believe when you accept glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from God? Faith dies where we live for applause.

And he says: If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me. The Old Testament is the word of God, and it speaks of Christ. That is why at the Holy Qurbana we hear the Law and the Prophets before the Gospel.

This week, let us speak the name of Jesus without fear, let ourselves be carried by the prayer of others, and seek only the glory that comes from God. Alleluia! Amen.

For children

Children, Saul used to be against Jesus. But when he met Jesus he changed completely and told everyone, ‘Jesus is the Son of God!’ His friends even saved him by lowering him down a wall in a basket!

Jesus can change any heart. Let us pray for someone who does not know Jesus yet.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of the living God, you turned Saul the persecutor into Paul the apostle, and you were proclaimed by him in the synagogues of Damascus. Open our eyes as you opened his, and our lips to speak your name. Deliver us from seeking the glory that comes from one another, and teach us to seek only the glory that comes from God. Open to us the Law and the Prophets, so that in every page we may find you, risen and living for ever. Amen. Alleluia!

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings ask who Jesus is and where the evidence for him lies. In Damascus, Saul, newly converted, proves from the Scriptures that Jesus is the Messiah and proclaims him as the Son of God, and soon he must escape by night in a basket. In John, Jesus tells those who opposed him that Moses himself wrote about him. The Scriptures of Israel, rightly read, lead to Christ.

Acts 9:20–25 · He is the Son of God

Setting.

Saul had come to Damascus with letters from the high priest to arrest believers (9:1–2). On the road the risen Lord met him, and Ananias baptized him (9:3–19). Now, “immediately”, the persecutor becomes a preacher in the same synagogues where he meant to make arrests.

Paul gives his own account in his letters. He went away into Arabia and returned to Damascus (Galatians 1:17), and he tells of being let down in a basket through a window in the wall when the governor under King Aretas was guarding the city to seize him (2 Corinthians 11:32–33). The “some time” of v. 23 may include the stay in Arabia.

Structure.

  • v. 20: Saul proclaims Jesus as Son of God in the synagogues.
  • v. 21: amazement: is this not the persecutor?
  • v. 22: he grows stronger and proves that Jesus is the Messiah.
  • vv. 23–25: the plot; the watch on the gates; the escape in a basket.

Key words.

  • huios tou theou, Son of God: A rare title in Acts. Saul's first preaching goes to the centre of the faith he had fought against: Jesus is not only a teacher or prophet but the Son.
  • symbibazōn, proving: Literally “bringing together”. Saul places the Scriptures side by side with what happened to Jesus and shows that they fit.

Verse by verse.

“and immediately he began to proclaim Jesus in the synagogues, saying, “He is the Son of God.”” (9:20)
“Saul became increasingly more powerful and confounded the Jews who lived in Damascus by proving that Jesus was the Messiah.” (9:22)

The debate takes place among Jews, about the meaning of Israel's own Scriptures. Saul himself is a Jew and remains proud of it (Philippians 3:5).

“but his disciples took him by night and let him down through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket.” (9:25)

The great apostle begins his ministry in a humble way, hidden in a basket. Paul himself lists it among the things he boasts of, his weaknesses (2 Corinthians 11:30–33).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that the confession of Christ's divine sonship stood at the centre of the apostolic faith from the beginning: Paul, to whom the Father revealed his Son, at once proclaimed in the synagogues, “He is the Son of God” (CCC 442). The story also shows the Church's confidence in God's grace: the man who came to bind believers is now carried to safety by them.

John 5:41–47 · Moses wrote about me

Setting.

After Jesus healed a man at the pool of Bethesda on a sabbath (5:1–9), some of the authorities in Jerusalem began to oppose him, and John says they sought to kill him because he called God his own Father (5:16–18). Jesus answers with a long discourse. In its last part he names his witnesses: John the Baptist, his own works, the Father, and the Scriptures (5:31–40). These closing verses are spoken to those opponents, not to the Jewish people as a whole.

Structure.

  • vv. 41–42: Jesus does not seek human glory.
  • vv. 43–44: they receive one who comes in his own name, not the one who comes in the Father's name.
  • vv. 45–47: Moses, in whom they hope, will be their accuser, for he wrote about Jesus.

Key words.

  • doxa, glory: In John, glory is the weight of honour. The problem is not a lack of learning but of desire: those who seek honour from one another cannot receive the honour that comes from God alone.
  • katēgorōn, accuser: Moses was the great intercessor for Israel (Exodus 32:11–14). Jesus says that the one they trust to speak for them will speak against their unbelief, because his writings point to Jesus.

Verse by verse.

“I do not accept glory from human beings.” (5:41)
“How can you believe when you accept glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the one who alone is God?” (5:44)

Faith is blocked less by hard questions than by the wish to be admired. To believe, a person must care more for what God thinks than for what others think.

“If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me.” (5:46)

Christians have read this especially of Moses' promise, “The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me” (Deuteronomy 18:15).

How the Church reads it.

The Church reads the Old Testament in the light of Christ, while keeping its own lasting value. The Second Vatican Council, quoting St Augustine, says that the New Testament is hidden in the Old and the Old is made manifest in the New (Dei Verbum 16; CCC 129). Jesus' words are no rejection of Moses. They are an invitation to read Moses to the end, where he leads.

Questions people ask

Acts says “the Jews plotted to kill him” (9:23). How should we read this?

Luke means some Jewish opponents in Damascus at that time, not the Jewish people. Paul himself says that the governor under King Aretas was guarding the city (2 Corinthians 11:32), so others were involved too. Saul, the disciples who saved him and the first Church were also Jews. The Church firmly rejects any blame of the Jewish people as a whole (Nostra Aetate 4; CCC 597).

For study and teaching

  • Saul preached “immediately”. Is there a grace I have received that I have been slow to share?
  • Jesus says that seeking glory from one another blocks faith. Whose approval matters most to me in my daily choices?
