Word for the day

“Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the treasury.” Mark 12:43

Reflection

Saint Paul is never more honest than in the seventh chapter of his letter to the Romans. He has just said that Christians have died to the law through the body of Christ, and he knows the objection that will come at once: is the law then something bad? “What then should we say? That the law is sin? By no means!” The law is not the enemy. It is a lamp that shows what is in the room. “If it had not been for the law, I would not have known sin. I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, ‘You shall not covet.’” Paul chooses the last of the Ten Commandments, the only one that concerns the heart rather than the hand. No court can see covetousness; only God and conscience can. And here Paul describes something every one of us has felt: “sin, seizing an opportunity in the commandment, produced in me all kinds of covetousness.” Tell a child not to open a box, and the box becomes the most interesting thing in the house. Sin is a parasite. It has no life of its own; it lives on what is good and twists it. The same commandment that “promised life proved to be death to me,” not because the commandment was bad, but because “sin, seizing an opportunity in the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me.” The word “deceived” takes us back to the garden, where the serpent used God's own command to plant suspicion in Eve's heart. Paul's verdict stands firm: “So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and just and good.” What is wrong is not the law but the heart that cannot keep it.

Why does this matter in the Season of the Apostles? Because the law can show us the right road but cannot give us legs to walk on it. In later Jewish tradition the feast of Weeks, Pentecost, came to be remembered as the day the Law was given at Sinai. Fifty days after the Resurrection, God gave his people something the tablets of stone could not give: the Holy Spirit, writing the law of love on human hearts, as Jeremiah and Ezekiel had promised. St Augustine summed it up in a famous sentence (in substance): the law was given so that grace might be sought; grace was given so that the law might be fulfilled. The apostles went out after Pentecost not with a longer list of rules but with the power to keep the commandments from within. The tenth commandment still stands, and it is still hard: in a world of advertisements, comparison and endless scrolling, covetousness is the air we breathe. But the Spirit who came at Pentecost is stronger than the desire that grows in the dark. Paul will cry out a few verses later, “Who will rescue me from this body of death?” and answer at once, “Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (Romans 7:24–25).

The Gospel puts a living picture beside Paul's teaching. Jesus is in the Temple in the last days before his Passion. He warns against the scribes “who like to walk around in long robes, and to be greeted with respect in the marketplaces, and to have the best seats in the synagogues and places of honor at banquets!” We must read this carefully. Jesus is not condemning all scribes; only a few verses earlier he told a scribe who answered wisely, “You are not far from the kingdom of God” (Mark 12:34). He is warning against a temptation that lives in every religious person in every age, in priests and lay leaders, in parish committees and prayer groups: the love of being seen. Then Jesus sits down “opposite the treasury, and watched the crowd putting money into the treasury.” He watches. The rich put in large sums, and no doubt the sound of their coins was loud. “A poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which are worth a penny.” Jesus calls his disciples, as if to give them a lesson for their whole apostolic life: “Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the treasury. For all of them have contributed out of their abundance; but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on.” The covetous heart of Romans 7 grasps; the widow's heart lets go. She is the opposite of the scribes who “devour widows’ houses”: they take, she gives. And she gives not out of what is left over, but out of her whole life. Within a few days the Lord who watched her will give everything he has, his own body and blood, for the life of the world. The widow is the last person Jesus praises in Mark's account of his public ministry, and she is a picture of him.

Today the First Set calendar also lists Sts Gervasius and Protasius, martyrs of the early Church in Milan. Little is known of their lives. In 386, during a hard struggle between St Ambrose and the imperial court, the bishop found their bodies and laid them beneath the altar of his new basilica in Milan, saying (in substance) that the victorious victims should rest in the place where Christ is the victim. St Augustine, who was in Milan at the time and not yet baptised, recalls in his Confessions the joy of the people and the healing of a blind man. Every altar stone with relics, and every Holy Qurbana offered over the bones of the saints, recalls that day: the martyrs, like the widow, gave everything, and their offering is joined to the one sacrifice of Christ.

Full homily

Dear friends, Saint Paul asks a question today: is the law sin? And he answers, “By no means!” (Romans 7:7). God's commandments are holy and just and good. The problem is not the commandment; the problem is the heart.

Paul gives an example: “You shall not covet.” This is the only commandment about the heart. As soon as we hear it, we notice how much we want what others have: their phone, their house, their success, their life. Sin is like a parasite. It takes the good commandment and twists it against us.

That is why we need more than a law. We need the Holy Spirit. At Pentecost the Spirit came to write the law of love on our hearts. Saint Augustine said: the law was given so that we might look for grace; grace was given so that the law might be kept.

In the Gospel Jesus sits opposite the treasury of the Temple and watches. The rich put in large sums. A poor widow puts in two small coins. And Jesus says that she has given more than all of them, because she gave “everything she had, all she had to live on” (Mark 12:44).

The covetous heart grasps; the widow's heart lets go. She is a picture of Jesus himself, who a few days later gave his whole life on the Cross, and who gives himself to us today in the Holy Qurbana. Let us bring him not what is left over, but our whole life. Amen.

For children

Children, a poor lady put two tiny coins in the offering box at the Temple. Jesus said she gave more than all the rich people, because she gave everything she had, with love. God does not count how much we give; he looks at how much we love. What can you give to Jesus today with a big heart?

Prayer

Holy Father, your law is holy and just and good, but our hearts are weak and easily deceived. Send us the Holy Spirit whom you poured out at Pentecost, to write your commandments on our hearts and to heal the desire that grasps at what is not ours. Lord Jesus, you watched the poor widow give everything she had, and you gave your whole life for us: teach us to give without being seen and to trust you with all we have. With Sts Gervasius and Protasius and all your martyrs, may we offer ourselves with you on the altar. To the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be glory for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings look beneath the surface. Paul asks how a holy commandment could become an occasion of death, and answers that the fault lies not in the law but in sin, which uses even good things against us. In Mark, Jesus looks past the long robes and the large gifts to a poor widow whose two small coins are worth more than all the rest. God judges the heart, and he gives the grace that the law alone could not give. Today the Church also remembers Sts Gervasius and Protasius, early martyrs of Milan whose relics St Ambrose discovered in 386.

Romans 7:7–13 · The law is holy

Setting.

Paul has just said that Christians have died to the law through the body of Christ (7:4–6). A reader might conclude that the law itself was bad. Paul rejects this at once. He speaks in the first person, “I”, but most interpreters, ancient and modern, hear more than his private story: the story of Adam, who received one command and was deceived, and the story of everyone who meets God’s law while still under the power of sin.

Structure.

  • v. 7: the question, “That the law is sin?” and the answer: by no means; the law made sin known, as the command “You shall not covet” made coveting known.
  • vv. 8–11: sin seized its opportunity in the commandment, deceived and killed.
  • vv. 12–13: the conclusion: the law is holy, just and good; sin is shown to be what it is.

Key words.

  • epithymia, coveting, desire: The word of the tenth commandment in the Greek Old Testament (Exodus 20:17). Paul chooses the one commandment that touches the heart rather than the hand: no outward act is needed to break it.
  • aphormē, opportunity: Literally a starting point or base of operations, used of an army’s base. Sin is pictured as an enemy that turns the commandment into its base of attack.
  • exapataō, to deceive: The same verb Paul uses for the serpent deceiving Eve (2 Corinthians 11:3). The echo of Genesis 3 is deliberate: a good command, a lie, and death.

Verse by verse.

“I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, “You shall not covet.”” (7:7)

The law is like a light switched on in a dark room: it does not create the dirt, it shows it.

“For sin, seizing an opportunity in the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me.” (7:11)

Sin is almost a person here, a power that lies and kills. Paul does not blame God’s gift but the enemy who abuses it.

“So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and just and good.” (7:12)

This verse settles the matter. Whatever Paul says against relying on the law, he never says the law is evil.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, quoting this verse, teaches that according to Christian tradition the Law is holy, spiritual and good, yet still imperfect: like a tutor it shows what must be done, but does not of itself give the strength, the grace of the Spirit, to do it (CCC 1963). The Old Law is a preparation for the Gospel (CCC 1964). What the law could not do, Christ does, as Paul will say in Romans 8:3–4. St Augustine, writing on Paul’s teaching, says the law was given so that grace might be sought, and grace so that the law might be fulfilled (in substance, On the Spirit and the Letter).

Mark 12:38–44 · Two small coins

Setting.

These are the last scenes of Jesus’ teaching in the Temple before his words about its destruction (13:1–2). He has been questioned by priests, Pharisees, Sadducees and a scribe. A scribe has just been told he is not far from the kingdom (12:34), so Jesus is not condemning every scribe, but a way of using religion for honour and gain.

According to the Mishnah, there were thirteen trumpet-shaped chests for offerings in the Temple court, probably in the Court of the Women, where both men and women could go. Anyone could see how much a person gave.

Structure.

  • vv. 38–40: a warning against religious display and greed.
  • vv. 41–42: Jesus sits and watches the giving; a poor widow puts in two small coins.
  • vv. 43–44: Jesus calls his disciples and teaches them how God counts.

Key words.

  • lepta duo, two small coins: The lepton was the smallest coin in use. Mark explains for readers in the Roman world that two of them make a quadrans (NRSV “a penny”), the smallest Roman coin.
  • holon ton bion autēs, all she had to live on: Literally “her whole life”. The widow gives not a part but everything. A few days later Jesus will give his whole life.

Verse by verse.

“They devour widows’ houses and for the sake of appearance say long prayers.” (12:40)

Widows were among those the Law of Moses told Israel to protect (Exodus 22:22; Deuteronomy 24:17). Jesus warns against any who would exploit them while appearing devout.

“He sat down opposite the treasury, and watched the crowd putting money into the treasury.” (12:41)

Jesus watches. He sees what no one else sees, and he sees the heart.

“For all of them have contributed out of their abundance; but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on.” (12:44)

God does not measure the size of the gift but what it costs and the love behind it.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism sets this widow before every disciple: Jesus asks us to prefer him to everything and everyone, and gives as an example the poor widow of Jerusalem who gave out of her poverty all that she had to live on (CCC 2544). St Cyprian of Carthage holds her up to the wealthy who come to the Lord’s table empty-handed: a poor widow gave two coins, and the rich should be ashamed to give nothing (in substance, On Works and Almsgiving 15).

Questions people ask

Does Jesus praise the widow, or lament that she was made to give her last coins?

Some modern readers hear the scene as a lament, because Jesus has just condemned those who devour widows’ houses. The words of Jesus, though, are words of praise: she “has put in more than all” the others. The Church has always read it so (CCC 2544). Both truths stand: God treasures the gift of the poor, and those who lead God’s people must never exploit them.

For study and teaching

  • The command “You shall not covet” showed Paul what was in his heart. Which commandment shows me most clearly where I need God’s grace?
  • The widow gave “all she had to live on”. What would it mean for me to give God not my spare things but something of myself?
