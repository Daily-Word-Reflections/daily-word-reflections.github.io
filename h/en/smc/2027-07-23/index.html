Word for the day

“and that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, as you are being rooted and grounded in love.” Ephesians 3:17

Reflection

On this Friday, the day on which the Church remembers the Lord's death, the Epistle gives us a prayer of Saint Paul on his knees: “For this reason I bow my knees before the Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth takes its name.” In a land where the family is so dear, it is a joy to hear that every family, in heaven and on earth, takes its name from the Father of Jesus Christ. Paul then makes three requests. The first: “that, according to the riches of his glory, he may grant that you may be strengthened in your inner being with power through his Spirit.” The second: “that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith.” The third: “as you are being rooted and grounded in love.” The heart in the Bible is not only the seat of feeling; it is the centre of the whole person, where we decide and where we meet God. Christ does not visit the heart as a guest who leaves; he dwells there. And Paul uses two images that a Kerala farmer and a builder understand at once: roots and foundations. A coconut palm that has sent its roots deep survives the monsoon storm; a house with a good foundation stands in the flood. The prophet had already said, “They shall be like a tree planted by water, sending out its roots by the stream” (Jeremiah 17:8).

Then comes the great prayer for understanding: “I pray that you may have the power to comprehend, with all the saints, what is the breadth and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, so that you may be filled with all the fullness of God.” Paul does not name what has breadth, length, height and depth; it is the love of Christ. Saint Augustine (in substance), in a letter, saw in these four dimensions the shape of the Cross: the breadth is the crossbeam where the Lord's hands were stretched out, the length is the upright on which his body hung, the height is the part that rises above the beam, and the depth is the part fixed in the earth and hidden. His love reaches to the whole world, to the whole of time, to the heights of God and to the depths of our sin. It “surpasses knowledge”, and yet we are asked to know it, not as we know a fact but as we know a person, with all the saints, within the Church. The reading ends with the song that every Christian sings: “Now to him who by the power at work within us is able to accomplish abundantly far more than all we can ask or imagine, to him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus to all generations, forever and ever. Amen.”

The Gospel puts before us a blind man and a Lord who does not hurry. “They came to Bethsaida. Some people brought a blind man to him and begged him to touch him.” The man has friends who carry his need to Jesus. “He took the blind man by the hand and led him out of the village; and when he had put saliva on his eyes and laid his hands on him, he asked him, ‘Can you see anything?’” Jesus uses ordinary things, a hand and spittle, because he heals the whole person, body and trust together. The answer is the most honest sentence in the Gospel of Mark: “I can see people, but they look like trees, walking.” This is the only healing in Mark that comes in two stages. “Then Jesus laid his hands on his eyes again; and he looked intently and his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly.” The Lord does not scold the man for seeing so little. He touches him again.

Mark has placed this story with great care. Right after it Jesus asks his disciples, “Who do you say that I am?” and Peter answers, “You are the Messiah” (Mark 8:29): he sees the shape, but not yet clearly, because he cannot yet accept the Cross. The disciples too are men who see people like trees walking. Saint Bede (in substance) and other Fathers saw in the two touches the way the Lord enlightens us gradually, and we should not be ashamed to be beginners. Today's two readings are about seeing with the heart: Paul prays that we may comprehend the dimensions of Christ's love, and the blind man is healed in stages. We do not understand the Cross all at once. We learn it year by year, in Qurbana, in sorrow, in prayer, until the day we see everything clearly. Let us bring our blind friends to Jesus, as the people of Bethsaida did, and let us let him take us by the hand, outside the village of our habits, and touch us again.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Saint Paul kneels and prays for us: “that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, as you are being rooted and grounded in love” (Ephesians 3:17). That prayer is also for you and for your family.

Christ wants to live in your heart, not to visit it. A tree planted by a stream does not fear the heat, because its roots are deep. A house with a deep foundation stands in the flood. So the love of Christ is the ground where our life stands.

Paul speaks of the breadth, length, height and depth of Christ's love. Saint Augustine said that this is the Cross, which stretches its arms to the whole world. On this Friday we remember that nothing can be too wide, too long, too high or too deep for the love of Christ: not our worst sin, not our deepest sorrow.

In the Gospel Jesus heals a blind man in two steps. The first time the man sees people like trees walking, and Jesus does not scold him. He touches his eyes again, and the man sees everything clearly (Mark 8:25). The Lord is patient with our dim sight.

This week let us kneel for our family each evening, bring someone to Jesus by prayer, and ask him to touch our eyes again where we see only a little. Amen.

For children

Children, a man could not see. His friends brought him to Jesus. Jesus touched his eyes, and the man saw people like trees walking! So Jesus touched him again, and then he could see everything. Jesus is never in a hurry with us. Let us bring our friends to Jesus by our prayers, and ask him to help us see how much he loves us!

Prayer

Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth takes its name, we bow our knees before you. Strengthen us through your Spirit in our inner being; let Christ dwell in our hearts through faith, and root and ground us in love. Give us the power to comprehend, with all the saints, the breadth and length and height and depth of the love of Christ, and fill us with all the fullness of God. Take us by the hand as you took the blind man, and touch us again where we see only dimly, until we see everything clearly. Glory to you in the Church and in Christ Jesus to all generations, for ever and ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about seeing. Paul bends his knees and prays that his readers may be given strength to take in the breadth and length and height and depth of Christ’s love, a love beyond all knowing. In Mark, a blind man is healed in two stages, and slowly sees everything clearly. The Church has long seen in this a picture of the way faith grows: not all at once, but through the patient touch of Christ, until we see clearly and are filled with the fullness of God.

Ephesians 3:14–21 · Rooted and grounded in love

Setting.

The letter has described the mystery hidden for ages, that Gentiles are fellow heirs with the Jews in Christ (3:1–13). Now the writer breaks into prayer. The passage is the second great prayer of Ephesians (the first is in 1:15–23), and it is a single long sentence in Greek, with a prayer for strength (vv. 16–17a), for love and understanding (vv. 17b–19), and a doxology (vv. 20–21).

Ephesians presents itself as a letter of Paul. Many scholars think it was written in his name by a close disciple, while others hold that Paul wrote it himself; the Church receives it as inspired Scripture and as the apostolic teaching of Paul.

Structure.

  • vv. 14–15: Paul bends his knees before the Father, from whom every family takes its name.
  • vv. 16–17a: the prayer for strength by the Spirit, and for Christ to dwell in hearts through faith.
  • vv. 17b–19: rooted and grounded in love, to grasp the dimensions of Christ’s love and be filled with the fullness of God.
  • vv. 20–21: the doxology: glory to the God who can do more than we ask or imagine.

Key words.

  • patria, family, fatherhood: The Greek word is a play on patēr, father. Every group of people that descends from one father, on earth or in heaven, owes its name and existence to the Father of all.
  • errizōmenoi kai tethemeliōmenoi, rooted and grounded: Two images joined: a plant with roots, a building with foundations. Love is both the soil and the ground on which the Christian stands.
  • plērōma, fullness: The completeness of God’s own life. The prayer asks that believers be filled with it, which is nothing less than a share in God’s own life.

Verse by verse.

“I pray that, according to the riches of his glory, he may grant that you may be strengthened in your inner being with power through his Spirit” (3:16)

The strength is not human willpower. It comes from the Spirit, and it works in the “inner being”, where a person is most truly himself or herself.

“and that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith, as you are being rooted and grounded in love.” (3:17)

Christ makes his home in the heart as in a house. The dwelling is through faith, and the soil is love.

“what is the breadth and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge” (3:18–19)

Paul does not say what the four dimensions measure. St Augustine and others saw in them the shape of the cross; some read them as the whole of God’s plan. All agree that love of Christ is greater than any measure, and that it can still be known, though not fully grasped. “To know what surpasses knowledge” is a deliberate paradox.

“Now to him who by the power at work within us is able to accomplish abundantly far more than all we can ask or imagine” (3:20)

The prayer ends in praise. God’s generosity is greater than our prayers, and his power already at work in the Church.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism, quoting the opening verses, teaches that no one is father as God is Father, and that every human fatherhood and family takes its name from him (CCC 239, citing Ephesians 3:14–15). The passage is trinitarian in shape: the Father who grants, the Spirit who strengthens, Christ who dwells in the heart. St Augustine reads the “breadth and length and height and depth” as the cross of Christ, whose arms embrace the world and whose root goes deep into the earth (in substance, Letter 140).

Mark 8:22–26 · He saw everything clearly

Setting.

This miracle is found only in Mark. It comes at a turning point of the Gospel. Just before it, Jesus has asked his disciples whether their hearts are hardened, and whether, having eyes, they fail to see (8:17–18). Just after it comes Peter’s confession at Caesarea Philippi (8:27–30). The healing of a blind man stands between a question about the disciples’ blindness and the first moment they begin to see who Jesus is.

Bethsaida lay on the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee, a fishing town known to the Gospels as the home of Philip, Andrew and Peter (John 1:44). Its people had already seen Jesus’ deeds (Matthew 11:21).

Structure.

  • v. 22: friends bring a blind man and beg Jesus to touch him.
  • vv. 23–24: Jesus takes him outside the village, puts saliva on his eyes, lays hands on him; the man sees, but not clearly.
  • v. 25: a second laying on of hands, and he sees everything clearly.
  • v. 26: Jesus sends him home and asks him not to enter the village.

Key words.

  • diablepō, to look intently: After the second touch the man “looked intently”. The verb suggests looking through, seeing clearly. It is the beginning of true sight.
  • apokathistēmi, to restore: Used of restoring health and, in Mark 9:12, of the restoring of all things. The cure is a sign of the restoration that Christ brings.
  • kōmē, village: Jesus leads the man out of the village (v. 23) and sends him away from it (v. 26). The healing is private, and it does not seek publicity, a theme of Mark’s Gospel.

Verse by verse.

“He took the blind man by the hand and led him out of the village” (8:23)

Jesus does not heal from afar. He takes the man by the hand: the healing begins with closeness. The man’s journey with Jesus is already a journey from darkness.

“when he had put saliva on his eyes and laid his hands on him, he asked him, “Can you see anything?”” (8:23)

Jesus uses touch and saliva, as he does with the deaf man in Mark 7:33. He also asks a question: the man is invited to take part, to tell what he sees.

“I can see people, but they look like trees, walking.” (8:24)

An honest answer. The man does not claim more than he sees. Partial sight is real sight, but it is not yet clear.

“Then Jesus laid his hands on his eyes again; and he looked intently and his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly.” (8:25)

The only healing in the Gospels given in two stages. Many interpreters see here a picture of the disciples, who have begun to see (the confession of Peter) but do not yet see clearly (Peter will soon reject the Cross, 8:32–33).

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism notes that Jesus often asks the sick to believe, and uses signs such as spittle, the laying on of hands, mud and washing (CCC 1504). The Church has long read this gradual healing as a picture of the growth of faith and of Christian life: faith is a light that grows, not an instant possession. The gift of sight is always Christ’s doing, and he takes the person by the hand, as he does in Baptism, which the early Church called “illumination” (CCC 1216).

Questions people ask

Why did Jesus need two attempts to heal the man?

The Gospel does not say Jesus lacked power; it shows a gradual healing as a sign. Jesus is never limited. Most commentators understand that Mark placed the story here to illustrate how the disciples were slowly coming to see. There is also a kindness in the method: Jesus lets the man’s sight grow in the way he can bear, and asks him what he sees.

For study and teaching

  • Paul prays that Christ may dwell in our hearts “through faith”. What would it look like for Christ to be truly at home in my heart this week?
  • The blind man saw “people like trees” before he saw clearly. In which part of my faith do I still see only dimly, and what would let Christ touch it again?
