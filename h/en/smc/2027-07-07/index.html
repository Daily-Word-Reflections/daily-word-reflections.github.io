Word for the day

“My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me.” John 10:27

Reflection

Paul's letter to Timothy reaches its climax in a charge, and the charge begins with a title: “But as for you, man of God.” In the Old Testament this is the name of Moses, Samuel, Elijah and the prophets, people who belonged wholly to God. Timothy is a young bishop, and the title reminds him who he is. What follows is a list of what to run from and what to run after: “shun all this”, that is, the love of money which Paul has just called the root of all kinds of evil, and “pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance, gentleness”. Six words, and not one of them is loud. Then the athlete's picture: “Fight the good fight of the faith; take hold of the eternal life, to which you were called and for which you made the good confession in the presence of many witnesses.” The fight is not against people. It is the long struggle to stay faithful, and the prize is already in the hand, because eternal life is something to be taken hold of today.

Paul sets Timothy's confession beside the confession of Christ himself, “who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession”. Before the Roman governor Jesus said that he was a King, and that his kingdom was not of this world, and he said it knowing the cost. The charge ends in a hymn that Paul may have taken from the liturgy of his Church: Christ will be manifested “at the right time”; God is “the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords. It is he alone who has immortality and dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see.” We stand at the beginning of Kaitha, the season in which the Church looks at her growth and waits for her Lord. The reading reminds us that the harvest belongs to the One who dwells in unapproachable light, and that our task is to keep the commandment “without spot or blame” until he appears.

In the Gospel Jesus walks in the portico of Solomon at the feast of the Dedication, the winter feast in which the Jewish people remember the cleansing and re-dedication of the Temple. Some of the leaders gather round him: “How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.” Jesus has already told them, and he points them to his works: “The works that I do in my Father’s name testify to me.” Then he says something hard: “you do not believe, because you do not belong to my sheep.” This is not a rejection of the Jewish people, from whom Jesus, Mary and the apostles came; John's Gospel, in speaking of “the Jews”, usually means these particular leaders in Jerusalem, and the Church teaches that what happened in the Passion cannot be charged against all the Jews of that time, still less against the Jews of today (Nostra Aetate 4). The sentence names a danger for every listener, including us: one can stand beside Jesus, ask him questions, and never listen.

Then comes the shepherd's voice. “My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one will snatch them out of my hand.” Hearing, being known, following: three steps, and the whole of Christian life is in them. In the villages of Kerala a shepherd's call is not common now, but every mother knows how a child in a crowded market can pick out one voice from a hundred. Our phones fill every hour with voices, and many of them sound confident. The sheep of Christ learn his voice in the Gospel read at the Holy Qurbana, in daily prayer and in the family Rosary, and then they can tell it from the others. St Gregory the Great taught that when the Lord says “I know my sheep” he means “I love them”. We are not held by our own grip, which grows tired, but by the hand that no one can pry open.

Full homily

Dear friends, in today's Gospel some people ask Jesus: “If you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.” Jesus answers that he has already told them by his works. The problem was not that they had not heard; it was that they were not ready to listen.

Then Jesus says something very beautiful: “My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me” (John 10:27). Three things: they hear, he knows them, they follow. This is the whole of the Christian life. Listening in prayer, being known and loved by the Lord, and walking behind him.

Saint Paul tells Timothy, “Fight the good fight of the faith; take hold of the eternal life” (1 Timothy 6:12). The fight is not with people. It is the daily struggle to stay faithful: to be gentle when we want to shout, to be honest when it costs us, to pray when we are tired.

Many voices call us today: the phone, advertisements, fear, our own pride. But the sheep who spend time with the Shepherd know his voice. Let us read the Gospel every day and say the family prayer together, and we will know which voice to follow.

And the Lord promises: “No one will snatch them out of my hand” (John 10:28). We may be weak, but his hand is strong. Let us hold to him in this season of Kaitha, and he will bring us home. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus is the Good Shepherd, and you are his little sheep! A shepherd knows each sheep by name, and the sheep know his voice. Speak with Jesus every morning and every night, and you will learn his voice. Then, when someone asks you to do something wrong, you will hear Jesus in your heart say: ‘Come this way, little sheep.’ And nobody can take you out of his hand!

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Good Shepherd, you spoke plainly to those who asked, and you still speak to us in your word. Open our ears to your voice among the many voices of the world; let us hear you, be known by you, and follow you. Make us men and women of God, pursuing righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance and gentleness. Teach us to fight the good fight of the faith and to make the good confession by the way we live. Hold us in your hand, from which no one can snatch us, until the day when you are revealed, King of kings and Lord of lords, who with the Father and the Holy Spirit live and reign for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Both readings are about faithful witness and the Lord who keeps those who belong to him. In the First Letter to Timothy, a young minister is told to fight the good fight of faith, holding to the confession he made, as Christ made his own good confession before Pilate. In John, at the winter feast of the Dedication, Jesus is pressed to say plainly whether he is the Messiah, and answers that his works already speak and that his sheep hear his voice. In the first week of Kaitha, the Church turns from the Apostles’ season to the long summer of ordinary discipleship: to hear the Shepherd’s voice, to follow, and to keep the faith to the end.

1 Timothy 6:11–16 · Fight the good fight of the faith

Setting.

First Timothy is one of the Pastoral Letters, written to guide a young leader in the care of a church at Ephesus. The Church receives it as Scripture from the apostle Paul’s circle; it is traditionally attributed to Paul, while many scholars think a close disciple wrote it in his name after his death. The teaching is the same in either case.

The passage comes at the end of the letter, after warnings against love of money (6:3–10). “But as for you” turns from those false teachers to Timothy himself, and the letter closes with a solemn charge and a hymn of praise to God.

Structure.

  • v. 11: flee what is wrong; pursue six virtues.
  • v. 12: fight the good fight; take hold of eternal life to which you were called.
  • vv. 13–14: a charge in the presence of God and of Christ, who made the good confession before Pilate: keep the commandment until the Lord appears.
  • vv. 15–16: praise of God, the blessed and only Sovereign, King of kings, dwelling in unapproachable light.

Key words.

  • agōnizou ton kalon agōna, fight the good fight: The picture is the athletic contest. Agōn gives our word “agony”; it can mean a race or a struggle. The faith is not only believed but striven for.
  • homologia, confession: Open acknowledgement of faith, here probably made at Timothy’s baptism or ordination. Christ is the model: he also “made the good confession”.
  • epiphaneia, manifestation, appearing: The word the Pastoral Letters use for the coming of Christ, at his first coming and at the end. Timothy keeps the faith with that day in view.

Verse by verse.

“But as for you, man of God, shun all this; pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance, gentleness.” (6:11)

“Man of God” was the old title of Moses and the prophets. Timothy is named in their line. Shunning is only half the task; the other half is to pursue what is good.

“Fight the good fight of the faith; take hold of the eternal life, to which you were called” (6:12)

Eternal life is both a gift already offered and a prize to be held fast. The Christian runs toward what has already been given.

“In the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession, I charge you” (6:13)

The name of Pilate is in the Creed because the Passion happened in real history. Christ’s confession before an earthly judge is the pattern for all who must give witness.

“It is he alone who has immortality and dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see;” (6:16)

The charge ends in worship. The God we serve is beyond all our seeing, and yet he has come near in Christ.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that Christ “bore witness to the truth” before Pilate, and that Christians are not to be ashamed of testifying to the Lord (CCC 2471). The Church has long read Paul’s picture of the good fight as the whole of the Christian life, with Baptism as the beginning of the contest. The hymn in verse 16 is used by the Fathers to teach that God cannot be seen by creatures unaided and is known only as he reveals himself (in substance; compare CCC 1028).

John 10:22–28 · My sheep hear my voice

Setting.

The festival of the Dedication (Hanukkah) remembered the re-dedication of the altar of the Temple in 164 BC under Judas Maccabeus, after the Temple had been profaned (1 Maccabees 4:52–59). It fell in winter, and John mentions the cold. Jesus walks in the portico of Solomon, a covered colonnade on the east side of the Temple.

This scene follows the Good Shepherd discourse earlier in the chapter (10:1–18). “The Jews” in John’s Gospel here means the leaders gathered round him, not the Jewish people; Jesus and all his first disciples were Jews. Their question is a real one, and he answers it.

Structure.

  • vv. 22–23: the feast of the Dedication; Jesus in the portico of Solomon.
  • v. 24: the question: if you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.
  • vv. 25–26: his works in his Father’s name are his testimony; they do not believe because they are not of his flock.
  • vv. 27–28: the sheep hear, are known, follow, and receive eternal life; no one will snatch them from his hand.

Key words.

  • enkainia, dedication: The festival that gives the passage its place and light. At the feast of the re-consecrated Temple, Jesus appears as the one in whom God truly dwells among his people.
  • ta erga, the works: In John, Jesus’ works are signs that reveal who he is and whose Son he is. The question “are you the Messiah?” has been answered by what he does, not only by what he says.
  • akouō, I hear, to hear: In John, to hear his voice means to listen and obey. The sheep recognise the Shepherd’s voice (10:3–4).

Verse by verse.

“So the Jews gathered around him and said to him, “How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Messiah, tell us plainly.”” (10:24)

They ask in the language of their hope. The difficulty is not that Jesus is silent but that his answer is not the kind they wanted: a Messiah who is also the Son of the Father.

“Jesus answered, “I have told you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in my Father’s name testify to me;” (10:25)

He points to his deeds. The same Jesus who speaks the Father’s words does the Father’s works.

“My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me.” (10:27)

Three things mark the flock: they hear, he knows them, they follow. To be known by Christ comes first; it is his love that makes the following possible.

“I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one will snatch them out of my hand.” (10:28)

A promise of the Shepherd’s strength, not a licence for carelessness. We hold to it by hope and perseverance, listening to his voice each day.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that the Church is the sheepfold whose gate is Christ, and the flock of which God himself is the shepherd (CCC 754). The promise that no one will snatch the sheep from his hand is read by the Church as a ground of hope: Christians hope for the grace of final perseverance, and ask it in prayer (CCC 2016). The Fathers read the works of Christ in this passage as testimony of his divinity: the Son who does the works of the Father is one with the Father, as the next verses say (10:30).

Questions people ask

If no one can snatch the sheep from Christ’s hand, can a Christian still be lost?

The promise is a true one: the Shepherd does not let go. The Church also teaches that we keep our own will and can turn from him by grave sin, and so we are called to keep faith and to ask for perseverance (CCC 2016). Hope in Christ’s hand and care for our own walk belong together, as in Timothy’s charge to fight the good fight.

For study and teaching

  • Timothy is told to pursue six qualities: righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance, gentleness. Which one do I most need this week, and what is one step I can take toward it?
  • Jesus says his sheep hear his voice. In what quiet place or moment of the day do I give him room to speak?
