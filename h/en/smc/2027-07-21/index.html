Word for the day

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” 2 Corinthians 5:17

Reflection

Paul has just said why he cannot stay silent: “For the love of Christ urges us on, because we are convinced that one has died for all; therefore all have died.” Today's reading continues: “And he died for all, so that those who live might live no longer for themselves, but for him who died and was raised for them.” The death and rising of Christ is not only something that happened to him; it is something that happened to us, and it changes how we live and how we see. “From now on, therefore, we regard no one from a human point of view”: the neighbour I have labelled by caste, party, income or an old quarrel is now seen as someone for whom Christ died. Then comes one of the great sentences of the New Testament: “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” For the Syro-Malabar faithful this is the language of the baptismal font, where we go down with Christ and rise to “walk in newness of life” (Romans 6:4), and it is the language of every confession and every Qurbana in which the old is forgiven and the new begins. God had promised it long before through Isaiah: “I am about to do a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?” (Isaiah 43:19).

The whole movement begins with God: “All this is from God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ, and has given us the ministry of reconciliation; that is, in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting the message of reconciliation to us.” Reconciliation is not two parties meeting halfway; the offended party makes the first move, at his own cost. Then comes the astonishing title: “So we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us; we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” An ambassador lives in a foreign land, carries the word of the king, and is received as the king himself would be received. God makes an appeal, he does not command, and he makes it through human voices: a priest in the confessional, a mother who ends a family quarrel, a neighbour who says sorry first. The reading ends at the heart of the mystery: “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” The sinless one took our place, and in his sacrifice we are given a place in God's own righteousness.

The Gospel shows how this ministry began, with four fishermen. “As Jesus passed along the Sea of Galilee, he saw Simon and his brother Andrew casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen. And Jesus said to them, ‘Follow me and I will make you fish for people.’ And immediately they left their nets and followed him.” A little farther on he sees James and John “in their boat mending the nets”; “Immediately he called them; and they left their father Zebedee in the boat with the hired men, and followed him.” Nothing is said about their qualifications. Jesus does not call the learned of Jerusalem but men with the smell of fish and the habit of hard work, and he gives them a new work, to bring people out of the waters of death into life. This is the beginning of the apostolic Church which the Season of Kaitha honours. The fishermen of Galilee became the ambassadors of Paul's letter; the call was theirs, and the message was entrusted to them.

In Kerala, with its fishing villages along the coast, from Vypin to Vizhinjam, the picture needs no explanation: the early morning, the mended net, the boat pushed out into the waves. Every Christian has been called, in his or her own trade, to be an ambassador of reconciliation, in the home, at the workplace, in the parish and on the road. Gregory the Great (in substance) noticed that Peter and Andrew had little to leave, only a net and a boat, and yet the Lord accepted it as a great gift, because the kingdom of God is worth exactly as much as we have, and we give what we have. The first step is the same for all of us: be reconciled to God, and then go and be reconciled to the brother or sister whom we have not spoken to for years.

Full homily

Dear friends, today Saint Paul speaks of something wonderful: “So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (2 Corinthians 5:17). Christ died for all, so that we may live for him.

God has done the first thing: in Christ he reconciled the world to himself, not counting our sins against us (2 Corinthians 5:19). Now he gives this work to us. We are his ambassadors. God makes his appeal through us: “be reconciled to God” (2 Corinthians 5:20).

In the Gospel Jesus walks along the lake and sees fishermen at work. He says: “Follow me and I will make you fish for people.” And immediately they left their nets and followed him (Mark 1:17–18). They were ordinary men with ordinary jobs, and the Lord gave them a great mission.

Each of us has a net we are holding: a habit, a grudge, a fear, a comfort. And each of us has a person whom we have not forgiven. The Lord calls us to leave the net and to make the first move toward peace.

This week let us go to the sacrament of reconciliation, and let us ask someone's pardon, or give ours. Then we shall be what Paul says we are: God's ambassadors. Amen.

For children

Children, Jesus called four fishermen and they came at once! They left their nets and their boats. Jesus calls you too, to be his friend and helper. You can be his messenger of peace: when two friends quarrel, you can help them make up. Say sorry first, and forgive quickly. That is what an ambassador of Jesus does!

Prayer

Father of mercy, in Christ you reconciled the world to yourself and did not count our trespasses against us. Make us a new creation. Give us the courage of the fishermen of Galilee, who left their nets at your word, and make us ambassadors of your peace in our homes, our parish and our land. Where there is a quarrel, let us be the first to speak; where there is a wound, let us bring your healing. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, for ever. Amen.

Exegesis

The readings as one lesson

Paul describes what has happened in Christ: God was reconciling the world to himself, and the person who belongs to Christ is a new creation. Mark shows how that new creation begins in one ordinary place, a lakeshore, where Jesus calls four fishermen away from their nets. In both readings the first move is God’s. He reconciles; he calls. Our part is to accept the reconciliation and to follow, and then to carry the same invitation to others.

2 Corinthians 5:15–21 · A new creation

Setting.

Paul is defending his ministry to a community that has doubted him. He says that what drives him is “the love of Christ” (5:14): since one died for all, all have died, and those who live no longer live for themselves. These verses draw out the consequences: a new way of looking at people, a new creation, and a ministry of reconciliation.

Structure.

  • v. 15: Christ died for all, so that the living might live for him.
  • v. 16: a new way of seeing people and Christ himself.
  • v. 17: anyone in Christ is a new creation.
  • vv. 18–19: God’s work of reconciliation, and the message entrusted to Paul and his companions.
  • vv. 20–21: the appeal: be reconciled to God; the exchange by which Christ takes our sin and gives us God’s righteousness.

Key words.

  • katallassō, to reconcile: The word comes from the exchange of money and then from restoring friendship after a quarrel. Here God does the reaching out: it is not the offended person who is reconciled by our efforts, but God who reconciles us.
  • kainē ktisis, new creation: The same phrase is used in Galatians 6:15. It recalls Isaiah’s promise of “new heavens and a new earth” (Isaiah 65:17). In Christ the new age has begun.
  • presbeuō, to be an ambassador: An ambassador speaks for a king and carries his authority. Paul does not speak for himself; God is making his appeal through the apostles’ voice.

Verse by verse.

“So if anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: everything old has passed away; see, everything has become new!” (5:17)

The Greek has no verb in the first clause, which can be read “if anyone is in Christ: new creation!” The Church has long connected the phrase with Baptism, where a person is made new.

“in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them” (5:19)

Reconciliation begins in God. He did not wait for the world to make the first step; the initiative is in the cross and Resurrection.

“we are ambassadors for Christ, since God is making his appeal through us; we entreat you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.” (5:20)

Paul speaks to Christians as well as to the world. Reconciliation is a daily need as well as a first conversion.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (5:21)

One of the most compressed lines in Paul. “Made to be sin” does not mean that Christ sinned, for he “knew no sin”. Many translate it as “made a sin offering” (the Greek word can mean both); in either sense Christ took our sin on himself, and we receive God’s own righteousness.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that God sent his Son to take away our sins, quoting this very verse: Jesus “knew no sin”, but out of love for us, God made him “to be sin” (CCC 602–603). The ministry of reconciliation, passed by Christ to the apostles, is exercised in the sacrament of Penance (CCC 1442, citing 2 Corinthians 5:18). The Church also sees in the “new creation” the grace of Baptism (CCC 1265).

Mark 1:16–20 · Follow me

Setting.

Mark has just reported Jesus’ first words: “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has come near; repent, and believe in the good news” (1:15). The very next scene is the call of the first disciples. Jesus does not first collect a following and then teach; he first calls a few people to be with him, so that the good news will spread through them.

Capernaum and the north shore of the Sea of Galilee were busy with fishing, an honest trade that required teamwork. Simon and Andrew cast the net; James and John mend their nets. Zebedee has hired men, which suggests a family business with some means.

Structure.

  • vv. 16–18: Simon and Andrew are called while casting their net, and follow at once.
  • vv. 19–20: James and John are called while mending their nets, and leave their father and the boat.

Key words.

  • deute opisō mou, come after me: Literally “come behind me”. A disciple is one who walks behind a teacher. The word suggests not an idea but a person, and a road.
  • halieis anthrōpōn, fishers of people: Jeremiah 16:16 uses the picture of fishers sent by God. Here the image is not of capture for harm but of gathering people into the net of the kingdom.
  • euthys, immediately: A favourite word of Mark’s, used dozens of times. It gives his Gospel its urgency: the kingdom is near, and the answer must not be put off.

Verse by verse.

“Follow me and I will make you fish for people.” (1:17)

Jesus promises to make them what they are not yet. The call is also a gift: he will form them.

“And immediately they left their nets and followed him.” (1:18)

The response is prompt. They leave the tools of their trade, though Mark tells us a little later that Simon still had a home in Capernaum (Mark 1:29).

“they left their father Zebedee in the boat with the hired men, and followed him.” (1:20)

A real cost: leaving a father and a family business. Jesus does not despise the family; he asks that the call be put first.

How the Church reads it.

The Catechism teaches that from the beginning Jesus associated his disciples with his own life, revealed the mystery of the kingdom to them, and gave them a share in his mission (CCC 787). St John Chrysostom, commenting on the calling of the fishermen, remarks that Christ chose simple, unlettered men so that the power of the Gospel would be seen to be God’s and not human eloquence (in substance, Homilies on Matthew).

Questions people ask

Does “made him to be sin” mean that Christ became a sinner?

No. Paul says in the same sentence that Christ “knew no sin”. The Church’s faith is that Christ was sinless (Hebrews 4:15). The sentence means that he took on himself the burden of our sin, entering fully into our condition so as to heal it, as the suffering servant of Isaiah 53 bore the sins of many. Many scholars also hear a sacrificial sense: God made him a sin offering.

For study and teaching

  • “Everything old has passed away.” What is one old habit or grudge that I am being invited to leave behind through Christ’s reconciliation?
  • The four fishermen left their nets. What is the “net” that I find hardest to put down when Christ calls?
